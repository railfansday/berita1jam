Is Love For Dogs Always "A Many Splendored Thing?"

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Updated November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Kaja Perina

I know I'm not alone to have experienced people say something like, "I love my dog" and then observe countless different sorts of human-dog relationships that can change in the blink of an eye. I'm not saying that the people who claim they love their dog don't, but in many cases when they tell me that and I see how they mistreat their canine companion I can only say, "I glad they don't love me."

I've always wanted to know more about the variegated nature of human-dog relationships and was pleased to learn of Melina Jacques' recent book What It Really Means To Love Dogs: A Cultural And Ethical Exploration in which she discusses "a set of contradictions that are widely experienced but rarely named. Certain forms of intrusion become routine, while those expected to accommodate them are often left without a clear way to articulate what is happening."

Marc Bekoff: What are some of the topics you consider and what are some of your major messages?

Melina Jacques: One of the book’s central arguments is that dogs have become powerful cultural symbols associated with goodness, compassion, love, loyalty, and virtue. A significant portion of the book explores how this symbolic status emerged and why it is so vigorously defended.

The book examines how this symbolic status influences public attitudes toward dogs and can discourage critical discussion of issues that would be readily and openly addressed in other contexts. It explores the consequences of dog ownership for human communities, including attacks, chronic noise, neighbourhood conflict, relationship breakdown, and the diversion of resources and attention away from other priorities. For example, dog attacks in the United States result in reconstructive surgery approximately every 19 minutes, with children disproportionately represented among the victims. Yet discussions of dog attacks are often redirected toward individual circumstances or responsible ownership rather than examining broader questions about the normalization of dog ownership and public safety.

Persistent barking can have consequences far beyond sleep deprivation. Repeated exposure to unwanted, unpredictable noise can activate the body’s physiological stress-response systems, involving stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline. Over time, chronic noise exposure can contribute to sustained psychological distress and adverse cardiovascular and metabolic health effects, effectively making people’s own homes sources of chronic environmental stress.

Conflicts over dogs' behaviour and the prioritization of dogs' needs can also contribute to tension and relationship breakdown. These are not simply isolated inconveniences but consequences of a cultural environment in which the presence and interests of dogs are often treated as beyond question. Municipalities also devote resources to animal shelters and responding to complaints and attacks.

I also examine the ecological impacts of dogs, including predation, disease transmission, disturbance of wildlife, nutrient pollution, and threats to vulnerable species. Research discussed in the book implicates dogs in 11 vertebrate extinctions and identifies nearly 200 species worldwide that are threatened by their impacts.

These impacts extend beyond direct predation. The scent of dog urine, for example, can alter wildlife behaviour, causing wild animals to avoid otherwise suitable and valuable habitat. Dog waste also introduces nutrients into the environment, contributing to nutrient pollution and affecting surrounding ecosystems. Dogs can also introduce infectious diseases and parasites into wildlife populations. Free-ranging dogs, in particular, can act as reservoirs or sources of transmission for pathogens, such as parvovirus or canine distemper, that affect wild carnivores and other susceptible species, sometimes placing additional pressure on already vulnerable populations.

Hybridization presents another ecological concern. Domestic dogs can interbreed with wild canids, including grey wolves and golden jackals, introducing domestic-dog genes into wild populations and potentially compromising their genetic integrity.

Another major theme concerns dogs themselves. Although dogs are widely regarded as among the most loved animals on Earth, I question whether many aspects of modern dog ownership truly serve their interests. For example, breeding dogs to satisfy human preferences for particular physical characteristics can produce animals with serious inherited health problems, raising questions about whether commercial interests and aesthetic preferences are being prioritized over dogs’ physical well-being and quality of life.

Similarly, behaviours that may reflect frustration or unmet needs arising from unsuitable living conditions are increasingly being managed with psychotropic medications. Rather than addressing the underlying circumstances contributing to dogs’ distress, we may simply be medicating behaviours that humans find inconvenient or undesirable.

The book also explores the economic and psychological forces that sustain dog culture, including the role of the pet industry, social incentives, identity, and moral self-image. The pet industry, for example, has developed an enormous commercial ecosystem around dog ownership, encompassing food, accessories, grooming, training, veterinary services, insurance, and other products and services. These commercial interests benefit from the normalization and expansion of dog ownership.

At the psychological level, dog ownership can also become intertwined with personal identity and moral self-image. People may regard owning or adopting a dog as evidence of their compassion and kindness, making criticism of dog-related practices feel like a personal attack rather than an opportunity to examine the consequences of those practices. The book explores how these economic interests and cultural beliefs reinforce one another, helping to sustain a culture that is rarely subjected to critical scrutiny.

At its broadest level, the book asks a simple question: What does it actually mean to love a dog? My conclusion is that caring responsibly for animals requires us to look beyond sentiment and think honestly about the consequences of the relationships we create with animals.

MB: How does your book differ from others that are concerned with some of the same general topics?

MJ: Many books explore dogs through the lenses of behaviour, cognition, welfare, history, or companionship. My book differs in that it places these topics within a larger examination of dog culture itself and the consequences that arise from it.

Rather than treating dogs solely as animals, I examine them as cultural symbols and explore how that symbolic status shapes public attitudes, social norms, and policy decisions. The book also investigates an institution that is rarely subjected to critical scrutiny: pet ownership itself.

Drawing on multiple disciplines, it investigates why dogs occupy such a unique place in human society and what consequences follow from that distinction.

MB: Are you hopeful that as people learn more about what it really means to love a dog they will give their companion canine a better and more fulfilled life, a win-win for all?

MJ: Yes, although perhaps not in exactly that way. If readers come away with a deeper understanding of dogs and a greater willingness to think critically about their needs, many dogs could benefit. One of the book’s central themes is that genuine concern for dogs requires us to ask whether the lives we create for them are truly compatible with their interests.

At the same time, I hope readers expand their circle of concern beyond individual dogs to include human communities, wildlife, ecosystems, and other animals affected by our relationship with dogs. If the book encourages people to reflect more deeply on those questions, I would consider that a positive outcome.

Dog researcher Dr. Clive Wynne calls Jacques' book "a provocative corrective" and dog expert and bioethicist Dr. Jessica Pierce captures the essence of Jacques' book as follows:

"Dog lovers who care deeply about the well-being of dogs, and about our place in a shared social and ecological world, should read this book. Melina Jacques raises challenging questions that are too often ignored, asking us to examine both the burdens that contemporary dog-keeping can place on other people and communities, the impacts our practices can have on broader ecosystems, and the costs they can impose on dogs themselves. Thoughtful, provocative, and deeply humane, this is a book that deserves to be read with an open mind."

Dr. Jonathan Balcombe, ethologist, and author of What a Fish Knows and Beyond Tooth and Claw writes: "Melina Jacques has written a thought-provoking and highly controversial examination of our relationship with dogs. Her thesis challenges deeply entrenched assumptions about what it means to live with and care for dogs, and she makes a compelling case for reconsidering many of those assumptions. Jacques knows she is swimming against a powerful current, yet she goes forward determined and undaunted. I admire that. This is a worthwhile and provocative contribution to the literature on our relationship with other animals, and one that deserves a wide readership."

Ozempic and similar drugs have a surprising benefit that goes beyond weight loss

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

New research suggests that Ozempic and Wegovy may reduce long-term sick leave, benefiting employers and taxpayers without increasing workers' earnings.

Ozempic is best known for lowering blood sugar and body weight. But does better health also mean more time at work and higher earnings? A study from Denmark found that the answer isn’t so simple.

Adults there who started Ozempic spent less time on long-term sick leave in the years that followed. But they were no more likely to hold a job than similar adults who hadn’t started the drug yet, and their income didn’t rise.

The money saved went to employers and local governments, which pay for sick leave in Denmark, instead of to the patients themselves.

Meltem Daysal of the University of Copenhagen and four colleagues, including economists at the ROCKWOOL Foundation and Duke University, carried out the analysis.

Their paper is an early release that the authors may still revise, and it compares patients in national health and tax records instead of assigning anyone to take the drug.

Ozempic, a brand of the drug semaglutide, became available in Denmark in August 2018.

During the study years, it was approved there for adults whose type 2 diabetes wasn’t under control with diet, exercise and earlier medicines.

The team started with 9,794 Danes who began taking Ozempic in its first 17 months on sale.

Comparing them with people who never took it would mislead, because patients who start a new diabetes drug tend to differ from those who don’t.

So the researchers paired each early starter with someone who began Ozempic four years later. Each pair was matched on age, sex, diabetes and obesity, and then on income, education, marriage and how often they saw a family doctor.

The matching produced 7,011 pairs. Until the later starters began their own treatment, they showed what might have happened to the early starters without the drug.

Everyone was between 30 and 59 at the end of 2017. That way, all of them would still be under 65, and more likely to be working, when the study ended.

In the United States, many patients stop these drugs early. One study of American patients with private insurance found that only 40% to 60% were still taking them after a year.

The Danish patients stuck with it. Four years after their first prescription, 75% of the early starters were still filling prescriptions for semaglutide, and most of those who quit did so in the first year.

Ozempic is subsidized in Denmark, where a month’s supply cost 1,117 Danish kroner, about $212, in 2019. A patient’s yearly out-of-pocket spending on subsidized prescriptions was capped at 4,300 kroner, about $681.

Earlier research has often blamed side effects when patients quit. The authors suggested that what patients pay may also play a big part in whether they stay on the drug for years.

In this study, sick leave counted as long-term once a spell ran past 30 days. Before starting Ozempic, the patients spent about 5.5% of their months on long-term sick leave.

Over the four years after the first prescription, that share fell by about 1 percentage point compared with the later starters, a drop of 17.3%. Put simply, long sick leave became about a sixth less common among the people taking the drug.

In the first two years, the early starters spent about 0.8 percentage points less of their time on long sick leave than the later starters. In years three and four, the gap reached about 1.1 points.

Payments from local governments, which cover sick leave after the first 30 days, fell by 19.4% over the same period.

The authors interpreted the decline as evidence of a treatment effect, noting that sick leave had followed similar patterns in both groups before the early starters began treatment.

The decline appeared in nearly every group the team checked when it split patients by sex, age and education. Women had the largest drop, while the estimates for men and for people under 50 were less certain.

The records can’t show exactly why sick leave fell, but the team found some clues in them.

After starting Ozempic, patients were less likely to visit an emergency department and less likely to fill prescriptions for heart and blood vessel medicines.

Earlier randomized trials, including one from 2016, found that semaglutide lowers the risk of serious heart problems such as heart attacks and strokes in high-risk patients. Events like those often keep a person away from work for weeks.

The authors called that a plausible explanation for the drop in sick leave, not a proven one. Their records include no weight or blood sugar measurements, so the team couldn’t track how each patient’s health changed.

Over the same four years, the team found no change in whether patients had jobs, whether they were in the workforce at all, or how much they earned.

The estimates were precise enough to rule out a rise in employment of more than 0.4 percentage points and a rise in income of more than 0.7%.

The authors traced this to the way Denmark pays for illness.

Employers cover the first 30 days of a worker’s sick leave, and the local government takes over after that. For workers who earn below a cap, those payments can replace all of their usual pay.

So when a worker missed fewer weeks, their income stayed about the same. The savings went to employers and taxpayers.

The team estimated those savings at about 4,163 kroner a year for each employed patient, or 1.3% of a year’s earnings. If shorter sick spells fell by the same share, the total would reach 4,766 kroner, about $722, or 1.5% of earnings.

For Denmark’s budget, this means the drug costs the public less than its pharmacy price suggests. Even so, the authors wrote, the savings remain far smaller than what GLP-1 treatments cost in 2026.

Sick pay in the United States is less generous than in Denmark. There, the authors suggested, similar health gains might show up instead as higher earnings, more hours, or fewer people losing their jobs.

A 2026 study of American women who started GLP-1 drugs to lose weight found large increases in employment among those who hadn’t been working.

The Danish authors wrote that the two studies differ in both their setting and their patients.

The team also checked Wegovy, the semaglutide brand approved in Denmark for weight management.

Among 67,736 new Wegovy users, long-term sick leave fell by about 1 percentage point within the first year, and jobs and income again didn’t rise.

Less than a year of records is too short to judge the long run, and the authors treated the Wegovy job and income results with caution.

Whether the gains last for people who take these drugs mainly to lose weight is still an open question.

Answering it will take longer records of weight-loss patients, ideally from countries where a sick day costs workers more than it does in Denmark.

The study appeared as an IZA Discussion Paper.

Early Medical Pain Can Produce a Lasting Memory

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Several years ago, I worked with a woman who experienced trauma on the first day of her life. She was born with Rh incompatibility, a potentially deadly condition that occurs when an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive baby and her immune system attacks the baby's red blood cells. This woman required a blood transfusion in which all of the blood in her body was drained and was replaced with transfused blood. This medical procedure saved her life, but she is experiencing lasting psychological adverse effects from the trauma associated with this procedure.

As a medical student, I remember watching an infant with his arms and legs strapped down to a board in order for the doctor to perform a circumcision. As the baby cried and screamed, the doctor performing the procedure said that the young boy would not remember this procedure years later. No anesthesia was used. Was the doctor correct? I have wondered about this for more than 40 years, and I can tell you that my research into epigenetic memory has taught me that he was probably wrong.

As a father, I took my 2-year-old daughter to a routine pediatrician's appointment for a vaccination. She cried after the shot, but otherwise seemed OK. The doctor told us she would probably have a sore leg the next day, but Tylenol would take care of it. Well, the next day, our daughter wouldn't walk on her sore leg. She cried and cried, despite our efforts to manage her pain with Tylenol and Ibuprofen. Well, years later, our daughter grew up to be one of the most courageous women I know. She climbed every 14,000-foot mountain in Colorado—all 58 of them! But she has had to deal with a lifelong fear of needles. If she has blood drawn, she has to lie down because she is likely to pass out if she sees the needle. For years, I have wondered: Is her fear of needles related to that early vaccination?

For much of the 20th century, physicians believed that newborns didn’t feel pain. Infants commonly underwent surgery with little or no anesthesia. That belief began to change in 1987, when a review published in The New England Journal of Medicine showed that newborns can experience pain, and they can also experience powerful stress responses due to the pain.

This raises the question: Can pain experienced early in life create a memory that persists for years? Research involving epigenetic memory suggests it is possible.

Epigenetics refers to chemical modifications that alter how genes are expressed without changing the DNA sequence. These modifications help cells “remember” what has previously happened.

In 2004, a study in Nature Neuroscience showed that rat pups receiving little maternal care developed epigenetic modifications to a gene in the hippocampus that left them with exaggerated stress responses as adults. Five years later, another study found similar changes in the brains of suicide victims who had been abused as children. These studies suggested that early trauma can be remembered by modifications to our cells’ DNA.

Strong evidence in humans linking medical procedures to epigenetic change comes from studies involving premature infants. These babies may undergo about 10 painful procedures per day, including heel lances, needle sticks, intubation, and suctioning.

An Italian study found that very preterm infants exposed to more skin-breaking procedures showed increased changes to the serotonin transporter gene between birth and discharge. Serotonin helps regulate mood, anxiety, and pain. A follow-up study linked these changes to greater negative emotionality at 3 months of age.

These changes may persist. Researchers in Canada found that at age 7, children born very preterm had greater epigenetic changes than full-term peers. Neonatal pain predicted that epigenetic changes were associated with more behavior problems later in life. This same group later connected greater neonatal procedural pain to physical changes in the brains of these children at the age of 8.

Healthy, full-term infants are not immune to these trauma-induced changes. Newborn circumcision is one of the most common surgical procedures performed in the United States, and historically it was often done without anesthesia.

In a 1995 study, researchers noticed that circumcised boys cried longer and showed more pain behavior during vaccinations at 4 to 6 months of age than uncircumcised boys. Their prospective 1997 study confirmed it. Circumcised infants reacted more strongly to routine vaccination months later than those who had received a topical anesthetic before circumcision.

In other words, a single painful event in the first days of life appeared to change the infant’s pain system, and pain relief partly prevented it. Whether epigenetic changes underlie this effect has not been directly tested, but the pattern of lasting sensitization from early pain fits closely with the previously described findings.

Toddlers and school-age children remember procedures as well. A review found that a minority of children develop posttraumatic stress symptoms after surgery, and 12 to 31 percent of children undergoing cardiac surgery develop posttraumatic stress disorder. Medical fear in childhood can also shape how people approach health care as adults.

The evidence is preliminary that these changes are due to epigenetic memory. But it is encouraging to note that epigenetic changes appear to be potentially reversible, and maternal sensitivity can reduce these effects in full-term infants.

Necessary procedures should not be avoided because of fear. But the evidence argues that we should be taking infant and child pain seriously. Strategies for reducing the effects of painful and other traumatic procedures include providing anesthesia for circumcision and analgesia for other painful procedures, offering skin-to-skin contact, or breastfeeding during needle sticks, avoiding unnecessary procedures, and preparing older children for what to expect.

The infant may not remember the procedure. But their body may.

Yemen: Houthi clashes near Taiz leave residents in a 'state of panic'

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Fighting between Iran-backed Houthi rebels and Yemeni government forces has intensified around the city of Taiz. Residents face growing shortages of food, water and medical supplies as thousands flee their homes.

Four months into the fighting in Yemen between the Iran-backed Houthi movement and the Saudi-backed Yemeni government, clashes are concentrated around Taiz in the country's southwest. Yemeni authorities have warned civilians of sniper fire from surrounding hillsides. Houthi forces have also largely blocked the delivery of goods to Taiz, which is under the control of government forces.

Taiz is Yemen's third-largest city, with a population of around 1 million people. However, fighting has displaced around 134,000 people in the Taiz governorate region, accordingto the International Organization for Migration. The governorate accounts for almost half of the more than 319,000 people displaced across western Yemen, the organization said on November 1, 2026.

"The Houthis have blocked fuel, water and medical supplies, basically all things required for hospitals to function properly," Niku Jafarnia, Yemen researcher at Human Rights Watch, told DW. "Even those who can reach hospitals may not be receiving the care they need," she said, adding that it has had a devastating impact on people's health, particularly those with chronic illnesses.

Ammar, a 45-year-old photographer in Taiz, told DW on Monday that he sees people in a "state of panic" and yet he feels that "the determination not to submit to the Houthis is very strong."

Afnan, the mother of a schoolchild in Taiz, told DW that "neighbors share the little food they have and help each other and many people in Taiz refuse to flee." She and the other residents who spoke to DW asked that their full names not be published, fearing reprisals.

Hanan, a 30-year-old mother of three, said that prices have skyrocketed while there is almost no petrol, cooking gas or drinking water left in the city. "This increases the mental load on people who are already scared by hearing the clashes of the fighting day and night," she said. Despite this, she numbers among those who have decided to stay.

Meanwhile, Nabil Shamsan, the governor of Taiz, told DW that negotiations are underway to bring food, fuel and water back to the city. While it remains to be seen when and under what conditions this might happen, international aid organizations remain extremely concerned.

"Fighting and insecurity around Taiz is making it unsafe for civilians to move freely and also for our staff to deliver aid," said Alison Bottomley, an advocacy adviser with the Norwegian Refugee Council in Yemen. "This is preventing us from reaching the 7,000 people we are supporting with emergency tents, blankets and educational activities for displaced children," she told DW, calling on both factions to allow "civilians and humanitarian workers to move safely."

During the previous Yemen conflict, which began in 2014 and largely ended with a UN-brokered truce in 2022, Ansar Allah, the formal name of the Houthi group, had already laid siege to Taiz in 2015 and 2016. That siege brought the population close to famine and caused a humanitarian crisis, but the Houthis never captured the city.

In turn, Taiz, as a bastion of resistance, represents something the Houthis have struggled to subdue for more than a decade, Hisham Al-Omeisy, a senior Yemeni adviser with UK-based advisory firm Coral Core Consultants, told DW. "The city symbolizes a deeply rooted tradition of political pluralism, civic resistance and rejection of the Houthi model of rule," he said, adding that the city's continued resistance is both a military obstacle and a political challenge to the Houthis' claim that their authority across Yemen is inevitable.

Following the truce in 2022, the Houthis controlled the capital Sanaa, most of northern Yemen and parts of the center, while the internationally recognized government controlled much of the west and the south including the city of Aden. However, fighting reignited in late July and the clashes intensified in September, as the Houthis seized territory from forces loyal to the internationally recognized government.

Control of the strategically important Bab el-Mandeb Strait, which connects the Red Sea with the Gulf of Aden, is contested. The precise territorial situation remains unclear, while the Houthis retain the capacity to threaten maritime traffic. The conflict continues to escalate, alongside an exchange of missile and drone attacks between the Houthis and Saudi Arabia.

Data on the number of people injured or killed in Yemen varies because of the ongoing fighting and separate statistics given by the Houthi movement and the government's health authorities. According to the latest WHO Health Cluster situation report, at least 1,677 people had been killed and 7,395 injured as of October 25, 2026.

Meanwhile, seizing Taiz is highlighted as a key strategic step by analysts. "Taiz would give the Houthis a major advantage in consolidating their hold over northern Yemen while opening the way for further expansion toward the south and the Red Sea coast," Al-Omeisy said, adding that controlling Taiz would give them stronger cards in any future negotiations.

"Taiz is far more than simply another military front for the Houthis," he noted.

"However, the Houthis may not actually need to capture Taiz to achieve many of their objectives," Hisham Al-Omeisy said. "If they can isolate the city, sever its remaining supply lines and neutralize it as a military threat, they could consolidate their positions along the western coast while tying down significant government forces," he added.

"My greater concern is that the Houthis could use gains around Taiz to progressively weaken and fragment the anti-Houthi camp, rather than immediately launch a full-scale offensive against Aden," Al-Omeisy told DW, adding that the Houthis have repeatedly demonstrated an ability to exploit divisions among their opponents, and those divisions remain one of the government's greatest vulnerabilities.

A collection of intimate interviews with the late Jo Spence provides further texture to her life and legacy

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Jo Spence (1934-92) was a London-born and based British photographer, writer, class and health activist and educator. She is best known for challenging the assumptions of what photography could be, for example, by using her own image to question common tropes around female beauty, health, identity and societal expectations. Spence’s achievements centred on the small home and studio on Islington’s Upper Street that she shared with her partner and collaborator Terry Dennett (1938-2018).

The Unknown Recordings is an intimate self-portrait in text form. There are pictures, but surprisingly few. Still, in that respect, Spence’s disarmingly frank autobiography Putting Myself in the Picture (1986) could not be surpassed. Forty years on, this publication makes sense of a textual account largely in the form of transcripts of key historic recordings made with the artist.

The verbatim transcript of Spence’s interview for the British Library’s Oral History of British Photography, recorded in 1991, extends to more than 100 pages. It covers every aspect of her life from childhood experiences to early working life, before progressing to her photographic work, collaborations and politics, as well as her experience of breast cancer and leukaemia.

The conversation between Spence and the interviewer flows, with few fragments and diversions, in a manner that allows us (almost) to hear it. Here, he makes it clear that Spence’s work was done under difficult circumstances but that she remained focused on the importance of empowering others. Rather than concluding in some fashion or other, together these last documents point the reader to other rich visual and textual archival material that surrounds Spence’s life and legacy. She extended the possibilities of photography to speak about the unspeakable and photograph the unphotographable. Spence was not within the cultural elite, but found a way to tell her own story and hoped it would be a model for others to follow.

The authors posted the study on a research-sharing website on September 4. Other scientists have not yet peer-reviewed the work.

Charis Bridger-Staatz, PhD, a senior research fellow at UCL, led the study. Among all U.S. adults, around 31.7% were overweight, according to the CDC.

The findings can apply to Americans because British and U.S. adults share similar weight patterns, said Solveig Cunningham, PhD, a professor of global health at Emory University’s Rollins School of Public Health. Cunningham, who was not involved in the research, told Healthline that Americans tend to gain weight across adulthood.

“By the time you get into your 50s, close to the majority of Americans already have obesity. Many others have overweight,” she said.The World Health Organization (WHO) calls obesity a chronic disease. Healthcare professionals diagnose overweight and obesity using a person’s weight and height, or body mass index (BMI).

Bridger-Staatz said the team set out to learn who lost weight and kept it off before GLP-1 weight loss medications became available, and whether that weight loss improved health over time.

Of those, 6,309 took part in a health check in their early 60s, according to the report.The team compared each person’s highest weight between ages 23 and 42 with their weight between ages 50 and 55. Researchers sorted people by whether they gained or lost more than 5% of their body weight and kept the change. For a 200-pound person, 5% equals 10 pounds.To track heart health, the team compared blood tests and blood pressure readings at ages 44 and 62 with those of people who maintained a healthy weight.“We know everyone’s heart health is kind of going to get a little bit worse over that time because you are aging and that’s expected,” Bridger-Staatz said. The question, she said, was, “Does it slow the rate at which people’s heart health is getting worse?”

Only about 6% lost weight and kept it off, according to the study.“To me, that’s sort of the stronger message,” Cunningham said of the findings. Her advice for adults in their 50s is to avoid gaining weight.

People with obesity who lost and then gained the weight back saw faster drops in “good” cholesterol and faster rises in inflammation and long-term blood sugar.

When the weight comes back, “most of the weight they gain is fat, not muscle,” he said. Even someone who loses and regains 5% to 7% of their body weight may end up with slightly less muscle and more fat, he said.Some research has shown that weight fluctuation is associated with a higher risk of high blood pressure and cardiovascular disease-related death.

A quartet of medical organizations has published a comprehensive set of reports calling for a coordinated plan of action to improve heart healthcare for women throughout their lifetimes.

The reports detail how cardiovascular health issues can be prevented, monitored, and treated from the early years of menstruation to peak fertility age to pregnancy to perimenopause to postmenopause stages.

The report authors call for healthcare professionals to identify heart health risks early in girls and women and to coordinate care across medical specialties.

They stress that females have specific biological factors that increase their risks of cardiovascular disease. Those components include hormonal and emotional factors.

The authors note that heart disease is the number one cause of death for females in the United States, as well as the number one cause of death during pregnancy. Yet, only 44% of females are fully aware of the dangers they face from heart disease.

The organizations issuing the reports are the American Heart Association (AHA), the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine.

The groups presented their findings in a trio of reports published in the AHA’s flagship journal, Circulation on October 26.

“Women experience their healthcare across clinical specialties, but it often is fragmented or disconnected,” said Sadiya Khan, MD, the chair of the collaborative writing group for the reports and a professor of cardiovascular epidemiology at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine in Chicago, in a news release.

“Specifically, these reports connect the dots for clinicians, for patients, and across health domains so that care is holistic and begins in adolescence, recognizing that women’s heart health is uniquely shaped by biological transitions not experienced by men — periods, pregnancy, perimenopause, and menopause.”

Experts not involved in the reports told Healthline the updates were a long time coming.

Nicole Weinberg, MD, a cardiologist at Pacific Heart Institute’s Women’s Heart Center in Santa Monica, California, said the reports present a positive framework for women’s health.

“Women have unique cardiovascular risk factors related to pregnancy and menopause, and certain traditional risk factors, including diabetes, may increase their risk of heart disease more significantly than they do in men,” noted Nissi Suppogu, MD, a cardiologist and medical director of the Women’s Heart Center at MemorialCare Heart & Vascular Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California.

“Recognizing these differences is essential to identifying women at risk earlier, improving prevention strategies and ensuring they receive appropriate treatment,” she said.

“I am incredibly excited to see these new reports on women’s heart health,” added Svati Shah, MD, the president-elect of the AHA, as well as the associate dean of Translational Research and the director of Precision Genomics Collaboratory in the Duke School of Medicine in North Carolina.

“They are a call to action for increasing education, research, and coordinated clinical care for women’s heart health,” Shah said.

The organizations spelled out their recommendations in three reports.

The first report is a presidential advisory that lays out a “female-specific life course approach to the prevention and management of cardiovascular disease.”

In it, the authors state that “girls and women uniquely experience fragmented care” across medical fields, including pediatrics, obstetrics, and gynecology.

The authors call for a “unified life course approach that addresses the female-specific interconnectedness of hormonal, psychosocial, and cardiovascular health.”

This report breaks down the life cycle for girls and women into five stages.

“Emerging data suggest that unique biologic transitions or life stages experienced by girls and women are linked with changes in [cardiovascular disease] risk, which may be due in part to physiologic changes in sex hormones,” the authors wrote.

Weinberg said this life-stage breakdown is important, especially for risk reduction.

She said issues with menstruation during the teen years may be a harbinger of heart health problems later in life. The same is true for health issues during pregnancy, especially high blood pressure or high blood sugar levels.

“We need to be aware of these heart disease risks so we’re not caught by surprise 10 to 20 years down the road,” Weinberg said.

Suppogu agreed. “Medical professionals should emphasize that heart health is a lifelong priority and prevention should begin early. Encourage girls and women to establish healthy habits, understand their cardiovascular risk factors, and maintain regular checkups, even when they feel healthy.”

“Women’s heart health has a unique constellation of risk factors and manifestations that are shaped by biological transitions that vary across a woman’s lifetime,” said Shah.

The second report is a science advisory that recommends a comprehensive plan for women’s cardiovascular care.

In it, the authors call for a system that “integrates assessment of hormonal, psychosocial, and cardiovascular health across life stages.”

They propose a “national infrastructure” of centers that would coordinate clinical care, education, training, research, and community engagement around women’s heart health.

“These centers can reduce fragmentation, enhance earlier risk identification, and expand opportunities to increase representation of women in clinical research,” the authors wrote. “In doing so, they offer a bold opportunity for a scalable and equitable model to improve cardiovascular health for all women.”

To illustrate their point, the authors noted that only about 60% of women with hypertensive disorders during pregnancy in the United States have a follow-up visit with a healthcare professional about their condition within the first 6 months after giving birth.

“Given that pregnancy is a cardiovascular stress test and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are risk-enhancing factors for long-term [cardiovascular disease] risk, this represents a major missed opportunity to address cardiovascular risk,” the authors wrote.

“Pregnancy and the postpartum period are important windows into cardiovascular health throughout the life course,” said William Grobman, MD, the vice-chair of the writing group for these reports and a professor of obstetrics and gynecology at The Warren Alpert Medical School of Brown University in Providence, Rhode Island, in the press release.

“An adverse pregnancy outcome can unmask increased cardiovascular risk, and that information should guide care well beyond pregnancy,” he continued.

The third report is a scientific statement that suggests a “consensus guideline for the female-specific prevention and management of cardiovascular disease.”

In it, the authors state that there is “growing recognition that the optimal prevention and management of cardiovascular disease in girls and women requires attention to sex-specific considerations relevant to the interconnected domains of hormonal, psychosocial, and cardiovascular health across the life course.”

The authors note several age-related warning signs of heart issues. These are spelled out in the AHA press release.

Females comprise around half the population, and their cardiovascular health “must be a part of everyday care across medicine,” said Mariell Jessup, MD, the chief science and medical officer of the American Heart Association, in the press release. “These publications give clinicians and health systems a shared foundation for recognizing risk throughout a woman’s life.”

In a scientific statement published in February 2026, the AHA projected that 60% of females in the United States will have at least one type of cardiovascular disease by 2050.

The organization also predicted that 25% of U.S. women will have diabetes and 60% will have obesity by that year if current trends continue.

Letter From the Editor-in-Chief: What a Decade of Bedtime Stories Taught Me About Health

I’ve read to my children each night at bedtime for as long as I (and they) can remember. We started with the likes of “Goodnight Batcave” and the lesser-known-in-the-US but oh-so-wonderful collection of verse-driven classics from former UK Children’s Laureate Julia Donaldson.

We moved on to B.J. Novak’s anarchic love letter to the power of words (and to great typography), “The Book With No Pictures.”

We eventually worked our way up to Roald Dahl’s wickedly dark back catalog, discovering more complex characters, language and plotting as we went.

When my eldest, Amelia, hit 6, it was time to roll out the big guns.

Over the last 5 years (she’s now 11), I’ve read the seven Harry Potter books aloud twice, complete with all the voices: once to Amelia on her own, once to both her and her brother, Henry, two years her junior.

I had feared she wouldn’t be keen on another helping, but Amelia immersed herself once more in the world of Hogwarts with no hesitation and took immense joy in watching Henry discover for himself every twist and turn on Harry’s journey.

We have done the same thing with Tolkien’s “The Hobbit” and his fantasy masterpiece “The Lord of the Rings,” and are currently midway through “The Two Towers” on our second go around.

There’s a huge amount of reciprocal joy we take from this nightly ritual, beyond the practical benefits of providing a calming demarcation between the end of the day and the wind-down to sleep.

For me, it comes as either a break in my working day or a signal that it’s over, depending on how hectic things are.

For the kids, it’s an all-too-rare chance to get me all to themselves, no deadlines, screens, or other distractions to pull me away from being anything but 100% present.

For all three of us, it’s a literal (and literary!) warm blanket — a chance to escape under the covers to familiar worlds and familiar faces. It is, in short, incredibly good for our well-being.

And yet, in the world of health information — including on this site — thousands, if not millions, of reading hours are dedicated to the benefits of nutrition, sleep, and exercise as the building blocks of good health. Reading itself rarely makes that shortlist.

For me, it unequivocally deserves a place on it. Research seems to support that case. Even if we need more of it to really understand how and why.

A recent editorial in Psychological Medicine co-authored by Barbara Sahakian, Professor of Clinical Neuropsychology at Cambridge University, draws on a broad range of research linking reading for pleasure to better mental health and greater empathy.

The positive impact of reading on young people — just like Amelia and Henry — lso carries evidence.

Among more than 10,000 US adolescents, those who started reading for pleasure early had fewer mental health problems, spent less time on screens and slept more. Although, per an earlier column of mine, I can’t vouch for the sleep side of things when it comes to early bird Henry.

For many, reading can be a form of therapy that improves well-being. Bibliotherapy is a complementary therapeutic tool for emotional release and reduced stress and anxiety, with its roots traced back to ancient civilizations.

There’s even research suggesting a link between a love of reading and a lower risk of cognitive decline.

In a 2003 study of people aged 75 and over, reading was associated with a 35% lower risk of dementia over roughly five years.

A separate 14-year study from 2022 found that people who read at least weekly saw less cognitive decline, whatever their level of education.

A happy (and healthy) by-product of this love of being read to is that both my kids have now started reading to me at bedtime. Henry recently read “Jeffy the Burglar’s Cat” to us, a lesser-known but no less compelling chapter book by Ursula Moray Williams that was a staple of my own childhood. And we’ve enjoyed several books from Amelia, including the wistful tale of friendship and the circle of life, “Charlotte’s Web” by E.B. White – not a dry eye in the house for the final few chapters of that one.

They’ve also gone off and re-read the Harry Potter books and “The Hobbit” to themselves.

The research above links reading to many health benefits: better attention and memory, fewer mental health problems, more sleep, less time on screens, and greater empathy. I haven’t measured any of those impacts empirically in our house. Nor am I naive to the fact that as they get older, they’ll ultimately grow out of me reading to them, with school, extracurricular activities, their friends, and inevitably screens competing for more and more of their time.

Come what may, though, I know that a child who considers missing a reading session as the most cardinal of cardinal sins, and who scrambles for the chance to read aloud to their dad at bedtime is, at that moment, concentrating, empathizing, discovering and growing.

Researchers are clear that studies espousing the benefits of reading show association rather than proof, and it’s evident that more experimental work and deeper research are needed to ensure the complex cause and effect stands on firm scientific ground.

Regardless, it’s a healthy habit Amelia, Henry and I are very happy to keep, and with a bit of luck, I’ll get to pass it on to their kids one day.
