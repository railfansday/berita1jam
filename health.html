What Is Rhabdo? What Every Woman Should Know About the Rare Condition

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A woman’s death during the New York City Marathon has put the condition back in the spotlight.

At the New York City Marathon on Sunday, 24-year-old Rachel Apa of Lewisberry, Pennsylvania, collapsed two miles from the finish line. She later died at the hospital.

The medical examiner’s office is still investigating her cause of death. Her father, Steve Apa, told the New York Times that the cause was rhabdomyolysis, commonly known as rhabdo. The rare condition occurs when skeletal muscle breaks down, releasing its contents into the bloodstream, says Robert Glatter, MD, an emergency room doctor and an assistant professor of emergency medicine at Lenox Hill Hospital.

Apa’s death comes amid growing conversations about rhabdo on social media. People have shared their experiences with the condition after intense exercise, particularly at cycling classes. https://www.tiktok.com/@bryybell_/video/7693630991839153421?_r=1&_t=ZT-9Axae4rjSXu

But while these stories are alarming, rhabdo is still relatively rare. Yet they raise questions about the risks of intense exercise, how to recognize when something is wrong, and just how concerned the everyday exerciser should be. Here’s what experts want you to know.

Rhabdomyolysis happens when skeletal muscle breaks down and releases its contents into the bloodstream. The resulting spill can produce dangerous electrolyte imbalances and kidney injury, particularly when someone is dehydrated, Dr. Glatter says.

Although rhabdo is often associated with intense exercise, physical exertion is just one possible cause. “Rhabdomyolysis can also follow crush injuries, seizures, prolonged immobilization, heat stroke, severe infections, certain drugs or toxins, metabolic abnormalities and, more rarely, inherited muscle disorders,” Dr. Glatter says.

Exercise-related rhabdo typically happens when an individual suddenly performs an unusually difficult, repetitive workout involving large muscle groups—particularly when doing eccentric exercise, or when the muscle contracts while lengthening. The risk may be higher when the activity is unfamiliar or far more intense than the person is accustomed to. Some examples include high-repetition squats, lunges, downhill running, and intense cycling, Dr. Glatter says.

“The key issue is often not simply that the workout was ‘hard,’ but that its intensity or volume was far beyond what that person’s muscles were conditioned to tolerate,” he says.

Symptoms include muscle pain "dramatically out of proportion to ordinary post-workout ache,” Dr. Glatter describes, as well as dark-colored urine, swelling, weakness, and difficulty moving the affected muscles. Other possible symptoms include dizziness, fainting, confusion, persistent vomiting or feeling generally ill, he says. “Someone who develops severe or rapidly worsening muscle pain or swelling after a strenuous workout—especially together with marked weakness, dark urine or substantially decreased urination—should be evaluated urgently.”

It’s important to note that symptoms can start while someone is still exercising. An athlete may notice muscle pain that feels disproportionate to effort, unusual heaviness or weakness, severe fatigue, or a drop in pace or intensity that would normally be manageable, Dr. Glatter says. In severe cases, athletes can collapse, appear confused, or develop nausea, dizziness, and swelling.

Other risk factors also include dehydration, exercising in extreme heat or humidity, a recent viral illness, certain medications or drugs, and underlying genetic or metabolic disorders, such as sickle cell.

Although many recent social media accounts of exercise-related rhabdo have come from women, it’s important to know that an unaccustomed high-intensity workout can overwhelm muscles regardless of sex. Those stories don’t necessarily reflect who is most likely to develop the condition, and studies have shown generally higher rates in men.

It’s complicated. “It’s probably a combination of greater awareness and a genuine increase in some types of exertional rhabdomyolysis, but the evidence does not support describing rhabdo as a new epidemic,” Dr. Glatter says.

Rhabdo has been around for decades, and it’s difficult to determine overall incidence rates because mild cases may never be diagnosed. Still, some research suggests that exercise-related cases have increased in certain settings. A 2022 analysis published in the Physician and Sportsmedicine looked at emergency department data from 2000 through 2019. It estimated more than 40,000 cases of exertional rhabdomyolysis and found an increase over that period.

Dr. Glatter suggests this might be, in part, due to shifts in exercise culture. An increase in the availability of fitness classes such as high-intensity interval training, cycling, and CrossFit-style workouts can expose people to unfamiliar movement patterns at high intensities.

“These workouts are not inherently dangerous, and millions of people perform them safely. The risk arises when workload increases too quickly relative to conditioning, particularly when heat, dehydration, illness, medications or inadequate recovery are added to the equation,” Dr. Glatter says. “A highly motivated newcomer trying to keep pace with a class may continue well past the point at which her muscles are accustomed to working.”

It’s also important to note: Being well-trained does lower the risk of rhabdo, but it does not eliminate it. “In a trained athlete, rhabdomyolysis is often best understood as a ‘multiple-hit’ phenomenon, where several physiologic stresses occur at the same time,” Dr. Glatter says. Someone may be proportionally prepared for the activity, but factors such as unusually high intensity or duration, heat and humidity, dehydration, recent illness, inadequate recovery, electrolyte abnormalities, and certain medications or supplements can lower the threshold for injury. Rare underlying conditions, such as sickle cell trait or certain metabolic conditions, can also predispose a healthy athlete to rhabdo.

That doesn’t mean you should fear strenuous exercise. Starting at a manageable level, building up gradually, and staying hydrated can help reduce the risk.

“The body adapts extraordinarily well to exercise when intensity and volume are increased gradually,” Dr. Glatter says. “Going from relatively little training to an all-out, high-repetition workout involving large muscle groups is a very different physiological challenge.”

Exercise-related rhabdo is uncommon, but can happen when a workout places demands on muscles that are far beyond what they’re accustomed to. High-intensity exercise can be part of a healthy routine; the key is to build toward it gradually and pay attention to how your body responds. If you develop severe muscle pain or swelling, weakness, dark urine, or substantially decreased urination, seek urgent medical care.

Energy Drinks, Stimulant Supplements Linked to Higher Heart Risks in Young Adults

New research suggests that caffeinated energy drinks and stimulant supplements are associated with significantly higher risks of serious heart problems, including cardiac arrest and heart attack in young adults.

The findings, to be presented at the American Heart Association (AHA) Scientific Sessions 2026 November 6–9 in Chicago, are preliminary and can’t prove that energy drinks cause heart problems.

Still, the results highlight concerning cardiovascular effects of these popular products, particularly among adults under 35 who consume them regularly.

“These products are marketed as everyday pick-me-ups and sold next to soda and bottled water, so most people assume they carry no real risk,” study author Sai Samayamanthula, a fourth-year medical student at the University of Virginia School of Medicine in Charlottesville, VA, said in a news release.

“For many healthy people, if these products are consumed occasionally, the risk is probably small. However, individuals who consume energy drinks or other stimulant-containing supplements regularly can quickly exceed the recommended daily limit for caffeine, so it’s important for people to know that there may be a risk of cardiovascular harm with these products,” Samayamanthula continued.

Researchers from the University of Virginia School of Medicine analyzed 28,345 reports submitted to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) between 2017 and 2025.

Of these, 854 involved caffeinated energy drinks or stimulant supplements, while the remaining reports involved non-stimulant products.

Compared with non-stimulant products, cardiac arrest was reported approximately 11 times more often with energy drinks and stimulant supplements. Heart attacks were reported 4.5 times more often, while ventricular arrhythmias and palpitations were reported about four times more frequently.

Nearly 45% of heart-related reports involved adults ages 18 to 34.

“Our findings support the case for more systematic safety monitoring of caffeine- and stimulant-containing products. In addition, healthcare professionals should remember to ask their younger patients who have palpitations, chest pain or fainting spells if they recently or regularly consume these products,” Samayamanthula said.

Gregory M. Marcus, MD, professor of medicine at the University of California, San Francisco School of Medicine, and chair of the 2026 AHA scientific statement on caffeine and cardiovascular disease, wasn’t involved in the study, but said the research “raises concerns that products with very high amounts of caffeine may be harmful to the cardiovascular system.”

“Although there are biological reasons why that connection could be possible, the study does not prove that caffeine products cause these events, because other factors may have influenced the results found in this analysis,” Marcus said in a news release.

“More research is needed to understand whether these stimulant products are truly responsible for the reported heart-related events.”

Cheng-Han Chen, MD, board certified interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA, said the findings, although concerning, are not surprising. Chen wasn’t involved in the study.

“We know that energy drinks can have significant effects on the cardiovascular system, primarily because of their stimulant properties,” he told Healthline.

“Caffeine can increase blood pressure and heart rate, while other ingredients, such as guarana and taurine, may further contribute to cardiovascular stimulation and increased stress hormone levels. What is particularly concerning is the potential for these products to trigger serious complications, including abnormal heart rhythms, especially in people with underlying cardiovascular conditions.”

Chen agreed that occasional energy drink consumption is unlikely to have long-term consequences for heart health in most healthy individuals. However, he said that “frequent or excessive consumption can potentially increase the risk of hypertension and cardiac arrhythmias.”

An AHA scientific statement published in July 2026 notes that up to 400 milligrams of caffeine daily is generally considered safe for most healthy adults. That’s roughly equivalent to four or five 8-ounce cups of brewed coffee, depending on how it’s prepared.

However, some energy drinks contain up to three times as much caffeine per fluid ounce as brewed coffee.

Certain energy drinks may also contain stimulants, such as guarana, yerba mate, theobromine, synephrine, and yohimbine.

These ingredients may compound caffeine’s effects on the cardiovascular system.

“Caffeine stimulates the cardiovascular system, primarily by increasing sympathetic nervous system activity. This can cause the heart to beat faster, raise blood pressure, and increase the workload on the heart,” Chen said.

“Energy drinks may contain additional stimulants, such as guarana and taurine, which can further increase blood pressure and stress hormone levels. With excessive consumption, these effects can become more pronounced and potentially interfere with the heart’s normal electrical activity, increasing the risk of abnormal heart rhythms. People with underlying cardiovascular conditions, particularly hypertension or a predisposition to arrhythmias, may be especially vulnerable to these effects.”

Other research has echoed these concerns. A 2025 review suggests that high-volume energy drink consumption can prolong the heart’s QTc interval, increase blood pressure, and raise heart rate beyond the effects of caffeine alone.

“There is growing concern about the cardiovascular effects of energy drinks and stimulant-containing supplements, particularly among younger adults,” Chen said.

“One challenge is that younger individuals may not recognize that these products can have significant effects on their heart and blood pressure, especially when consumed frequently or in combination with other caffeinated products.”

Young adults accounted for a substantial portion of the FDA reports included in the study, highlighting potential cardiovascular concerns for this age group.

People with existing heart conditions, a history of irregular heart rhythms, or heightened caffeine sensitivity may face additional risks. Those with undiagnosed genetic heart conditions may also be vulnerable.

A 2024 study identified cases of sudden cardiac arrest occurring shortly after energy drink consumption, particularly among people with underlying inherited heart conditions.

“As a cardiologist, I am particularly concerned about people who may have underlying cardiovascular conditions that have not yet been diagnosed,” Chen said.

“Excessive stimulant intake can potentially trigger symptoms such as palpitations, chest discomfort, and lightheadedness. In susceptible individuals, it may also increase the risk of more serious complications, including abnormal heart rhythms.”

Additionally, combining energy drinks with strenuous exercise or alcohol may introduce additional cardiovascular risks.

The findings suggest an association between energy drinks, stimulant supplements, and reported cardiovascular events, but they are preliminary and do not establish cause and effect.

The FDA database also relied on voluntary adverse-event reports, meaning some events may never be reported, while others may receive disproportionate attention.

Researchers also do not yet know how many people consumed these products without experiencing any problems. Without this variable, the reports cannot establish actual risk.

Other factors, including underlying health conditions, medications, and lifestyle habits, may also influence the results. More systematic research and controlled clinical trials are needed.

For most healthy adults, consuming up to 400 milligrams of caffeine per day is generally considered safe.

However, Chen noted that individual tolerance for caffeine may vary, and people with underlying cardiovascular conditions, particularly high blood pressure or abnormal heart rhythms, may need to limit or avoid caffeine altogether.

“Energy drinks can vary considerably in their caffeine content and serving sizes,” Chen said.

“Depending on the product, a single can may contain anywhere from 80 to 300 milligrams of caffeine. This means that consuming even one or two energy drinks can put someone close to or above the recommended daily limit, particularly when combined with coffee, tea, or other caffeinated supplements.”

A simple swap for salt could spare millions of cardiovascular disease-related deaths, new research suggests.

In a study published in Nature Health on November 4, researchers at The George Institute for Global Health modeled the potential impact of 193 countries switching from regular table salt to potassium-enriched salt.

They found the switch could prevent nearly 3 million deaths and more than 10 million new cases of heart and kidney disease worldwide each year.

In the United States, this simple salt swap could prevent nearly 105,000 deaths and about 492,000 new cases a year, the researchers estimated.

About 130.6 million U.S. adults have cardiovascular disease, according to the American Heart Association (AHA).

Liping Huang, PhD, a senior data analyst in the Food Policy Division at The George Institute for Global Health and co-lead author of the study, told Healthline the team started with current blood pressure levels by sex, age, and country.

People who used potassium-enriched salt had a 14% lower risk of stroke, a 13% lower risk of cardiovascular events, and a 12% lower risk of early death than those who used regular salt, according to a 2021 study in The New England Journal of Medicine that summarized the trial’s results.

The World Health Organization (WHO) endorsed potassium-enriched salt in a 2025 guideline, saying it can reduce blood pressure and the risk of cardiovascular disease.

The AHA said potassium-based salt substitutes can help lower blood pressure in some adults. I’ll try just about anything that promises to make me look and feel better, but drinking olive oil? That one gave me pause—until I read up on the impressive benefits and found out Hailey Bieber does it every day. Suddenly, I was down to ditch my green juice habit to sip this alleged liquid gold.

But is there any real advantage to drinking it straight versus incorporating it into your regular diet? Or is it just another TikTok fad that sounds better on your FYP than it works IRL? I decided to take pre-made olive oil shots for an entire month to put the hype to the test. Here’s what happened.

If you didn’t already know, extra-virgin olive oil is a nutritional powerhouse! It’s rich in monounsaturated fats and antioxidants, and research has linked consumption to lower LDL (aka the “bad” cholesterol), better cardiovascular health, and reduced inflammation. It may also support healthy blood sugar levels and brain health, which is pretty cool considering it’s an ingredient we all probably have in our kitchen cabinets.

“Olive oil also contains polyphenols, which are natural plant compounds that help protect our cells from oxidative stress and support a healthy inflammatory response,” Maura MacDonald, M.S., R.D., C.S.S.D., registered dietitian at Thorne, tells Delish. “These compounds can benefit gut health too by interacting with the bacteria in our digestive system and helping to support microbial diversity and a healthy gut microbiome. It’s one of the reasons olive oil is such a staple of the Mediterranean diet, which has long been associated with better overall health and longevity.”

Listen, I knew that chugging olive oil wasn’t going to be a pleasant experience, but I wasn’t expecting it to be quite so, ahem, interesting. My main gripe was with the consistency: It was just a little too thick and viscous to go down easily, and every shot required a full pep talk.

I didn’t notice much difference after day 5, which left me feeling discouraged. I had hoped for an immediate click—glowing skin, boosted energy levels, less bloating, something!—but the only tangible evidence of my participation in the experiment was a glue-like coating that clung to my tonsils after every morning shot.

By week two, I called an audible and started adding lemon juice to the olive oil. Anything to cut the oiliness just a little—plus, the citrus brings its own nutritional perks! And while it wasn’t a perfect fix, it made the process a little more bearable.

Midway through the month, I started to notice improvements. My skin seemed more hydrated, my digestion was more regular, and I wasn’t dealing with the same post-meal bloating I’ve dealt with for years. I also found myself filling up faster, and I didn’t feel the same pull to go back for seconds. Whether it was the olive oil or a placebo effect, I’m not entirely sure, but at least I finally felt like the morning routine was doing something.

As the experiment progressed, I noticed my energy levels improve. I wasn’t exactly jumping out of bed like a morning person (let’s not get carried away here), but I was able to avoid the usual afternoon slump. I had fewer cravings too and wasn’t automatically reaching for snacks between mealtimes. My body just felt like it was functioning better.

I’m so glad you asked! Yes, yes, it does. According to MacDonald, it actually makes a big difference.

“Extra-virgin olive oil is generally the best choice for maximizing health benefits because it’s cold-pressed and unrefined, meaning it retains more of its natural antioxidants and polyphenols,” she explains. “This is the one you’re likely eating at a restaurant when they serve the bread.”

Regular virgin olive oil is also cold-pressed and offers similar benefits, but it has a slightly higher acidity level and milder flavor, while refined olive oil goes through additional processing that reduces even more of those benefits.

“I'd recommend reaching for extra-virgin olive oil whenever possible, especially formaking salad dressings, drizzling over vegetables, or adding to meals,” MacDonald adds. “It's an easy way to get the most nutritional value out of the olive oil you're already using.”

While, for the sake of the experiment, I drank it straight, MacDonald says that it’s actually not the most practical way to consume it.

“I'd recommend incorporating extra-virgin olive oil into your everyday meals instead,” she says. “Not only is it easier on digestion, but it can also help your body absorb fat-soluble vitamins from other foods.”

She doesn’t have to tell me twice. I’ll drizzle it on salads and roasted veggies, mix it with lemon (but for a dressing this time!), and add it to fish and whole grains.

“Using olive oil, a source of fat containing both mono- and polyunsaturated fats, in place of saturated fats like butter is another easy way to support heart health,” MacDonald continues. “It's really about making olive oil part of a balanced eating pattern, rather than treating it as something you need to incorporate each morning separately.”

While it’s not a downside per se, MacDonald points out that a typical serving of olive oil (a.k.a. one tablespoon) has about 120 calories and 14 grams of fat, so a two-tablespoon shot would total about 240 calories and 28 grams of fat. The TL;DR? While it’s a great source of heart-healthy monounsaturated fats and antioxidants, it’s also calorie-dense.

“Taking a shot every morning can add up quickly, especially if you're adding it on top of your usual diet—which may already include using olive oil or other fats in your meals and cooking processes,” she says. “Rather than thinking of olive oil as something extra you need to consume, I’d recommend using it in place of saturated fats, like butter or coconut oil, when applicable, as part of your everyday meals.”

I did notice changes in how I felt overall after taking olive oil shots every day, especially with my digestion. But given MacDonald’s advice that you can reap the same benefits from simply incorporating it into your everyday diet, I think I’ll retire the morning shots in favor of something I’ll actually enjoy—like O.O. on a salad or with a fresh piece of sourdough. I’ll get my daily dose the old-fashioned way, and reserve the shot glasses for my Friday nights.

People over the centuries have referred to spiritual companionship and assistance as a relationship with a “spiritual friend.” The Gaelic word anamchara refers to the spiritual friend as a “soul-friend.” A soul-friend is an assisting force on our journey. This is the reliable confidant with whom we can share our innermost spiritual conundrums, visions, and realizations.

As St. Aelred (1111-1167), the Cistercian abbot of Rievaulx Abbey in medieval England, wrote in On Spiritual Friendship: “It is no small consolation in this life to have someone who can unite with you in an intimate affection and the embrace of a holy love. Someone in whom your spirit can rest, to whom you can pour out your soul, to whose pleasant exchanges, as to soothing songs, you can fly in sorrow. To the dear breast of whose friendship, amidst the many troubles of the world, you can safely retire. A person who can shed tears with you in your worries, be happy with you when things go well, search out with you the answers to your problems, whom with the ties of charity you can lead into the depths of your heart. A person who, though absent in body, is yet present in spirit, where heart to heart you can talk to him, where the sweetness of the Spirit flows between you, where you so join yourself and cleave to him that soul mingles with soul and two become one.”

In Buddhism we find the idea of Kalyana-mittata, “virtuous friendship.” The spiritual friends support one another’s practice with candidness, wisdom, and trustworthiness. In the Upaddha Sutta, Buddha explained to Ananda, his disciple, cousin, and friend, that such spiritual companionship can be considered the whole of the Dharma as the “holy life.” Buddha went on to say that when we have Kalyana-mittata in place with friends, they cheer and encourage us on the path, and when our friend is Buddha, we are fully freed from suffering. In the Dighajanu Sutta, Buddha says that when a practitioner is committed to dialogue with a variety of upstanding people, he learns to imitate their commitment to the Dharma, their compassion, their discerning wisdom. They have become allies of enlightenment to one another.

Another text, the Anguttara Nikaya, lists the qualities of a monastic spiritual friend: he is amiable, venerable, able to give good counsel, patient in listening, respectful, capable of speaking in depth about the Dharma, and serious enough to share on a depth level. A teacher is a prime example of what is required in spiritual friendship. He, she, or they offer guidance, challenges, comfort, and cheer on the path to virtue and awakening—all the scaffolding of authentic support. A support group, a faith community, or a sangha, a coterie of practitioners of Buddhism, are examples.

Peer companionship is certainly valuable on the spiritual path. Our spiritual practice and our psychological health are enlivened when we have a trusted friend to whom we can speak openly about ourselves. This will not be the “friend” who supports and cheers our ego and its inflation—a narcissistic style of alliance. The ally of our higher Self supports our journey toward generosity, humility, enlightenment, and loving-kindness. This ally warns and saves us from what in Buddhism are called the three poisons: greed, hate, and the illusion of separateness. These are descriptors of the inflated ego at its worst. Spiritual friends will be the ones who show and lead us from greed to generosity, from hate to love, and from ignorance to wisdom.

“Ubuntu” is an African term meaning “humanity toward others.” It is also translated as “I am because you are” or “I am what I am because of who we all are.” Ubuntu tells us that all beings can be allies, contributing to our becoming what we can best be in this moment. Ubuntu is also a spiritual calling to reach out to and care about all beings as relatives in the family of humanity. Ubuntu transcends race and creed. All diversity becomes, or rather is, only one reality, just what the Buddhas and mystics in all traditions came to realize. We feel an intense, ineradicable sense of belonging and we pass on to others that same joyous sense. We remind ourselves that we are not stand-alone beings; we exist only with everyone else, the solidarity that Thich Nhat Hanh calls “inter-being.”

We notice unanimous agreement among the assisting-counseling forces in all times and traditions: We can be allies to one another only if there are no boundaries to our assisting outreach. Indeed, such boundaries are really barriers. The wise, courageous assisting forces in all traditions have made the journey from insulation to universal embrace. They see diversity as a path to unity. Their caring, committed connection has no frontier.

Updated November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Kaja Perina

I know I'm not alone to have experienced people say something like, "I love my dog" and then observe countless different sorts of human-dog relationships that can change in the blink of an eye. I'm not saying that the people who claim they love their dog don't, but in many cases when they tell me that and I see how they mistreat their canine companion I can only say, "I glad they don't love me."

I've always wanted to know more about the variegated nature of human-dog relationships and was pleased to learn of Melina Jacques' recent book What It Really Means To Love Dogs: A Cultural And Ethical Exploration in which she discusses "a set of contradictions that are widely experienced but rarely named. Certain forms of intrusion become routine, while those expected to accommodate them are often left without a clear way to articulate what is happening."

Marc Bekoff: What are some of the topics you consider and what are some of your major messages?

Melina Jacques: One of the book’s central arguments is that dogs have become powerful cultural symbols associated with goodness, compassion, love, loyalty, and virtue. A significant portion of the book explores how this symbolic status emerged and why it is so vigorously defended.

The book examines how this symbolic status influences public attitudes toward dogs and can discourage critical discussion of issues that would be readily and openly addressed in other contexts. It explores the consequences of dog ownership for human communities, including attacks, chronic noise, neighbourhood conflict, relationship breakdown, and the diversion of resources and attention away from other priorities. For example, dog attacks in the United States result in reconstructive surgery approximately every 19 minutes, with children disproportionately represented among the victims. Yet discussions of dog attacks are often redirected toward individual circumstances or responsible ownership rather than examining broader questions about the normalization of dog ownership and public safety.

Persistent barking can have consequences far beyond sleep deprivation. Repeated exposure to unwanted, unpredictable noise can activate the body’s physiological stress-response systems, involving stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline. Over time, chronic noise exposure can contribute to sustained psychological distress and adverse cardiovascular and metabolic health effects, effectively making people’s own homes sources of chronic environmental stress.

Conflicts over dogs' behaviour and the prioritization of dogs' needs can also contribute to tension and relationship breakdown. These are not simply isolated inconveniences but consequences of a cultural environment in which the presence and interests of dogs are often treated as beyond question. Municipalities also devote resources to animal shelters and responding to complaints and attacks.

I also examine the ecological impacts of dogs, including predation, disease transmission, disturbance of wildlife, nutrient pollution, and threats to vulnerable species. Research discussed in the book implicates dogs in 11 vertebrate extinctions and identifies nearly 200 species worldwide that are threatened by their impacts.

These impacts extend beyond direct predation. The scent of dog urine, for example, can alter wildlife behaviour, causing wild animals to avoid otherwise suitable and valuable habitat. Dog waste also introduces nutrients into the environment, contributing to nutrient pollution and affecting surrounding ecosystems. Dogs can also introduce infectious diseases and parasites into wildlife populations. Free-ranging dogs, in particular, can act as reservoirs or sources of transmission for pathogens, such as parvovirus or canine distemper, that affect wild carnivores and other susceptible species, sometimes placing additional pressure on already vulnerable populations.

Hybridization presents another ecological concern. Domestic dogs can interbreed with wild canids, including grey wolves and golden jackals, introducing domestic-dog genes into wild populations and potentially compromising their genetic integrity.

Another major theme concerns dogs themselves. Although dogs are widely regarded as among the most loved animals on Earth, I question whether many aspects of modern dog ownership truly serve their interests. For example, breeding dogs to satisfy human preferences for particular physical characteristics can produce animals with serious inherited health problems, raising questions about whether commercial interests and aesthetic preferences are being prioritized over dogs’ physical well-being and quality of life.

Similarly, behaviours that may reflect frustration or unmet needs arising from unsuitable living conditions are increasingly being managed with psychotropic medications. Rather than addressing the underlying circumstances contributing to dogs’ distress, we may simply be medicating behaviours that humans find inconvenient or undesirable.

The book also explores the economic and psychological forces that sustain dog culture, including the role of the pet industry, social incentives, identity, and moral self-image. The pet industry, for example, has developed an enormous commercial ecosystem around dog ownership, encompassing food, accessories, grooming, training, veterinary services, insurance, and other products and services. These commercial interests benefit from the normalization and expansion of dog ownership.

At the psychological level, dog ownership can also become intertwined with personal identity and moral self-image. People may regard owning or adopting a dog as evidence of their compassion and kindness, making criticism of dog-related practices feel like a personal attack rather than an opportunity to examine the consequences of those practices. The book explores how these economic interests and cultural beliefs reinforce one another, helping to sustain a culture that is rarely subjected to critical scrutiny.

At its broadest level, the book asks a simple question: What does it actually mean to love a dog? My conclusion is that caring responsibly for animals requires us to look beyond sentiment and think honestly about the consequences of the relationships we create with animals.

MB: How does your book differ from others that are concerned with some of the same general topics?

MJ: Many books explore dogs through the lenses of behaviour, cognition, welfare, history, or companionship. My book differs in that it places these topics within a larger examination of dog culture itself and the consequences that arise from it.

Rather than treating dogs solely as animals, I examine them as cultural symbols and explore how that symbolic status shapes public attitudes, social norms, and policy decisions. The book also investigates an institution that is rarely subjected to critical scrutiny: pet ownership itself.

Drawing on multiple disciplines, it investigates why dogs occupy such a unique place in human society and what consequences follow from that distinction.

MB: Are you hopeful that as people learn more about what it really means to love a dog they will give their companion canine a better and more fulfilled life, a win-win for all?

MJ: Yes, although perhaps not in exactly that way. If readers come away with a deeper understanding of dogs and a greater willingness to think critically about their needs, many dogs could benefit. One of the book’s central themes is that genuine concern for dogs requires us to ask whether the lives we create for them are truly compatible with their interests.

At the same time, I hope readers expand their circle of concern beyond individual dogs to include human communities, wildlife, ecosystems, and other animals affected by our relationship with dogs. If the book encourages people to reflect more deeply on those questions, I would consider that a positive outcome.

Dog researcher Dr. Clive Wynne calls Jacques' book "a provocative corrective" and dog expert and bioethicist Dr. Jessica Pierce captures the essence of Jacques' book as follows:

"Dog lovers who care deeply about the well-being of dogs, and about our place in a shared social and ecological world, should read this book. Melina Jacques raises challenging questions that are too often ignored, asking us to examine both the burdens that contemporary dog-keeping can place on other people and communities, the impacts our practices can have on broader ecosystems, and the costs they can impose on dogs themselves. Thoughtful, provocative, and deeply humane, this is a book that deserves to be read with an open mind."

Dr. Jonathan Balcombe, ethologist, and author of What a Fish Knows and Beyond Tooth and Claw writes: "Melina Jacques has written a thought-provoking and highly controversial examination of our relationship with dogs. Her thesis challenges deeply entrenched assumptions about what it means to live with and care for dogs, and she makes a compelling case for reconsidering many of those assumptions. Jacques knows she is swimming against a powerful current, yet she goes forward determined and undaunted. I admire that. This is a worthwhile and provocative contribution to the literature on our relationship with other animals, and one that deserves a wide readership."

Ozempic and similar drugs have a surprising benefit that goes beyond weight loss

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

New research suggests that Ozempic and Wegovy may reduce long-term sick leave, benefiting employers and taxpayers without increasing workers' earnings.

Ozempic is best known for lowering blood sugar and body weight. But does better health also mean more time at work and higher earnings? A study from Denmark found that the answer isn’t so simple.

Adults there who started Ozempic spent less time on long-term sick leave in the years that followed. But they were no more likely to hold a job than similar adults who hadn’t started the drug yet, and their income didn’t rise.

The money saved went to employers and local governments, which pay for sick leave in Denmark, instead of to the patients themselves.

Meltem Daysal of the University of Copenhagen and four colleagues, including economists at the ROCKWOOL Foundation and Duke University, carried out the analysis.

Their paper is an early release that the authors may still revise, and it compares patients in national health and tax records instead of assigning anyone to take the drug.

Ozempic, a brand of the drug semaglutide, became available in Denmark in August 2018.

During the study years, it was approved there for adults whose type 2 diabetes wasn’t under control with diet, exercise and earlier medicines.

The team started with 9,794 Danes who began taking Ozempic in its first 17 months on sale.

Comparing them with people who never took it would mislead, because patients who start a new diabetes drug tend to differ from those who don’t.

So the researchers paired each early starter with someone who began Ozempic four years later. Each pair was matched on age, sex, diabetes and obesity, and then on income, education, marriage and how often they saw a family doctor.

The matching produced 7,011 pairs. Until the later starters began their own treatment, they showed what might have happened to the early starters without the drug.

Everyone was between 30 and 59 at the end of 2017. That way, all of them would still be under 65, and more likely to be working, when the study ended.

In the United States, many patients stop these drugs early. One study of American patients with private insurance found that only 40% to 60% were still taking them after a year.

The Danish patients stuck with it. Four years after their first prescription, 75% of the early starters were still filling prescriptions for semaglutide, and most of those who quit did so in the first year.

Ozempic is subsidized in Denmark, where a month’s supply cost 1,117 Danish kroner, about $212, in 2019. A patient’s yearly out-of-pocket spending on subsidized prescriptions was capped at 4,300 kroner, about $681.

Earlier research has often blamed side effects when patients quit. The authors suggested that what patients pay may also play a big part in whether they stay on the drug for years.

In this study, sick leave counted as long-term once a spell ran past 30 days. Before starting Ozempic, the patients spent about 5.5% of their months on long-term sick leave.

Over the four years after the first prescription, that share fell by about 1 percentage point compared with the later starters, a drop of 17.3%. Put simply, long sick leave became about a sixth less common among the people taking the drug.

In the first two years, the early starters spent about 0.8 percentage points less of their time on long sick leave than the later starters. In years three and four, the gap reached about 1.1 points.

Payments from local governments, which cover sick leave after the first 30 days, fell by 19.4% over the same period.

The authors interpreted the decline as evidence of a treatment effect, noting that sick leave had followed similar patterns in both groups before the early starters began treatment.

The decline appeared in nearly every group the team checked when it split patients by sex, age and education. Women had the largest drop, while the estimates for men and for people under 50 were less certain.

The records can’t show exactly why sick leave fell, but the team found some clues in them.

After starting Ozempic, patients were less likely to visit an emergency department and less likely to fill prescriptions for heart and blood vessel medicines.

Earlier randomized trials, including one from 2016, found that semaglutide lowers the risk of serious heart problems such as heart attacks and strokes in high-risk patients. Events like those often keep a person away from work for weeks.

The authors called that a plausible explanation for the drop in sick leave, not a proven one. Their records include no weight or blood sugar measurements, so the team couldn’t track how each patient’s health changed.

Over the same four years, the team found no change in whether patients had jobs, whether they were in the workforce at all, or how much they earned.

The estimates were precise enough to rule out a rise in employment of more than 0.4 percentage points and a rise in income of more than 0.7%.

The authors traced this to the way Denmark pays for illness.

Employers cover the first 30 days of a worker’s sick leave, and the local government takes over after that. For workers who earn below a cap, those payments can replace all of their usual pay.

So when a worker missed fewer weeks, their income stayed about the same. The savings went to employers and taxpayers.

The team estimated those savings at about 4,163 kroner a year for each employed patient, or 1.3% of a year’s earnings. If shorter sick spells fell by the same share, the total would reach 4,766 kroner, about $722, or 1.5% of earnings.

For Denmark’s budget, this means the drug costs the public less than its pharmacy price suggests. Even so, the authors wrote, the savings remain far smaller than what GLP-1 treatments cost in 2026.

Sick pay in the United States is less generous than in Denmark. There, the authors suggested, similar health gains might show up instead as higher earnings, more hours, or fewer people losing their jobs.

A 2026 study of American women who started GLP-1 drugs to lose weight found large increases in employment among those who hadn’t been working.

The Danish authors wrote that the two studies differ in both their setting and their patients.

The team also checked Wegovy, the semaglutide brand approved in Denmark for weight management.

Among 67,736 new Wegovy users, long-term sick leave fell by about 1 percentage point within the first year, and jobs and income again didn’t rise.

Less than a year of records is too short to judge the long run, and the authors treated the Wegovy job and income results with caution.

Whether the gains last for people who take these drugs mainly to lose weight is still an open question.

Answering it will take longer records of weight-loss patients, ideally from countries where a sick day costs workers more than it does in Denmark.

The study appeared as an IZA Discussion Paper.

As the baby cried and screamed, the doctor performing the procedure said that the young boy would not remember this procedure years later. No anesthesia was used. Was the doctor correct? I have wondered about this for more than 40 years, and I can tell you that my research into epigenetic memory has taught me that he was probably wrong.

As a father, I took my 2-year-old daughter to a routine pediatrician's appointment for a vaccination. She cried after the shot, but otherwise seemed OK. The doctor told us she would probably have a sore leg the next day, but Tylenol would take care of it. Well, the next day, our daughter wouldn't walk on her sore leg. She cried and cried, despite our efforts to manage her pain with Tylenol and Ibuprofen. Well, years later, our daughter grew up to be one of the most courageous women I know. She climbed every 14,000-foot mountain in Colorado—all 58 of them! But she has had to deal with a lifelong fear of needles. If she has blood drawn, she has to lie down because she is likely to pass out if she sees the needle. For years, I have wondered: Is her fear of needles related to that early vaccination?

For much of the 20th century, physicians believed that newborns didn’t feel pain. Infants commonly underwent surgery with little or no anesthesia. That belief began to change in 1987, when a review published in The New England Journal of Medicine showed that newborns can experience pain, and they can also experience powerful stress responses due to the pain.

This raises the question: Can pain experienced early in life create a memory that persists for years? Research involving epigenetic memory suggests it is possible.

Epigenetics refers to chemical modifications that alter how genes are expressed without changing the DNA sequence. These modifications help cells “remember” what has previously happened.

In 2004, a study in Nature Neuroscience showed that rat pups receiving little maternal care developed epigenetic modifications to a gene in the hippocampus that left them with exaggerated stress responses as adults. Five years later, another study found similar changes in the brains of suicide victims who had been abused as children. These studies suggested that early trauma can be remembered by modifications to our cells’ DNA.

Strong evidence in humans linking medical procedures to epigenetic change comes from studies involving premature infants. These babies may undergo about 10 painful procedures per day, including heel lances, needle sticks, intubation, and suctioning.

An Italian study found that very preterm infants exposed to more skin-breaking procedures showed increased changes to the serotonin transporter gene between birth and discharge. Serotonin helps regulate mood, anxiety, and pain. A follow-up study linked these changes to greater negative emotionality at 3 months of age.

These changes may persist. Researchers in Canada found that at age 7, children born very preterm had greater epigenetic changes than full-term peers. Neonatal pain predicted that epigenetic changes were associated with more behavior problems later in life. This same group later connected greater neonatal procedural pain to physical changes in the brains of these children at the age of 8.

Healthy, full-term infants are not immune to these trauma-induced changes. Newborn circumcision is one of the most common surgical procedures performed in the United States, and historically it was often done without anesthesia.

In a 1995 study, researchers noticed that circumcised boys cried longer and showed more pain behavior during vaccinations at 4 to 6 months of age than uncircumcised boys. Their prospective 1997 study confirmed it. Circumcised infants reacted more strongly to routine vaccination months later than those who had received a topical anesthetic before circumcision.

In other words, a single painful event in the first days of life appeared to change the infant’s pain system, and pain relief partly prevented it. Whether epigenetic changes underlie this effect has not been directly tested, but the pattern of lasting sensitization from early pain fits closely with the previously described findings.

Toddlers and school-age children remember procedures as well. A review found that a minority of children develop posttraumatic stress symptoms after surgery, and 12 to 31 percent of children undergoing cardiac surgery develop posttraumatic stress disorder. Medical fear in childhood can also shape how people approach health care as adults.

The evidence is preliminary that these changes are due to epigenetic memory. But it is encouraging to note that epigenetic changes appear to be potentially reversible, and maternal sensitivity can reduce these effects in full-term infants.

Necessary procedures should not be avoided because of fear. But the evidence argues that we should be taking infant and child pain seriously. Strategies for reducing the effects of painful and other traumatic procedures include providing anesthesia for circumcision and analgesia for other painful procedures, offering skin-to-skin contact, or breastfeeding during needle sticks, avoiding unnecessary procedures, and preparing older children for what to expect.

The infant may not remember the procedure. But their body may.

Jo Spence (1934-92) was a London-born and based British photographer, writer, class and health activist and educator. She is best known for challenging the assumptions of what photography could be, for example, by using her own image to question common tropes around female beauty, health, identity and societal expectations. Spence’s achievements centred on the small home and studio on Islington’s Upper Street that she shared with her partner and collaborator Terry Dennett (1938-2018).

The Unknown Recordings is an intimate self-portrait in text form. There are pictures, but surprisingly few. Still, in that respect, Spence’s disarmingly frank autobiography Putting Myself in the Picture (1986) could not be surpassed. Forty years on, this publication makes sense of a textual account largely in the form of transcripts of key historic recordings made with the artist.

The verbatim transcript of Spence’s interview for the British Library’s Oral History of British Photography, recorded in 1991, extends to more than 100 pages. It covers every aspect of her life from childhood experiences to early working life, before progressing to her photographic work, collaborations and politics, as well as her experience of breast cancer and leukaemia.

The conversation between Spence and the interviewer flows, with few fragments and diversions, in a manner that allows us (almost) to hear it. Generous in her sharing of extremely personal ideas and experiences, Spence does not shy away from difficult topics, least of all her own experience of chronic illness and her attempts to regain power over her own body and identity. Her friendship with artist Rosy Martin and their development of phototherapy, a form of psychological therapy centred on photography, is especially raw.

The transcript of a short personal recording made in 1982, after the breast cancer diagnosis, is more unsettling. This is Spence recording her own thoughts, filled with distress, tears and self-analysis: while it became part of her archive and she often blurred the line between private and public, it does feel a little like eavesdropping. Recorded over two days, it is intensely emotional, as Spence struggles to process and come to terms with the various stressors in her life. In a particularly poignant moment, Dennett is recorded shouting words of support, presumably from another room.

Spence’s interview with visual activist and educator Andrew Dewdney and curator Shirley Read has a political slant. Recorded in the Cockpit Arts Workshop in London in 1989, this conversation gives acute insight into Spence’s commitment to a political as well as photographic practice. It ranges across her ideas relating to different areas of her work, such as the Photography Workshop, Hackney Flashers, Half Moon Photography Workshop and Camera­work magazine. Ultimately, these projects perhaps coalesced around Spence’s focus on people’s right to speak and be seen. It is a powerful conversation.

A series of four appendices act as a kind of postscript to Spence’s voice. There is the documentation of Spence’s sacking from Half Moon over ideological differences; recently discovered images of Spence’s retrospective of 1994; photographs, thoughts and exchanges with artist Pru Clements; and Spence’s CV from March 1987.

This section also includes Dennett’s reworked introduction to Spence’s Putting Myself in the Picture. Here, he makes it clear that Spence’s work was done under difficult circumstances but that she remained focused on the importance of empowering others. Rather than concluding in some fashion or other, together these last documents point the reader to other rich visual and textual archival material that surrounds Spence’s life and legacy. She extended the possibilities of photography to speak about the unspeakable and photograph the unphotographable. Spence was not within the cultural elite, but found a way to tell her own story and hoped it would be a model for others to follow.

Typical treatment for early onset type 2 diabetes is often a combination of lifestyle changes, like diet and exercise, and medications.

The RESET for Remission trial included 96 adults from England and Canada who were between the ages of 18 and 45, had early onset type 2 diabetes, obesity, and were not on insulin treatment.

The participants had an average age of 38 and an average body mass index (BMI) of 35.2 kg/m2. They were randomized into a control group that received usual care alone and the intervention group, which ate a low calorie diet and participated in supervised exercise programs.

During the first 12 weeks, participants in the intervention group followed an 800–900-calorie-per-day diet and completed twice-weekly supervised aerobic and resistance exercise sessions, along with one independent exercise session per week.

Both groups were supervised by physicians, and the intervention group was also closely supervised by licensed dietitians.
