Of course, ultimately what beard trimmer is best for you comes down to your personal needs and preferences (which is why this list has six trimmers, not just one). But there are a few things you can look for that are essential for any beard trimmer, no matter what sort of beard you're caring for. Here’s the lowdown on how to shop for the best beard trimmers in 2026.

You want a strong motor with enough juice to shave or cut to the right length cleanly, smoothly, and with a minimal number of passes (more passes make for more skin irritation). In most cases, you get what you pay for here. The most budget-friendly beard trimmers in our roundup (those under $50) all do a decent job, but they often can’t match the raw power of a more premium, $100-plus model.

This is why you’ll notice most budget options tend not to disclose their motor speed, while all the higher-end devices on this list start at either 6,000 rpm (rotations per minute) or 10,000 cuts per minute, depending on which metric they use to detail motor power.

Much like the motor, the blades of a trimmer will dictate how quickly and effectively it cuts through hair. Typically, you want to look for blades made of durable materials like stainless steel or metal that stay sharp for a long time (many trimmers will even have self-sharpening blades, which is a plus for longevity).

You will eventually need to replace the blades, so choosing one with replaceable blades will always be helpful, but blades made from strong materials, like metals, will last longer than those made of plastic.

Cordless trimmers are great for a lot of reasons, which makes them the preferred choice for most bearded gents. But they can’t match the sheer power of traditional, corded models. If you’re opting for the latter, consider models with a long, flexible power cord like Bevel’s. This makes them both easier to store and to maneuver around your face with better precision.

If you’re opting for a cordless model, it’s important to keep its battery life in mind. Knowing how often you’ll need to charge it can mean never needing to stop in the middle of a trim to recharge (believe us, it happens way more often than you’d think). If you can remember to charge your trimmer often, then battery life may not be super important to you, but if you can’t, travel a lot, or just don’t want the hassle, opting for one with a long battery life is important.

Of course, length attachments are important in a beard trimmer as they allow you the freedom to customize the length of your beard with ease (and ensure an even cut). Some beard trimmers include only the most essential tools and attachments, which is fine if you’re a beginner or just need the basics.

But other, more comprehensive kits are made more versatile with the addition of things like guide combs, extra trimmer heads, nose trimmer attachments, and more. Not having these isn’t necessarily a deal-breaker; again, it’s all about what’s right for you.

Imagine turning up to play for your semi-professional team on a Saturday afternoon and spotting Paul Scholes lacing up his boots for the opponents. Your first thought would be: ‘Wait a minute, is that Pau… no, can’t be!'

Stockport Georgians’ players experienced this in September 2018 when Scholes, in his early 40s at the time, came out of retirement to help out his son’s team. Royton Town, who were competing in the 11th tier of English football, managed to convince the Manchester United legend to pull on their maroon strip for one game only.

They were short on players and told their opponents that they were bringing in a 43-year-old dad to help out. Little did Stockport Georgians know that the unnamed father in question was one of the greatest midfielders in the history of English football.

Having retired in 2013, the appearance for Royton Town was Scholes' first bit of competitive action on a football pitch in five years. It was also officially recorded as the 500th appearance of his professional career. A couple of videos of the former midfielder in action were posted to social media at the time.

Even at 43 in a semi-pro game, Scholesy was still prepared to fly into a 50/50 tackle. However, the presence of a former England international and one of the best English footballers of all time wasn’t enough to prevent Royton slumping to a 1-0 defeat.

If you were part of the Stockport Georgians’ team that afternoon, you’d surely mention that you beat a team containing one of the greatest midfielders in Premier League history at every available opportunity. Despite the defeat, Royton manager Mark Howard had plenty of praise for Scholes.

Despite losing the game 1-0, Howard was very happy with what he saw from Scholes and credited him for "raising the level of those around him by 10-15 per cent” - including that of his own son, Arron. The manager spoke to BBC Sport about the game afterwards and explained why the Englishman had offered to play:

"We had eight or nine of our senior players missing and Paul said he'd be happy to help out if we were ever short as his son Arron plays for us. It's great for the league. Opponents don't want to look stupid in front of him."

The gaffer wasn't the only one grateful for Scholes' efforts and helping out in the game. Royton chairman Gary Leeming also praised the legendary midfielder, one of the greatest Premier League players of all time, following his cameo.

Following Howard's comments, Royton's chairman also got in on the action and publicly praised Scholes. He credited the former United man for boosting the team's attendance for the match and revealed that he'd occasionally been training with the side prior to playing for them anyway and had proven he was still very good despite his age.

"Usually, these sort of games attract 40 or 50 people but there were a lot more there when word got out that he was playing. I can't imagine Stockport Georgians have ever had so many people on their car park.

"We were down to the bare bones. He's 43 now but he's still got that magic wand and he was absolutely fantastic for us. He obviously has a lot of media commitments but he occasionally trains with us when he can and is still a great player."

Class truly is permanent. It's not what Scholes is used to, having shone on the grandest stages such as the Premier League and the Champions League, but when you truly love the beautiful game, it doesn't matter where you are - as long as you've got a ball.

A brief history behind the Rings of Power was included in The Fellowship of the Ring, and Rings of Power now continues to build out the concept.

Sauron helped craft 20 Rings of Power, which he gifted to the various races of Middle-earth not from the kindness of his cold, black heart, but to dominate and control them. Sauron gave nine to men, seven to dwarves, the Elves had three, and the Dark Lord kept for himself the one ring to rule them all. Some of the Rings of Power are more important than others in the grand tapestry of Middle-earth, but all have history on the fingers of important figures.

Sauron created many of his rings in Rings of Power season 2, but in season 3 he begins crafting the One Ring and distributing the remaining rings in his collection. It's the big moment of the series, so it's worth taking a look at just how these different rings impact the world around them, and in what ways Rings of Power has changed the canon story.

The most famous, most powerful, most precious Ring of Power is, of course, Sauron's One Ring, forged in the fires of Mt. Doom as a master controller for the other 19. Crafting the Ring required Sauron to pour a large portion of his own power into the melting pot, creating a symbiotic bond between the item and its master. Sauron would never be truly killed while the Ring was still whole, but neither could Sauron wield his full Maia power without the Ring on his finger.

As recounted in The Lord of the Rings, Sauron used the One Ring to bring Middle-earth to his knees, until the weapon was cut from his hand by Isildur during the Last Alliance of Elves and Men. Isildur put up the feeblest of fights against the Ring's temptation before resolving to keep it, but the next part of the Ring's story is lesser known.

After many years of tragedy and struggle, Isildur realized Hugo Weaving's Elrond probably had a point about the Ring being bad news, and set out to deliver it into the safe custody of the elven ruler himself. Isildur's company was beset by orcs during the journey, and though the King managed to slip away using the Ring's invisibility, it betrayed him by falling from its owner's finger into the River Anduin, exposing Isildur to orc arrows.

Over 2000 years, the Ring remained in its watery resting place until, as shown in The Return of the King's movie adaptation, Sméagol the stoor found it while fishing. The Ring passed from Sméagol to Bilbo Baggins, from Bilbo to Frodo Baggins and, following a brief stint with Samwise Gamgee, into the fire from whence it came.

While the One Ring was the most powerful, there were 19 other Rings of Power in Middle-earth, and each of them was a considerable force in its own right. Whereas Sauron crafted the One Ring in secret from his Mordor stronghold, the other 19 were made openly by the elves — albeit not without a dark guiding hand. While Sauron gifted the Three Rings to the elves, they were crafted by Cerebrimbor alone. The three rings of power given to the elves were Narya (the Ring of Fire), Nenya (the Ring of Adamant), and Vilya (the Ring of Air).

Operating in the shadows and sowing dissent among the already-fractured elven houses, Sauron offered Eregion magical knowledge and pushed them toward crafting the Rings of Power.

Celebrimbor, the greatest of the smiths, duly obliged, and personally crafted a trio of powerful rings — Narya, Nenya and Vilya. Since Celebrimbor alone made them, the Elves' Rings of Power didn't fall directly under Sauron's sway, and lacked the same corrupting qualities other Rings of Power imbued upon their unlucky owners. Nevertheless, all three still came under the influence of the Dark Lord's One Ring.

Though Tolkien is ambiguous in describing their abilities, each elven Ring of Power comes with unique benefits. Narya specializes in flame magic, while also giving the bearer power to inspire others and resist weariness. Nenya could be used as a shield and ward against evil forces. Vilya, the strongest of the three, is suspected to possess the same gift, but with an added affinity for controlling water.

Narya and Vilya were both originally held by Gil-Galad, the Elves' High Ruler. Gil-Galad passed Narya to Círdan the shipwright, one of the most ancient elves in Middle-earth, but when Círdan bore witness to Gandalf's arrival, he thought the wizard would find better use for it. Gandalf carries Narya through the entire Lord of the Rings trilogy.

Vilya, meanwhile, Gil-Galad gave to Elrond long before the events of The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings, and the third Ring, Nenya, is constantly held by Galadriel. After the Ruling Ring is destroyed by Frodo, all three become inert - nice to look at, but as useful in battle as Sam Gamgee's prized marrow.

It's well known that the Elves weren't the only race in Middle-earth to receive Rings of Power, as seven were also given to the Dwarf lords. However, this wasn't Sauron's initial plan. Lord of the Rings' famous rhyme implies Sauron always intended to spread his Rings of Power among the various races of Middle-earth, but the pretty verse doesn't tell the full story.

Sauron initially hoped all 19 Rings of Power would be used to corrupt the Elves, but after forging the One Ring and putting his villainous scheme into motion, his targets quickly wised up and took off their Rings. Sauron then fought to get them back, and though the Elves managed to hide the three strongest (Narya, Nenya and Vilya), the others were mostly reclaimed. Only at this point, thwarted by flowery forest folk, did the Dark Lord give seven Rings of Power to the Dwarves and nine to the kings of Men.

The Dwarves' seven Rings of Power are the ones Tolkien wrote least about. As with the Elves, Sauron let the Dwarf-lords wear his gifts, then tried corrupting them through the One Ring. He failed again, this time because the Dwarves were too hardy and resolute to be controlled. Rather than falling under Sauron's spell, dwarf ring-bearers accumulated great wealth, but became even more consumed by their greed, setting into motion the events of The Hobbit. Only one of the Seven is specified in Tolkien's lore — the Ring of Thrór, which was gifted by Celebrimbor, not Sauron.

Once again, Rings of Power changes the story here. In season 2, Sauron and Celebrimbor specifically craft the Seven for the Dwarves and the Nine for Men. While the Dwarven Rings are handed out before the fall of Eregion, Sauron has all of his Nine headed into Rings of Power season 3.

As the Dwarves' Rings of Power attracted considerable trouble from dragons, it's hardly surprising that four of the Seven were melted under the beasts' flaming breath. Sauron managed to recapture the remaining three following his return to prominence, then boldly attempted bargaining with the Dwarves, offering the trio in exchange for their allegiance. The Dwarves, however, declined.

Of all the races that received Rings of Power, it was the race of Men that was the most corrupted of all the inhabitants of Middle-earth. It's safe to say that Sauron's gamble with the Rings of Power failed, though with the human kings of Middle-earth everything went according to his dark design — which is how the Nazgûl eventually came into being.

Sauron finally tasted success with the nine human kings he bestowed Rings of Power upon. Less magical than the Elves and less resistant than Dwarves, Sauron's chosen Men-folk lapped up his tempting gifts, succumbing fully to the One Ring's invisible tendrils. As the Dark Lord promised, the nine kings acquired power, agelessness and riches — though this came with a heavy price.

The Nine humans given Rings of Power would've known Sauron's price was their tether to the mortal realm if only they weren't blinded by their greed. The kings gradually slipped into a nether-plane, existing only as ghostly wraiths subservient to the will of Sauron, and dependent on him for their power. These phantom slaves became known as the Nazgûl, or Ringwraiths, and continued to obey their master until being vanquished for good.

Little is known about the identities of the Nazgûl, with only the Witch-king of Angmar and Khamûl the Easterling named by Tolkien. The fate of the nine Rings of Power reserved for Men is also somewhat mysterious. The pieces themselves were never found, so either remained with Sauron in Mordor, or stayed with the physical bodies of their respective kings — wherever those were kept. The shroud of mystery surrounding so many of Sauron's Rings of Power makes the topic ripe for exploration in The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power.

While snowfall isn't as regular in many parts of the country as it used to be, storm systems can still dump big piles of snow at once. When it comes time to dig out, there are few tools as effective as a gas-powered snowblower.

Although electric snowblowers are certainly attractive—they are relatively lightweight, easy to use, and require little maintenance—they lack the raw power and long runtime you’ll need if a storm drops 18 inches of snow or more on your home, especially if you have a large driveway.

The best gas snowblowers can carve a path as wide as two feet—giving you plenty of room to walk through as much as 21 inches of snow on a single pass. Gas snowblowers are the best tools for heavy-duty snow removal jobs at home. They’re effective against snowbanks piled up to two feet high, and can clear out larger areas, including wide driveways and walking paths. If you live somewhere that gets hit by many large snowstorms each season, and you’ve got a considerable-sized area to clear, then this is what you need.

Not everyone needs a gas snowblower, though. For homes with smaller needs, they may feel like more trouble than they’re worth. Gas-powered machines require more maintenance. Plus, they’re usually quite heavy, which makes them hard to use on tiered walkways with steps. If heavy snow is a rare occurrence, we’d recommend going with an electric snowblower instead, which will be more nimble and won’t produce any fumes.

Most gas snowblowers feature either a single-stage or two-stage mechanism, depending on how the snowblower collects, processes, and expels snow.

Also known as “snow throwers,” they use an auger to pull in snow, break it up, and throw the snow out through the chute in a single action.

These also use an auger to pull in snow, but then mash it up with a spinning impeller before spitting it out through the chute. Separating the snow-pulling and snow-crushing/throwing mechanisms allows the two-stage snowblowers to move snow more quickly and throw it a longer distance, which can be helpful when clearing larger driveways. These robust machines also work better on gravel, since their augers are slightly elevated and won’t draw rocks into the tines.

With an extra component that requires a more powerful engine to operate properly, two-stage snowblowers are generally larger, making them more difficult to maneuver around tight spaces. If you have a small walkway or gardens with uneven ground to avoid, operating one may feel unwieldy. As you might expect, they also cost more than their single-stage counterparts.

As with deciding between electric and gas snowblowers, consider the size of the area you normally clear before picking a single- or two-stage model. Single-stage snowblowers work well for clearing walking paths and the average-sized two- to four-car paved driveway. Dual-stage models are ideal for clearing deep snow from long paved or gravel driveways.

A snowblower’s clearing width and intake height determine how much snow it can move with each pass. The larger the snowblower’s intake, the more quickly you can get the job done.

Most single-stage gas-powered snowblowers have intakes that range from 22 to 24 inches wide, while two-stage snowblowers can have intakes that are up to 32 inches wide. The height of the snowblower’s intake, which is sometimes referred to as “clearing depth,” indicates the maximum snow depth it can handle. The intake height for a gas snowblower ranges from 18 to 21 inches.

Purchasing the snowblower with the widest, tallest intake may seem like a no-brainer, but keep in mind that there’s a correlation between intake dimensions and the overall size of the snowblower. The largest gas snowblowers will have a harder time maneuvering through tight turns. Ultimately, you should choose the smallest option that can effectively clear the paths you need and the amount of snow you expect to see.

A snowblower's job is to take snow in front of you and move it somewhere out of the way. With a more powerful snowblower, you can send the snow farther away. That’s crucial when you’re clearing out a large space, and you need to pile it up dozens of feet from you. Single-stage gas snowblowers can launch snow up to 40 feet, while two-stage snowblowers have a snow-throwing range of 50 feet or more.

Some gas snowblowers come equipped with a throttle on the dashboard that allows you to adjust the throwing distance by changing the speed of the impeller, allowing you to target a specific snow-piling plot.

Beyond the basics, many gas snowblowers have additional features that make them easier to use. Since they weigh hundreds of pounds, many are self-propelled, so you don’t have to push them forward. Most also have electric starters that eliminate the need to pull a starter cord. Some even have built-in power steering that automatically slows one wheel for easy turning.

Other quality-of-life features abound: You may find dashboard controls that allow you to change the chute direction and snowblower speed with one hand, LED headlights for late-night (or early morning) jobs, and handlebars that heat up to keep your hands nice and toasty as you work.

We selected the best gas snowblowers based on recommendations from the Popular Mechanics test team, as well as research and personal testing experience. Some of our picks were recommended by PM test editor Brad Ford, who has logged hours and hours testing snowblowers over the years. The final lineup includes a mix of single-stage gas-powered models for small to moderate driveways that can house four to six cars, and larger two-stage models that can clear snow from the longer and larger driveways you usually find on rural properties.

There are a few things you need to do to make sure your gas snowblower runs smoothly through the season:

First, check the oil level between each use and top off as needed. You should change the oil once a year, even if you didn’t use your snowblower all winter.

Change the spark plug at the beginning of each season to ensure it starts smoothly. A dirty spark plug can make it difficult to get your snowblower going, especially in extreme cold.

Lastly, small gas engines require plenty of airflow to run smoothly, so check the air filter once a season and replace it if it's dirty.

Snowblowers use unleaded gasoline—the same thing you put in your car—which begins to degrade after about 60 days. Running a snowblower with old fuel can clog the carburetor and make the engine hard to start, so you should never store your snowblower with fuel left in the tank.

Before putting your snowblower to bed for the season, either run the fuel tank dry or add fuel stabilizer, which keeps gasoline fresh for up to 24 months, to the tank. If you use fuel stabilizer, run the snowblower for a minute or two after adding it to the tank to circulate it through the engine.

A snowblower should be able to handle the lion’s share of your snow removal duties, but you should definitely keep a snow shovel around to clean up whatever the snowblower leaves behind. You may also want to save the gas and dig out with a shovel after a light, soft snowfall.

A notable example of a Pure Titan would be the humanoid dubbed Bean, who first appeared when the gates of Trost District were broken by Attack on Titan's epic Colossal Titan.

Bean was one of the two Titans kept alive for the sake of experimentation in an attempt to better understand the nature of these beasts and their innate weaknesses.

The Abnormal Titans are one of the most bizarre Titan types. Abnormals are Pure Titans that display unusual behavior and intelligence, allowing them to perform unpredictable moves. They often tend to ignore nearby humans and move on to strategic locations to inflict more damage.

After Hange Zoë and Levi’s squad find the diary, it is revealed that the Titan addressed Ilse as “Lady Ymir”, mistaking her for Attack on Titan's Founder, Ymir Fritz.

Stronger and more frequent severe weather events are making a compelling argument for investing in a home generator. A large portable generator or a permanently installed standby generator can keep your entire home powered and operational for several days, or at least keep a few critical appliances such as a refrigerator running until the power comes back on. The cost of emergency power is never a small investment, though it is an increasingly important one.

The term “home generator” can be confusing. It’s an unofficial category that includes larger portable generators capable of powering several appliances or a few critical circuits using a transfer switch (not all portable generators are built for this task). It also includes permanently installed standby generators that can automatically switch to whole-home backup power when the lights go out. Choosing the right size generator primarily comes down to how much power you think you’ll need in an emergency.

There are many reliable home generators that deliver a great deal of power. Some offer you the choice of running on multiple different fuels, while other models use an inverter that can give you quieter and “cleaner” operation. There is a nice range of prices available, too, so you can easily find a generator below that fits your needs as well as your budget.

For the generators on this list that we tested, we considered the difficulty of unboxing, assembling, and starting each generator for the first time, because few things are as frustrating as buying a tool that turns out to be unnecessarily complicated from the get-go.

Once set up, the generators we reviewed were subjected to different load conditions, including things such as power tools, home appliances, and even recharging large power stations. We assessed the features that distinguish these generators from each other, including safety features, app connectivity, and portability, among other things.

A few picks on our list have not been tested, but we included them here after considerable research, taking into account the brand’s reputation, our knowledge of its product lines, various outside reviews, and our own experience testing similar models from those same brands.

You could just drive over to your local hardware store and buy a large portable generator to use as a home backup, but we wouldn’t recommend it. You want to make sure you’re getting a generator that can back up your home and power all the appliances, lights, and electronics inside. (Plus some extra watts in case you need them down the line.)

In addition, there are often state and local laws or homeowners association rules and restrictions around using them in residential neighborhoods. Choosing poorly or setting up your backup generator incorrectly could damage the generator or short out your house power at best, or lead to fire, electrocution, or carbon monoxide poisoning at worst.

Picking the best generator for your home starts with a little math. Ideally, you want to calculate how much energy you need to keep your house running, and buy a generator that can handle everything you want to power in an emergency, with room to spare.

The two key specs to pay attention to are the running wattage and starting wattage (e.g., 9,500 running watts / 12,500 starting watts). Running wattage is effectively your generator’s capacity: It tells you how much power a generator can produce steadily without any possibility of an overload. Starting wattage is the generator’s max output, or how much power it can produce for short, peak bursts when appliances first switch on.

When you are making your list, note what items are 120-volt and which ones are 240-volt appliances. It’s not hard to identify them. Refrigerators, small appliances, and most electronics run on 120-volt power and plug into standard outlets. Anything that requires 240 volts—electric dryers, well pumps, and HVAC systems—is either hardwired into your electrical system or has a dedicated 240-volt outlet. If you need to power the latter, the installation of a transfer switch and a generator that outputs at least 30 amps/240 volts may be required.

If you’re just looking to keep the fridge, lights, and a few modest appliances going through a short blackout, you can usually get away with a generator that supplies 3,000 to 5,000 watts. If you want to run the heat, hot water, and several appliances at once, plan on using at least 6,000 to 8,000 watts. If you think you’re going to be adding large appliances such as laundry machines and dishwashers, expect to need 10,000 watts or more.

A piece of advice we have: Once you figure out how much you need, opt for buying a generator that outputs around 20 percent more than that. It’s not uncommon to realize you need more than you thought you would.

It’s also important to consider that, while generators are certainly a hefty investment, they usually stand the test of time. Consumer-grade portable generators can run anywhere from 1,000 to 3,000 hours, sometimes more. If you run one for 100 hours per year, it should last for 10 to 30 years.

There are four basic types of generators, each with advantages and disadvantages.

The most traditional of the generator category, these run on carbon-based fuels like gasoline, propane, and natural gas. Some allow you to switch between them. Dual-fuel models add the option of either propane or natural gas, while tri-fuel versions offer all three fuel options. These generators can be small, offering as little as 1,000 watts of power output, or run very large, as much as 20,000 watts or more.

Different fuels offer different power capabilities. For example, a tri-fuel generator may offer 9,500 running watts and 12,500 starting watts with gasoline, but only 8,500/11,200 with propane and 7,500/9,500 with natural gas. Regardless of the fuel you choose, this type of generator must always be operated 5 to 20 feet away from the home or any window or door, because the carbon monoxide exhaust is deadly. Even a garage with an open door is not safe.

Often this type of generator is called portable, but that's a relative term: Almost all of them weigh more than 100 pounds, and many are upwards of 200. Many are on wheels, making them relatively easy to store in a garage and wheel outside when you need them, but actually moving one will likely require at least two people.

Inverter generators look and operate the same as other portable generators, but have an added component–a power inverter– that converts the alternating current (AC) power generated by its fuel source to DC (Direct Current), then inverts it back to AC before putting it out.

Generators without an inverter generate varying degrees of distortion in the sine wave of the alternating current, which can trigger power surges and other issues in some electronics. The inverter stabilizes the power, almost like a filter, “cleaning” it for more sensitive electronics like TVs and laptops.

So why doesn’t every generator have an inverter? Inverters are expensive, so the generators are, too.

Realistically speaking, you don’t need an inverter generator to power major appliances and your house power, but if you’re running a full home backup (or if you count a TV or laptop among your essential electronics), holding out for an inverter is a smart move.

While often referred to as “solar generators” because they can be paired with solar panels, power stations are not really true generators. They're advanced battery banks that store power, which you precharge using AC power or solar panels. They can’t generate electricity in real time.

But because they can offer many of the same electrical outputs as a fuel generator, they can function similarly to a traditional generator for as long as the batteries retain a charge. Some stations are very small and meant only for off-grid adventures like camping trips, but some are large enough to run whole-home systems that can produce 14,400 watts or more with expandable batteries.

Since power stations don't run on fuel or have an engine, they can be used indoors without the risk of carbon monoxide poisoning and operate with almost no noise.

The downside is that once the power is depleted, the power station must be recharged. If the power grid is still unavailable, it can take many hours in ideal sunny conditions if all you have are solar panels. You can use a traditional generator to recharge a power station, but some may view that as defeating the purpose.

Standby generators are permanently installed generators designed to automatically take over for your house power in the event of an outage. You can set them to power your whole house, or only specific circuits, giving you precise control. Standby generators start at around 10,000 watts (10 kilowatts) but can go up to much higher. For the average home, a range of 10 to 25 kilowatts is usually enough.

They typically require professional installation, making them the most costly option. Since many large portable generators can effectively back up a house for a few hours for a lot less money, standby models are still considered a more niche option.

When you draw too much power from your generator, its performance will start to decline. At home, your lights will start to dim or flicker, and appliances may turn off momentarily. The generator itself may also start to get louder, or sound like it’s working extra hard to keep up.

If you notice these signs, start turning off and unplugging non-essential power sources immediately, or turn the generator off entirely for ten minutes or so to reduce the power load.

This is an urgent problem, so do not wait to take action: If you overwork your generator for too long, it’s only a matter of time before it starts to overheat, produce smoke, or break down. The damage from an extended overload could permanently damage the generator or, worse, start a fire.

With the right size for your home, a portable or standby generator can run as long as you keep feeding it fuel and stay within its running wattage capacity; however, you should not run them indefinitely. Manufacturers recommend you allow them to rest for 30 minutes or more after at least 24 hours of use. Check the manual of your generator for its specific recommendations.

You will get more time from each tank of gas if you draw less power, so it helps to pay extra attention to your electricity usage when you’re running the generator.

Make sure to turn your gas and/or propane generators off when it’s time to refill the tank. Failing to do so could lead to a fire.

Power stations, since they rely on electricity or solar power, can almost always be charged while in use.

Never run a fuel-based generator (gasoline, propane, natural gas) indoors. Burning fuel releases carbon monoxide (CO), which will quickly build up to deadly levels in an enclosed space.

Set your generator up outside, between 5 and 20 feet away from your home and/or any garages, sheds, or other walls. Position it as far as possible from doors, windows, and vents, with the exhaust positioned away from the house, to minimize the possibility of fumes wafting in. If you keep it outside all year round, find a naturally covered position where snow won’t naturally pile up around it.
