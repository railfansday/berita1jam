Manchester City suffered a third consecutive Premier League defeat at the hands of Nottingham Forest on Saturday, allowing Arsenal to stretch their lead at the top of the table to six points.

Enzo Maresca's men desperately needed a victory at the City Ground to keep pace with the in-form champions, who have taken 27 points from a possible 30, but lost 3-1.

City had a mountain to climb after finding themselves 2-0 down in the 18th minute following goals from defender Jair Cunha and forward Liam Delap.

The visitors were much improved after the break and Rayan Cherki cut the deficit with an emphatic strike into the roof of the net.

But Delap restored Forest's two-goal advantage in the 73rd minute, rifling home with his left foot past the outstretched hand of Gianluigi Donnarumma to earn his side breathing room.

Forest manager Oliver Glasner sprinted down the touchline to celebrate with his team, who had not managed to score a single Premier League goal at home before Saturday.

City, who won their first seven league games under new manager Maresca, know that even if they close the gap to Arsenal, it might ultimately all be in vain.

The Etihad club are still waiting to discover their fate after being found guilty of more than 100 breaches of Premier League financial rules -- with a huge points deduction one potential punishment.

Earlier, Arsenal made hard work of beating struggling Hull to underline their title credentials on the eve of the international break.

Captain Martin Odegaard finally broke the deadlock for the champions and title favourites in the 72nd minute.

Arsenal, who bossed possession at the Emirates, had a claim for a penalty in the closing minutes of the first half after Kai Havertz had his shirt pulled by Nobel Mendy.

But VAR ruled that the German forward was in an offside position in the build-up.

Frustration mounted as the second half unfolded but Odegaard came to Arsenal's rescue, scoring his first goal since early September with a poacher's finish.

Noni Madueke cut in from the right before unleashing a left-footed shot that Hull goalkeeper Konstantinos Tzolakis spilled and Odegaard fired home from close range.

Arsenal manager Arteta praised his players but wants them to be more clinical after they recorded a third 1-0 win in four league games.

"We can still be much better, and we will improve in many phases, and we will get certain relationships to a different level that will be even more efficient," he said.

"That's the next step, to have bigger margins and when we have them, the opportunities, put them away to make sure that we don't have to hang in there the last few minutes."

In the early kick-off, Leeds struck in the 79th minute through substitute Noah Okafor to end Tottenham's three-match winning run.

The major talking point in the first half was a challenge by Leeds defender Jayden Bogle on Spurs winger Mathys Tel, which was reviewed for a potential red card by VAR officials before eventually being cleared.

Tel had to leave the field shortly before half-time.

Spurs boss Roberto De Zerbi, disappointed by his team's second-half showing, refused to be drawn on the incident, which was not reviewed on the pitchside monitor by referee John Brooks.

"In the first situation of Mathys Tel I didn't see, I can't say nothing," he told TNT Sports after the 1-0 defeat.

He added: "I have big confidence in the referee. Nothing changes if he goes to watch the clip or not. I think there was the VAR and if they didn't take the right decision, I don't know, but we have confidence."

Elsewhere, Jacob Murphy put Newcastle on course for a much-needed win with a spectacular strike in the 80th minute at Craven Cottage but substitute Rodrigo Muniz levelled just three minutes later to earn a 1-1 draw.

Forest's win against City means Michael Carrick's Manchester United will kick off against Aston Villa on Sunday in the relegation zone.

Richard Lloyd Dies: Guitarist With Influential Punk Pioneers Television Was 75

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Richard Lloyd, whose intricate guitar work for the pioneering punk band Television was a fiery counterpart to the sinewy stylings of the band’s co-guitarist and singer Tom Verlaine, died today of the bile duct cancer called cholangiocarcinoma at his home in Chattanooga, Tenn. He was 75.

His death was reported by his family to The New York Times. Lloyd had been diagnosed with the cancer in March.

With his Television bandmates Verlaine, bassist Richard Hell (later replaced by Fred Smith) and drummer Billy Ficca, Lloyd was among the innovative, even revolutionary, New York bands in the mid-1970s to establish the East Village dive bar CBGB as the epicenter of a new downtown music scene. Along with Patti Smith and the Ramones, Television led the way in creating and developing a new sound – defined later (and loosely) as punk – that was often as literate and playful as it was sonically powerful. Related Stories News Paul Howard Dies: Son Of Three Stooges' Moe Howard, Keeper Of The Flame Was 91 News Kimmarie Johnson Dies: 'Beverly Hills, 90210' & 'Dude, Where's My Car?' Actor Was 50

Formed in 1973, Television grew out of the teenage friendship of two Delaware boys, Thomas Miller and Richard Meyers, who by the early ’70s had changed their names to, respectively, Tom Verlaine and Richard Hell and set about developing the spiky guitar sound and keening vocal style (courtesy Verlaine) that would reach its apotheosis in the band’s 1977 masterpiece Marquee Moon. Watch on Deadline

By the time of their 1974 debut at CBGB, Verlaine and Hell had recruited Lloyd to be the group’s second guitarist, a move that gave Television its signature and distinctive dueling Strat-versus-Jazzmaster sound. Unlike the more power-chord-heavy guitar bombast of other bands on the scene, Television specialized in complicated, even byzantine interplay between the six-stringed instruments. And like Patti Smith, Verlaine sang lyrics that signified a devotion to poetry as surely as did his chosen name.

Born Richard John Alt on Oct. 25, 1951, in Pittsburgh, Lloyd grew up in Manhattan’s Greenwich Village and began playing guitar before he hit his teens. He became a regular in the audience of the famed Fillmore East rock palace, mesmerized by the music of The Grateful Dead and other bands of the era. He would be forthcoming about working as a male hustler to support himself during the lean years.

With an early residency at CBGB, Television quickly won over audiences as well as fellow musicians like Patti Smith, Lou Reed and David Bowie, who had caught the band during a trip to New York and set about spreading the word to anyone who would listen. Bowie would later record the group’s song “Kingdom Come” for his 1980 Scary Monsters album

The band’s initial line-up didn’t last long, though, as the old friends Verlaine and Hell began to bicker over everything from their music to the way it was performed on stage. Hell quit in 1975 and formed a sort of punk supergroup called The Heartbreakers with ex-New York Dolls members Johnny Thunders and Jerry Nolan. He eventually left that group to form Richard Hell and the Voidoids.

Hell was replaced in Television by ex-Blondie bassist Fred Smith (not to be confused with Patti Smith’s husband Fred “Sonic” Smith).

Despite its quick success, Television called it quits in 1978, in part because of Lloyd’s increasingly dire heroin addiction and mental health issues. Lloyd would release nine solo albums after Television, and he and Verlaine, despite a prickly relationship, occasionally reunited the band. Neither guitarist would ever match the success or influence of Television in its genre-building heyday.

Lloyd is survived by wife Sheila O’Keefe Lloyd; son Dylan; and brother Eric Lloyd.

Noah Okafor was the match-winner for Leeds United as they recorded a much-needed 1-0 victory over Tottenham on Saturday.

Okafor, 26, struck less than two minutes after coming on as the Whites earned a first win in five matches and moved back into the Premier League top-half.

The Switzerland international, who missed the 2-0 defeat at Bournemouth last time out with an adductor issue, was introduced as a 77th minute substitute at Elland Road.

He replaced Lukas Nmecha, who started as the left forward, looking to make an impact and help his side find a breakthrough after finding the Tottenham defence a hard nut to crack.

And just 97 seconds after his introduction, Okafor found the net. He combined with Harry Wilson inside the left channel before finding some space and slamming the ball past Antonin Kinksy.

It was a piece of quality and invention that was missing from Leeds, and there is no doubt that Okafor should be starting week-in-week out when fit.

He is someone that can create a chance out of nowhere and is Leeds' most potent attacker, barring Dominic Calvert-Lewin, of course. Lukas Nmecha worked hard and is a good back-up option, but Okafor, who scored eight goals last season, is the best Farke has in that position.

With Okafor coming back in and showing that he should be in Farke's XI, Jean-Matteo Bahoya's Leeds career could now be over. The Frenchman, 21, who joined the Whites on loan from Eintracht Frankfurt in the summer, was left out of the 20-man squad to face Tottenham.

But Bahoya has played just 11 minutes for Leeds since his arrival, making three appearances in the process. He came on as a late substitute in the defeat at Bournemouth, but was unable to impact the game as the Whites were beaten at the Vitality Stadium.

There is no doubt Bahoya has talent, having showed that in glimpses at Frankfurt, but he has been unable to show that since he moved to Leeds. He might not be showing enough in training, but what is clear is that Farke isn't having him right now.

With Leeds scoring just once in three matches prior to the win against Tottenham, it did feel as though Farke should have been using Bahoya more. But the German provided a 10-minute explanation last month on the reason the forward isn't getting more minutes.

Farke outlined the fact he is a "young lad still getting used to the Premier League" but reiterated he does "love" Bahoya and that he is going to get his chance.

At this stage, it doesn't look promising for Bahoya, however. While Leeds were struggling in attack, he wasn't called up and that felt like the perfect time for him to get more game time.

For now, Bahoya is going to have to bide his time, but his Leeds career might be over before it even got started, especially if Frankfurt consider recalling him in January.

Okafor should start for Leeds on the left-hand side with Nmecha as back-up and Bahoya down as third-choice, though Brenden Aaronson could even be in front of the ex-Angers man.

’Saturday Night Live’s Famous Formula Has 1 Unfixable Problem

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Greer Riddell is a London-based film and television journalist writing about comedy, streaming and the evolving media industry in the UK and USA. She works at Collider, covering the latest in film, television and entertainment through interviews, features, reviews and industry coverage.

Her recent brand collaborations include HBO, Warner Bros., Netflix, Hulu, Sky, Disney+ and DC Studios. She has covered titles including Saturday Night Live UK, Rivals, Scrubs, Stuart Fails to Save the Universe, Ludwig, Supergirl and Stranger Things.

Saturday Night Live has spent 5 decades establishing a format that is instantly recognizable and runs like clockwork. Bringing the show to the UK meant deciding which of those traditions were worth keeping and which could be left behind. At the Edinburgh TV Festival 2026, SNL UK director Liz Clare and cast member Emma Sidi discussed two elements of the original that prompted very different reactions — the famous crane shot and the end-of-show credits.

While Clare rejected the crane because of the practical demands of the British production, Sidi admitted that the cast initially found the closing tradition unbearably "naff." SNL UK could not reproduce the American original and expect every production choice or cultural convention to translate, but neither could it reinvent everything without losing elements that make the format successful. So before any episode aired, the crew behind SNL UK had the mammoth task of working out what it could change and what it needed to keep, and while one problem solved itself, another remains unfixable.

One of the defining features of Saturday Night Live is its production setup, which allows the show to move between the monologue and sketches while maintaining the pace and energy of a live performance. SNL UK has adopted much of the American show's approach, but director Clare decided against using its famous crane shot. Speaking at the Edinburgh TV Festival's The SNL Masterclass, director Clare explained, “The camera setup is probably pretty standard. In fact, it's based on the American show, bar the fact that we don't use the Americans have got this big old bit of kit, big crane that's become a feature of the show,” Clare said. “We decided not to employ that camera because it takes up an awful lot of space.”

Britain makes more detective television than any country has any business making, and somehow almost all of it is good. Here is the one you belong in, what it says about you, and the case for it being the best of the four.

You want the tea, the gossip and the vicar behaving oddly. Midsomer is a county where the scenery is beautiful, the fete is on Saturday and the murder rate is roughly that of a small war, and absolutely nobody involved thinks this is strange.

You feel other people's grief as if it were handed to you personally. Broadchurch is less a whodunnit than a study of what a small town does to itself when it finds out that the answer was always going to be somebody everybody already knew.

You go through things rather than around them. John Luther is the best detective in London and a liability to everybody who works with him, and the show is completely honest that those two facts are the same fact.

You say the thing and you do not dress it up. Vera Stanhope turns up in a hat and a mac, is rude to almost everybody, and is the only person in the room prepared to ask the question that the family has been waiting years for somebody to ask.

The decision makes sense for a show that has to accommodate multiple sets, rapid changes and performances staged in the round. A large crane might be a recognizable part of the American production, but that does not mean it is essential to the format. Instead, the British production has adapted its equipment to suit the way the show operates. “But we do have a jib, and we've got some pads,” Clare explained, describing some of the alternatives available to her team. Her approach is less about reproducing the American show's visual identity exactly and more about understanding what its camera work is trying to achieve. As she put it, “It's really just about making sure that what is interesting about the show is that it's played in the round in a way that then helps all of those resets.”

Jamie Dornan’s turn as host in Season 1, Episode 2, offers a good example of how the money saved on the crane allows the series to follow the technical needs of each joke. Dornan played a criminal taking Annabel Marlow hostage, a role that required the production to recreate the appearance of a video recorded on a camcorder. Rather than relying on a conventional studio camera to capture the scene, Clare's team found a more specific solution. “We knew that we needed some kind of camera that he could look like he was recording a sort of hostage video,” she explained. “So we actually got a live really lovely sort of digicam that was a live picture, live feed that we were able to take out of the camera and put in and used to make it look as authentic as possible.”

'SNL UK' Head Writer Jonno Johnson lifts the curtain on the UK’s buzziest comedy.

The closing sequence of Saturday Night Live is one of its most familiar traditions. As the credits roll, the cast gathers around the host, exchanging hugs and saying goodbye to the audience. For Sidi, however, the practice initially seemed awkward and unnecessary. Her reaction highlights a different challenge for SNL UK. Technical decisions can be made according to the needs of the production, but cultural traditions can be harder to assess up front. Sidi admitted that she had never been particularly fond of the American show's endings. “That's something with the American show... the ending, the goodbyes bit. I would just see it and go, ‘This is just so naff. I can't bear to see this,’” she said at the Edinburgh TV Festival. From the viewer's perspective, the sequence can look like an overly sentimental ritual, particularly when performers who have spent the episode confidently delivering jokes suddenly show their relief that it is over in the farewell.

However, making SNL UK changed Sidi's perspective. The cast were experiencing the pressure of putting together a live show, working through rehearsals, changes, and the demands of performing. Sidi explained that she understood the tradition differently once she had experienced that process, “And when we're doing it now, I get it because we have done the heist during the week, you know?... So we're hugging and crying... fuck it, we're luvvies now,” she joked. “You are just like, thank god we did it. It's so real that hugging at the end.”

SNL UK could have dropped the closing sequence to avoid something the cast found embarrassing, just as Clare dropped the crane. Removing them would not change the quality of the series or make a sketch easier to stage. Yet it would have removed a moment that the people making the show have come to value. Sidi even acknowledged that viewers might still find the sequence uncomfortable: “So sorry you have to watch that, but we're gonna keep doing it!” Now halfway through its second season, Saturday Night Live UK is still developing its own identity away from the American original, but some things stay essential to the show regardless of where it is being made.

Palantir Stock Just Fell 8 Days in a Row. History Says What Followed Its Only 2 Longer Losing Streaks.

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Did the AI software company's two longer slides say anything about what happened next?

Palantir Technologies (PLTR +3.48%) stock closed lower for eight straight trading sessions through Thursday, Nov. 5. Over that span, shares dropped from $198.84 to $154.99, a fall of around 22%.

That put the AI software maker's stock about 26% under its record close on Oct. 9.

By my count of daily closes since Palantir started trading in September 2020, only two losing streaks have lasted longer. One was a 10-day slide ending May 10, 2021. The other was a nine-day slide ending March 1, 2023.

Each time, shares were up six months later. But a year out, the two cases ended up far apart. And two cases is a tiny sample, so I wouldn't rely on the streak to say much about what happens next.

Interestingly, both earlier slides were close in size to this one. The 2021 streak took the stock from $24.11 to $18.47, a fall of around 23%. The 2023 streak took shares from $10.11 to $7.69, a decline of around 24%.

(I measured what followed from each streak's final close, treating 21 trading days as a month.)

After the 2021 slide, Palantir stock gained around 31% in the next month and was up about 24% three months out. Six months later, it was about 41% above that final close. But a year after the slide ended, shares were down about 60% to $7.46.

The 2023 case went the other way over the full year. Shares were up only around 6% a month later. In the next three months, though, they rose by about 91%, and they were up 112% after six months.

Even more, one year out, the stock had more than tripled, gaining around 224% to $24.93.

The two seven-day streaks, which ended in September 2022 and June 2026, don't settle much either. After the 2022 slide, shares climbed about 16% in a month, dipped about 1% over three months, and were up about 12% after six months. A year out, the stock had more than doubled.

The June 2026 slide is too recent for a six-month read. But once it ended at $107.27, shares gained around 23% in the next month and about 80% in three.

So, after all four of Palantir's longest losing streaks before this one, the stock was up a month later. But with 12-month returns ranging from a 60% loss to a 224% gain, the streak arguably told investors little about the year ahead.

One difference between 2021 and 2023 sticks out to me, and it's the starting price. At $18.47, Palantir's stock traded at around 30 times its 2020 sales. At $7.69 in 2023, it cost under 9 times its 2022 sales. And Palantir lost money in both those years, though it had posted its first quarterly profit right before the 2023 slide started.

Today, Palantir's market value is more than 50 times its sales for the last four quarters, a higher sales multiple than in either case.

Sure, the business is very different now. Palantir's third-quarter revenue climbed 96% year over year to around $2.3 billion. This kept a streak of faster growth going, after rates of 70%, 85%, and 93% in the three quarters before that. And Palantir now turns a big part of its sales into profit, with a 47% operating margin last quarter.

Not every number is speeding up, though. The total contract value of the deals Palantir closed in the third quarter rose just 6% from a year before. The growth rate had already slowed sharply in the previous three quarters, dropping from 138% to 61% and then 49%. Deal timing can make the number lumpy. But if it keeps slowing, revenue growth might eventually follow.

Management's fourth-quarter revenue guidance of about $2.65 billion already implies growth of about 88%, down from the third quarter's 96%.

Palantir made $1.43 a share in its past four quarters. So at roughly $160 as I write, the stock trades at about 112 times earnings. Earlier this week, I put my buy price at 120 times those earnings, or about $172 a share.

In the end, the length of this streak doesn't tell me much about where Palantir stock heads next. What matters more to me is that shares now trade below my buy price.

Yes, a growth stock that just dropped 22% over eight sessions could easily keep swinging. But I think shares are worth a look at this level, and I'd build a position slowly instead of all at once.

Arsenal 1-0 Hull: The Gunners’ manager called for more pragmatism and chaos after Martin Odegaard’s goal secured a familier scoreline for Arsenal

After a familiar scoreline, Mikel Arteta has a new phrase.

The Arsenal manager used some variation on “insist” several times throughout his press conference for the win over Hull City, after his team had almost been left standing at the door.

Martin Odegaard instead offered a captain’s goal, for a third 1-0 win in four in the league. Like many of those, it could have been worse, but could have been better – especially in terms of finishing.

Arsenal currently always feel one setback away from a big discussion about which forward they need to sign in January. They are obviously monitoring a few options, and are expected to do some business, but Arteta clearly doesn’t want his players thinking like that.

When asked about what was actually a sixth win by one goal out of 10 Premier League games so far, he said they “need to be more efficient”.

It could be argued that such a return is the height of efficiency, and that Arsenal could have been further ahead had they benefited from that debated penalty decision – but you know what he meant, especially with regard to finishing.

Arteta lamented how his team had two further one-on-ones in the game, only to waste them. He said it was like they “are trying to score the perfect goal”.

That’s another familiar refrain with this club, if from a different era to the 1-0s. Arteta’s desired solution is to be “more pragmatic”, that they “need to be more chaotic”.

That’s undeniably true, but the manager being the one to say this poses some questions of his own.

Another phrase that Arteta used was “volume”. That, when you face a defence as well structured as Hull City, you need to be “generating volume”. In other words, numbers, a critical mass of chances where you eventually score – which is pretty much what they did. Insistence.

It’s been discussed on these pages before that Arteta is a huge “probabilities guy”, where he sees almost every game as an equation. If Arsenal consistently generate an xG of 2 or above and limit the opposition to 0.5, they are likely to win enough games to win the league.

A natural extension of this is an exactingly calibrated team, where Hull City players – like so many others – were left praising “the shape”.

But is that mentality something that encourages “pragmatism” or, as Arteta elaborated, “chaos”? Other descriptions for that are sparkle, ingenuity… magic.

That’s hard to find sufficient space for in a system that demands a lot of energy devoted to pressing and structure.

Odegaard, the match-winner, is almost the ultimate example. The playmaker is capable of some of the most ingenious and exquisite touches in the league, but it does not feel like he is as characterised by that as much any more.

When you ask people at Arsenal about him, in fact, they praise how he understands Arteta’s pressing better than anyone else. This is why he is captain and what they were said to really miss last season when he was out. Odegaard leads the press. When he goes, everyone immediately knows when and where to follow.

An irony is that Noni Madueke is one of the most chaotic players in the league – in a good way. He is a rare attacker that takes people on. As is the case when you see his and Christos Tzolis’s names on the teamsheet, you don’t quite know what you’re going to get in terms of performance level. Club insiders say it could be anything between a two and an eight out of 10.

The most frustrating thing about Madueke, in fact, is that he usually ends such runs with a predictable cross rather than real variation. He doesn’t shoot enough, which touches on Arteta’s point about trying to score “a perfect goal”.

All of this came together for a moment that should have sealed the win.

Arsenal’s attack put together a brilliant flowing move at 1-0, to leave Madueke through for one of those one-on-ones. It was close to perfect. Until, with just Konstantinos Tzolakis to beat, Madueke tried to roll the ball past him only to get his feet wrong.

While the forward clearly has potential, the point of this is that Arsenal still feel at least one attacker – but arguably two – away from really being at the level they want to be.

It should be stressed that their current level isn’t exactly much of a problem. They’ve won nine out of 10 in the league. They’re brilliantly efficient in everything outside the scoring.

Even this match has to be put in the context of a tough match against a very respectable Hull City side. They’re so well structured you can see them being this season’s Sunderland.

Arsenal were known to be impressed afterwards, with Arteta especially respectful. His team still get through, and you can see them being convincingly ahead by Christmas.

But the league isn’t the level anymore. It’s about beating Paris Saint-Germain in the Champions League, about being European champions as well as English champions.

That is something they really have to insist on.
