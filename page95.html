GameStop boss Ryan Cohen (last seen attempting to buy eBay) has called for the return of physical game discs, lamenting the lack of control players have over digital purchases in a chat with Bloomberg held on the eve of Grand Theft Auto 6's release.

In case you missed it, a media meteor impacted yesterday and blasted our landscape free of life. Grand Theft Auto 6 is here and—despite our Ted Litchfield's noble attempts to get you all thinking about Queen's Domain instead (you should play Queen's Domain)—it's all the world can talk about. It's also only available digitally.

"Unquestionably, the decision to move to all digital has hurt consumers, and it’s also hurt GameStop", said Cohen, remarking that players ought to be able to do with their purchased games as they will: "When you buy a game, you should be able to sell it, trade it or lend it to a friend". Cohen did not namecheck GTA 6 specifically, but did say that he had been speaking about this issue with all "the obvious" publishers.

GameStop, of course, makes a tidy chunk of change from resales of games traded in at its stores—a segment of its business that becomes impossible to maintain in a world without physical discs for players to bring in and sell. Publishers oppose trade-ins "because removing a used copy eliminates an alternative to buying new", says Cohen, accusing them of "taking away competition" by restricting sales to digital-only channels.

GTA 6 is a portent of the world to come. Earlier this year, Sony announced that PlayStation would drop physical discs starting in January 2028, and has held firm to that course in the face of a vocal player uproar.

Issues around game ownership have reached the point that politicians are weighing in: last month US lawmaker Richie Torres announced the Power To The Players Act, that would prompt the US Federal Trade Commission to protect players' access to purchased games, even in the case of online-only games that get unplugged.

"When you pay for a game, you should own it", said Torres. "Consumers must have the right to own, the right to play, and the right to transfer the games they buy. A game that can be taken away the moment a company pulls the plug is not a purchase. It is a rental disguised as a sale".

‘Josephine’ Will Floor You

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

This is the question that an eight-year-old named Josephine (Mason Reeves) asks her dad, Damien (Channing Tatum). She’s a sporty kid, the kind of youngster who accompanies her jockish pops on long runs through Golden Gate Park every Sunday morning. It’s during one of these weekly father-daughter bonding sessions that Josephine sprints ahead of her dad and strays off their usual path. Stopping to catch her breath, she sees a female jogger go into a public restroom at the bottom of a hill. Then Josephine watches as a stranger pulls the woman out, knocks her to the ground, and sexually assaults her.

The cops arrest the perpetrator, the survivor goes to the hospital, and eventually, Damien is left with his daughter and that question, hanging in the air. Josephine can’t comprehend what she’s witnessed. Her father doesn’t know how to answer. Instead of comforting or explaining, Damien just resumes their usual training exercises; he’s got to strengthen her up for soccer, he tells his daughter. His intentions are noble. He wants to protect her by pretending nothing’s happened. But Josephine’s father is simply the first adult who fails to help her process the traumatic experience this young girl has gone through. He won’t be the last.

The Grand Jury Prize winner at this year’s Sundance Film Festival, Josephine doesn’t just follow its namesake as she’s forced to navigate a sudden loss of innocence; it sticks so closely to her perspective that you’re allowed to see every confusing gesture, every mixed-message reply, every argument among her parents, and every acting-out response through her eyes. That itself isn’t unusual — most coming-of-age movies let you walk miles in their hero’s Converse All Stars. Yet writer-director Beth de Araújo’s sophomore film handles this shattering take on a kid wrapping her head around trauma with such grace, such delicacy, such force and a lack of false notes, that the work feels seismic even in its silences. It’s not interested in debating how crimes are handled by the cops or the courts, or how society talks around instead of about sexual violence in a constructive way. The movie wants you to reckon with the impact of someone figuring out a foreign landscape filled with unforeseen terrors. Josephine, c’est moi. So much of this rests on the tiny shoulders of Reeves, a first-time actor who seems to intuitively understand how to simply be in front of the camera. When Araújo isn’t replicating Josephine’s point of view, letting things unfold from a lower angle looking up or glimpsed from a distance, she’s training her lens directly on Reeves’ face in close-up. You can see the character attempting to grasp and contextualize situations for which she has no reference point in real time. And when Josephine begins to parrot statements and repeat patterns of behavior — she sees Damien spit on the rapist, then later spits on her dad in a fit of rage — we can see her trying on toxic versions of adulthood for size. It’s an incredible performance by any parameter, much less one delivered by a preteen.

Josephine makes space for her parents as well, with Tatum weaponizing his varsity-athlete physicality and kinder, gentler masculinity to play Damien as a well-meaning yet grasping dad, a paterfamilias in over his head when it comes to talking about tough subjects. His solutions: Take a self-defense class and either sweat out the pain or bury it. Gemma Chan plays Josephine’s mom, a dancer named Claire, as someone both more sympathetic to their child’s bewilderment and equally adrift. (A sequence in which she’s asked by her daughter if she’s ever been assaulted is all the more painful by the lack of an actual answer; Chan’s look alone tells you more than you feel you should know.) Tempers get lost, voices get raised, and Josephine herself lashes out in ways that suggest she doesn’t know how to deal with the emotional turbulence coursing through her. You don’t need to know that Josephine comes from an extremely personal place to feel that Araújo (whose debut, a Neo-Nazi Karen parable titled Soft and Quiet, now feels like an artist clearing her throat before speaking harshly and loudly) is unpacking this youngster’s story with an extraordinary amount of acuity and sensitivity, even as she refuses to pull punches: The assault is shown forensically, pitilessly, and in full. So is Josephine’s cross-examination on the stand by the defense’s lawyer (Dana Millican) when the child is forced to testify as a witness, which represents the ways our legal system can put everyone involved on trial. Surreal touches, such as the ghostly presence of the rapist (Philip Ettinger) hovering around everyday situations, add to the feeling that you’re viewing all of this through a highly subjective lens. Josephine sees this person everywhere, as a phantom both menacing and eerily childlike. So why shouldn’t we?

“Do you think the man is bad on the inside?” Josephine asks one of her caretakers. “Or good on the inside, but does bad things?” Nobody really knows the answer to that, she’s told. Neither Araújo nor the movie itself pretends to have a satisfying reply. But it’s desperate to ask the question and push you to wonder how a child, in some ways wise beyond her years yet still very much a kid, can reckon with a morality that exists in deepening shades of gray. Josephine is a movie that begins in a moment of fear — Josephine is scared to run out of her garage before the automatic door closes — and ends with a portrait of courage under fire. That the courage comes from someone who can barely comprehend her own bravery only makes the moment that much more impressive. It’s hard to think of a work of art that feels this simultaneously scarring and healing. Or that is tougher to watch yet something that must be seen as soon as possible and by any means necessary.

Bolton Wanderers dodged a transfer bullet in walking away from £3m striker pursuit amid new blow: Opinion

On Deadline Day, both Bolton Wanderers and Preston North End were in the market for a striker. At the last hour, Celtic striker Johnny Kenny, who had spent the second half of the season prior at the Trotters, was made available.

Steven Schumacher‘s side would make a move for the 23-year-old, but Celtic’s price tag and PNE’s £3m offer would see BWFC walk away from a deal. And, Republic of Ireland international Kenny duly made the move to Deepdale.

Now though, according to The Lancashire Post’s Dan Murphy, Kenny is now out for the next couple of months with a hamstring injury. The striker’s injury struggles have meant that, in hindsight, Bolton Wanderers were better off looking elsewhere.

At the start of summer, Schumacher made it clear he’d welcome back a number of Bolton Wanderers loan stars, including Johnny Kenny. However, BWFC would not have the same spending power they had in League One, and being frugal with their budget would be at the top of the priority list.

The Trotters made 13 signings and Kenny would have been their marquee addition of the summer. The 23-year-old scored six goals, with a total of nine goal involvements in his time with the Trotters. In the end, the £3m price tag put Wanderers off, and they look right to have walked away.

Kenny has two goals so far for Preston North End but has played just eight games, having also suffered an injury that made him miss the play-offs with BWFC. The North West outfit would not have been able to bring in as many players if they had signed the forward, and so far they are nine points above their Lancashire rivals.

So far, Kenny has missed three games for the Lilywhites and could now miss at least the next 15 as he recovers from his new hamstring injury. The striker was one of Preston North End’s big signings, and with both them and Bolton Wanderers in a relegation fight, it is easy to see which team is in a better position after their summer recruitment.

For the Trotters, £3m is a lot of money, if not all their transfer budget, and it looks like they’ve dodged a serious problem by walking away from the deal.

Theo Bair, who signed the same day as Kenny joined PNE, has three goal involvements whilst Sam Dalby has six goals, sitting sixth in the Championship goalscoring charts. BWFC may still need more firepower at the top of the pitch, but they also need players on the pitch, and right now their double act is doing just that, whilst Kenny remains sidelined.

When the client is a good fit on paper, but still not a good fit

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I was so excited to work with a new client. 

Perfect for my expertise and the type of company I prefer to work with. Meeting the client for an exploratory call felt like a dream. A well-respected brand to add to my portfolio.

But once the contract was signed, it was a struggle from Day 1. We parted ways six months later. 

Most advice about struggling client relationships assumes there is an obvious problem, like that the client is overly demanding or hard to reach. But in this case, it was something else. The vibes were off. When that happens, it’s less obvious what to fix. 

Recognizing the difference between a difficult client and a poor fit can help you decide sooner whether to adjust the working relationship or simply walk away.

Why a client can look right on paper and still not work

The things I vet for during the sales process can be different from the things that make a working relationship successful. 

Before signing, I look at the potential client’s industry and the scope of work. The client’s budget has to be aligned with my rate, and I have to feel confident that I can deliver what the client is asking for. That’s the relationship “on paper.”

But what’s often unknown is how the client interacts with a consultant or contractor (like me). I’m also unaware of any internal politics at the company that might impact the working relationship. Those details usually become apparent once I start delivering actual work. 

Is it a difficult client or a poor fit?

A difficult client has problems like not paying on time or asking for work outside of the scope. These can be fixed (or at least managed) with a solid contract and clear boundaries. Most of the biggest client red flags solopreneurs face are in this category.

A poor fit is different. The client may pay on time and stay within scope, but other parts of the working relationship feel strained. Maybe stakeholder feedback gets under your skin because of how it’s presented. Or you find yourself dreading the work, even though, on paper, it’s what you had agreed to do.

One thing to ask yourself: Would a different consultant or contractor have the same trouble with this client? If the answer is yes, you’re likely dealing with a difficult client. But if you can picture someone else doing great work for them, the problem is probably fit.

A second question: If this client always pays on time and never asked for anything outside of scope, would you still want the work? A “no” usually means fit is the issue.

What to try before you walk away

A poor fit sometimes might improve if you’re able to directly address the thing that’s causing you angst. It’s worth trying before you decide.

If the problem is, for example, “too many cooks” providing you with feedback and contradictory opinions, you might be able to solve this. Something like:

“It’s easier for me to make sure I deliver what you need if you consolidate your feedback. Can you work on your end to send me a list of thoughts/changes? That way, I don’t start down one path only to find that you want me to go in a different direction.”

In some cases, your client might be willing to change — they may simply not have experience working with a contractor or consultant. 

You might also give yourself a checkpoint, like an additional month of work after the conversation. If nothing has changed by then, you have your answer.

Admitting the relationship isn’t working

When you work solo, a poor fit can drag you down. In a company, a colleague might take over the account, or a manager could reassign the work. As a solopreneur, there’s no one to hand it to. And since your income depends on the work, ending the relationship means replacing that revenue.

But staying has a cost. Stress. Frustration. All the things you were supposed to escape by choosing a solopreneur life instead of a corporate job.

If the conversation with your client doesn’t change anything, more effort probably won’t fix it. At that point, calling it a poor fit and moving on is a business decision. 

It helps to remember that a poor fit says nothing about your skills or the client’s character. It simply didn’t work out.

Dutch data watchdog highlights safety issues with AI glasses

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Devices found to misinterpret medication instructions and other scenarios impacting human safety

The Dutch Data Protection Authority (AP) has officially advised consumers against buying AI-powered smart glasses after safety tests revealed concerning behaviors beyond their controversial recording capabilities.

The AP recently tested six different models of smart glasses and found glaring issues, including a willingness to display dating advice to an adult glasses-wearer who expressed an interest in a minor.

In another case involving a cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) simulation, testers noted that one pair of glasses informed the wearer that emergency services had been called and were scheduled to arrive at the scene, despite no one having made the call.

Some glasses did not properly process safety warnings on children’s toys, others made mistakes when reading medication leaflets, and some misinterpreted warnings for extreme weather events.

The AP did not specify the models it tested, saying only that they tested six models ranging from under €100 ($110) to over €500 ($550), including two that "were extensively tested in simulated real-life situations where human safety played a key role."

A spokesperson also declined to match any specific behavior to a model by price point (minor dating advice linked to a €354 pair of glasses, for example), adding that the research was intended to highlight the risks of AI glasses in a broader sense.

"Glasses that film you unnoticed are already concerning. Glasses that also give you incorrect advice when someone's life is at stake go a step too far," said Monique Verdier, vice chair at the AP.

"AI offers many opportunities, but we must be able to trust that technology. With the camera glasses examined, that trust cannot currently be justified."

The warning issued this week follows the regulator's previous work on smart glasses. The AP reminded the public in September that everyone has the right to privacy and encouraged organizations to ban them where necessary.

The same warning mentioned the potential risks regarding data processing under the GDPR, as well as those related to the training of AI.

The AP doubled down on these earlier criticisms, saying the latest tests showed that the AI functions rely on processing data using servers mostly located outside of the European Union. Relatedly, it found that users were not given sufficient information about how the AI was processing the data they captured.

On a cybersecurity note, the tests threw up a range of issues, including the ability for people nearby the glasses-wearer to connect to the device without permission, view recordings, and in some cases operate the camera.

Further, the regulator echoed the pre-existing concerns about smart specs - those that helped give them their colloquial name “pervert glasses.”

Its tests found that with "virtually all of the camera glasses examined," the recording lights that are supposed to clearly show when a user is recording were dim and easily coverable.

The AP said that models could also be wangled into recording even when that light indicator was covered, which goes against what manufacturers so often claim.

"As a result, bystanders can barely see whether they are being filmed," it said.

The technology has a number of beneficial use cases, such as assisting the visually impaired, those with lower literacy levels, or for any number of other scenarios where people need help understanding the world around them

However, the watchdog's official stance on the technology, as it is today, deems smart glasses "unsafe… due to the risks to users and bystanders."

Verdier said politicians in the Netherlands and across Europe should take note of the results when developing AI policy.

"It is important that we, as a society, learn to deal with these types of high-risk AI products," she said.

"Simply banning everything provides some leeway in the beginning, but we cannot sustain that indefinitely. It is important that we collectively work on awareness, resilience, and ethical conscience."

When DEF CON announced a no pervert glasses policy ahead of its event in August, the move was broadly welcomed by attendees.

"Love to see a ‘no pervert glasses’ policy at DEF CON," said Eva Galperin, EFF director of cybersecurity, at the time.

Several other organizations have implemented similar restrictions, including Monopoly Events, which manages UK Comic Cons, national treasure pub chain Wetherspoons, and even Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE).

Most members of the general public will have at least come to terms with the fact that the smartphone generation, and their willingness to snap shots anywhere and everywhere, means there is no enshrined human right to privacy in public spaces.

Public video recording once carried an air of expectation or consideration for bystanders’ personal privacy, although smartphone users have slowly eradicated that over time.

Like it or not, it's generally understood that a smartphone is usually recording something - or someone - at any given time, especially in urban areas.

On paper, the fact that smart glasses can record in public spaces isn’t wildly different from what’s already come to be expected from smartphones.

However, given how similar they appear to analog glasses and the ability to cover the recording light, it makes the act of public recording via glasses far more surreptitious, and thus concerning.

With smartphones, it's easy to see from the user's body language - holding the phone out in front of them - that they are recording. There are no overt signals displayed by wearers of smart glasses during image or video capture, which fuels a sense of unease about the technology in general.

A quick search online for "smart glasses privacy violation" yields a slew of complaints about the technology, often regarding behavior toward women and children.

There are also worries about how easily the devices can get past security checks, potentially providing opportunities to those who wish to cause greater harm than a mere blight on personal privacy.

A joint investigation by The Observer and BBC Radio 4 recently revealed how AI-enabled glasses went undetected through the airport-style security scanners used in the UK parliament.

The two news outlets found that a £60 ($78) pair of Chinese-made glasses bypassed the scanners and could be used to read upside-down documents on MPs' desks, recording data such as phone numbers and addresses to be accessed at a later point. ®

Conte on Chelsea success story, meeting Kante and Fabregas destiny

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Antonio Conte reveals what changed to win the Premier League at Chelsea, the Arsenal player that ‘killed’ them, his funny reaction to meeting N’Golo Kante, and why Cesc Fabregas was ‘destined’ to be a coach.

The coach is currently waiting for his next role, after walking away from Napoli with the Scudetto and Supercoppa Italiana in his two seasons at the Stadio Diego Armando Maradona.

He sat down with the Cronache di Spogliatoio podcast and discussed his long career as a coach, including the experience at Chelsea from 2016 to 2018.

In that spell at Stamford Bridge, he won the Premier League title immediately, then the FA Cup, but admits it required a radical rethink of his Juventus tactics.

“I had this sort of 3-3-4 formation at Juventus, so when I went to Chelsea, I started with the idea of a 4-2-4 formation, but we didn’t have the players at that moment to make it work,” said Conte.

“I had Diego Costa, Eden Hazard, Wilian and Pedro as the wide men, and Oscar as a second striker, so I used a 4-2-3-1 with all of them and kept some of the same movements.

“We had started well, but after every game I told the staff, we are winning, but I don’t feel this is my team. We were top of the table, but after the break for international duty we took on Francesco Guidolin’s Swansea and it was chaos.

“Anything could’ve happened, it was 2-2, but when there is a sense of instability, these are not my teams. I need sides who control the game. I felt that I didn’t have complete control.”

The turning point came from a painful defeat to local rivals Arsenal.

“It was my first season in the Premier League, but there were Pep Guardiola, Jurgen Klopp, Jose Mourinho, all the best managers. We lost 2-1 at home to Liverpool, then were 3-0 down at half-time to Arsenal.

“Hector Bellerin was killing us down the left, it was one of the few times I was ashamed,” said Conte on the Italian language podcast.

“I realised that was the time I had to change, because I needed to give more protection to Oscar.

“I put Ivanovic on the right with David Luiz and Cahill, Azpilicueta on the right, basically a 3-4-3. It was the turning point, as the next game against Hull City I put Azpilicueta in the back three, he is a soldier and works wherever you put him, and Moses who Chelsea had wanted to sell.”

Conte also revealed that some of the meetings with Chelsea players didn’t quite go as planned, but they worked out in the end.

“It was not a transfer session worthy of Chelsea. Kante had won the Premier League title with Leicester City, I asked for two players, Wanyama and Kante. I’d seen Kante play several times, with him it was as if we were playing with 12 or 13, he never felt tired.

“He walked in behind his agent, I couldn’t even see him. Kante is not a tall man, but he looked like a child, with this big smile. I thought, I expected him to be taller…

“I hoped I hadn’t made a mistake asking for this player, the Premier League they were all huge and physically strong, but the fear only lasted a minute. I spoke to him, Kante is one of the best players I ever coached, and an exceptional lad.”

Conte reveals the aim at Chelsea that season was for a top 10 finish, but they ended up winning the Premier League.

“We won 13 games in a row, that is when I felt this was my team, it was in control. Before that, playing three at the back was practically blasphemous in England, but now it has become a cult and many teams do it.”

Cesc Fabregas was in that Chelsea team too, and has now gone on to become a coach in his own right at Como, qualifying for the Champions League.

“His curiosity, his desire to learn, to modify, Cesc was destined for greatness,” insisted Conte.

“He was a thinker on the field too, so that helped. He could see the move before he got the ball, was in the right position to receive it. When you get these players who think, they are destined to be coaches.”

The Most Unexpected Ski Adventure in North America Might Be in Michigan

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Marcus Caston, Mt. Bohemia, Mich. (Photo: Keri Bascetta)

It’s breakfast in the main yurt, so the girl from the kitchen is again standing on a chair and rubbing the dome of a snow globe. Eyes shut tight, she shouts over the gathered skiers.

“Grant us snow, almighty spirits of winter,” she begins, raising the globe above her head. “By your power, give us powder. Amen.” She is our shaman.

The Midwest is a peculiar place for a ski pilgrimage. But on Michigan’s isolated Keweenaw Peninsula, known as “the fin,” Mount Bohemia has no shortage of true believers. And they’re not coming for the corduroy. This expert-only backcountry resort is ungroomed, technical, and seriously steep. Signs across the mountain are clear: NO BEGINNERS ALLOWED.

While skiers scour the globe for powder, Michigan has been sitting on one of the country’s most unlikely stashes. Bohemia sees almost 300 inches annually, with no snowmaking. Here, bad weather is usually good news. Arctic air sweeping over Lake Superior’s warmer water produces storms that bury the Keweenaw. This lake-effect snow is remarkably light and dry—the kind you’d travel to Utah or Japan to find.

On paper, Bohemia’s numbers seem modest: 900 vertical feet, 585 acres, and just two lifts. Yet over 90 percent of its runs are black diamond or better, including the region’s only triple-blacks. Boho has the untamed feel of backcountry skiing, minus the skinning. Its dozen or so named territories—mostly sprawling glade districts—each have distinct temperaments.

“It can take two or three visits before guests get a feel for the mountain,” says Vern Barber, Bohemia’s general manager.

I spend one morning adrift in one of the territories, Extreme Backcountry, a half-mile-wide expanse of Bohemia’s toughest terrain. Its high-angle tree runs, cliff drops, and narrow chutes kept funneling me onto unexpected lines. Supposedly, somewhere out there is a frozen waterfall drop I never managed to find. I wasn’t going to hit it. I just wanted to look.

Now I’m farther out in Middle Earth, another territory, surfing Boho’s longest runs through twisting hardwoods. Less steep, but somehow more exhausting. This is where skiers come to explore, hunting for pockets of untouched powder.

I stop at a trail marked “Helm’s Deep.” It took several attempts to find this place. Even then, it felt like an accident. The entrance is just a gap interrupting the tangle of trees—my only way down. Two clicks of my poles, and I drop in.

Dry powder explodes on my first turn. Branches rake both sleeves as I weave between trunks. Each bank hides what lies ahead. I barrel over a blind rise straight toward a stand of maples. I slash hard right and spray snow against the bark. Off balance, I slip through a drift and down a steep-sided gully.

“You’re rowdy,” I grunt as the trees finally open. With room to breathe, I find my rhythm. Ahead lie 30 yards of untracked pillows. I point my tips and charge.

By the time I pop out at the bottom, a throng of skiers is already waiting. Not for a chairlift, but for a bus. Bohemia’s farthest backcountry runs spill onto Lac La Belle Road, where a fleet of buses collects those who make it out of the woods. Over four days, I’ve shuffled through far more bus lines than lift lines. With luck, the next shuttle will be the elusive ABBA bus, plastered with Swedish memorabilia and bumping “Dancing Queen.” Today, I’m not so lucky. I climb into a regular bus for the day’s last ride, legs shot.

As we pull into base, the parking lot is just getting started. Around a fire barrel, a haggard congregation passes bottles and joints. Skiers still in bibs boil noodles on camp stoves between RVs and Sprinter vans. This crowd feels familiar—as if every little ski hill I grew up on in Michigan sent its most adventurous regulars.

The village itself is a jumble of yurts, stitched together by gangways. It houses bars, a hostel, and a surprisingly elaborate Nordic spa. Its 30-person hot tub was dubbed “dude stew.” But Vern says they don’t call it that anymore.

For a place that wears you out by noon, Boho has a strange amount of energy after dark. Inside the North Pole Bar, skiers running on beer and pasties belt out “Country Roads” with the band. The party stumbles outside to packed pools and saunas. Much later, some hooligans will hike halfway up the frontside and sled down on stolen cafeteria trays. I am one of them.

On my last morning at Mount Bohemia, a particularly nasty blizzard has blown in off Lake Superior. Word is spreading through the yurts: high winds and whiteout have shut down the lifts. The buses aren’t running.

Skiers gather at the back of the village, their helmets and jackets already covered in powder. Out of the blowing snow, a single snowcat materializes. Vern himself is behind the wheel. The wind forces him to park at an angle to wrestle the door open. Skiers cram aboard, a handful at a time, bound for the summit.

I join the others in line. We laugh and curse as the snowcat is swallowed again by the whiteout. Powder is piling up around our shins. Above us, the glades are already buried. I can’t feel my toes. It’s a perfect day.

The Spectrum of Family Estrangement

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

They say to write what you know. For better or worse, I know this topic from both sides of the couch.

A 2025 YouGov survey found that 38 percent of American adults reported being estranged from at least one immediate family member (Orth, 2025). When you hear the word estrangement, you may think of Hollywood-style relational pyrotechnics, with family members storming out of the house, never to speak to one another again.

To be fair, that does happen. But estrangement isn’t always about screaming fights and punishing silence. Researcher Kristina Scharp (2019) describes family estrangement as a continuum involving multiple dimensions, including emotional closeness, communication, physical distance, and the desire for reconciliation.

Here are four ways family estrangement can show up.

Proximity is a factor that can contribute to closeness, but is insufficient by itself. You can live near your family, see them every week, or even reside under the same roof, but physical proximity doesn’t guarantee emotional intimacy. More simply put, seeing your family regularly doesn’t mean that you share emotional closeness with them.

With structured contact, the relationship remains intact, but you have a non-negotiable list of rules that need to be in place in order to avoid conflict either interpersonally or intrapsychically. You still maintain contact, but you’ve established limits around when, where, and how interactions happen. Structured contact may look like time limits when attending family gatherings, staying in a hotel instead of with your family, or limiting conversations to topics that don’t escalate. Structured contact can provide a way to maintain a connection while reducing exposure to harmful dynamics.

With low contact, you may have rules about how and when to engage, or you may find yourself stuck in the same conversational loops on repeat. With low contact, you limit your exposure to family to only the minimum amount necessary to “check the box” of spending time with family. Low contact can often be a healthy way to preserve what remains available in a family, or it can be a way to mitigate the guilt and shame that come when you go full no contact.

No contact is exactly what it sounds like. There are no phone calls, emails, texts, or visits. No contact with family can run counter to deeply held beliefs about family connection, and many people agonize for years or even decades before they do it. Contrary to public opinion, going no-contact with family is not simply a trend, and ethical psychotherapy involves carefully exploring the reasons for considering estrangement rather than encouraging it without thoughtful consideration.

Research by Reczek et al. (2023) found that periods of parent-child estrangement can be followed by renewed connection. When all parties involved are committed to the change process, even long-standing periods of estrangement may find their way toward repair. Often, when repair fails to be an option, it’s because one or more parties may be unwilling or unable to participate in the process.

The decision to estrange yourself from family, regardless of which level of estrangement is appropriate, is one that is best made with informed supporters. Sometimes, estrangement isn’t even a choice, but the consequence of another family member’s rejection or withdrawal. While it can be a grief-laden experience, and one that should never be taken lightly, estrangement can also open up possibilities for loving and reciprocal relationships that do not cost you your energy or well-being.

Angelina Jolie Feels ‘At Home’ in Cambodia as She Expands Business Overseas—Inside Her Plans to Be ‘a Bit Nomadic’ After Ditching Los Angeles

Angelina Jolie is ushering in a new era after quitting Los Angeles.

In the wake of selling the $24.75 million California estate she snapped up less than a year after filing for divorce from Brad Pitt in 2017, the actress has revealed plans to enjoy more time overseas and less time tied down to a singular location.

Jolie recently visited Cambodia, where local outlets reported she met with religious leaders. The “Maleficent” star reportedly “paid her respects and presented religious offerings” to Samdech Preah Maha Sumeatheathibodi Am Limheng, supreme patriarch of the Mohanikay Buddhist Order.

She also visited a traditional craft village on Cambodia's Koh Dach, which is known as Silk Island, to observe local artisans.

The mom of six detailed her special connection to the country during earlier travels to the Philippines, where she spoke on a Nov. 11 panel alongside Cambodian filmmaker Rithy Pahn.

“I found this place and the people to be so warm and so open, and I couldn’t believe their spirits were so strong after all they have suffered,” Jolie said of Cambodia. “You go to certain places, and you just feel at home.”

According to People magazine, the actress plans to expand her creative collective, Atelier Jolie, with a residency in Cambodia in 2017.

The effort will be a partnership between Pahn’s Bophana Center and the Maddox Foundation, which Jolie founded in 2003 and named for her eldest child, Maddox Jolie, whom she adopted from Battambang, Cambodia in 2002.

According to the Atelier Jolie website, the business is focused on “the discovery of art, craft and design work from cultures around the world.” She founded the certified B Corporation business in 2023, with a headquarters in Manhattan’s NoHo neighborhood that serves as a retail store, art space, and cultural center.

Jolie spoke about her plans for the future in a September 2026 interview with L’Officiel, noting that she plans to spend more time “out of the States.”

“The hope for the Atelier was always to be global,” she said. “The idea would be to visit the different locations around the world and meet local artists and makers.”

She noted a desire to expand the Atelier to Brazil, as well as “get back to doing more hands-on work” with the Maddox Foundation in Cambodia.

She also expressed a desire to live a life that’s less tied down to one specific locale.

“I don’t really plan to have a base,” Jolie admitted. “I’d like to have a home base for my family, a place where some of the mom stuff and the family photos are. And then I’d like to be a bit nomadic for a while.”

The star also noted that she has a home in Cambodia, which “will also be a residency a few months out of the year for the Khmer culture.”

Jolie’s appreciation for the Southeast Asian country began when she first visited to film the 2001 movie, “Lara Croft: Tomb Raider.” She returned a year later to adopt her son, and has since spent lots of time traveling back and forth with her entire family.

In 2017, she directed “First They Killed My Father,” a Khmer-language biographical thriller based on a memoir of the same name by Cambodian human rights activist Luong Ung.

In a People magazine interview, Jolie revealed Maddox—who was 15 at the time—was deeply involved in the creation of the film, and helped with research.

Jole revealed her son spent “over four months” in the country at that time, “really reading, listening, learning, and absorbing all things about his culture and his country, including the very, very dark parts.”

Although Jolie was born and raised in Los Angeles, she has long expressed her desire to leave the city.

“I grew up in this town. I am here because I have to be here from a divorce,” she told The Hollywood Reporter in 2024. She explained that once her youngest children—twins Knox Leon and Vivienne Marcheline—turned 18, she’d be “able to leave.”

The milestone birthday passed this July, paving the way for a new chapter for Jolie.

“It’s the end of a certain time for me,” she told L’Officiel weeks later.

Jolie made moves quickly, putting her idyllic Los Angeles compound on the market for $29.9 million in May, seemingly in anticipation of Knox and Vivienne’s 18th.

The six-bedroom, 10-bathroom abode found a buyer in September, selling for $24.75 million. Jolie had originally purchased the home for $24.5 million in 2017, and it served as her primary residence for nearly a decade.

While Jolie plans to spend the bulk of her time jetsetting, she hasn’t said goodbye to the United States for good, and has reportedly recently purchased a Connecticut mansion, according to Page Six Hollywood.
