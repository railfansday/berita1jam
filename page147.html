Nvidia’s startup stakes ballooned to $65B, but Huang’s exit puts future deals in doubt

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia’s equity in private companies continues to grow: it revealed its stakes are now worth $65.5 billion on Tuesday, up from the $22 billion reported in January. The increase includes both new investments and significant markups of existing portfolio companies.

The company invested $16 billion into equities in the past quarter alone, it said, and $58 billion in the past nine months of its fiscal year. This amount includes buying shares in public and private companies, yet it shows how the chipmaker made massive bets over the course of the year, especially compared to last year; in November 2025, the company reported spending $5 billion buying equity in the first nine months of that fiscal year.

But it’s unclear if this growth will continue under the interim CEO and Jensen Huang’s successor.

Nvidia stated in late 2023 that the goal of its corporate investing is to expand the AI ecosystem by backing startups it considers to be “game changers and market makers.”  Over the nearly three years that followed, the chipmaker has invested in nearly 350 startups, according to PitchBook. The companies Nvidia backed include Anthropic, OpenAI; neolabs like Thinking Machines and Reflection; neoclouds, like Lambda, Nscale, and Crusoe; as well as other companies developing AI infrastructure, robotics startups, and applications.

It’s also become a big financier of the data center build out, reporting $118 billion in guarantees with $105 billion of that the backstop it supplied for OpenAI’s $500 billion data center in Ohio.

The company made those commitments by allocating a meaningful portion of its excess cash flows. In Tuesday’s earning call, Kress said Nvidia would continue providing financing assistance to strong customers and that “as the market matures and additional sources of capital become available,” the company would be “rigorously evaluating future investments, commitments, and guarantees.”

But the question for the VC ecosystem is whether the company will continue to back startups at anywhere near the same level without Huang at the helm.

Huang’s rampant dealmaking has reportedly been a source of concern for Kress. Investors have told TechCrunch that they anticipate that she will take a more conservative approach to startup investing, becoming more selective with new investments and writing smaller-sized checks into its existing portfolio companies.

“We intend to return all excess free cash flow net of strategic uses to shareholders during this last quarter and going forward,” Kress said on the earnings call on Tuesday. Just how strategic startups will be under her leadership, and under the future permanent CEO, remains to be seen. But for now, VCs are bracing for a meaningful pullback in capital.

Researchers train AI in simulation and unlock real robot skills

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers trained a 27-billion-parameter AI model in simulation to control an unfamiliar robot, succeeding in 60% of real-world trials.

Researchers behind PEAR, a system for training AI models to control robots, reported that a model completed 18 of 30 physical trials after learning its robot skills entirely in simulation.

The preprint submitted Nov. 13 describes robot post-training of Qwen3.8-27B, an open-weight reasoning model with 27 billion parameters. Researchers used reinforcement learning without demonstrations during that stage.

The result offers evidence that simulated practice can teach skills that carry over to different physical hardware. The final model had already practiced the two evaluated task types, pickup and block alignment, in simulation.

The physical test used a dual-arm YAM robot, which none of the compared models received training on in this study. It included 20 pickup attempts across ten objects and ten alignment attempts across five block layouts, with each object or layout tried twice.

The final model succeeded in 12 pickup attempts and six alignment attempts. A checkpoint trained only on the LIBERO simulation benchmark completed seven of the 30 trials.

The final model had continued training on the corresponding two tasks in RoboDojo using a different robot, according to the paper's physical-trial account.

At each turn, the model reads camera images and the robot's state, reasons in text, and specifies changes to the gripper's pose. Inverse kinematics translates the target pose into joint movements, which a controller executes.

The prompt supplies coordinate frames, gripper orientation, cameras, and task-completion conditions, and the authors acknowledge that performance depends on those descriptions.

All three models used the same high reasoning-effort setting, with trials having a 25-round budget and failing if a single round exceeded 32,000 reasoning tokens. The final model's successful attempts required 7 to 24 rounds.

The authors say reasoning before every move slows tasks. Their separate decoding demonstration reached 747 tokens per second on a GB300 using lower-precision NVFP4 weights and DFlash2, a decoding accelerator.

That suggests about 4.3 seconds to generate an average high-effort reasoning round. The reported evaluations used BF16 weights because quantization reduced accuracy, so the speed demonstration and physical success rate describe different operating conditions.

The public PEAR repository provides training infrastructure and deployment recipes. The result remains an author-reported experiment pending independent replication, and turning it into a broadly useful robot system will require wider task coverage and shorter reasoning delays.

Why Similarweb Stock Surged Today

The intelligence platform provides its customers with a comprehensive view of the digital economy.

Shares of Similarweb (SMWB +13.04%) climbed 13% on Tuesday after the data analytics provider reported strong gains in sales and profits.

The software company's revenue rose 16% to $83 million in the third quarter.

Similarweb acts as a digital intelligence supplier to over 10,000 organizations around the world. Its ever-expanding data set spans more than 100 million websites, 4 million apps, and 235 million e-commerce products.

Demand for Similarweb's insights is rising as competition within the digital economy intensifies. Customers generating annual recurring revenue (ARR) of $100,000 or more grew 6% to 474.

These customers are also spending more on Similarweb's platform. Its dollar-based net retention rate (NRR) for clients with ARR of at least $100,000 increased to 114% from 105% in the prior-year period.

Notably, artificial intelligence (AI)-powered offerings are helping to accelerate Similarweb's growth. AI-related business accounted for approximately two-thirds of ARR added in the third quarter, according to chief financial officer Ran Vered.

Better still, Similarweb is growing more profitable as it scales its operations. Adjusted operating margin improved to 12% from 6% in the year-ago quarter. Adjusted net income, in turn, more than doubled to $9.4 million, or $0.10 per share.

Management now expects full-year revenue to grow by roughly 13% to $319 million in 2026, resulting in adjusted operating profit of about $30 million.

FAA official oversaw router work just before nationwide ground stop, report claims

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An official with the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) was on the ground in Arkansas when new telecommunications wires were replaced, an action that preceded Friday's national ground stop by eight minutes, according to the Washington Post.

The FAA official oversaw the work, tested it, and signed off on it, according to the Post's report. The work involved replacing a router and swapping old copper wires for new fiber-optic cables, part of a national effort by the FAA to upgrade the airline industry's communication infrastructure.

The fact that an FAA official greenlit the work complicates Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy's efforts to point fingers at L3Harris Technologies, a tech services provider and contractor tasked with updating the wires in Arkansas and leading the FAA's fiber-optic upgrades. Duffy said on Saturday that his agency is looking into whether "incompetence" by L3Harris led to the 4.5-hour ground stop.

Senate Republicans Seek to Again Raid States’ Coffers & Threaten EV Charging

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

FY 2027 Transportation Proposal Puts EV Charging Investments at Risk

WASHINGTON, D.C. — On Monday, Senate Republicans released an Appropriations package that proposes to take back nearly $2 billion in federal funding already allocated to states to build out electric vehicle (EV) charging infrastructure under the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act and repurpose it for general transportation uses, putting critical clean transportation investments at risk.

Republicans already clawed back roughly $500 million from the National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure (NEVI) Formula Program in the FY 2026 appropriations bill. Now, they are proposing to further divert these funds, even as all 50 states, Washington, D.C., and Puerto Rico have approved plans for their use and are working to deliver critical charging infrastructure to their communities. More than half of available NEVI funding has already been committed to projects, and the proposal threatens to upend additional awards that states continue to make to charging developers.

For example, Texas recently made an announcement of $226 million in awards that will support 321 stations. The Senate proposal would threaten Texas’s remaining NEVI funding, along with funding for states like Ohio, which has one of the largest numbers of operational NEVI-funded stations in the country.

In response, Clean Transportation for All Director Katherine García released the following statement:

“It is unacceptable for Senate Republicans to once again seek to claw back critical EV charging investments. Trump’s day-one freeze of NEVI set states back, and they are only now back on track after we successfully challenged that freeze in court. This latest proposal threatens affordable transportation for American families, clean air for communities, and states’ efforts to build the infrastructure of the future. We have successfully defended NEVI against the Trump administration’s attacks, and we will continue fighting to protect the program and ensure these investments reach communities across the country.”

The NEVI program, established through the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law in 2021, provides funding to states to build a nationwide network of EV fast-charging stations. Protecting that investment is essential to expanding charging access, supporting EV adoption, reducing dependence on volatile gasoline prices, and cutting transportation pollution.

Since the Trump administration’s unlawful freeze of the NEVI program was lifted following successful litigation by Sierra Club, its partners, and a coalition of states, hundreds of millions of dollars in NEVI funding have been committed to charging projects.

Congress must preserve funding for EV charging infrastructure while providing dedicated resources for passenger rail, public transit, and other clean transportation priorities.

Pope Leo’s Magnifica Humanitas (2026), subtitled “On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence,” has been heralded as one of the most significant and morally serious visions for how humanity ought to think about and navigate the advent of the AI era. Acknowledging “the ambiguity of tools,” which are neither morally good nor bad in and of themselves but “can cause harm when not oriented toward the good,” the Pope insists that the end toward which our aspirations aim is as important as the aspirations themselves.

For Leo, protecting and fostering the dignity of the human person must be the goal of everything we do. Contrasting two images taken from Scripture, the Pope calls us to consider how the desire that orients our ambitions changes the things we create.

In Leo’s first example, the construction of the Tower of Babel (Genesis 11:1–9) is motivated by fear and vainglory:

Fearing being scattered across the earth, they [the builders] sought to guarantee stability and power for themselves, and above all to ‘make a name’ for themselves.” Such motives cannot but end in ruin. Leo continues, “When a city is built on pride and the claim to self-sufficiency, communication breaks down, languages are confused and people no longer understand each other. The result is not unity, but dispersion. Babel thus reveals the limits of any effort that, however grandiose, arises from self-affirmation, sacrifices human dignity for efficiency and aspires to reach heaven without God’s blessing.

In his second, which comes from the Book of Nehemiah, Nehemiah returns to the city of Jerusalem after the Babylonian exile only to find it in ruins. Heartbroken, he takes in the destruction in silence. Then, before setting to work, he “convened the families, assigned each of them a section of the wall to rebuild, listened to their concerns, coordinated their efforts and addressed any opposition.”

Nehemiah, that is, kept the dignity of those around him at the center of his project rather than running headlong toward apparent solutions. In so doing, he was able to resurrect what had been lost while simultaneously creating something new. The Pope writes:

The narrative shows how the city is reborn, not through the initiative of one man, but through the shared responsibility of all: men, women, priests, artisans, heads of households and young people all play a part. It is an undertaking with God at the center, which rebuilds relationships before rebuilding with stones. Thus, ancient Jerusalem rediscovers a common language—not one of uniformity, but one of communion, namely the harmony that arises when all persons assume their own role and recognize that their strength comes from the Lord.

What makes these images apt for our current moment—and what many commentators have overlooked—is the role language plays in each. For, if the desire of those constructing the Tower of Babel was to create a single, unified language with which all could understand and be understood, so too is the aim of Artificial Intelligence to provide a kind of totalizing language capable of flattening ambiguity and rendering difference obsolete. And if Nehemiah’s success was rooted in the fact that he sought out the input of those he hoped to serve, and even (perhaps especially) those with whom he disagreed, so too must dialogue be at the foundation of technological advance.

Consider another example, this one taken from contemporary fiction, that draws out this tension inherent in language—the tension between the desire to communicate directly, if only superficially, and the desire to express how fraught and complex human experience actually is, even if that expression can only be accomplished indirectly and ambiguously.

It comes from Paul Auster’s great work of metareal fiction City of Glass (1987), in which the story’s chief antagonist Peter Stillman—is he still a man, still human, or has he been deformed by his mad pursuit of “perfectibility”?—obsesses over the duplicity of language, the way one word can mean two equal and opposite things. For instance, the word cleave “means both ‘to join together’ and ‘to break apart.’”

For Stillman, the Genesis narrative to which the Pope alluded “records not only the fall of man, but the fall of language” from clarity to opacity. Once, he conjectures, language was simple, direct, and stated exactly what it meant to say without confusion or ambiguity. There was a time when “words had not been merely appended to things . . . they revealed their essences . . . A thing and its name were interchangeable.” But after the destruction of the Tower of Babel, this was no longer true. Now words conceal as much as they reveal, and deception is built into the very nature of how we communicate.

Driven mad by the desire to use language to reunify what appears to him to be a fractured and fragmentary existence, Stillman sets out to realize a utopian future in which his model of a single, unified language makes absolute knowledge and mastery possible. (Sound familiar?)

If the fall of man also entailed a fall of language, was it not logical to assume that it would be possible to undo the fall, to reverse its effects by undoing the fall of language? . . . If man could learn to speak this original language of innocence, did it not follow that he would thereby recover a state of innocence within himself?

The trouble is that Stillman is not able to conjure up this mythical language model, and his attempts to do so inflict immense suffering on those around him.

In an ironic twist, Stillman ultimately resorts to deception himself in a vain attempt to accomplish his goal. Creating a character named Henry Dark, a fictional disciple of John Milton, he attributes his most controversial ideas to Dark and even falsifies the historical record to make it appear as though Dark had heralded his, Stillman’s, project over 300 years in advance.

What could offer a better image of the LLMs that seem so powerful and omniscient today—with their false citations and hallucinated facts—than a fictional character invented by a mad genius to dupe others into believing that his language model could solve the mysteries and riddles of the human condition? And yet, what could offer a better reason to hope? For, although Dark was invented by Stillman to serve darker ends, still his creation was—as Pope Leo recently called the craft of writing fiction—a profound “act of humanity,” one which points to the supreme dignity of its author even if he, like many of us today, is too consumed with his utopian ideas to appreciate it.

IDA Awards Nominations: ‘Concrete Land’ Lands 6, With ‘To Hold A Mountain’ Close Behind; Elizabeth Holmes Doc In The Mix

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Concrete Land, which has received little attention in the U.S. so far, surprised with a leading six nominations Tuesday as the 42nd IDA Documentary Awards announced its nominees. Sundance winner To Hold a Mountain came in close behind with five.

Concrete Land, directed by Asmahan Bkerat, centers on the the Najar family, “one of the last Afro-Palestinian Bedouin families near Amman,” who face eviction as luxury villas encroach on their land. It won the Grierson Award at the BFI London Film Festival last month but has otherwise largely gone under the radar. Along with Best Documentary Feature, it is nominated for Best Production, Best Documentary Not in the English Language, Best Editing, Best Sound Design, and Best Writing. Related Stories Documentary Oscar Contender 'To Hold A Mountain' To Open In Theaters In December Ahead Of HBO Debut Documentary IDA Documentary Awards Shortlists: 'You Can See Everything,' 'To Hold A Mountain,' 'Nuisance Bear' Make Features Cut, But What Got Snubbed?

Joining Concrete Land in the marquee Best Documentary Feature category is To Hold a Mountain, directed by Biljana Tutorov and Petar Glomazić. That film – Montenegro’s choice for the Oscar category of Best International Feature – tells the story of Gara, a remarkable woman who is determined to shield her daughter from harm while simultaneously attempting to prevent the Montenegrin government from turning beloved mountain pastures into a military training ground. Watch on Deadline

Also nominated for Best Documentary Feature: A Fox Under a Pink Moon, directed by Mehrdad Oskouei and Soraya Akhlaghi; Rehearsals for a Revolution, directed by Pegah Ahangarani, and You Can See Everything, directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim.

The latter film, the box office smash about imprisoned Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, earned three nominations overall, including Best Director and Best Original Music Score, recognizing composer Ari Balouzian. Rehearsals for a Revolution and A Fox Under a Pink Moon landed four nominations apiece.

Rehearsals for a Revolution (primarily in Farsi), A Fox Under a Pink Moon (in Farsi, Dari, and Turkish) and Concrete Land (primarily in Arabic) earned nominations for Best Documentary Not in the English Language. Curiously, To Hold a Mountain, in Montenegrin, didn’t earn a nomination in that category. Had it done so, it would have tied with Concrete Land for a leading six nominations.

Birds of War, directed by Janay Boulos and Abd Alkader Habak, scored three nominations – for Best Documentary Not in the English Language, Best Writing (for Jonathan Key, Janay Boulos, and Abd Alkader Habak), and Best Original Music Score, recognizing composers Harpal Mudhar and Darren Sng.

My Undesirable Friends: Part II – Exile, Julia Loktev’s follow up to her Oscar-shortlisted My Undesirable Friends: Part I – Last Air in Moscow, earned two nominations: for Best Director and Best Editing (recognizing Loktev and Michael Taylor). Closure, directed by Michał Marczak, and Nuisance Bear, directed by Gabriela Osio Vanden and Jack Weisman, both earned two nominations each, for Best Cinematography and Best Sound Design.

Also of note are nominations for Oscar winner Laura Poitras – for her short documentary They’re Here, co-directed with Rachel Lauren Mueller; Oscar winner Ahmir “Questlove” Thompson, nominated for Best Music Documentary for Earth, Wind & Fire (To Be Celestial vs. That’s the Weight of the World), and Oscar winner Morgan Neville, nominated for Best Music Documentary for Man on the Run. Prominent Hollywood hitmakers Baz Luhrmann and Paris Barclay earned nominations for their music documentaries – Luhrmann for EPiC: Elvis Presley in Concert, and Barclay for Billy Preston: That’s The Way God Planned It.

The 42nd IDA Documentary Awards will take place Friday, December 4, at The Ebell of Los Angeles, hosted by Tabitha Jackson, head of Film Forum and former director of the Sundance Film Festival.

In a statement, IDA board president Ina Fichman said, “I couldn’t be more excited to celebrate these meaningful and creative documentaries. It’s a challenging time for documentary makers, but also a really inspiring moment for IDA and for the field. This year we see nominees whose deeply personal stories share narratives of resistance and exile, and we see artful, innovative filmmaking that is capturing new and robust audiences. I hope many will join us on December 4th as we honor our incredible community.”

The IDA says it received a record 650 entries in all categories from 95 countries, “an increase over last year both in the total number of entries and the countries represented. This year, 32 countries are represented among the nominees.” IDA members from around the world vote to determine the winners in the Best Feature Documentary and Best Short Documentary categories (balloting began November 17 and extends to November 29). All other category winners are selected by juries representing the international documentary community.
