Despite the tensions of war, ex-Barclays CEO Bob Diamond is long Gulf. Here’s why

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“The region isn’t hiding in their bunker; they’re doing business,” Bob Diamond tells me on a call from Bahrain, where the Gateway Gulf investment conference is taking place. He is talking to Fortune against a backdrop of simmering tensions around the U.S. midterms and the possibility that the President may restart attacks on Iran.

“I don’t want to be dismissive, there is a war, there is a conflict, whatever term you want to use,” he said. “It’s real, and I’m not going to be dismissive of the impact that’s having on the UAE, on Bahrain, on Qatar. But I will tell you, if you take the time to get here and you’re on the ground, it’s business as usual. The kids are in school. People are working from the office. They’re not hiding at home. They’re doing business. They’re investing.”

The former CEO of Barclays is in Bahrain to announce that the U.K.-based advisory firm, Panmure Liberum, is opening its first regional office in the kingdom. Diamond’s investment vehicle, Atlas Merchant Capital, bought Panmure Gordon for £15.5m ($21m) in 2017. It agreed an all-share merger with Liberum in 2024.

Panmure Liberum is now one of the biggest advisors to U.K.-listed companies based on the number of clients, which total more than 250. It specializes in mid-market companies, with valuations below those served by the bulge-bracket investment banks such as Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs.

Panmure’s move to Bahrain will allow it to serve the whole region and increase the deal-flow for the advisory firm. Activity levels in the Gulf are in sharp contrast to the number of deals announced in the U.K. In the first half of 2026, deals in the U.K. were down 13% compared with the same period a year ago, according to PwC, a consultancy.

“You have all the talent in the world in London. It’s still a very attractive place for a lot of people,” Diamond said. “The skill set of advisory is very, very deep. But there’s not a lot of deals. I think the statistics on the number of companies that have gone public or raised equity is paltry compared to what it used to be, and they’re losing out not to Frankfurt or Dublin, but to the U.S. and, more and more, to the Middle East.”

This creates an opportunity for firms keen to expand.

“It means that we can build an advisory business here in Bahrain to serve middle market and family-owned businesses, which are dying for cover,” he said. “And we can then expand that to the Gulf Cooperation Council.”

“You have all the talent in the world in London, but not a lot of deals. Companies going public or raising equity are paltry compared to what they used to be, and they’re losing out to the U.S. and more, the Middle East”

As one of the smaller kingdoms in the region, Bahrain, says it is able to offer agility and clear and simple regulatory rules to investors. At Gateway Gulf, Mastercard announced an expansion of its deal with the Bahrain-based fintech firm, Infinios, to facilitate business-to-business travel payments. Olayan Kimberly-Clark, the partnership between Saudi Arabia and the tissue paper production giant, announced that it would be expanding production at its Bahrain paper mill.

“It’s a highly regulated banking sector,” Diamond said about why the kingdom was attractive. “Great banks want great regulation. It’s also a gateway, to steal a phrase from the conference, to the GCC. So all of a sudden, Panmure, which is a U.K.-focused advisory business, is now U.K. and GCC, so we’re very happy with it.”

Diamond said that there were also opportunities for Bahrain and the rest of the Gulf to develop stablecoin payment systems, one of the fastest ways to reduce the cost of doing business. As a digital currency pegged to the dollar, it is seen as a more secure way to settle transactions.

“Tokenization is just digitizing assets,” he said. “It’s making them more efficient. All of that relies on blockchain, which is an underpinning of 24/7 instantaneous settlement, a permanent record of every transaction. So, the regulators love it, at a fraction of the cost. It is going to be the future underpinning [for finance]. It’s infrastructure.”

A new book looks at how Marcel Duchamp’s ‘impossible’ love for the sculptor Maria Martins changed his work

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Marcel Duchamp’s final work, Étant donnés (1946-66), an assemblage created in collaboration with Maria Martins © Artists Rights Society/ADAGP/Association Marcel Duchamp

Glacial, calculated, distant, sly: these are among the adjectives used to describe the “godfather of conceptual art” Marcel Duchamp (1887-1968). In the opening chapter of Impossible, a monograph by the prominent Duchamp scholar Francis M. Naumann, the artist appears as a (mostly) reticent introvert. But over the course of 192 richly illustrated pages, that familiar, spectral figure becomes flesh and blood: tender, wistful, even romantic. Duchamp falls in love.

Naumann’s account of the French-American artist’s relationship with the Brazilian sculptor Maria Martins (1894-1973) unfolds through a series of speculative scenes that draw, at times uneasily, on the conventions of the romance novel, interwoven with formal analysis that demonstrates reciprocal artistic influence. The author’s nearly five decades of writing on Duchamp, along with the rigorous notes and chronology included at the back of the volume, help temper any initial scepticism about the balance between projection and documentation. From Duchamp and Martins’s first meeting in March 1943 until their respective deaths five years apart, Naumann argues that the two sustained a profound emotional connection, even if the most intense—and presumably physical—phase of their relationship lasted less than a decade. Despite Duchamp’s evident longing, Martins never left her three children or her husband, a Brazilian ambassador to the US.

What first attracted the two artists intellectually to one another is not fully known. But from early on, Duchamp was no longer indifferent, at least where Martins was concerned. Asked to design an announcement for an exhibition at Julien Levy Gallery in December 1943, he placed a cupid figure atop a chessboard depicting an endgame problem with no way to win. “There is no solution,” the artist had famously said, “because there is no problem”. Now, however, there was a problem. Despite a previous marriage and a long-term relationship with the American artist Mary Reynolds, Duchamp had never been in love before. At this point, 26 years had passed since he made Fountain and 20 since he had declared The Bride Stripped Bare by Her Bachelors, Even (The Large Glass) definitively unfinished. It was the problem of Martins, Naumann suggests, that reanimated Duchamp’s interest in making—shifting the affair from a biographical register into a lens through which to view Duchamp’s late work.

The Impossible, III (1946) by Maria Martins is a comment on her relationship with Duchamp © MoMA, New York

As the relationship develops, Naumann focuses on the lovers’ sculptures, exchanged or produced in parallel, as a metonym for the physical encounters he is otherwise obliged to omit. There is Paysage fautif (faulty landscape), an intimate piece made with his own seminal fluid on plastic and backed with black satin, that Duchamp gave to Martins in 1946. And then her cast bronze The Impossible, III (at the Museum of Modern Art in New York) from the same year, in which two creatures, their heads ringed in tendrils, reach toward one another in a gesture that is at once an embrace and a devouring. Martins was, Naumann writes, “unquestionably referring to the impossibility of her relationship with Duchamp”. The goddess figures borrowed from Brazilian folklore that populated her second show at the Valentine Gallery in New York became increasingly abstract and psychologically charged as her career progressed, perhaps reflecting Duchamp’s own preference for the cerebral over the real, the concept over the image.

There is then no shortage of confusion surrounding Duchamp’s final work, Étant donnés: 1º La chute d’eau, 2º Le gaz d’éclairage (Given: 1. the waterfall, 2. the illuminating gas, 1946-66). Why, after decades of apparent withdrawal, would the celebrated Modernist devote 20 years to a highly illusionistic installation of a nude woman posed in a landscape that recalls Gustave Courbet’s The Origin of the World (1866)—the very tradition of “retinal art” he had long disavowed? Naumann’s retelling, which frames the work as the culmination of their coupling, is in this sense instructive. Martins emerges not merely as muse or model but as collaborator. She lends her body to be cast, but also choreographs its position, advises on process and technique. There is no precedent in Duchamp’s oeuvre for the imagery, yet echoes of Martins’s prints and sculptures persist in both the figure and the vegetal setting. Even after she returned to Brazil and their contact waned, Duchamp retained, through work on the diorama, a means of beholding her body and sustaining their artistic tête-à-tête.

Duchamp’s notebook page inscribed with “That is for Maria”

Interspersed passages from Duchamp’s letters to Martins reveal the force of his affections, rendering it difficult to regard Étant donnés as anything other than an act of devotion, however strange or disconcerting it may appear from the outside. The florid sentiments strain against the claim that it is just another one of his jokes. “At heart, my little one, I am profoundly sad, for I can see all our days passing rapidly and fading away,” he writes. And elsewhere: “I feel totally lost now that we are cut off from each other… This isolation hurts.” Reproduced in one of the final chapters is a notebook page with the words “Please do not throw this on the floor” typed above a hand-drawn heart, filled in with red crayon, and scrawled below it: “that is for Maria”.

Although Martins’s letters to Duchamp are not available to the public, it is said that she kept a photograph of him in her studio, and another on her charm bracelet, alongside a lock of his hair—observations that complicate Naumann’s repeated casting of her as a “femme fatale” who “did whatever she needed to get a man to fall in love with her”. The absence of her subjectivity on the page creates an asymmetry that the text acknowledges yet cannot quite overcome. What she truly made of Duchamp—of his work, their relationship—remains largely unknown.

After her husband’s death in 1965, Martins returned to New York and visited Duchamp, who was by then married to Alexina “Teeny” Duchamp. As Naumann notes, it is possible that he signed and dated Étant donnés then and there, unable to complete it until finally, at long last, they were together again.

A collection of intimate interviews with the late Jo Spence provides further texture to her life and legacy

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Jo Spence (1934-92) was a London-born and based British photographer, writer, class and health activist and educator. She is best known for challenging the assumptions of what photography could be, for example, by using her own image to question common tropes around female beauty, health, identity and societal expectations. Spence’s achievements centred on the small home and studio on Islington’s Upper Street that she shared with her partner and collaborator Terry Dennett (1938-2018).

The Unknown Recordings is an intimate self-portrait in text form. There are pictures, but surprisingly few. Still, in that respect, Spence’s disarmingly frank autobiography Putting Myself in the Picture (1986) could not be surpassed. Forty years on, this publication makes sense of a textual account largely in the form of transcripts of key historic recordings made with the artist.

The verbatim transcript of Spence’s interview for the British Library’s Oral History of British Photography, recorded in 1991, extends to more than 100 pages. It covers every aspect of her life from childhood experiences to early working life, before progressing to her photographic work, collaborations and politics, as well as her experience of breast cancer and leukaemia.

The conversation between Spence and the interviewer flows, with few fragments and diversions, in a manner that allows us (almost) to hear it. Generous in her sharing of extremely personal ideas and experiences, Spence does not shy away from difficult topics, least of all her own experience of chronic illness and her attempts to regain power over her own body and identity. Her friendship with artist Rosy Martin and their development of phototherapy, a form of psychological therapy centred on photography, is especially raw.

The transcript of a short personal recording made in 1982, after the breast cancer diagnosis, is more unsettling. This is Spence recording her own thoughts, filled with distress, tears and self-analysis: while it became part of her archive and she often blurred the line between private and public, it does feel a little like eavesdropping. Recorded over two days, it is intensely emotional, as Spence struggles to process and come to terms with the various stressors in her life. In a particularly poignant moment, Dennett is recorded shouting words of support, presumably from another room.

Spence’s interview with visual activist and educator Andrew Dewdney and curator Shirley Read has a political slant. Recorded in the Cockpit Arts Workshop in London in 1989, this conversation gives acute insight into Spence’s commitment to a political as well as photographic practice. It ranges across her ideas relating to different areas of her work, such as the Photography Workshop, Hackney Flashers, Half Moon Photography Workshop and Camera­work magazine. Ultimately, these projects perhaps coalesced around Spence’s focus on people’s right to speak and be seen. It is a powerful conversation.

A series of four appendices act as a kind of postscript to Spence’s voice. There is the documentation of Spence’s sacking from Half Moon over ideological differences; recently discovered images of Spence’s retrospective of 1994; photographs, thoughts and exchanges with artist Pru Clements; and Spence’s CV from March 1987.

This section also includes Dennett’s reworked introduction to Spence’s Putting Myself in the Picture. Here, he makes it clear that Spence’s work was done under difficult circumstances but that she remained focused on the importance of empowering others. Rather than concluding in some fashion or other, together these last documents point the reader to other rich visual and textual archival material that surrounds Spence’s life and legacy. She extended the possibilities of photography to speak about the unspeakable and photograph the unphotographable. Spence was not within the cultural elite, but found a way to tell her own story and hoped it would be a model for others to follow.

Why You Shouldn’t Take Anything Personally

Posted November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Don’t take anything personally. Easier said than done. This represents the second agreement in the bestselling book The Four Agreements written by Don Miguel Ruiz (1997). In it, he astutely states, "Even when a situation seems so personal, even if others insult you directly, it has nothing to do with you. What they say, what they do, and the opinions they give are according to the agreements they have in their own minds."

Despite this, we find ourselves allowing someone's criticism, disapproval, or lack of responsiveness to determine how we feel about ourselves. Our mood and confidence can instantly shift because of a perceived disapproving tone or sensing that we’re the cause of someone’s irritation or frustration. We may react irrationally, impulsively, and intensely based on someone’s reactions, and our perceptions and interpretations.

Taking things personally is often an automatic psychological response shaped by our insecurities, attachment experiences, learned beliefs, and fundamental desire and need to belong. Research suggests that perceived rejection can activate our sympathetic nervous system, helping explain why interpersonal slights can feel disproportionately painful (Eisenberger et al., 2003).

1. You do not like everyone; therefore, you cannot expect that everyone will like you.

Consider how many people you have encountered whom you did not particularly connect or resonate with. Sometimes we have negative feelings that get evoked solely by someone’s presence or mannerism, even if there isn’t any rational justification for it.

Our personal preferences are influenced by many factors such as another’s personality, values, expectations, etc. When we’re not readily accepted, or outwardly rejected, we assume and interpret it as evidence of our inadequacy or lack of worthiness.

Our brain’s tendency is to seek external approval and acceptance and gauge it as a valid measure of our worth. We’re taught this early on in our development and societal standards reinforce it. We frame it as: “If they think I have value, then I must. If they think I do not, then I must not.”

We do not learn that someone else’s preference or opinion is just that, and that our standards and opinion about ourselves matter most. You can reframe and consider that someone's preference for you doesn't determine your value, and you can be an incredible person, despite not being someone’s preferred person.

2. You cannot read minds. Your triggers, perceptions, judgments, and assumptions are all yours.

We frequently confuse our thoughts, feelings, and interpretations as absolute facts. If someone mistakenly doesn’t acknowledge us, we conclude that they don’t care about us and we’re not important enough to them. We also sometimes fail to own and take accountability for our own triggers and often get defensive, deflect, and blame to avoid facing our own fears and vulnerabilities.

In relationships, mind reading, personalization, catastrophizing, and other cognitive distortions can skew reality and be counterproductive and harmful (Beck, 2020). The inaccuracy is understandable given that our mind constructs explanations based on our past experiences, expectations, and emotional states.

To balance out your thinking, you can ask yourself: "What do I actually know, and what am I assuming? How else can I see this?" This is approaching the circumstance and person with overarching openness, curiosity, and expansiveness.

3. Criticism, aggression, and other harsh responses may reflect someone else's internal struggles.

Individuals can externalize their shame, frustration, insecurity, or unresolved emotional pain through hostility, defensiveness, or criticism. Psychodynamic theory describes projection as a defense mechanism through which individuals attribute aspects of their internal experience to others (Bailey & Pico 2023).

For example, a highly insecure person may be especially critical toward others. To distinguish whether a projection is being enacted, you can consider their delivery, intensity, or emotional reaction.

4. Negative energy is to be observed, not absorbed.

Imagine someone is speaking to you impatiently and with irritability. Within seconds, your chest tightens, your heart rate accelerates, your thoughts spiral, and you feel compelled to relent to protect yourself or actively defend yourself. Their emotional state has comingled and become yours.

Research on emotional contagion suggests that people can influence one another's emotional experiences, sometimes without conscious awareness (Wang, 2024). Self-awareness, self-regulation, and self-compassion don't require emotional absorption.

You can connect with and offer empathy for someone else’s reaction and experiences without absorbing and taking on their negative energy. It requires thoughtfully and mindfully deciphering what is theirs and what is yours, and how you choose to react to it.

5. Someone's negative mood is not automatically about you

You may encounter distance, irritability enacted by a harsh tone or body language. Your immediate thought might be, "What did I do?" We often personalize others’ behavior, as connected to something we said or did, without evidence to support it.

Their mood can be reflective of their disappointment, fatigue, hormones, stress, or concerns that can be entirely unrelated to you. Before jumping to conclusions, approach with curiosity and consider asking, "I wonder if something’s going on for you. Is everything okay?" You can choose to approach with compassion and be attuned to someone's emotions without automatically assuming you're responsible for them.

Rejection can be particularly painful because belonging is a fundamental psychological need. Experiencing rejection can contribute to negative self-confidence, social anxiety, and the ability to sustain healthy trusting friendships and intimate relationships.

When someone rejects us, we often transform a specific experience into a more global conclusion. We make inferences such as “They didn’t choose me" becomes "I'm not good enough." Even though intellectually we know that rejection can reflect differing needs, incompatibility, timing, or circumstances beyond what we can control, we tend to personalize and generalize it.

You can remind yourself that your worth isn't determined by whether someone recognizes, attunes to, appreciates, or chooses you. You can’t avoid rejection, but you can strengthen your self-respect and self-compassion based on how you respond to it.

7. People's reactions provide you with vital information

You can learn a lot from observing others’ reactions. It can give you a glimpse of their expectations, perspective, judgments, and emotional state. Emotional maturity involves vulnerability in noticing and acknowledging each person’s contribution, taking accountability, and repairing and healing when appropriate.

In order to accomplish this, you can identify if there is a difference of opinion, a divergent way of seeing circumstances, or an altered way of behaving based on differing core values. The goal is to evaluate, validate and reach mutual understanding so that future interactions can be healthier and more satisfying.

8. Proactively protect your emotional energy

We can spend hours self-doubting, replaying conversations, anticipating rejection, questioning ourselves, or trying to change someone else's opinion of us. This rumination or perseveration spirals into thoughts about the thoughts, feelings about the feelings, thoughts about the feelings, and feelings about the thoughts. The emotional cost is exhaustion, exasperation, and typically extends beyond the original interaction.

Learning to notice, acknowledge, and interrupt your thoughts allows you to redirect your attention, even though your mind wants desperately to problem-solve. You could more readily focus on what you can directly influence, such as your interpretations, boundaries, responses, and choices.

The greatest emotional freedom comes when you no longer require other people to behave differently to feel secure within yourself. You recognize that you have choices regarding the way you respond.

You can recognize that you can be impacted by someone’s behavior without it defining you or automatically driving your behavior.

It can be challenging to notice parts of yourself that you would rather avoid or rid yourself of. Your activation, which can manifest itself as aggression, distancing, or in other ways, is about you. No one triggers you; you get triggered. Recognizing that can change not only how you experience others, but how you experience yourself.

Old Town Sportsman PDL 120 Pro Kayak Review

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

We may earn revenue from the products available on this page and participate in affiliate programs. Learn more

The Old Town Sportsman PDL 120 Pro is a beast. I tested this boat on a 6-mile fishing trip, standing, pedaling, and accessorizing the kayak to see what it can do—and I was impressed. If the name Sportsman PDL 120 Pro sounds familiar, that’s because it’s been in the Old Town lineup for some time. But this year Old Town completely redesigned the top half of this kayak and re-released it. The new model features horizontal rod tubes, a deeper and wider tankwell and cockpit, pre-set grommets for mounting motors and anchors, and more. The seat’s mounting system is different, too. Basically, everything from the water line up is different—and better. Here’s a closer look at the redesigned Old Town Sportsman PDL 120 Pro.

The top half of this boat has been completely redesigned with thinner walls and deeper wells to create more room for storage and operating space. The Sportsman PDL 120 Pro has a combined 150 inches of track, pre-cut through-hull wiring ports, pre-set holes for mounting motors and shallow water anchors, and three access hatches to get down into the hull. That means you can modify this boat however you want. Plus, the front hatch is twice as big as the previous design, and there are four horizontal rod tubes.

Old Town also redesigned the seat so the angler sits higher and has a better paddling angle. The seat sits on adjustable tracks, so you can slide it forward or back for a custom fit. Four knobs beneath the seat can be loosened and tightened from the seated position, so you can adjust the seat even on the water. A large rudder at the rear of the boat deploys with a lever to the right of the angler and is controlled with a knob to the left. Overall, the seating and controls are top-notch. The Sportsman PDL 120 Pro retails for $3,599.

I tested the Sportsman PDL 120 Pro on Thurlow Reservoir while targeting bass and catfish. I laid my bass rods in the rod tubes, with the reels to the sides of the seat. I put my tackle under the seat, my phone and keys in the little waterproof hatch on top of the PDL Drive console, and a large cooler and 14 floats in the rear tankwell.

I peddled about 6 miles during that trip, targeting bass for the first 5 hours. I liked being able to control the boat relatively hands-free with the PDL Drive while still actively fishing. I had to turn the rudder control knob, of course, when moving from spot to spot. But while fishing, I found I could backpedal if I drifted too far forward, and I could stop or correct myself when needed. The PDL Drive let me cover significantly more water than my traditional paddle kayak.

I stood up a couple of times and found the boat extremely stable in both scenarios. I was also really impressed with how sturdy and powerful the PDL Drive is. I could scoot along pretty well with this pedal drive system, and it didn’t rock back and forth like some others I’ve tried in the past. Towards the end of the trip, I baited up 14 floats and tossed them in the lake to try to catch catfish. That ended up being the most fun I had all day. As the floats started bobbing, I chased them down with the PDL Drive, giggling as I got close. I landed three solid fish and tossed them in the cooler to clean up for a buddy.

What really impressed me was how sturdy this boat is. I weigh 260 pounds, and leaning over to grab something out of the water is enough to topple many kayaks. But even when I stretched as far as I could to grab the float, the Old Town PDL 120 Pro didn’t budge, with the top of the gunwale staying several inches above the water. But the court referred the jury to Instruction No. 10, which focused solely n monetary damages.

The backstory, from the opinion this April Judge Rebecca Pennell (E.D. Wash.) in Riera v. Central Wash. Univ., which let the case go forward:

Mr. Riera was employed in a fixed term, non-tenure track faculty at Central Washington University (CWU)…. On the morning of April 1, [2024,] Mr. Riera called the CWU police to report an older, "apparently homeless," woman wandering around Samuelson Hall. He said he wanted to make a report "before things get … out of control." An officer reported to Samuelson Hall and confirmed the identity of the woman as a CWU professor. No further action was taken by campus police or Mr. Riera.

The CWU professor shared her experience with two colleagues. The colleagues immediately filed bias complaints with CWU, alleging the target of Mr. Riera's call—a Black woman—had been the victim of racial profiling.

This led to a great deal of institutional response, including a discussion at a faculty senate meeting. Defendant Erdman, "a lecturer at CWU and member of the faculty senate, emailed unofficial minutes [of the meeting] to non-tenured faculty," and her notes included this:

Without naming names or describing the incident, [CWU President] Wohlpart expressed his outrage at a recent unconscionable incident on campus.

From my own knowledge, I think I can tell you the basics: It was an incident of blatant racial profiling. Campus police were called because a person of color was sitting quietly in the lobby of a CWU building for a brief interval. The person turned out to be a highly distinguished faculty member—but was compelled to produce and show ID before being left in peace.

(I must remark that this is outrageous on multiple levels. All CWU buildings are public; they belong to the State of Washington and its people. Anyone may sit in the lobby of any of our buildings. There was no reason at all to call the police—except, I guess, that this was a person of color.)

Wohlpart called the incident "unacceptable" and said that Central is working to see to it that such a thing doesn't happen again on our campus….

Riera sued Erdman, among others, and the court allowed his defamation claim to go forward in part:

[M]ost of [Erdman's] statements cannot be characterized as false. The only exception is Defendant Erdman's statement that what happened on April 1 "was an incident of blatant racial profiling." A jury could conclude this statement falsely asserts that the person who called police on April 1 engaged in intentional racial profiling. And, given widely-shared public records revealed Mr. Riera as the caller, a jury could also conclude Defendant Erdman's statement was about Mr. Riera.

Defendants argue that regardless of truth or falsity, Defendant Erdman's statement cannot be considered defamatory because it falls under the common interest privilege. "The common interest privilege applies with the declarant and the recipient have a common interest in the subject matter of the communication." The privilege applies to "persons involved in the same organization, partnerships, associations, or enterprises who are communicating on matters of common interest." Examples include officers of a nonprofit association or partners to a partnership. The privilege arises "when parties need to speak freely and openly about subjects of common organizational or pecuniary interest." …

If the privilege exists, it can be lost in two circumstances (1) if the speaker is not acting in the ordinary course of their work or (2) the speaker's statement was made with actual malice; i.e., reckless disregard for the truth. Whether the privilege has been lost under either scenario is a question of fact.

Here, the parties appear to agree that members of CWU's faculty senate are members of the same organization who share a common interest. The dispute lies in whether an exception applies.

The Court concludes there are issues of fact regarding whether Defendant Erdman's statements fell outside the scope of the privilege. Going to the first exception, Defendant Erdman had no official responsibility for taking notes at faculty meetings and she was not required to share her notes with other members of the senate faculty. A jury could therefore conclude that her statements were outside the ordinary course of her work as a member of a senate.

With respect to the second exception, Defendant Erdman was not a witness to the April 1 incident and no investigation had yet taken place. A jury could therefore conclude Defendant Erdman was reckless in asserting that the call to CWU police was an act of blatant racial profiling…. [T]he allegations against Defendant Erdman raise questions of fact that must be resolved by a jury.

Note that, by the time the case came to summary judgment, plaintiff had abandoned any defamation claims against the University, and the court granted summary judgment as to defendant's defamation claims against President Wohlpart. The one remaining defendant at trial was Erdman.

As A Therapist, I Thought Caregiver Burnout Wouldn’t Come For Me. I Was Wrong.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The sandwich generation is sorely lacking in support.

Tuesday, 8:10 p.m. I’m helping my 87-year-old mother brush her teeth and change for bed. My 10-year-old calls from the room next door that he and his dad are done reading his latest Big Nate book. He asks (again) when I’m coming to his room to say goodnight. I try not to rush my mother, but I’m exhausted after a long day, and keenly aware of missing those precious minutes right before bedtime when you get the real deal from your child about everything that happened that day.

After months of juggling my work as a therapist with caring for my aging mother (who has been diagnosed with dementia), and being a present, engaged mom and wife, I had to admit that I was dropping some balls. Having all of this on my plate was clearly not working.

I knew I was not alone — I’ve been having this conversation with many friends and clients, also in their 40s or early 50s. In fact, 47% of caregivers under the age of 50 are part of the sandwich generation, those of us who are raising children and caring for aging parents at the same time. If we aren't talking about perimenopause with all its patches and pills, we’re talking about the health concerns of our parents and, potentially, our grandparents as well.

The burnout I was feeling falls particularly heavily on the shoulders of women. Even if you have siblings, most of the caregiving responsibility consistently falls on one person, and that person is typically a woman. Women shoulder the majority of caregiving responsibilities, often to the detriment of their careers. To make matters worse, some caregivers may have a complicated relationship with the parent who is now dependent on them.

Caring for your parent can be immensely rewarding and depressing. Sometimes I feel deeply honored to care for the person who dedicated her life to caring for me. Other times, my mom can’t remember why her beloved husband hasn’t come home yet — my father passed away several years ago. She really doesn’t enjoy having to be bathed by her child, for that matter.

As a mental health professional who has sat with clients going through this very thing, I expected to have more of the answers, to be able to cope and balance more than someone else due to my experience. But the truth is, nothing can truly prepare you for your induction into the sandwich generation.

No matter how we try to overcome the difficulty of caregiving as individuals, we must acknowledge that there’s minimal financial or systemic help for consistent care for the elderly living at home. Some Medicare programs can provide all your healthcare in one place, which is obviously better than driving all over town talking to doctors who aren’t talking to each other. But they often only offer last-minute daily help and, because of the high need in our country, you typically see a new caregiver each day. While better than nothing, it’s clearly not a great situation for a parent who is already confused in their day-to-day life. Some programs actually pay you, the caregiver, for your work, but that doesn’t give you back the missed moments with your own children or restore those career opportunities.

Eventually my husband and I realized that we needed help. Fortunately, we could afford to pay a nurse out of pocket, but the costs have been steep: tens of thousands of dollars annually to have carers for my mother seven days a week, and that’s with me still doing bedtime duty on the weekends. It’s simply not realistic or sustainable for most people.

And one of the challenges that we don’t often talk about is the guilt. Even if you are able to defy the odds and pay someone to come in to help, you still feel guilty about it and get caught up in the “shoulds.” I should be able to care for my parents and my children at the same time; people have been doing this forever with a lot less help. I should be grateful to be able to spend whatever time I have left with my parent. The important part missing from that is the fact that many of us don’t have villages in the way we used to — that nearby family network that would support each other during all the stages of life.

Our lack of a village came into focus for me recently when we adopted a baby into our family. She has brought us more joy than we could have imagined, and I see how much a community would change this experience for us. In my Mommy and Me classes, so many of the young mothers have discussed having their parents come and stay with them during the early weeks of having a newborn. What a stark contrast for me as a mother, in a different phase in my life, where I am taking care of my parent, rather than her taking care of me and my children.

This is a problem that is not going anywhere. Around the country and the world, people are making tough decisions every day — draining bank accounts, missing out on kids’ milestones, declining promotions and date nights with their partner — to take care of a loved one. The physical, financial, and emotional toll can cause extensive damage to the caregiver’s well-being in so many ways.

We also know what to do about this problem. Studies show that while caregiver burnout is a worldwide issue, it is less so in the countries where (surprise, surprise) there is a strong public healthcare system, flexible work environments for caregivers, and affordable private health insurance. These kinds of safeguards can be a key part of rebuilding a new kind of village and community support. The answer is there. The question is if we will take heed before we all burn out.
