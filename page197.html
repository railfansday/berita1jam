What Democratic control of Congress means for healthcare in 2027" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Tuesday’s forecast-busting blue wave puts wind in the sails of Democrats who have promised a stark departure from Trump-era healthcare policy—though whether or not the shifts will be incremental or seismic hinges on intra-party debates and the president’s veto.

As of noon Wednesday, Democrats had flipped seven seats in the Senate and 21 in the House, setting the party up for comfortable majorities as unexpected battleground races continue to be tallied.

Healthcare issues were a major talking point through many midterm election campaigns. In battleground Senate races, for instance, Democrats spent $65 million on ads primarily focused on healthcare, more than twice that of their next-most prominent topic. Healthcare was overall the second-most prominent issue featured in ads (as measured by spend) across the two parties, behind a push on taxation topics from Republicans.

And within those ads: promises to begin delivering lower costs, greater coverage and federal oversight, starting Jan. 1, 2027.

The immediate legislative impact will be seen in the bills new chamber leaders bring to the floor.

A health policy agenda circulated in September by a working group under Rep. Hakeen Jeffries (D-N.Y.)—the House’s current minority leader and likely speaker in the next Congress—outlined immediate, “First 100 Hours” goals that include repeal of the Medicaid coverage and funding cuts of 2025’s H.R. 1 (The One Big Beautiful Bill Act) as well as restoration of enhanced Affordable Care Act premium tax credits that expired at the top of 2026 and vastly increased marketplace plan premiums.

Other 100-hours priorities outlined in the document include medical debt protections, removal of visa program fees for healthcare workers, an end to an AI prior authorization program in Traditional Medicare, restoration of withheld National Institutes of Health funding, a block on immigration enforcement operations in hospitals and guaranteed no-cost coverage of recommended vaccines regardless of changes in federal recommendations.

Additionally, the new majorities would install key Democratic policy heads to congressional committees responsible for advancing healthcare legislation and conducting oversight into government and private sector conduct. Among those new chairs would be Vermont’s Bernie Sanders, a vocal opponent of corporate interests in healthcare and the country’s chief Medicare for All advocate, who would chair the Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions (HELP) Committee; and Oregon’s Ron Wyden, who as Finance Committee ranking member recently pursued oversight of Medicaid stakeholders, endorsed Sanders’ Medicare for All bill, led calls to hold “Big Insurance” accountable for administrative roadblocks to care and summarized his midterms reaction with a single word: “Subpoenas.”

Underpinning the party’s legislative leaders are a slew of new-to-office healthcare advocates. Several of those elected to Congress have backgrounds as physicians or other COVID-era public health experience. Among the standouts are Ohio Governor-elect Amy Acton, a pediatrician who was director of the state’s Department of Health and resigned early in the pandemic in protest of state leadership; and Michigan Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed, an epidemiologist and Detroit’s former health director, who has made progressive health policy goals like Medicare for All a cornerstone of his campaign.

For the public and the healthcare industry, the key question is how Democrats will pursue their healthcare agenda under a divided government and lame-duck president.

Molly Turco, who covers healthcare policy at TD Cowen Washington Research Group, wrote in a Wednesday policy note that “with Trump in the [White House], passage of major reforms are very unlikely and even medium-sized policies, like rollbacks of Medicaid [state-directed payment] cuts or restoring ACA subsidies, are an uphill battle. But, the midterm results and catalysts like the debt ceiling give Ds leverage.”

Consultants at BRG, in a media roundtable late last week, generally agreed with the veto hurdle, but noted that a stymied Congress still has room to write and pass bills that could become new policy in a future majority, as was the case with Medicaid work requirements pushed by Republicans during the end of the Biden administration but ultimately codified last year.

There is still an avenue for dealmaking between the parties, analysts and advisors noted, such as on areas with general bipartisan agreement (think this year’s pharmacy benefit manager reforms, or other major pain points like physician pay or surprise billing arbitration reform) as part of must-pass spending packages slated for this December and the coming years. The president, for his part, told outlets late Tuesday night that he intends to work with Democrats to pass bills that he believes will benefit the country.

PricewaterhouseCoopers, in a Wednesday policy note, said “targeted bipartisan legislation is most likely to move. Look for action on cybersecurity, AI, price transparency, modernizing drug development and prior authorization processes. Regardless of congressional gridlock, continued regulatory implementation will shape near-term business and compliance requirements.”

Turco’s note predicts a dual messaging strategy for congressional Democrats of affordability and accountability, with the latter involving “an aggressive corporate investigation and hearings strategy.”

The TD Cowen analyst also questioned whether Democrats’ midterm overperformance and gains by progressive candidates will embolden the party to pursue and campaign on “bold” healthcare reforms like Medicare for All ahead of 2028.

Wyden’s recent endorsement of Sanders’ reform bill “is a powerful signal that even long-tenured moderates are under pressure to have a vision that reimagines [healthcare],” Turco wrote, though “many other D members are worried about blowback from M4A support that has almost no chance of being a viable policy. These members are vocal about their concerns and want to work on more realistic policies like restoring the ACA and Medicaid, building on IRA negotiation, reducing premiums/ deductibles for employer-based coverage, adding more benefits to Medicare, etc.”

Investment banking firm William Blair’s equity research team, in an election day brief, noted that Democratic governorships bring immediate impact to the healthcare industry as states make strategic decisions on how to implement, or offset, the provisions of H.R. 1.

The limited avenues for swiftly rolling back that sweeping bill mean industry players, including Medicare Advantage plans and margin-strapped hospitals, should begin preparing for its financial headwinds, consultants and health systems heads have said. But that doesn’t mean Democrats’ strong night has dashed all hopes for rollbacks or other tweaks to ease the pain.

“While the election has changed who controls Congress, it doesn't change the financial challenges hospitals will face in 2027,” Chris Van Gorder, president and CEO of nonprofit integrated health system Scripps Health, said in a statement reacting to midterm election results. “Democrats won't have the votes to simply reverse major federal health care cuts over a presidential veto. But Congress is far from powerless. Lawmakers can use their oversight and budget authority to press for changes in how those cuts are carried out, protect critical health care funding where possible and work across party lines to ease the growing financial pressures on hospitals and impact on patients."

Even if political gridlock takes hold, Tuesday’s results ensure that affordability will be center stage in Washington, analysts said, especially as consumers’ cost pressures will likely persist well through the 2028 election cycle.

“Even where legislation stalls, don’t dismiss the headlines,” PwC advised healthcare executives. “Democratic hearings, investigations and proposed bills offer insight into the policies taking shape for 2028, while growing pressure around affordability continues to push the healthcare market toward consumer expectations. Executives who look beyond near-term political conflict, plan for multiple policy scenarios and adapt to evolving consumer demands will be better positioned for potential healthcare reform after 2028.”

On the virtual care front, ATA Action, the advocacy-focused affiliate of the American Telemedicine Association, said the election presents an opportunity to build on strong bipartisan support for telehealth and virtual care at the federal and state levels. The group plans to continue working across party lines to make Medicare telehealth policies permanent, expand digital health access and remove regulatory barriers to virtual care, Zebley, CEO of the ATA and Executive Director of ATA Action, said in a statement.

Energy Drinks, Stimulant Supplements Linked to Higher Heart Risks in Young Adults

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

New research suggests that caffeinated energy drinks and stimulant supplements are associated with significantly higher risks of serious heart problems, including cardiac arrest and heart attack in young adults.

The findings, to be presented at the American Heart Association (AHA) Scientific Sessions 2026 November 6–9 in Chicago, are preliminary and can’t prove that energy drinks cause heart problems.

Still, the results highlight concerning cardiovascular effects of these popular products, particularly among adults under 35 who consume them regularly.

“These products are marketed as everyday pick-me-ups and sold next to soda and bottled water, so most people assume they carry no real risk,” study author Sai Samayamanthula, a fourth-year medical student at the University of Virginia School of Medicine in Charlottesville, VA, said in a news release.

“For many healthy people, if these products are consumed occasionally, the risk is probably small. However, individuals who consume energy drinks or other stimulant-containing supplements regularly can quickly exceed the recommended daily limit for caffeine, so it’s important for people to know that there may be a risk of cardiovascular harm with these products,” Samayamanthula continued.

Researchers from the University of Virginia School of Medicine analyzed 28,345 reports submitted to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) between 2017 and 2025.

Of these, 854 involved caffeinated energy drinks or stimulant supplements, while the remaining reports involved non-stimulant products.

Compared with non-stimulant products, cardiac arrest was reported approximately 11 times more often with energy drinks and stimulant supplements. Heart attacks were reported 4.5 times more often, while ventricular arrhythmias and palpitations were reported about four times more frequently.

Nearly 45% of heart-related reports involved adults ages 18 to 34.

“Our findings support the case for more systematic safety monitoring of caffeine- and stimulant-containing products. In addition, healthcare professionals should remember to ask their younger patients who have palpitations, chest pain or fainting spells if they recently or regularly consume these products,” Samayamanthula said.

Gregory M. Marcus, MD, professor of medicine at the University of California, San Francisco School of Medicine, and chair of the 2026 AHA scientific statement on caffeine and cardiovascular disease, wasn’t involved in the study, but said the research “raises concerns that products with very high amounts of caffeine may be harmful to the cardiovascular system.”

“Although there are biological reasons why that connection could be possible, the study does not prove that caffeine products cause these events, because other factors may have influenced the results found in this analysis,” Marcus said in a news release.

“More research is needed to understand whether these stimulant products are truly responsible for the reported heart-related events.”

Cheng-Han Chen, MD, board certified interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA, said the findings, although concerning, are not surprising. Chen wasn’t involved in the study.

“We know that energy drinks can have significant effects on the cardiovascular system, primarily because of their stimulant properties,” he told Healthline.

“Caffeine can increase blood pressure and heart rate, while other ingredients, such as guarana and taurine, may further contribute to cardiovascular stimulation and increased stress hormone levels. What is particularly concerning is the potential for these products to trigger serious complications, including abnormal heart rhythms, especially in people with underlying cardiovascular conditions.”

Chen agreed that occasional energy drink consumption is unlikely to have long-term consequences for heart health in most healthy individuals. However, he said that “frequent or excessive consumption can potentially increase the risk of hypertension and cardiac arrhythmias.”

An AHA scientific statement published in July 2026 notes that up to 400 milligrams of caffeine daily is generally considered safe for most healthy adults. That’s roughly equivalent to four or five 8-ounce cups of brewed coffee, depending on how it’s prepared.

However, some energy drinks contain up to three times as much caffeine per fluid ounce as brewed coffee.

Certain energy drinks may also contain stimulants, such as guarana, yerba mate, theobromine, synephrine, and yohimbine.

These ingredients may compound caffeine’s effects on the cardiovascular system.

“Caffeine stimulates the cardiovascular system, primarily by increasing sympathetic nervous system activity. This can cause the heart to beat faster, raise blood pressure, and increase the workload on the heart,” Chen said.

“Energy drinks may contain additional stimulants, such as guarana and taurine, which can further increase blood pressure and stress hormone levels. With excessive consumption, these effects can become more pronounced and potentially interfere with the heart’s normal electrical activity, increasing the risk of abnormal heart rhythms. People with underlying cardiovascular conditions, particularly hypertension or a predisposition to arrhythmias, may be especially vulnerable to these effects.”

Other research has echoed these concerns. A 2025 review suggests that high-volume energy drink consumption can prolong the heart’s QTc interval, increase blood pressure, and raise heart rate beyond the effects of caffeine alone.

“There is growing concern about the cardiovascular effects of energy drinks and stimulant-containing supplements, particularly among younger adults,” Chen said.

“One challenge is that younger individuals may not recognize that these products can have significant effects on their heart and blood pressure, especially when consumed frequently or in combination with other caffeinated products.”

Young adults accounted for a substantial portion of the FDA reports included in the study, highlighting potential cardiovascular concerns for this age group.

People with existing heart conditions, a history of irregular heart rhythms, or heightened caffeine sensitivity may face additional risks. Those with undiagnosed genetic heart conditions may also be vulnerable.

A 2024 study identified cases of sudden cardiac arrest occurring shortly after energy drink consumption, particularly among people with underlying inherited heart conditions.

“As a cardiologist, I am particularly concerned about people who may have underlying cardiovascular conditions that have not yet been diagnosed,” Chen said.

“Excessive stimulant intake can potentially trigger symptoms such as palpitations, chest discomfort, and lightheadedness. In susceptible individuals, it may also increase the risk of more serious complications, including abnormal heart rhythms.”

Additionally, combining energy drinks with strenuous exercise or alcohol may introduce additional cardiovascular risks.

The findings suggest an association between energy drinks, stimulant supplements, and reported cardiovascular events, but they are preliminary and do not establish cause and effect.

The FDA database also relied on voluntary adverse-event reports, meaning some events may never be reported, while others may receive disproportionate attention.

Researchers also do not yet know how many people consumed these products without experiencing any problems. Without this variable, the reports cannot establish actual risk.

Other factors, including underlying health conditions, medications, and lifestyle habits, may also influence the results. More systematic research and controlled clinical trials are needed.

For most healthy adults, consuming up to 400 milligrams of caffeine per day is generally considered safe.

However, Chen noted that individual tolerance for caffeine may vary, and people with underlying cardiovascular conditions, particularly high blood pressure or abnormal heart rhythms, may need to limit or avoid caffeine altogether.

“Energy drinks can vary considerably in their caffeine content and serving sizes,” Chen said.

“Depending on the product, a single can may contain anywhere from 80 to 300 milligrams of caffeine. This means that consuming even one or two energy drinks can put someone close to or above the recommended daily limit, particularly when combined with coffee, tea, or other caffeinated supplements.”

Seek emergency medical attention if you experience chest pain, particularly if it’s accompanied by shortness of breath, fainting, or other symptoms of a possible heart attack.

If you live with a heart condition, ask your doctor about your daily caffeine intake to determine whether you need to cut back.

Healthier Salt Substitute May Prevent Millions of Deaths From Heart Disease

A simple swap for salt could spare millions of cardiovascular disease-related deaths, new research suggests.

In a study published in Nature Health on November 4, researchers at The George Institute for Global Health modeled the potential impact of 193 countries switching from regular table salt to potassium-enriched salt.

They found the switch could prevent nearly 3 million deaths and more than 10 million new cases of heart and kidney disease worldwide each year.

In the United States, this simple salt swap could prevent nearly 105,000 deaths and about 492,000 new cases a year, the researchers estimated.

About 130.6 million U.S. adults have cardiovascular disease, according to the American Heart Association (AHA).

Liping Huang, PhD, a senior data analyst in the Food Policy Division at The George Institute for Global Health and co-lead author of the study, told Healthline the team started with current blood pressure levels by sex, age, and country.

The researchers then estimated how replacing all salt with a salt substitute would change people’s sodium and potassium intake, blood pressure, and disease risk, she said.The team modeled a blend of 75% sodium chloride and 25% potassium chloride. The study estimated these yearly results worldwide:

The authors wrote that the model relied on data from a few years ago and on many assumptions, and that any large-scale switch would take years.

Potassium-enriched salt replaces some of the sodium chloride in regular table salt with potassium chloride, according to The George Institute.Asked where other salts like kosher salt and sea salt fit in, Huang said those salts may contain some trace elements “which has those health halos around them, but essentially they’re still primarily sodium chloride.”Huang noted that potassium-enriched salt tastes “about the same as a regular table salt.” She said it’s possible a small number of people with a sensitive palate could taste a difference.

Huang said that “it’s really hard for people to cut back their salt intake.”“But the easier way is just to replace part of the sodium chloride in our normal salt with potassium chloride, because increasing potassium intake also helps to reduce blood pressure,” she said.The two minerals play opposite roles in the body, according to Cheng-Han Chen, MD, a board certified interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA. Chen wasn’t involved in the study.

“While sodium causes water retention and increased blood pressure, potassium will help the kidneys remove sodium from the body and lower blood pressure,” Chen told Healthline.Huang said she worked on an earlier trial of about 21,000 people in 600 rural villages in northern China in 2021.

People who used potassium-enriched salt had a 14% lower risk of stroke, a 13% lower risk of cardiovascular events, and a 12% lower risk of early death than those who used regular salt, according to a 2021 study in The New England Journal of Medicine that summarized the trial’s results.

The World Health Organization (WHO) endorsed potassium-enriched salt in a 2025 guideline, saying it can reduce blood pressure and the risk of cardiovascular disease.

The AHA said potassium-based salt substitutes can help lower blood pressure in some adults. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued labeling guidance in 2020 allowing an alternate name for potassium chloride on ingredient labels to include the word “salt.”“We think that blood pressure begins to improve as soon as 1 week after cutting back on sodium intake. The full benefit typically takes up to 4 weeks,” Chen said.

“We generally recommend a daily sodium limit of 2,300 milligrams (mg), ideally under 1,500 mg a day. Most people consume over 3,000 mg of sodium a day, well above the recommended limit,” Chen said.The WHO recommends less than 2,000 mg of sodium per day and suggests at least 3,510 mg of potassium a day.In the United States, more than 70% of salt intake comes from processed and restaurant foods, according to the AHA.

Huang said that some people with advanced chronic kidney disease should avoid potassium-enriched salt.When the kidneys don’t work well, the body may not clear extra potassium efficiently, she said. That can lead to hyperkalemia, or high potassium levels in the blood, which can raise the risk of cardiac arrest, Huang said.Among people with stage 3 to stage 5 kidney disease, switching to salt substitutes could prevent about 750,000 deaths a year by lowering blood pressure and cause about 100,000 deaths a year from hyperkalemia, the researchers estimated.The AHA said that people with kidney disease or who take medications that affect potassium should check with a healthcare professional before using these products.

An estimated 37 million U.S. adults have chronic kidney disease, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

The sound came rolling down in waves, crashing down around us as I sat in the Sir Alex Ferguson Stand, in the moments after Manchester United had conceded a most dreadful first goal. It was low and sonorous and quite extraordinary, really.

When a club go nine games without winning and fall behind again, you expect an air of sullen resignation – not that ‘UNITED’ which took hold and grew in a collective, magical act of defiance.

Much more would follow in a significant escalation of protest against the Glazers and their minority partner Sir Jim Ratcliffe. But in that moment – and once more when Roma’s second goal threatened to turn this into a catastrophe – it was just about the terraces and their team. A fanbase refusing to go gently into a night of quiet desperation.

Much has been made of the supposed need to let go of a history which has weighed heavy on this club since Sir Alex stepped away. In turn, United try to release it and then cleave to it again, blindly staggering around in their wilderness.

But on Tuesday night, in the stand bearing the great manager’s name, you looked around and were reminded of how the folk memory of Law, Best, Charlton, Cantona and Rooney is such a powerful weapon. It binds the fans back to what was once great. It is fuel.

What those fans brought into the stadium was not a naive and nostalgic sentimentality. They still demanded the best of the players. There is a clear acceptance among many that Bruno Fernandes is trying to repair things, but when he went down theatrically and protested about a challenge, he was told, ‘stop whinging’.

Harry Maguire, who has lived this period of United struggle in the raw and was terribly exposed in a wretched first half, still wore that perpetual look of defiance on his face. When United were fatefully awarded the retaken penalty kick, some were saying that Maguire should be handed the ball. ‘At least he’ll put his foot through it.’

There was a frustration with the way that moments of promise broke down, as passes from Kobbie Mainoo and Carlos Baleba went astray. And with the time it takes Senne Lammens to decide how to distribute the ball, which slows United dreadfully. Yet a recognition of the fight shown by individuals who were patently playing for and engaging with the struggles of Carrick, while contending with a scorching scrutiny.

The shadow on the wall was the owners, of course. It was significant that the chants of derision for them struck up on the Stretford End each time United scored. As if to say that the green and gold campaign won’t fade with the slightest prospect of hope this time. There were tennis balls, balloons and a sense that something is building.

Even now, after 21 years, do the Glazers, hiding in their Florida redoubt, really know that they are playing with fire? This now feels as visceral as Liverpool fans’ hate for Tom Hicks and George Gillett Jnr, 20 years ago.

Healthcare payment software maker Waystar again outperformed analysts’ estimates in the third quarter as the company builds out new artificial intelligence capabilities and drives growth by adding new customers and selling more of its platform to existing clients.

Waystar brought in $325.6 million in Q3 revenue, up 21% from the same period a year ago, according to its Q3 financial results published Monday afternoon. It beat Wall Street analysts’ estimates by 0.46%.

During Q3, the company reported $181 million in subscription revenue, up 34%, while provider revenue grew 28% to reach $243 million. Patient revenue came in at $82 million, up 5% year-over-year and volume-based revenue grew 8% to $143 million.

Waystar ended the quarter with 1,488 clients generating more than $100,000 of trailing 12-month revenue, up 14% year-over-year. Year-to-date bookings grew 25% year-over-year.

Waystar reported net income of $31 million and GAAP net income per share of 16 cents. It also reported adjusted EBITDA of $139.3 million and non-GAAP earnings per share of 44 cents.

Waystar provides healthcare payment and revenue cycle management tools to 30,000 clients, representing over 1.5 million distinct providers. Its platform processes over 8.5 billion healthcare payment transactions annually, representing more than $3 trillion in gross claims, according to CEO Matt Hawkins. Waystar says its clinical intelligence spans approximately one-third of U.S. hospital discharges. In 2026, Waystar added more than 100 direct connections to health plans, supporting nearly 50 million transactions annually, executives said.

Hawkins told investors and analysts during the Q3 earnings call that the quarter “reflects strong progress” on three strategic priorities of “driving broader adoption of the Waystar platform, extending our reach through partners and integrations, and advancing AI and innovation.”

“Our near-term roadmap includes more than 20 planned AI capabilities, accelerating the autonomous revenue cycle vision to reduce manual work, automate increasingly complex tasks and improve financial and operational outcomes for our clients,” Hawkins said.

Waystar is focused on using AI to reshape the RCM process with a vision of building an "autonomous revenue cycle" that can reduce manual work, automate increasingly complex tasks and improve financial and operational outcomes for healthcare organizations, executives said.

In Q3, Waystar launched an autonomous claim resubmission capability that interprets payer responses, applies payer-specific intelligence to determine the next action and autonomously resubmits eligible rejected claims with minimal human intervention, Hawkins said.

"Based on early adopter results and the initial claim types supported, we estimate this capability could autonomously resubmit up to $5 billion in previously rejected claims annually with the potential to eliminate more than 400,000 hours of manual work each year," he said.

The company is also targeting mid-cycle processes. "We're combining Waystar's financial data, payer intelligence, workflows and connectivity with Iodine's clinical intelligence to bring greater automation earlier in the revenue cycle," Hawkins noted.

The company acquired AI-driven RCM company Iodine Software a year ago to add its clinical intelligence capabilities to Waystar's software. Iodine bookings exceeded management's year-to-date plan by 74% in Q3, Hawkins said. Iodine's CDI and prebill anomaly detection products generated nearly $30 million in bookings through Q3, including about $15 million during the quarter, and have a combined pipeline exceeding $100 million.

The company is winning bigger, broader deals in the healthcare market, Hawkins asserted. Large health care organizations increasingly look to Waystar to support more of the revenue cycle through a single connected platform, he said on the call. "Within our large client cohort, which we define as clients generating more than $100,000 in LTM revenue, the average client now uses approximately five Waystar product modules. With more than 30 modules across six product families, we see significant opportunity to continue driving broader platform adoption within these relationships," he said.

Hawkins added the company “secured the largest new client win in Waystar’s history” during Q3.

“In a competitive process, a major East Coast health system representing approximately $7 billion in annual net patient revenue selected Waystar's full platform across patient access, mid-cycle, and back-end revenue cycle operations,” he said. “The organization plans to replace more than 20 separate vendors with Waystar as it simplifies its technology infrastructure and streamlines operations.”

Moreover, Hawkins said the company is expanding its reach with “strategic channel relationships,” with an emphasis on the ambulatory market—noting an expanded partnership with a leading physical therapy practice management system that expands Waystar’s reach to “nearly 4,000 providers across more than 2,500 sites, representing approximately 17 million claims annually.”

“Decisions like these reflect the trust we've built with channel partners, the breadth of our platform, and our ability to deliver real ROI,” Hawkins said.

Alpana Wegner, who took over as Waystar’s chief financial officer in August, said large client relationships “now represent approximately 75% of revenue,” adding the company is seeing momentum across growth in number of clients, strong bookings and implementation backlog expansion.

But larger, broader platform implementations have longer revenue conversion times, which can extend to 12 to 18 months, Wegner noted. This creates a longer period between booking and recognized revenue. The company reported that the backlog of larger deals, those in excess of $100,000 in annual sales, was up more than 50% year-over-year.

In response to a question from an analyst about the disconnect between bookings and revenue growth, Hawkins said, "We are seeing strong demand. We're not seeing a change in the competitive environment or our win rates, and we're pleased with that," adding that the company sees a "long-term double-digit opportunity to drive revenue."

Data centers are driving up electricity demand, but flexible power use could ease grid congestion and speed up connections

Jim West/UCG/Universal Images Group via Getty Images

Picture a soccer club that bought a passenger van for its tournaments a few times a year. The van sits in a driveway the rest of the year. Buying it was the right call, because a van that wasn’t there on tournament weekend would have stranded the team.

The American power grid works the same way, and now we have numbers to show just how little of it gets used. This month, under a new state law, Virginia’s utilities reported theirs. Over the year ending in July, the distribution grid run by Dominion $D Energy, the utility behind the northern Virginia data center boom, was being used at 41% of its capacity on average.

Even in its busiest hours, the system averaged 80%, with some pockets running higher and others lower. In the state’s rural southwest, where demand has been falling, Appalachian Power reported 14% for the year and 54% at peak.

Grids are sized for the few hours a year when everyone runs the air conditioner at once, and building for those few hours is expensive. A data center that pulls full power around the clock raises that peak, and raising the peak means buying another van that mostly sits in the driveway, costing money while it gathers dust.

Researchers argued there might be a cheaper route than building, and it required something we prize in people: Flexibility. Duke $DUK researchers calculated that the existing grid could absorb nearly 100 gigawatts of new demand if the newcomers agreed to cut back its power use for roughly half a percent of the year.

The study caused a stir in energy circles, and Google $GOOGL hired its lead author. The idea came down to a trade. A customer accepts being throttled in the few hours when the local grid is stretched, and in return connects in months instead of years.

The clearest track record of this working belongs to California’s PG&E. EV charging companies, which draw heavily on the grid, saw the wait for power fall to four to eight months from one to three years before its Flex Connect pilot.

In the pilot’s first two years, the power participants asked for was there nine hours out of ten. In the hours it wasn’t, the limits cut into operations less than 1% of the time.

Batteries offered another way to ease the pressure at peak. In 2025, a data center company in Oregon secured a faster hookup from Portland General Electric $GE by agreeing to add a 31-megawatt battery, enough to run about 25,000 homes for two hours, that it could switch to if needed.

Others flexed the computing itself. Google started by putting off work that could wait whenever grids in Belgium and Taiwan ran tight. In 2024, it dialed back machine learning jobs three times when the grid around Omaha was under strain. This year it promised five utilities it would hold back as much as a gigawatt of data center demand when the grid was stretched.

Startups are selling software that slow, pause or relocate AI jobs when the grid is tight. There were plenty of ways to do it, and more kept coming.

Flexibility is already becoming required in some regions. A 2025 Texas law requires new large data centers to install equipment that lets the grid operator cut their power remotely in emergencies. Late last year, FERC, the federal agency that regulates the grid, directed the operator PJM to create interruptible transmission options for data centers built next to power plants. The Southwest Power Pool, which runs the grid across much of the Great Plains, made a similar move.

Of course, waiting is expensive, and data center queues in popular markets can run for years. Take an Nvidia $NVDA chip that costs $30,000 and is depreciated over three to five years, or $6,000 to $10,000 a year. Picture 100,000 of them in one cluster, waiting for power the way Microsoft $MSFT’s chief executive, Satya Nadella, said some of his company’s chips were last year. A year of waiting represents $600 million to $1 billion in potential computing value that goes unused.

The trade cost something on the other side too, although it was much less. For companies racing each other to build more powerful AI, even a few curtailed hours counted. If those 100,000 chips sat idle for the equivalent of 44 hours a year, the lost computing time would be worth $3 million to $5 million.

Flexibility will not fully replace the building the aging grid needs, because old equipment has to go regardless. But utilities planned to spend at least $1.4 trillion through 2030, with data centers and load growth among their top reasons, and a large transformer could take more than two years to arrive. Flexibility could shrink that bill, and that wait.

Cost per task: Using cheap classifiers to route across Gemini models and thinking levels

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Per-token API prices keep falling, yet engineering bills for autonomous coding agents keep climbing. Price per million tokens isn't the metric that actually governs production economics anymore. The dominating metric is now cost per successful task: total spend across every loop iteration, tool call, retry and internal thinking pass divided by the number of tasks that pass verification.

When an agent calls a high-thinking reasoning configuration for every micro-decision in its harness, you're paying deep-reasoning prices for questions a lightweight configuration already answers accurately. To prevent this unnecessary overhead, consider adding a lightweight routing layer that can intercept routine decisions before they ever reach an expensive reasoning model.That is the premise behind wiring TypeSafe's Jev classifier in front of Google Antigravity's model calls. Rather than generating text token by token, Jev—a specialized structured-output classifier designed for high-throughput decision pipelines—evaluates structured classification primitives in a single forward pass: a Choice from a list, a numeric Score or a Noul, which returns a 0 to 1 probability for a binary statement.

For full disclosure, Google has no partnership with TypeSafe, and this article is not an endorsement of TypeSafe or its products. Jev is used here as one example of a lightweight classifier, and the same routing pattern can be built with other classifiers, including Google-native ones. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab, for example, runs the same kind of classifier on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, in your own Google Cloud project.

We ran a 50-question, auto-graded, coding-agent benchmark against live Gemini and Jev APIs to test whether a near-free classifier can route each task to the cheapest model that still gets it right. Here's what we learned:

The naive way to ask Jev to route a coding task is a single broad Choice question: simple or complex. When we tested that first on 16 coding-agent tasks, Jev routed nearly everything to simple, even tasks requiring an LRU cache trace or modular exponentiation. TypeSafe's documentation warns against this exact anti-pattern. If a question weighs multiple factors or requires a meta-judgment, decompose it into an atomic check.

When we replaced the broad Choice question with one atomic Noul question, it fixed the separation immediately. The question was: does answering this require performing more than one sequential calculation, simulation step or logical deduction, rather than a single direct lookup, format check or one-step classification?

On those initial 16 tasks, Jev routed 13 of 16 in agreement with our hand-labeled difficulty on the first run and 14 of 16 on the second pass. Many engineering teams ask whether they even need a classifier API when they can write a zero-cost regex and prompt-length heuristic in Python, matching words like sequence, modulo and race condition or escalating any prompt over 35 words.

On the 16 development questions used to write those exact rules, the local heuristic scored 15 of 16, or 93.8%. To test whether that heuristic survived contact with realistic coding-agent traffic, we froze its code without changing a character and added 34 unseen held-out tasks across seven coding-agent buckets: fact extraction, schema validation, short classification, single-hop code checks, state simulation traces, math or logic chains and subtle language-semantics bugs.

On the 34 held-out questions, the frozen heuristic dropped 23.2 percentage points to 70.6%. Realistic simple tasks such as extracting a port from a Kubernetes YAML manifest, a file path from a unified git diff header or a line number from a Python traceback exceeded 35 words and triggered false escalations. Conversely, concise multi-step code traces such as mutable default arguments across calls, Python dictionary key hashing on {1: "a", True: "b", 1.0: "c"} and C short-circuit evaluation lacked the heuristic's hardcoded puzzle words and were falsely routed to the simple path. Jev classified all 34 unseen tasks with 100.0% agreement, 34 of 34, reaching 48 of 50, or 96.0%, across the full suite.

A common mistake when building a fast-path router is assuming that setting a low maxOutputTokens cap on a reasoning model will make it spend less on easy tasks. Our 50-question data shows why that assumption fails.

When we compared gemini-3.8-flash with maxOutputTokens=300 against gemini-3.8-flash with maxOutputTokens=4096 while leaving thinkingConfig at the API default, both configurations spent almost identical thinking tokens on the 27 tasks Jev labeled SIMPLE:

Capping the model's output at 300 tokens didn't save any money on easy tasks. The cap is only a ceiling, and easy answers never came close to it. On the easy tasks, the capped version actually cost slightly more than the uncapped one. All the cap did was break 7 of the 25 hard tasks. On six of them, the model ran out of room while still reasoning and never gave an answer. On the seventh, it squeezed its reasoning to fit, finished on time and got the answer wrong.

So a Jev router that sends easy tasks to the capped model and hard ones to normal Flash got all 50 right. It still cost slightly more than skipping the router and using normal Flash for everything, and it was slower. If your cheap option doesn't actually make the model think less on easy tasks, no router can save you money. Not even a perfect one that costs nothing.

The obvious way to spend less on thinking is to ask the model to think less. So we ran a second round of tests with two lighter options: Flash-Lite, which doesn't think at all by default, and Gemini 3.8 Flash with its thinking level set to low. We added those results to the same results file, matched question by question.

The strongest setup without a router was Gemini 3.8 Flash on low thinking. It solved all 50 tasks. That's the number a router had to beat.

The Jev router beat it. It sends every task to Flash-Lite first and escalates only what Jev flags as complex. It also solved all 50 tasks, and it can help reduce both cost and response time compared with running low thinking on everything. On prompts this short the cost difference per task is small, so for an interactive agent the shorter wait is the bigger benefit.

Why it works. On its own, Flash-Lite answered every simple task in half a second but missed 6 of the 25 hard ones. Jev flagged all 6 as complex with high confidence and escalated them. That brought its cost close to what a perfect router would achieve.

The keyword heuristic was cheaper, but only because it got things wrong. It sent 5 hard tasks to Flash-Lite, and 2 of them failed, for 48 of 50.

Escalating to a heavier setting didn't pay here. Routing hard tasks to Flash on default thinking was cheaper than running default thinking everywhere. It still cost more than running low thinking on everything, which already solved all 50. A heavier escalation target only makes sense when the low setting starts failing.

Two things in our results only show up once you look past it.

Routing and caching pull in opposite directions. Our test prompts were short, standalone tasks, and on those the Jev router helped reduce both cost and response time. Real coding agents often look different. They send the same big block of context on every turn, such as a map of the repository and the tool definitions. Agent Platform Context Caching stores that block so you pay 75% to 90% less to resend it. The catch is that a cache belongs to one model. If your agent switches between Flash-Lite and Flash from turn to turn, it either keeps two caches or loses the discount. The practical split: route short, self-contained tasks like log parsing or schema checks, and keep long, context-heavy turns on one model.

Match the model to the workload. Gemini 3.1 Pro is built for deep, multi-step reasoning, and it brings that same depth to every request, including short lookups that don't need it. For a workload made mostly of quick checks and extractions, a Flash configuration is the better fit, and Pro is best saved for the tasks that need its reasoning depth. Model configurations can also apply different safety settings to security-related prompts. In our run, the Pro configuration declined two short code-security review prompts that Flash answered. If your agent performs security reviews, test those prompts on the configuration you plan to use.

Other ways to build the router. Jev is one way to get a fast classifier, but not the only one. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab runs the same Choice, Score and Noul questions on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, hosted on a Compute Engine GPU VM or on Cloud Run in your own Google Cloud project. An open-source server exposes the same API in front of it, so the routing code in this article only needs a different endpoint. Our results come from Jev, so measure accuracy and latency on your own tasks before switching, and keep in mind that a self-hosted model is billed by the GPU-hour rather than by the token.

Taken together, the results give three practical rules.

Start with Flash on low thinking before you build a router. It solved all 50 tasks and cost considerably less than the default setting. Lowering the output token limit is not a substitute, because it doesn't reduce how much the model thinks on easy tasks. Set the thinking level directly, with thinkingLevel: "LOW".

Use Jev to route short, standalone tasks. Send simple tasks to Flash-Lite and hard ones to Flash on low thinking. Ask Jev one simple yes-or-no question per factor. In our test, this helped reduce cost and response time compared with running Flash on low thinking for everything, and came close to what a perfect router would achieve. Keep long, context-heavy turns on a single model so caching keeps working. For high-volume warehouse workloads, Jeff Nelson's BigQuery and Cloud Run cascade pattern applies the same idea.

Measure what your agent actually spends. Send your agent's traces and thinking-token counts to Cloud Logging and BigQuery, so you can see which tasks drive your spend and catch truncation or retries that inflate it. The benchmark code will be published after open-source review.

In summary, the most cost-effective way to run an agent is to match each task to the model that fits it. Start with a fast model on a light setting. You'll often find it already gets the job done. Then add a small, almost free classifier like Jev to send only the hard tasks to a stronger model, and ask simple yes-or-no questions rather than one broad one. Judge every option by what it costs per task done right, not per token.

The three Americans currently living off Earth just made sure their electoral voices were heard.

Anil Menon told us today (Nov. 3) that he and fellow NASA astronauts Luke Delaney and Jessica Watkins voted in the U.S. midterm elections from the International Space Station (ISS).

"Yes, you can even vote from space! All three U.S. crew members had the privilege of voting from space station. We filled out an electronic ballot and sent it securely to the election officials," Menon wrote in an X post this morning.

That post included a 29-second video, in which Menon — wearing, like many of us today, an "I voted" sticker — shows us his "voting booth."

It's actually his room on the ISS, Menon explained. "Inside of it is my computer, where I was able to fill out an electronic form and send it to Harris County so I could vote today," he said.

Most NASA astronauts register to vote in Texas' Harris County; it's home to Johnson Space Center in Houston, the agency's human spaceflight hub.

Americans have been able to vote from space since 1997, when Texas passed a law allowing this to happen. The first person to take advantage of the legislation was NASA astronaut David Wolf, who cast his ballot from Russia's Mir space station that same year. (The ISS didn't welcome its first crew until November 2000.)

"Just like any other American away from home, astronauts may fill out a Federal Post Card Application to request an absentee ballot," NASA officials wrote in an astronaut-voting explainer.

The process is somewhat involved, however.

"After an astronaut fills out an electronic ballot aboard the orbiting laboratory, the document flows through NASA's Tracking and Data Relay Satellite System to a ground antenna at the agency's White Sands Test Facility in Las Cruces, New Mexico," they added. "From New Mexico, NASA transfers the ballot to the Mission Control Center at NASA Johnson and then on to the county clerk responsible for casting the ballot. To preserve the vote's integrity, the ballot is encrypted and accessible only by the astronaut and the clerk."

Delaney and Watkins launched to the ISS on Oct. 1 on SpaceX's Crew-13 mission. Menon has been there since mid-July, arriving aboard a Russian Soyuz spacecraft.

The trio currently share the orbiting lab with four other people — Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk and cosmonauts Anna Kikina, Pyotr Dubrov and Sergey Teteryatnikov. Kutryk and Teteryatnikov are members of Crew-13, whereas Dubrov and Kikina shared the Soyuz with Menon.
