EV Owners Aren’t Worried About Their Batteries. They’re Worried About Myths

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

The battery always seems to be the big thing people cite as a key concern with electric cars. Will it need replacing? Will it catch fire? And what happens to your local electricity grid when everybody plugs in their EVs at the same time?

However, if you ask someone who already owns an EV, you’ll likely get some very different answers. The 2026 Global EV Driver Survey found that 74% of respondents identified myths as a leading barrier to EV adoption in their country.

That put misconceptions ahead of purchase prices, cited by 49%, and charging access in apartment buildings, at 47%. Respondents could select up to three points, so these weren’t mutually exclusive answers.

The survey gathered responses from 29,547 EV drivers across 34 countries and ran from September through October. Participants signed up through EV associations, volunteers, and enthusiasts, with global results weighted according to countries’ shares of the EV fleet.

It’s pretty interesting to see how the opinions of EV owners differ so much from those who don’t have one. These are existing owners explaining what they think holds other people back. The findings don’t prove that misinformation matters more than affordability to someone shopping for their first EV. They do show how owners perceive the gap between living with an electric car and hearing about one.

Unsurprisingly, battery longevity was the most commonly reported misconception, identified by 72% of respondents. It’s the topic that invariably comes up early in conversations with someone claiming they are skeptical about switching to electric.

Next came claims that EVs catch fire more easily (58%) and that widespread adoption would eventually collapse the electricity grid (45%). Yet 65% disagreed with the statement that they were concerned about their own battery’s lifespan. Only 18% agreed. Apparently, the battery looks considerably less frightening when it’s been getting you to work every morning.

There’s evidence supporting that confidence. Ford collected battery health data from 36,000 Mustang Mach-E EVs. The 2021-model-year examples retained an average of 92% of their usable battery capacity after 5.2 years and 55,000 miles (88,500 kilometers).

Ford also revealed that more than 2,000 Mach Es with over 100,000 miles (161,000 km) still had 91% of their original capacity left. Those are averages and are not a guarantee for every car, but they hardly support the idea that EVs become expensive paperweights after a few years. Losing some capacity is inevitable, but losing enough to require a battery replacement is rarer than you may think.

Fire fears need similar context. The idea that EVs are more prone to going ablaze than gas cars is not something studies support. The data has limitations, including differences in vehicle age, but generally points to lower fire rates than ICE vehicles.

Granted, when they do occur, EV battery fires are notoriously difficult to extinguish. They can even reignite after being put out and require special procedures from emergency responders.

Then there’s the issue of the grid being overwhelmed when lots of EVs plug in at the same time. Smart charging can help by replenishing cars only when electricity demand is lower. Adding more EVs will require planning and grid upgrades, but they don’t all need to charge at the same time.

Another 40% identified the claim that EVs aren’t better for the climate as a common misconception. However, comparing emissions means looking at a car’s entire life, including manufacturing and the energy it uses. Making batteries adds emissions upfront, but EVs generally make up for that through lower emissions while driving. How much cleaner they are depends partly on where their electricity comes from.

EVs are still expensive, and charging is difficult for people without a place to plug in at home. Owners acknowledged both problems in the survey. But buyers shouldn’t assume they’ll also have to pay for a new battery a few years down the road, which seems to be a misconception that isn’t going away so easily.

Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin believes younger generations investing in stocks and digital assets instead of housing could help ease pressure on property prices. However, he says crypto still needs something that makes housing attractive: stability.

Buterin thinks that housing creates incentives for owners to keep property prices high, even when cheaper homes would benefit society. Digital assets could offer an alternative, but today’s platforms often encourage excessive speculation.

“We need the equivalent of those good properties that housing investment has, for modern digital assets,” Buterin wrote on X

His comments raise a question for Ethereum. Can the network evolve beyond speculative trading and become dependable infrastructure for people building long-term digital wealth?

One benefit of younger generations investing more in stonks and other digital things instead of housing is that it reduces perverse incentives that drive people to push to keep housing expensive, instead of rejoicing when prices drop as we do for all other life necessities.That…

Ethereum’s development roadmap increasingly focuses on making the network faster, more efficient, and harder to disrupt. Its next major upgrade, Glamsterdam, is currently undergoing devnet testing, with a mainnet launch expected in Q4 2026. The upgrade introduces several changes designed to improve Ethereum’s capacity without sacrificing decentralization.

One major feature is Enshrined Proposer-Builder Separation (ePBS). It moves the relationship between block proposers and builders directly into Ethereum’s protocol, reducing reliance on external relays and additional trust assumptions.

Another important development is Block-Level Access Lists. The feature prepares Ethereum to process unrelated transactions in parallel rather than handling everything sequentially.

These improvements could help Ethereum support more financial applications while keeping its infrastructure accessible to independent node operators. So, if digital assets are expected to become long-term financial instruments rather than vehicles for short-term speculation, this is very much needed.

Ethereum also provides an established foundation for staking, stablecoins, decentralized lending, and tokenized real-world assets. These applications could give investors more ways to hold and use digital wealth without constantly trading volatile tokens.

Yet, security, reliable applications, and responsible financial products must develop alongside network capacity.

Ethereum’s market performance presents a contrasting picture. ETH is trading just a notch above $1,800. This leaves the coin 63% below its all-time high of $4,946. The decline highlights the gap between Ethereum’s technological ambitions and its current market valuation.

Ethereum’s network utility and ETH’s investment performance are also not interchangeable. More applications and transactions can strengthen the ecosystem, but they do not create proportional demand for ETH.

The token still plays an important role as Ethereum’s native transaction currency, staking asset, and collateral across decentralized finance. For this reason, continued growth in staking, stablecoins, and tokenized assets could strengthen ETH’s underlying investment narrative.

Buterin’s comments are bigger than a debate about housing affordability. He is effectively challenging the crypto industry to build assets and platforms that encourage long-term ownership rather than constant speculation.

Ethereum has several pieces of that foundation already, including proof-of-stake security, smart contracts, and an expanding financial ecosystem.

Its upcoming upgrades aim to improve the infrastructure supporting those applications. But the network still needs to prove that its technology can deliver dependable financial products at scale.

Exclusive: Charlton boss Nathan Jones among managers objecting to FA rule change

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Charlton manager Nathan Jones is among a number of head coaches in English football’s top four divisions to have objected to the reduction in how many yellow cards leads to a touchline ban.

Standard Sport understands that at a recent managers’ meeting, Jones and other head coaches from clubs among the 92 argued a 2023 change in FA rules towards stricter punishments for technical-area misconduct remains unjustified at a time when football managers have never faced greater pressure and volatility.

It is the view of some managers, including Jones, that the FA’s move to bring forward touchline bans from four yellow cards to three is incongruous with an increasingly demanding working environment.

Head coaches across the Premier League and EFL are understood to have this season been shown research that suggests managers’ behaviour usually improves once they reach three bookings.

Managers are regularly reminded of a variety of regulations including technical-area misconduct rules during meetings with the FA’s disciplinary teams, who often present research and statistics to contextualise changes to rules or emerging trends.

Ahead of the 2018-19 season, new rules were introduced to clean up touchline behaviour. Managers across the FA Cup, EFL, Carabao Cup, EFL Trophy and National League could receive yellow and red cards for the first time, with four yellows resulting in a one-match touchline ban.

It is the view of some managers, including Jones, that the FA’s move to bring forward touchline bans from four yellow cards to three is unreasonable

Premier League managers would receive only verbal cautions for “irresponsible behaviour” but then the top flight fell in line with the same level of punishment from 2019-20 onwards.

A further update was made ahead of the 2023-24 campaign when the FA launched a new ‘Participant Behaviour Charter’ as part of its ‘Love Football, Protect the Game’ campaign, developed alongside the Premier League, EFL, Women's Super League, Women's Championship, National League System (Steps 1–4), and PGMOL (now Pro Ref), with input from the League Managers Association (LMA) and Professional Footballers' Association (PFA).

The charter’s aim was to improve the image of the game and maintain a positive environment. Four yellows for a managerial suspension was reduced to three and has remained so ever since.

A new cut-off for technical-area cautions was introduced this season. Managers who receive their third technical-area booking after the 37th round of EFL league matches do not incur an immediate suspension. At that point an automatic one-match touchline ban is triggered only if a fourth yellow card is picked up in a subsequent game.

Compared with its peak, the number of technical-area cautions issued has decreased by 17 per cent since the introduction of the ‘Participation Behaviour Charter’, FA sources say.

Under the FA’s ‘Laws of the Game’, other staff and substitutes in a technical area can also be cautioned or even sent off.

In certain circumstances, managers and head coaches can get booked or sent off on behalf of the misconduct of others in their technical area if the fourth official reports a situation deserving of punishment to the referee but cannot verify who in the technical area committed the offence. In such situations, FA rules state cards can be shown to the “manager or most senior coach in [the technical] area”.

NI will carry momentum into Euro 2028 qualifiers

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Justin Devenny's equaliser in Budapest took Northern Ireland into Nations League A for the first time

Speaking before his side ground out the required point against Hungary to seal top spot in Nations League Group B2, Northern Ireland's Trai Hume told reporters that automatic promotion had not been the most immediate aim coming into the campaign.

Two years ago, this side were a League C team, battling to return to the second tier from a group containing Belarus, Bulgaria and Luxembourg.

Fast forward 24 months and a League A place was secured on the final night of an unbeaten campaign that had looked a serious challenge. Prior the trip to Georgia in September, Northern Ireland had a record of played 10, lost eight and drawn two at this level.

Across six games in eight weeks, the side won three and drew three, conceding just once along the way. Justin Devenny's brilliant equaliser in Budapest on Tuesday secured the draw required to top the group and earn promotion to the top tier for the first time.

"It's a great feeling for us," said manager Michael O'Neill.

"In a way, we sort of set out when we came into this level of competition first of all to try and stay in it. Obviously to do that, you have to, you have to be in the top two positions.

"We knew that would help us potentially with the draw for Euro 2028, but to come away unbeaten, to to finish top and to give the performances we've given against some very good teams, it's very encouraging."

With such a young team on the international stage, there will always be more intangible markers of progress too.

It is perhaps easy to forget that not so long ago Pierce Charles' shot-stopping was being questioned as a weaker part of his game when compared to his range of passing.

Quite apart from a penalty save from Georgia's Khvicha Kvaratskhelia to start the group, the Queens Park Rangers loanee made momentum-changing stops from Georges Mikautadze in Tbilisi and Danylo Sikan against Ukraine in Belfast last week.

All three of the Ruairi McConville-Tom Atcheson-Ciaron Brown defensive triumvirate have enhanced their reputations too, while, perhaps not yet in a first-choice XI, Patrick Kelly, Terry Devlin and Brodie Spencer feel increasingly trusted members of a deepening pool of players.

O'Neill may have only made nine changes to his starting teams across the six games, but it felt notable how often those coming in did not look out of place.

The bench then made a huge impact in the draw that sealed promotion with two players at opposing ends of the experience spectrum, Josh Magennis and Kieran Morrison, to the fore.

"We have a squad now where I think the younger players are coming in and making an impact straight away," said O'Neill, who still has the likes of Conor Bradley and Daniel Ballard to come back into his squad.

"Which is a real positive, particularly if you look at the likes of Tom and Ruairi, but also Kieran Morrison and Ceadach [O'Neill] coming in and getting minutes early in their international careers.

"I just think that like the the squad is still very much still in development, we we still have areas where we still need to improve."

The next staging post for this Northern Ireland team is the Euro2 2028 qualifying draw in Belfast on 6 December.

They will be among the second seeds thanks to their top-two Nations League finish and, depending on whether they land in a four or five-team group, could face qualifiers as early as March next year.

"The great thing is we finish on a high and the players go back to their clubs now, they have to deal with a lot of football, they're all playing and demanding leagues," added O'Neill.

"Depending on the draw, we might come in in March and have friendly games which might be a nice thing for us to have.

"But we can just go in and see what the draw brings in December and then we can sort of plot the next 12 months."

After a string of impressive performances in the past three months, they will do so with serious momentum.

West Ham’s £18m Castellanos Problem Could Prove Difficult To Solve

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Reports originating from Argentine journalist Renzo Pantich have suggested that Taty Castellanos wants to leave West Ham United and has already rejected a lucrative offer from Flamengo in favour of remaining in Europe.

Where in Europe, one may ask – and that’s a question no one knows!

Pantich’s claim has generated plenty of attention, but when you dig beneath the surface, it raises a lot of questions!

The suggestion appears to be that by rejecting Flamengo, Castellanos has demonstrated a desire to leave West Ham while remaining in Europe. Yet that conclusion ignores several important realities.

Firstly, Flamengo are reportedly facing financial difficulties with high transfer debts and sponsorship issues due to the Brazilian government banning football clubs from receiving payments from betting and online casinos.

The Brazilian midfielder reportedly accepted a sizeable wage reduction to join Flamengo. It seems highly unlikely that Castellanos, who is approaching what should be the peak years of his career, would be prepared to make a similar sacrifice.

Flamengo’s interest in the striker is nothing new. The Rio giants admired him during his time at Lazio but were unable to make a deal happen because the finances simply didn’t work. The transfer fee and salary package required were beyond what they were prepared to pay. Indeed, it was suggested at the time the player wanted to remain in Europe – sounds like a déjà vu moment!?

Taty wants to leave but who can realistically afford him?

Lastly, and most importantly, there is no mention of any transfer discussions between West Ham and Flamengo on any news wires.

Nobody doubts the emotional attraction. Castellanos has never hidden his admiration for the Argentine giants, and a move back home would undoubtedly appeal on a personal level. The problem is affordability.

Matching a transfer fee acceptable to West Ham while also covering the striker’s wages would represent a huge financial commitment, even for one of South America’s biggest clubs. As C&H has previously reported, River Plate simply can’t afford the player.

In fact, C&H exclusively revealed that it is West Ham who have been actively exploring ways of finding a buyer for the underperforming forward rather than the player agitating for a move.

Despite accepting a wage reduction following West Ham’s relegation, Castellanos remains on a highly attractive contract. His current deal runs until 2030 and includes a one-year club option.

Even following the Championship wage reduction, he is still earning only marginally less than the reported £48,000 per week he received at Lazio.

Should West Ham return to the Premier League this season and want to activate that extension, Castellanos could still have effectively four years of Premier League-level earnings ahead of him.

At around £77,000 per week, that’s potentially close to £18 million in future wages.

Those are numbers very few clubs outside England’s top flight can realistically match.

There will almost certainly need to be compromise from both sides. West Ham appear keen to move the striker on, while Castellanos may have to accept that replacing those wages elsewhere will be extremely difficult.

For all the speculation, one thing seems clear: finding a buyer may prove considerably harder than finding rumours!

Remembering the night Eden Hazard scored a hungover hat-trick

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

When the time came for Eden Hazard to wave goodbye to Lille, his club legend status had long been secured. Needless to say, he wasn’t going out with a whimper.

Hazard had already been a standout player in Lille’s stunning 2010-11 Ligue 1 triumph, the club’s first league title in over 50 years, while his star shone even brighter the following year, finishing as their top scorer with a career-best 22 goals.

Twenty of those goals came in the league, with a further 16 assists. Obscene numbers beyond what even Neymar is reaching in the French top-flight today.

It was obvious that the Belgium international’s talent belonged at one of Europe’s elite clubs.

Sure enough, at the end of the season, he abruptly ended years of protracted tabloid speculation and announced on Twitter that he was joining the champions of Europe, Chelsea.

But while Roberto Di Matteo’s Blues played out that final in Munich, Hazard was still in France, out on the lash one last time with his Lille team-mates.

“We were certain to finish third and we were facing Nancy the following day,” former Lille captain Rio Mavuba later revealed. “It was Eden Hazard’s last match with Lille and he wanted to organise a little something.

“We decided to go have a little drink. A little drink that dragged on somewhat. The following morning, Eden was still drunk. That evening against Nancy, not even with 30 minutes played, Eden had already bagged a hat-trick.”

There was certainly no sign of a hangover come kick-off, with a boyish grin as he looked out across a packed-out Stadium Lille Metropole – also Lille’s last game there, after an eight-year exile in the out-of-town athletics venue – full of banners reading ‘Merci Eden’ among Belgian flags, showing their love for a 21-year-old who’d already become one of the greatest players in the club’s history.

Brazilian striker Tulio de Melo led the line, while Hazard supported from the right, with Dimitri Payet on the opposite flank, Joe Cole operating in the No.10 role, future PSG man Idrissa Gueye at the base of midfield and 18-year-old academy graduate Lucas Digne making an early appearance at left-back.

It didn’t take that special side long to wreak havoc against midtable Nancy, and the players seemed to revel in the lack of pressure in a game that had more the air of a testimonial. Just like the night before, they were there to enjoy themselves.

Not even 10 minutes had gone by before Hazard was put through after some sublime one-touch interplay between Cole and De Melo in the centre of the park. He made no mistake with his finish.

Stood in a huddle to celebrate, there was some degree of choreography in what appeared to be a less-than-subtle nod – or the opposite, in fact, tilting their heads back to mime necking booze – to the previous night’s antics.

While there was no lack of sharpness in the slick passing or dead-eyed finishing for the opener, some sloppy defending at a set-piece allowed the visitors to get back on level terms within a couple of minutes as Nancy full-back Reynald Lemaitre found himself free to send a glancing head in at the near post.

But Lille weren’t going to allow Hazard’s farewell party to be ruined, and soon enough Payet produced a trademark silky touch to turn his man before nutmegging another Nancy defender to tee up De Melo, who tapped home to regain their lead in the 21st minute.

Nancy had enough to worry about with a young Hazard at the peak of his powers. Facing an in-the-mood Payet as well? That’s just cruel.

With a lead restored and the pouring rain failing to dampen the party atmosphere, Lille sniffed blood and quickly put Nancy to the sword.

Having received the ball just outside the centre circle, Payet exhibited another string to his bow, by sending a looping through ball over the top of Nancy’s backline.

The alcohol in his system evidently hadn’t dulled Hazard’s attacking instincts, given he was on his toes to read the pass and dart into the empty space for a second time in just half an hour.

If you’re the wrong side of 30, chances are you’ll look back wistfully on your 21-year-old self’s ability to shake off the after-effects of a heavy night as if it were a mild cold.

Chances are you wouldn’t have been able to time your run, casually control a ball arriving over your shoulder, before showing enough composure to slot it past an onrushing goalkeeper. That’s just showing off.

After just 34 minutes, the Belgian was gifted a golden chance to score his third when Payet was clipped in the area.

As with his first two gilt-edged chances, it never felt like a possibility that he could miss. Not on that night.

After he completed his first-half hat-trick, De Melo lifted Hazard onto his shoulders to celebrate, showing his gratitude to the best player he’d ever play alongside, and “a very good boy – but a bit debauched,” as he later told Goal.

10 ways wildfire burn scars turn heavy rain into flood danger

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Wildfire burn scars and flash floods are closely linked, and these 10 points explain how fire changes soil, runoff and the risk to communities downstream

For people living below a burned hillside, the danger can arrive long after the flames are out. It can come with the first hard rain. Ground that once soaked up a storm can send water racing downhill. That water can carry ash, soil, rocks and charred wood toward homes, roads and streams below.

The link between wildfire burn scars and flash floods comes down to how fire changes the way land handles water. A healthy forest slows rain at almost every step. Leaves and branches catch it. A layer of needles, leaves and decaying plant matter absorbs it. Porous soil lets it sink in. A severe fire can weaken or remove several of those defenses at once.

Fire also leaves things behind. Heat can create a water-repellent layer a short way below the surface. Fine ash can clog soil pores and form a crust. Loose soil and burned wood sit on slopes with little to hold them in place. Together, these changes mean a burned watershed can shed water faster than it did before the fire.

Government agencies treat this as a distinct hazard. The National Weather Service issues flash flood warnings that name burn scars. The U.S. Geological Survey assesses post-fire debris-flow hazards after major fires. Burned Area Emergency Response teams from the USDA Forest Service assess burned land and recommend treatments to help stabilize it.

The effects can reach well beyond the fire perimeter. Sediment and debris can travel downstream into channels, roads, reservoirs and water intakes. Communities well outside the fire's path can still face damaged infrastructure or problems with drinking water.

The 10 slides that follow are a selection of key points, not a complete or ranked list. They explain what fire does to soil and vegetation and why a brief, intense storm can overwhelm burned ground. They also cover how runoff can become a debris flow, how far the effects can reach and how the danger changes as plants return. The final slide looks at the tools land managers and forecasters use to reduce the risk, and at their limits.

A wildfire can leave a water-repellent layer a short distance below the ground surface. Rain that would normally soak into that soil stalls at the layer and runs off instead.

The process starts with the fuel. Leaves, needles, twigs and other plant litter contain organic compounds, including waxes, oils and resins. As fire moves through that material, the heat turns some of those compounds into gas.

Not all of that gas escapes into the air. Some of it is driven downward into the soil, where temperatures are lower than at the burning surface. When the vapor reaches cooler soil particles, it condenses and coats them.

The coated particles repel water rather than absorbing it. The condition is known as soil water repellency, or hydrophobicity. The layer forms where the vapor condenses. It typically sits beneath a thin band of surface soil instead of right at the top.

That placement matters. The loose soil above the layer can still take in some water. Once that thin band fills up, the water cannot move deeper. It flows downhill along the top of the repellent layer. The saturated surface material can then wash away with the runoff, carrying sediment into small channels on the slope.

Some soils repel water to a degree even without fire. This is common under shrubs and trees that drop waxy or resinous litter. Fire can strengthen that existing tendency or create it where it was absent.

Field crews check for the condition with a method called the water drop penetration test. A drop of water is placed on exposed soil, and the crew notes whether it soaks in or beads up on the surface. Repeating the test at different depths helps locate where the repellent layer sits.

The layer is rarely uniform across a burned slope. It can be patchy — strong in one spot and weak or absent a few steps away. Where it is continuous, water has fewer places to soak in. On those stretches, more of each storm becomes surface runoff rather than groundwater.

A burned forest canopy can no longer catch and slow falling rain. Storm water strikes the ground directly and arrives faster. That change removes a watershed's first layer of protection against a downpour, before the soil or the forest floor even comes into play.

In a living forest, leaves, needles and branches intercept rainfall. Some of that water clings to foliage and bark. It then evaporates back into the air without ever reaching the soil. Hydrologists call this interception loss.

The rest of the rain reaches the ground in a delayed, uneven way. It drips from leaves and needles as throughfall. It also runs down branches and trunks as stemflow.

That delay matters during an intense storm. A canopy spreads the delivery of water over a longer period. Soil gets more time to absorb what lands on it. Less water collects on the surface at once.

When fire kills or consumes the canopy, that buffer disappears. Rain falls straight onto exposed ground at the full intensity of the storm. The same storm now delivers more water to the surface, and it delivers that water sooner.

Raindrops also hit bare soil with force. On unprotected ground, the impact of each drop can knock soil particles loose and throw them into the air. This process is called splash erosion. Water moving downhill can then pick up those loosened particles and carry them along as sediment.

Fire does not affect every canopy the same way. A low-intensity surface fire can leave much of the overstory alive. A crown fire, which spreads through the tops of trees, can strip a stand of its foliage entirely. In shrublands such as California's chaparral, fire often burns plants down to their stems and leaves little cover above the soil.

Trees scorched by a less intense fire may hold on to browned needles for a time. Those needles can fall in the months after a fire and partly cover the ground. Trees killed outright do not grow new foliage. Where a stand was killed, interception returns only as new trees and shrubs grow back.

Dead trees also stop pulling water out of the soil through their roots. Without that uptake, soil on a burned slope can stay wetter between storms.

Wildfire can burn away the forest floor's layer of litter and duff, which normally soaks up rain and holds soil in place. Once that layer is gone, water moves across the ground faster and carries more soil with it.

Litter is the top part of the forest floor. It is made of recently fallen needles, leaves, twigs and bark that have not yet broken down much. Duff lies beneath it. Duff is a darker mat of partly decomposed organic matter that sits on top of the mineral soil.

Together, the two layers work like a sponge. They take in rainfall, hold it, then release it slowly into the soil below. That storage gives water time to soak into the ground instead of collecting on the surface.

The forest floor also slows water that does start to move downhill. Fallen needles, sticks and uneven clumps of organic debris create a rough surface. Runoff has to wind around and through that material, and its speed drops. Slower water carries less sediment and has less force when it reaches a stream.

A third role is protection. Litter and duff cover the mineral soil and absorb the impact of falling raindrops. Bare soil has no such shield. Raindrops striking it can knock loose individual soil particles. The next flow of water then picks up those particles and carries them away.

Fire can remove all three functions at once. Flames consume dry litter quickly. Duff can keep smoldering after the main fire front has passed. Dry duff can burn all the way down to the mineral soil. What remains is a bare, exposed surface with little capacity to store water.

On that surface, post-fire runoff gathers quickly and gains speed on slopes. Shallow sheets of water can concentrate into small channels called rills. Over repeated storms, rills can deepen into gullies. Each step moves more soil off the hillside and into drainages below.

The forest floor does not burn evenly across a fire. Patches where duff survives keep some of their ability to absorb and slow water. So do spots where scorched trees drop a fresh layer of needles after the fire. Areas burned down to bare mineral soil lose that buffer.

Fine ash left on a burn scar can clog the small pores in the soil surface and harden into a crust. That seal slows the rate at which rainwater soaks in. More water then runs across the ground during a storm.

Ash is the powdery residue left after vegetation, fallen needles and dead wood burn. Much of it is made of very fine particles. They are small enough to wash into the gaps between soil grains. Those gaps are the openings water normally uses to move downward into the ground.

Once those openings fill, the soil surface behaves less like a sieve and more like a lid. Water reaching the ground has fewer paths to follow into the soil. It begins to pool on the surface and then moves downhill.

Rain helps build the seal. Raindrops striking bare ground knock loose fine material and drive it into surface pores. On a freshly burned slope, ash is often the loosest and finest material available. As wet ash dries, it can stiffen into a thin crust that remains in place when the next storm arrives.

This process differs from the water-repellent layer that fire can create a short way below the surface. An ash crust works at the very top of the soil. It blocks water physically by plugging gaps, not through a change in soil chemistry. Both effects can occur on the same burned hillside. Together, they leave the ground less able to take in water.

Ash cover is uneven across a fire area. Where flames burned hot and long, fuel is often consumed more fully, leaving fine gray or white ash. Where burning was patchier, blackened char and partly burned material can remain. That variation is one reason infiltration can differ between neighboring patches of the same burn scar.

Ash is also easy to move. Wind can strip it from exposed ridges before any rain falls. Early storms can wash it off slopes and into the channels below.

Soil burn severity maps show where a wildfire changed the ground most. Land managers rely on them to judge post-fire runoff risk. Burned soil is rated low, moderate or high severity. High severity ground usually carries the greatest risk of runoff and erosion.

The rating describes the soil, not the trees. A crown fire can strip a slope's canopy while the ground beneath burns only lightly. The reverse can also happen. Severity measures how much the fire altered the surface layers that control how water moves into the ground.

Mapping usually begins with satellite imagery. Analysts compare images taken before and after the fire to see how much the land surface changed. The result is a first-draft map, often called a Burned Area Reflectance Classification. Field crews then visit sample sites to check and adjust it.

On the ground, crews look for physical clues. They note how much litter and duff remain and the color and depth of the ash. They also check whether fine roots near the surface survived and whether the soil still holds its crumbly structure. Ground where the organic layer is gone and the soil looks loose and powdery tends to fall into the high category.

Crews also test for water repellency. A common field check is to place drops of water on the soil and time how long they take to soak in. Drops that bead up and sit on the surface point to a repellent layer.

The finished map shapes decisions about what happens next. Planners use it to choose which slopes need treatment first. Hazard modelers use it as an input when they estimate the chance of debris flows and floods.

A severity map is a guide, not a guarantee. Water repellency varies with soil type and with fire conditions, such as how hot the fire burned and how long flames lingered in one spot. Two patches with the same rating can respond differently to the same storm. Severity is also patchy, with lightly burned and unburned ground scattered among heavily burned areas.

A burned watershed can produce a flash flood from a brief, heavy downpour that the same slopes would have absorbed before the fire. On a burn scar, the rate at which rain falls matters more than the total that falls over a full day.

Hydrologists call the process infiltration-excess runoff. Every soil can absorb water only so fast. When rain arrives faster than that rate, the surplus flows across the surface instead of sinking in. Fire lowers the rate. A burst of rain that once filtered into the ground now runs downhill almost at once.

That makes short storms the main concern. A thunderstorm that drops heavy rain for a few minutes can send water racing off a scar, even if its total rainfall looks modest on paper. A gentle, steady rain lasting hours can pose less danger than a sharp cloudburst.

Timing adds to the risk. Runoff from a steep burned slope can reach a canyon mouth or a road crossing soon after the heaviest rain. That leaves little time to react. The storm also does not have to pass over the people it affects. Rain falling on a scar high in the mountains can flood a neighborhood below that stays dry.

The rainfall needed to trigger trouble is not a single figure. It depends on slope steepness, soil type, how severely the ground burned and the size of the drainage. A threshold that fits one scar may not fit another a few valleys away.

The National Weather Service handles this variation with flash flood warnings that name specific burn scars. Local forecast offices watch radar for intense storm cells moving over recently burned ground. Naming the scar lets residents downstream judge whether their area lies in its path. In the U.S., flash flood warnings can also reach mobile phones through Wireless Emergency Alerts.

Seasonal weather patterns shape when this danger peaks. In the Southwest, summer monsoon thunderstorms bring the short, intense bursts that burned slopes handle worst. In Southern California, winter storms can deliver heavy rain onto scars left by the previous fire season.

A post-fire debris flow forms when water racing off a burn scar picks up ash, loose soil, rocks and burned wood until it becomes a thick, fast-moving slurry. It carries far more force and weight than an ordinary flood of water.

The difference between a debris flow and a muddy flood lies in how much the water carries. Floodwater mostly holds sediment in suspension. A debris flow carries so much solid material that it behaves more like wet concrete than a stream. That density lets it push boulders, snap trees and shove buildings off their foundations.

On a burned slope, the process often begins in small channels and gullies. Runoff concentrates there and scours loose material from the channel bed and banks. Each new load of sediment adds weight and erosive power. That added power lets the flow tear up still more material. By the time it reaches a canyon mouth, runoff that started as a thin sheet of water can have grown into a surge of mud and rock.

Communities at the foot of steep mountain fronts face particular exposure. Debris flows tend to spill out where canyons open onto flatter ground. Over time, these deposits build fan-shaped landforms called alluvial fans, and foothill towns are often built on them. The flow slows and spreads there, dropping its load across streets, yards and homes.

After major fires, the U.S. Geological Survey produces hazard assessments for post-fire debris flows. These estimate how likely debris flows are in individual drainage basins and how large they could be. Emergency managers use that information to decide where to focus warnings and evacuations.

The Montecito, California, debris flow shows what the hazard looks like on the ground. The Thomas Fire burned the steep slopes of the Santa Ynez Mountains above the town in December 2017. On January 9, 2018, heavy rain fell on that burn scar. Mud, boulders and burned trees surged down the creeks toward the neighborhoods below. The debris flow killed 23 people.

Montecito's setting fits the broader pattern. The community sits on a narrow strip of land between steep mountains and the Pacific Ocean. Its creeks run from those recently burned slopes straight through residential areas. That layout gave the debris flow a direct path into town.

Water that runs off a burn scar does not stay on the burn scar. It carries sediment, ash and debris into streams and rivers. That flow can flood towns, roads and water systems well beyond the edge of the fire.

The reach comes from the way watersheds are built. Every slope drains into a channel, and every channel feeds a larger one. When a burned hillside sheds water faster than it once did, that extra flow joins the stream network and keeps moving. A community that never saw flames can sit at the bottom of that network.

The storm can also be somewhere else entirely. Heavy rain can fall on a burned mountainside while the town below stays dry. Floodwater may then arrive in a creek or arroyo under clear skies. Residents may have little sign that anything is happening upstream.

The water also carries a heavy load. Sediment and burned debris travel with the flow toward lower ground. Some of that material settles in channels and raises the streambed. A shallower channel holds less water, so the next surge spills over the banks more readily.

Roads and bridges often sit directly in the path. Culverts sized for clear water can plug with logs and mud. Once a crossing is blocked, water backs up behind it. It can then overtop the road or wash it out. Damaged routes can cut off access for residents and emergency crews.

Drinking water is another downstream exposure. Ash and fine sediment can wash into reservoirs and river intakes that supply towns. Murky, debris-laden water is harder to treat. Sediment that settles at the bottom of a reservoir also takes up space meant for storing water. Utilities may have to change how they draw and clean water, or stop drawing from an affected source for a time.

New Mexico has seen burn scar flooding in recent years. Flooding followed the 2022 Hermits Peak and Calf Canyon fire in the northern part of the state. Flooding also followed the 2024 South Fork Fire near Ruidoso, in south-central New Mexico.

Flood and debris-flow danger on a burn scar is highest in the first years after a fire. It declines gradually as plants return and the soil rebuilds its ability to absorb water. There is no fixed date when a burned slope becomes safe again.

Recovery starts with whatever survived underground. Many grasses and shrubs resprout from roots, bulbs or root crowns that the fire did not kill. In California chaparral, shrubs such as chamise and scrub oak can send up new shoots from their bases soon after a burn. Seeds stored in the soil also germinate. Some species in fire-adapted ecosystems have seeds that respond to heat or smoke.

New growth helps in several ways at once. Stems and leaves break the force of falling rain. Roots knit loose soil together and open channels that let water move downward. Dropped leaves begin to rebuild a protective layer on the ground.

Below the surface, soil structure recovers more slowly. Soil organisms, fungi and roots help bind particles into small clumps with pore space between them. That structure lets water soak in rather than race across the surface. As it returns, more rain infiltrates and less becomes runoff.

The pace varies widely from one burn scar to another. Burn severity, soil type, slope steepness and the amount of rain that falls during recovery all shape the timeline. A wet growing season can speed regrowth, while drought can stall it. A slope burned at high severity usually takes longer to recover than one that burned lightly.

Some changes can work against recovery for a time. The roots of trees killed by the fire decay underground. As they rot, they lose their ability to hold slopes together. That process can leave hillsides more prone to shallow landslides even as surface vegetation comes back.

A second fire can reset the process. When a recovering area reburns, the young vegetation and rebuilt soil can be lost before they have stabilized the ground.

Communities below a burn scar can still face the hazard after the first winter or monsoon season following the fire. The risk tapers over time rather than switching off.

Treating burned slopes and warning the people below them are the main ways to cut post-fire flood risk. USDA Forest Service Burned Area Emergency Response teams assess burned land and recommend mulch, seeding and erosion barriers. Debris basins and alerts help protect communities downstream.

The program, known as BAER, is built for speed. Its work often begins while a fire is still burning. A damaging storm can arrive before burned ground has any cover at all.

A BAER team typically draws on specialists such as hydrologists, soil scientists, engineers and biologists. They survey the burn and identify threats to life, property and critical natural and cultural resources. Then they propose emergency treatments. The Department of the Interior runs its own BAER program for land managed by agencies such as the Bureau of Land Management and the National Park Service.

Mulch gives bare soil immediate cover. Straw or shredded wood spread across a slope shields the ground from raindrop impact. It also slows water moving downhill. On steep or remote terrain, crews often drop mulch from helicopters.

Seeding aims to rebuild ground cover with grasses or other plants. It works more slowly than mulch. Seed needs moisture and time to sprout before it holds any soil. Land managers choose seed mixes with care. Nonnative species can crowd out recovering native plants.

Erosion barriers trap sediment on the hillside before it reaches a channel. Crews may lay burned logs across a slope or set out straw wattles. They may also upgrade roads and culverts to carry more water and debris.

Private land falls outside federal BAER authority. The Natural Resources Conservation Service can help landowners there through its Emergency Watershed Protection program.

Other defenses sit downstream. Debris basins catch mud and boulders before they reach neighborhoods. Crews must clear them after storms to restore their capacity. Evacuation warnings and National Weather Service flash flood warnings give residents time to leave before a storm peaks.

None of these measures removes the hazard. Mulch can wash away in a heavy storm. Seed may fail to take hold, and a basin can overfill. The treatments lower the risk on a burn scar, but they do not eliminate it.

How long before flood insurance takes effect: 10 rules and exceptions

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The flood insurance waiting period is usually 30 days for NFIP policies, but mortgage closings, map changes and home sales can shorten or skip it entirely

The moment most homeowners think about flood insurance is often the moment it can no longer help them. A hurricane track shifts toward the coast, or a river gauge starts climbing, and the search for coverage begins. Under the standard rules, a new policy bought at that point would not take effect for weeks.

That delay is the flood insurance waiting period. For policies written through the National Flood Insurance Program, which FEMA manages, coverage generally begins 30 days after the application and the full premium are received. A flood that starts before that date is not covered, even if the policy has already been paid for.

A homeowner who assumes flood protection begins the day the payment clears can end up paying for repairs without insurance. The question of how long before flood insurance takes effect has a firm answer in most cases. That answer is measured in weeks, not days.

The 30-day rule is not absolute, though. Several situations shorten the wait or remove it. Coverage purchased in connection with making, increasing, extending or renewing a loan takes effect at closing. A flood map revision can move a property into a high-risk zone. If the owner buys a policy within a limited window set by FEMA, the wait is much shorter. A buyer who takes over a seller's existing NFIP policy keeps that coverage without starting the clock again.

Other rules work in the opposite direction. A policy that lapses can bring a fresh waiting period when it is replaced. Adding coverage partway through a policy term generally comes with its own wait unless a loan transaction is involved.

The 10 slides that follow cover a selection of these rules and exceptions, one per slide. They explain what each one requires and when it applies. They also look beyond the federal program to private flood insurance, where insurers set their own terms. The final slide covers lender-placed coverage, which a lender can buy when a borrower's required policy lapses. Each slide stands on its own. Readers can go straight to the situation that matches theirs.

A new policy from the National Flood Insurance Program generally takes effect 30 days after the application and the full premium are received. Until that date arrives, the policy is not in force. That holds even when the paperwork is complete and the buyer has a confirmation in hand.

The National Flood Insurance Program, known as the NFIP, is run by the Federal Emergency Management Agency, or FEMA. Congress created it through the National Flood Insurance Act of 1968. Insurance agents sell its policies. So do private insurers that write and service NFIP coverage under FEMA's Write Your Own arrangement. The 30-day standard is the same no matter which company issues the policy.

The clock depends on two things: the application and the full premium. An application submitted without payment does not start the countdown. If the premium arrives later, the effective date moves later with it. For anyone buying coverage, the date the money is received matters more than the date the form was signed.

The rule applies to both parts of an NFIP policy. Building coverage pays for damage to the structure itself, including its foundation, electrical and plumbing systems and permanent fixtures. Contents coverage pays for belongings such as furniture, clothing and electronics. Renters can buy contents-only policies. Those follow the same 30-day standard when bought outside an exception.

The waiting period carries extra weight because standard homeowners insurance policies generally exclude flood damage. A household that assumes its existing policy covers rising water may learn otherwise only when it looks into separate flood coverage. The 30-day count starts from that purchase, not from any earlier point.

The calendar also plays a role. The Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 to Nov. 30. A policy whose application and premium arrive on the first day of the season does not take effect until July 1.

The 30-day period is the default rather than a fixed rule for every policy. Some transactions and map changes set a different start date, and each one comes with its own conditions.

The flood insurance waiting period exists to stop people from buying coverage only when a flood is clearly on its way. Without it, a homeowner could skip premiums for years. That same owner could then buy a policy the week a storm is forecast and collect on damage that was close to certain.

Insurance works by pooling risk. Many policyholders pay premiums, and that money covers the losses of the smaller group who suffer damage in a given year. The arrangement holds only if people buy coverage before they know whether they will need it.

The insurance term for the opposite pattern is adverse selection. It occurs when the people most likely to file a claim are also the most eager to buy, and they buy when their risk is highest. If enough buyers waited for a forecast, premiums collected would fall short of claims paid. The pool would then shrink toward the households facing imminent losses.

Flooding is especially exposed to this problem because much of it can be seen coming. A house fire or a burst pipe usually strikes without notice. A hurricane, by contrast, is tracked across the ocean for days before it reaches land. The National Hurricane Center issues a hurricane watch 48 hours before tropical-storm-force winds are expected in an area. The National Weather Service forecasts river crests ahead of time. Snowmelt flooding along northern rivers tends to follow a seasonal pattern each spring.

That lead time is the gap the waiting period is designed to close. The delay on a new National Flood Insurance Program policy runs well past the warning a hurricane watch provides. A buyer cannot treat a forecast as the signal to purchase coverage.

The rule also shapes how flood coverage fits into household planning. Standard homeowners policies in the U.S. generally exclude flood damage. Flood protection is therefore a separate purchase. The waiting period turns that purchase into a decision made ahead of hurricane season or the spring thaw, not a response to a specific weather event.

A flood policy bought in connection with making, increasing, extending or renewing a loan takes effect at the loan closing. The standard waiting period on National Flood Insurance Program coverage does not apply in that situation.

The exception reflects how flood coverage and mortgages are tied together in U.S. law. The Flood Disaster Protection Act of 1973 requires flood insurance on buildings in high-risk flood zones, known as Special Flood Hazard Areas. The requirement applies when the property secures a loan from a federally regulated or federally insured lender. Loans backed by federal agencies, such as the Federal Housing Administration and the Department of Veterans Affairs, carry the same requirement.

A lender in that position cannot make the loan on an uninsured building. Any waiting period would leave the collateral exposed from the first day of the loan. Starting the policy at closing lets the coverage and the debt begin together.

The exception covers four kinds of loan events. Making a loan is the most common, as when a buyer finances a home purchase. Increasing a loan applies when a borrower takes on a larger balance secured by the same property. Extending a loan lengthens its term. Renewing a loan restarts an agreement that has reached its end date.

Timing still matters. Under FEMA's rules, the application and premium need to be submitted at or before the closing for the policy to start that day. A borrower who arranges coverage after the closing has passed falls back under the standard wait.

For home buyers, flood insurance in a high-risk zone becomes a closing-table item alongside homeowners insurance. The lender checks whether the property sits in a Special Flood Hazard Area, typically on FEMA's Standard Flood Hazard Determination Form. If it does, the borrower must show proof of a flood policy before the loan funds.

Federal rules also set the minimum amount of coverage. It must equal at least the smallest of three figures. Those are the outstanding loan principal, the maximum coverage the NFIP offers for that type of building and the building's insurable value. For a single-family home, the NFIP building coverage limit is $250,000.

A property that a flood map revision moves into a high-risk zone can get NFIP coverage that starts one day after purchase. The shortened wait applies only when the owner buys the policy within a limited window that FEMA sets after the revision takes effect.

The maps in question are Flood Insurance Rate Maps. FEMA produces them to show flood risk community by community. The highest-risk areas on these maps are called Special Flood Hazard Areas. They mark land with at least a 1% chance of flooding in any given year. Their zone labels begin with A or V.

FEMA revises these maps as it gathers new data on rainfall, river flows, coastal conditions and local development. A revision can redraw the boundary of a high-risk zone. A home that sat outside that boundary for years can end up inside it once the new map becomes effective.

The new zone can change what an owner is required to buy. Federal law requires flood insurance on buildings in Special Flood Hazard Areas that serve as collateral for mortgages from federally regulated or federally insured lenders. An owner who never needed a flood policy may suddenly be required to carry one.

The one-day rule gives newly mapped owners a path to coverage without the standard 30-day gap. Without it, they would go a month without protection at the point the government formally classified their property as high risk.

The exception depends on timing. The date that matters is the effective date of the map revision, not the date an owner hears about it. A policy bought after FEMA's window closes falls back under the usual 30-day rule unless another exception applies.

Map changes generally pass through a public process before they take effect. That process includes preliminary maps and a period for appeals. Owners who follow it at the local level can learn that a revision is coming well before the new map is final.

The shortened wait is tied specifically to a move into a high-risk zone. An owner whose property was already mapped as high risk, and who simply decides to buy coverage, faces the standard 30-day period.

A National Flood Insurance Program policy can be assigned to the buyer of a home. The buyer takes over the seller's existing coverage and does not have to sit through a new waiting period.

Assignment works differently from buying a fresh policy. A new application sets its own effective date in the future. An assigned policy is already in force on the day the buyer takes it over. The buyer steps into coverage that was already running under the seller's name.

Continuity around a sale has practical weight. A home can change hands during the Atlantic hurricane season, which runs from June 1 to November 30. A standard homeowners policy does not cover flood damage. A buyer who relies only on a new policy bought outside an exception could own the property for weeks with no flood coverage.

Assignment can matter most to buyers who pay cash. The exception that lets a new policy start at closing depends on a loan transaction. A cash purchase involves no loan. Assignment gives a cash buyer a way to have NFIP coverage in force from the first day of ownership.

Buyers who finance a purchase have the loan-closing route as well. For a property in a high-risk flood zone, a federally regulated or insured lender requires flood insurance as a condition of the mortgage. For those buyers, assignment is one of two ways to have flood coverage in place when the sale closes. A financed buyer can compare the seller's existing coverage with a new policy before choosing.

The policy's declarations page lists the coverage limits and the dates of the policy term. Those details show a buyer how much coverage they would inherit and when the policy comes up for renewal. For a single-family home, NFIP building coverage tops out at $250,000, and contents coverage tops out at $100,000.

The assignment rule belongs to the NFIP, the federal flood program. A policy from a private flood insurer is governed by its own contract, including any terms on transfer to a new owner.

A flood policy renewed with its premium received before the expiration date carries on without a break in coverage. If the policy lapses instead, replacement coverage can face a fresh waiting period before it protects the property.

The difference comes down to timing. A renewal is not a new purchase. It extends an existing contract. The protection already in place rolls into the next term.

Policies under the National Flood Insurance Program are generally written for one-year terms. The renewal question comes up every year, whether or not a flood has threatened the area.

A lapse happens when the renewal premium does not arrive before the policy expires. Once that occurs, the property has no flood coverage. Any flood damage during the gap falls on the owner.

The larger cost can come afterward. Restarting coverage after a lapse may be treated as buying a new policy rather than renewing an old one. In that case, the owner can be left exposed again while a new waiting period runs. A homeowner who misses a deadline in a quiet month could still be uninsured weeks later, when storm season arrives.

The payment method matters as well. Many homeowners with mortgages pay flood premiums through an escrow account managed by their loan servicer. In that arrangement, the servicer sends the payment. The policy can still lapse if that payment is late or missed. Confirming that the renewal was actually paid, rather than assuming it was, reduces that risk.

Owners who pay directly need to watch for the renewal notice from their insurer. They also need to make sure the premium will be received, not merely sent, before the expiration date. The rule turns on when the insurer gets the money.

Grace-period rules for late renewal payments exist, but their terms are set in program rules that can change. Policyholders can confirm the current terms with their insurer or agent rather than counting on extra time.

A mid-term increase in National Flood Insurance Program coverage generally comes with its own waiting period. The higher limit does not take effect right away unless the increase is tied to a loan transaction, such as a mortgage closing or a refinance.

The existing coverage stays in place while the extra amount waits. Take a homeowner with a $150,000 building policy who asks to raise it. That $150,000 stays in force the whole time, and only the added portion is on hold. If a flood hits before the increase takes effect, the claim is paid under the old limit, not the new one.

The split matters most to owners who find partway through a policy year that they are underinsured. Rebuilding costs can rise after a renovation or an addition. A policy written years earlier may no longer match what it would cost to replace the house. Raising the limit closes that gap, but only after the waiting period has passed.

The loan exception applies to an increase the same way it applies to a new policy. A lender may require more flood coverage as part of a new, larger, extended or renewed loan. In that case, the higher limit can take effect at the closing. A refinance with a larger loan balance is a common example. The borrower does not have to wait out a separate period for the added coverage.

NFIP coverage also has a ceiling that no mid-term change can exceed. For a home, residential building coverage tops out at $250,000 and contents coverage at $100,000. Building and contents carry separate limits on the same policy. A homeowner who needs more than those amounts can buy excess flood insurance from a private insurer. That coverage sits on top of the federal policy.

An increase also adds to the cost of the policy. The higher limit carries an additional premium, which covers the added amount for the rest of the current policy term.

A flood insurance policy does not pay for damage from a flood that was already in progress when the coverage began. The standard National Flood Insurance Program policy excludes that loss, even if water reaches the insured building only after the policy takes effect.

This exclusion is written into the Standard Flood Insurance Policy, the contract used for NFIP coverage. It applies to a flood in progress at the time and date the policy term begins. It also applies to coverage a policyholder adds at their own request during the term.

In a claim, the key question is when the flood started, not when the house got wet. The policy defines a flood as a general and temporary condition of partial or complete inundation of normally dry land. That condition must cover two or more acres of normally dry land, or two or more properties, at least one of which belongs to the policyholder. The water can come from the overflow of inland or tidal waters, from the unusual and rapid accumulation or runoff of surface waters from any source, or from mudflow.

Under that definition, a flood can be underway in a neighborhood before water reaches a particular home. A river that has already left its banks upstream, or streets already under water nearby, can mark the start of the event. A policy that begins while that condition exists does not cover the damage that follows from it.

The exclusion closes a gap that the waiting period alone would leave open. Without it, a policy that started on schedule in the middle of a slow-moving flood could still pay for that flood. River flooding can build over days or weeks as water moves downstream. A policy bought early in that kind of event could otherwise come into force before the water arrived.

The rule also covers policies that skip the standard wait. A buyer whose coverage starts at a loan closing is not covered for a flood that began before the closing. The same is true for a homeowner using the one-day effective date after a flood map revision.

For a property owner, the practical issue is timing. Coverage bought after heavy rain has started, or after a river crest is already moving through a region, does not cover that event if the flood was already in progress. The purchase has to happen before any flooding begins in order to protect against it.

Private flood insurers set their own waiting periods, and some are shorter than the 30 days that applies to most National Flood Insurance Program policies. The wait depends on the insurer and the policy. The effective date printed on the declarations page is the date that counts.

Private flood insurance is coverage sold by insurance companies outside the federal program. The NFIP, run by FEMA, writes most residential flood policies in the U.S. Private carriers compete with it for some of the same homes and businesses.

The federal waiting-period rule does not bind these companies. Each insurer writes its own terms into the policy contract. The wait can differ from one carrier to the next. It can also differ between states for the same carrier. A homeowner comparing quotes can treat the waiting period as one of the terms to check, alongside price and coverage limits.

A shorter wait does not mean coverage is available at any moment. Property insurers commonly use binding restrictions, which pause the sale of new policies in an area when a named storm threatens it. A buyer who holds off until a hurricane forecast can find that no private policy is on offer at all.

Private policies also matter for mortgage borrowers. The Biggert-Waters Flood Insurance Reform Act required federally regulated lenders to accept private flood insurance that meets certain standards. Federal banking regulators later issued a joint rule setting out how lenders apply that requirement. A qualifying private policy can satisfy a lender's flood requirement in place of an NFIP policy.

The companies behind these policies are not all regulated the same way. Some private insurers are admitted carriers licensed by a state insurance regulator. Others sell through the surplus lines market. Surplus lines policies are generally not backed by state guaranty funds if the insurer fails.

The coverage itself can differ from the federal product. Some private policies offer building limits above the NFIP's residential cap. Some also cover additional living expenses during repairs, which NFIP policies do not.

When a borrower's required flood coverage lapses, the lender can buy a flood policy on the borrower's behalf and charge the borrower for it. This is known as lender-placed or force-placed flood insurance. Its job is to keep a mortgaged property insured while the loan is still outstanding.

The obligation comes from federal law. The Flood Disaster Protection Act of 1973 and the National Flood Insurance Reform Act of 1994 require flood insurance on loans from federally regulated lenders. The rule applies when the property sits in a Special Flood Hazard Area in a community that takes part in the National Flood Insurance Program. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac apply the same requirement to loans they buy.

The coverage has to stay in force for the life of the loan, not just on closing day. A lender that finds coverage has lapsed, or has dropped below the required amount, must respond.

The process starts with a notice. Under the 1994 law, the lender must tell the borrower that coverage is missing or insufficient. If the borrower does not buy adequate coverage within 45 days of that notice, the lender must purchase it. The premium is then charged to the borrower.

A lender-placed policy may come from the NFIP's Mortgage Portfolio Protection Program or from a private insurer. Its terms are built around the lender's need to protect the loan. The limits, the deductible and the price can differ from what the borrower would have picked.

The borrower can replace lender-placed coverage by buying a policy and sending proof to the lender or loan servicer. The Biggert-Waters Flood Insurance Reform Act of 2012 requires the lender to end the force-placed coverage within 30 days of receiving that proof. The lender must also refund premiums and fees for any period when the two policies overlapped.

The effective date on the replacement policy determines when the borrower's own coverage begins. Confirming that date before the lender cancels its policy keeps the property from going uninsured in between.

AI's one-model era is over

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Businesses stopped picking one AI. That’s a problem for the companies selling them, a threat that comes even as Anthropic is about to IPO

Photo Illustration by Cheng Xin/Getty Images

A version of this article originally appeared in Quartz’s AI & Tech newsletter. Sign up here to get the latest AI & tech news, analysis and insights straight to your inbox.

Anthropic is days away from asking the public to buy in. Investors are weighing a pitch that values the Claude maker at up to $1.4 trillion, and Thursday is the expected first day of trading.

That price assumes customers who picked Claude will keep picking Claude. But by the time the IPO roadshow started, many of them were already shopping around.

AT&T $T was one of them. In May, open models handled about a fifth of the telecom company’s AI work. By September, the share had doubled to 40%, and its AI costs had fallen by as much as 80% since earlier in the year.

The company did not drop Claude or ChatGPT. Instead, a router scored each request for difficulty, sent the easy ones to open models from Meta $META, Google $GOOGL, and Nvidia $NVDA, and saved the expensive models for the hard problems.

Revolut went further. The British fintech rolled out Claude Code in early 2026, and watched some engineers burn through up to $10,000 a month before capping their budgets.

Then it moved roughly 2,000 engineers onto GLM, an open-weight model from the Chinese developer Z.ai, running on 56 Nvidia chips operated in-house. The switch cut coding costs by about 90% in under a year.

They are not the only ones being a little promiscuous. On Vercel’s AI Gateway, a service that lets developers access models from many providers through one connection, almost a third of teams changed their most-used provider in September alone.

Open-weight models handled 71.5% of traffic that month and captured almost a quarter of spending, up from about one-twentieth in June.

The growing competition put pressure on prices, even as more tokens are used. Spending through the gateway ran about 13 times higher than in December 2025, while the average price per token dropped 47%.

The consumer data offered another warning for the model makers. As providers' capabilities expanded, consumers became less likely to pay for multiple services at once. They were switching between providers, but increasingly saw little reason to pay for more than one. People were dating around, one subscription at a time.

For the giants, this is an awkward moment to discover how little loyalty their customers owed them. Anthropic has committed close to $900 billion to data center capacity, much of it through long-term agreements that would be difficult to unwind. Its third-quarter operating loss was $567 million. The economics depend on keeping customers willing to pay a premium for its models.

Fortunately for Anthropic, it still led where the money was. Claude Opus 5.5 took 11% of September spending on Vercel’s gateway, more than double the share of OpenAI’s top model, which came in second. A Chinese model, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, ranked third.

The shift also created opportunities for the middlemen, as it tends to in a gold rush. Gateways and routers were becoming the point where customers chose between models, while American hosting providers collected more than two-thirds of the September spending on Chinese models.

None of it should have come as a surprise. A Google researcher had laid out the whole problem in an internal memo that escaped onto a public Discord server in 2023. They argued that neither their employer nor OpenAI had a moat, and that customers would stop paying for a model once a free one was nearly as good.

Anthropic’s prospectus ran to more than 400 pages. While it never used the word moat, its risk factors warned that customers might drift toward “smaller, cheaper, or open-source alternatives.”
