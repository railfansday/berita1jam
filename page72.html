Rashee Rice: Lamborghini Rental Company Attorney Blasts NFL Star For Filing For Bankruptcy

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

The attorney representing a Lamborghini rental company that claims Rashee Rice never paid them after his 2024 crash is speaking out after the NFL star filed for bankruptcy ... and he thinks Rice is "incapable" of being accountable for his actions.

Kyle Coker -- who represents The Classic Lifestyle -- tells TMZ ... "I think the only reason Rashee Rice filed for bankruptcy was to avoid the impending jury trial related to injuring others during his drag-racing escapades."

Rice caused a six-car pileup after drag-racing his leased Lamborghini in Dallas in March 2024 ... before pleading guilty to felony racing on a highway and felony collision causing serious bodily injury.

The Kansas City Chiefs wide receiver spent over $2.1 million connected to the civil lawsuits involving his 2024 Dallas car crash. Coker says Rice still hasn't paid The Classic Lifestyle after the wreck, despite not having insurance.

Rice filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy this week, claiming he's more than $3.45 million in debt with only $677,000 in assets ... and Coker is blasting Rice's legal move.

Coker tells us ... "The Court in that case wouldn't give him the continuance he desired, and the other parties involved were ready to hold Rice accountable. Upon discovering that Rice would finally have to face the consequences of his actions, he instead rushed to inform the Court that he had filed for bankruptcy over $3 million in debt, with $10s of millions in expected earnings off his next contract."

Coker says he plans on appearing in the bankruptcy court case on behalf of TCL to ask the court to dismiss his bankruptcy and to "further award sanctions against Rice for this illogical procedural delay."

The attorney also takes issue with Rice's legal team ... who previously described him as a "young man that made a mistake" after the crash.

Coker tell us ... "I believe Rice's actions from the start, continuing through his attorney's original 'he's just a kid, give him a break' speech through Rice's subsequent legal troubles, have shown that Rice is incapable of and wholly disinterested in taking accountability for his actions."

Rice is in the final year of his rookie contract and will be a free agent after the NFL season. He's been a productive player and looks due for a raise.

Fantasy Football Week 9 Fades & Busts: Bad times to continue for Panthers

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Welcome to the ninth of 17 weekly articles spotlighting the fantasy fades of the week! I am your host, Matt Okada, and I'll be bringing you half a dozen potential busts each and every Friday, from now until fantasy football championships.

As a note, just because a player earns a "fade" or "bust" designation doesn't automatically mean they should be benched — it depends on the rest of your roster or the options on your waiver wire. But you can expect them to fall short of expectations (when I get them right). I include a "What to Do" section for every player to help with that.

Without further ado, here is my list for Week 9 of the 2026 season.

Over the first two weeks of the season, Bryce Young was brilliant, throwing for three touchdowns and hitting 24+ fantasy points in both games. Then, he suffered a minor knee injury in Week 3, which he has played through with a brace, limiting his mobility in the pocket. He hasn't thrown three touchdowns in any of Carolina's six contests since, clocking in at just seven TDs and four interceptions over that span.

In the meantime, the Panthers' offensive line has been ravaged by injuries and, in combination, these factors have led to a severe drop-off in fantasy production for Young. He's been pressured on 50% of his dropbacks over the last two weeks and has logged a 35.4 passer rating and 20% success rate against that pressure. Largely as a result, he's averaged just 9.39 fantasy points per game in those two outings.

Now, he draws a matchup with a Broncos defense sporting the league's fourth-highest pressure rate (40.3%) and second-highest quick pressure rate (21.7%), per Next Gen Stats, both of which have increased in recent weeks. Young is likely to be in trouble early and often in this game and is staring down the barrel of another fantasy dud.

What to do ❓ Young is well outside QB1 range and is honestly closer to the back of QB2 range this week. I'd much prefer a streamer like Tyler Shough, Malik Willis, Kirk Cousins or even Michael Penix Jr.

Ollie Gordon II has been shockingly good for fantasy since taking over for De'Von Achane several weeks ago. Most of the fantasy community couldn't even decide whether he'd get the starting job, and he's proceeded to average 18.8 touches per game since the Week 3 takeover and score 13+ fantasy points in four of those five contests. However, I'm pumping the brakes for Week 9.

Coming off his first dud — 6.9 points against the tough Patriots run defense — Gordon has an even tougher matchup in the Lions. Detroit is a massive pass-funnel opponent, meaning that it encourages offenses to throw — allowing a league-high 298.7 yards per game on a league-high 41 attempts per game — rather than run (where it's bottom-10 in YPG and attempts per game allowed). As a result, it's allowing the fourth-fewest fantasy points per game to running backs. And unfortunately, Gordon has essentially zero role in the Miami passing attack. If he's lucky enough to see a goal-line carry or two, Gordon could score a touchdown and eke out a decent fantasy day, but it feels far more likely that he has another medium-volume, low-efficiency day and ends up in single-digit fantasy points once again.

What to do ❓ Gordon isn't an absolute must-sit, especially given the shallow nature of the position. But he's a low-end RB2 at best and I'd definitely lower expectations and avoid him in DFS.

Speaking of the Lions' run defense, they turned Jordan Mason into an absolute dud last week, with just 20 rushing yards on eight carries for 2.0 fantasy points. That brings his total since returning from injury in Week 7 to 88 yards on 23 carries (3.8 per carry) with zero catches and zero touchdowns. Fantasy managers who waited on his return are justifiably frustrated but might see a glimmer of hope in the "green" Bills matchup here in Week 9.

Buffalo has been a good matchup for running backs because its allowed the third-most receiving fantasy points per game, at 11.0, behind only the Dolphins and 49ers. In rushing fantasy points, its much closer to the middle of the pack, and Mason is only guaranteed to see a portion of that production with Aaron Jones Sr. still involved. The Vikings offense has been extremely lackluster all season and they're an underdog against the Bills in what figures to be a relatively low-scoring affair. None of this bodes well for the run-only half of Minnesota's RBBC, so Mason is better suited on the bench until a better opportunity arises ... if ever.

What to do ❓ Mason is well outside RB2 range and I wouldn't even consider him a promising FLEX. You're better off starting committee-backs like Zach Charbonnet or Blake Corum, as they should have better chances at scoring.

Remember everything I said about Bryce Young, the Panthers' O-line and the mess that pressure has created for this passing attack lately? All of that trickles down in a very discouraging way for early breakout candidate, Jalen Coker. He has just three catches on 10 targets all season when Young is pressured and just three catches for 45 yards during this particularly troubling downturn the past two weeks. He had a brilliant game against the Eagles three weeks ago, but Philly is currently the best matchup for wide receivers, so we have to take that 97-yard game with a grain of salt. And it makes sense that pressure and Coker don't get along: he's averaged 11.2 air yards per target and 20.5% of his targets have come on deep passes this season (nearly twice as high as the rate for Tetairoa McMillan).

Meanwhile, Denver has allowed the fifth-fewest fantasy points to wide receivers and the fourth-fewest yards per target on downfield throws (10+ air yards). Just as Young is a fade, Coker is a complementary fade for all the same reasons. I like his upside down the stretch if Young and the O-line can get healthy, but he's a very risky play in the meantime.

What to do ❓ Coker is outside WR2 range and while he's a playable FLEX in a pinch, I'd rather go with a lesser name in a better situation, like Keenan Allen or Stefon Diggs.

It seems like there's a bit of growing optimism about Jaylen Waddle in the fantasy community ... but I'm not entirely sure why. Sure, he caught a touchdown in Week 8 against the Chiefs ... just his second of the year, by the way. He also finished that game with three catches for 39 yards, marking his sixth-straight game with fewer than six catches and fewer than 50 receiving yards. His only truly impressive outing all season came back in Week 2 against the Jaguars — who have proven to be a major pass-funnel defense — when he saw eight catches for 138 yards on 10 targets. But Waddle had 138 receiving yards in his other six games combined entering Week 8 and is averaging just 39.4 per game on the season, even when including that aberration of an outing against Jacksonville.

He's averaging the fourth-fewest yards per target (6.2) among all wideouts with 50+ targets and doesn't have a game over 14 fantasy points in the last six weeks. All told, his floor and ceiling are concerningly low and I see little reason for optimism against the Panthers — even with injuries to their starting cornerbacks — given how spotty and inefficient Waddle's usage has been.

What to do ❓ Waddle falls a couple spots behind Coker in my rankings, so we can apply the same logic for him in Week 9 — WR3 at best, iffy FLEX play, would rather go with Allen or Diggs.

This pains me, as I am a huge Dalton Kincaid fan, and there are a lot of reasons to believe in him rest-of-season — like his position-leading 2.7 yards per route run and his robust-for-a-tight-end air yards share (19.7%). But the Minnesota Vikings are terrifying. They've allowed a league-low 5.5 fantasy points per game to tight ends and have held every single TE they've faced to single-digit fantasy points. Tucker Kraft set the current high-water mark at just 8.0 points, while Sam LaPorta (5.0), Juwan Johnson (3.6), Colston Loveland (0.8) and others have been complete duds in this matchup.

On average, they've surrendered just 3.1 catches for 39.0 yards per game to the position, and have yet to allow a touchdown to a tight end all season. Sitting Kincaid won't be easy, especially given the shallow nature of the position, but it might be necessary given the heavily reduced ceiling against Minnesota.

What to do ❓ Try to find a streamer in redraft if you can, a la T.J. Hockenson, Kyle Pitts Sr., or even Brenton Strange, rather than playing Kincaid against this defense. And stay away in DFS.

I finally had a chance to watch The Invite with my HBO Max subscription, and as someone who went through a divorce a few years ago, it felt like Déjà Vu. So many similar arguments, so many conversations. I didn't have to know about Olivia Wilde's divorce from Jason Sudeikis to know it was all rooted in experience.

The only part that didn't feel right for me was the ending. While there's insistence that the ending is ambiguous, I'm not too sure I agree, having lived a similar version of those events. Here's what I think, as well as what Wilde has said about the movie's ending.

The decision to move forward with a divorce made sense, as did sleeping in separate rooms. Then it ended with a tender piano duet and a show of intimacy that, frankly, wasn't there between my ex and me when we finally decided to end our marriage.

Obviously, there are tons of divorces every year, and many different scenarios. Maybe there were moments when people about to live separate lives indefinitely played the piano together and shared a tender cuddle before consulting a mediator or attorney, but that wasn't my experience.

To me, it only makes sense that Angela and Joe ended up working on their marriage and didn't split after all. I think for all the chaos of the trailer, seeing the ending with Joe finally playing his piano again shows that he's willing to change and move forward. It's the birth of a new stage of their relationship, and Angela joining him at the piano shows she's ready to embrace it as well.

Olivia Wilde told USA Today that the ending is left up to the audience, but admitted she and Seth Rogen had different ideas about what happened. Rogen thought that Joe and Angela would be together forever, while Wilde believed that two people who break up can still love each other.

‘I Just Kept Saying Thank You.’ Seth Rogen On Filming Intimate Scenes With Penélope Cruz

Seth Rogen's optimistic take could come from the fact that he mainly does comedy movies, but I'd like to think he has the same takeaway I do about the final moment. Sure, people who divorce can still "have love" for each other without being in love, but I think that comes over time. Not in the instantaneous decision to divorce, but that's just my thought.

Conversely, I think it would take a lot more for a couple who's been together a decade plus to end their marriage. To make such a big decision in one night because the sex therapist who lives upstairs said your relationship is broken? To me, that feels like the most unrealistic part of The Invite, though again, it's just my take.

The Invite is available to stream on HBO Max and is a great watch from earlier in the 2026 movie schedule for those who missed it. It's considerably less funny than I was led to believe, but I am ultimately glad that I watched it.

Did You Get Blamed for Everything in Your Family?

The first time I learned about the concept of an “identified patient,” it immediately resonated with me. So many of my clients have had the experience of being the “problem” in their family. They were scapegoated, blamed, and gaslit for struggling with their mental health. Often a highly sensitive or neurodivergent child (or both), they felt the family’s turbulence deeply, unable to pretend everything was fine and ignore all the chaos.

The idea of an identified patient is used in family therapy, where sessions involve the whole family, not just the person (often a child or adolescent) who is struggling. It’s common in these sessions for other family members to say, “Well, they have all these problems, but we’re just fine.”

If this resonates with you, it’s likely you were blamed by your family for being “difficult,” “too sensitive,” or “selfish,” because you often got upset as a child. I even hear these descriptions of babies who cried a lot, didn’t sleep through the night, or struggled with colic, as if a baby could somehow control their behavior to be easy or difficult.

It’s true that babies are especially sensitive to their parents’ moods, facial expressions, and tone of voice, so they can react strongly to, say, a depressed mother or angry father. But babies can’t express their discomfort in words, so they cry—their only means of communicating distress. Even older children will struggle to express themselves verbally, as their emotions are so intense and the prefrontal cortex—the brain region we need for complex thought and emotional regulation—remains undeveloped until well into adolescence. That’s why babies and children of all ages are a lightning rod for problems in the family.

This is especially true for kids with highly sensitive nervous systems, because they pick up all the negative energy in a room, whether expressed or unexpressed. It hurts them, like a throbbing headache, invisible but impossible to ignore.

Let’s explore this idea through Jo’s story. Jo* was an unusually sensitive kid who struggled with chronic anxiety as far back as she could remember. “My brother always called me ‘scaredy-cat’ because I was terrified all the time. Dogs, strangers, loud noises—you name it, I was freaked out by everything,” she told me in our first session.

Like so many clients, at first, Jo told me her family was “totally normal.” “I had a regular, happy childhood,” she claimed, despite struggling with a raft of anxiety disorder diagnoses her whole life. When I pointed this out, she initially blamed herself for being too sensitive, too thin-skinned, just too much as a kid.

It took some slow, patient detective work to explore what lay beneath the surface of this “normal” family, which was anything but. As part of my psychoeducation with clients, I explain the idea from schema therapy of “core emotional needs,” which every child has, no matter their culture or country of origin. These include needs for love and a secure attachment, being kept safe and protected from harm, and feeling valued and accepted for who they are, not how they perform.

Jo’s father was a violent alcoholic who took his frustration and rage out on Jo’s poor mother. Tearfully, Jo recalled the “walls shaking” as her dad punched them or trashed their home. Jo would hide under the covers with her younger brother, cradling him in her trembling arms. He was too little to understand, and her dad left the family when he was just five, so the chaos and dysfunction didn’t affect him as much as it did her.

As she admitted to me how awful things had been, Jo began to feel more compassion for the little girl who was so traumatized by her father’s rage attacks. Little by little, we helped transform her self-blame into self-compassion.

Like so many of my clients who were stigmatized by their family, Jo was constantly gaslit by other family members. Even though she rarely saw her father, who continued to drink heavily, he would tell her she was “weak” and needed to “toughen up,” taking zero responsibility for his behavior or role in making her so anxious. Even her brother, whom she tried so hard to protect, said she was “self-centered” and “attention-seeking” for being so anxious all the time. This broke her heart, but she had long just accepted that everyone’s version of their shared reality was true.

We worked hard to change that. I was a tireless cheerleader for Little Jo, that sensitive kid who did nothing wrong, despite the critical family stories. Jo now had two daughters of her own, so I asked her how they would react if their dad smashed up the living room or hit Jo in front of them. She was horrified, knowing how profoundly that would affect them. This helped her see that the family stories told by her dad and brother were neither true nor fair. “You know, I’m starting to see it wasn’t my fault for getting so upset,” she told me one day. “Any kid would have struggled with all that anger and chaos. If anyone’s to blame, it’s my dad, not me.”

For Jo, seeing the truth of her childhood eventually set her free. She learned to treat herself kindly, as well as how to set boundaries and be assertive, even with her overbearing father. When our therapy ended, she was flourishing—loving and accepting herself as “highly sensitive and proud of it.”

If any of this resonates with you, I’m sorry—I know all too well how painful it can be to fill that identified patient role. But I also hope you understand that this was a label given to you by a family desperately trying to avoid responsibility for its own dysfunction.

You may need help from a skilled professional in untangling the knotty story of your childhood, but finally understanding that you were just a sweet, innocent kid in a painful environment might just change your life.

*All case studies in these posts are composites—I never tell the stories of real clients, as confidentiality is very important to me.

Dallas Wants More Gates At Love Field—And To Give Southwest Airlines A Bigger Monopoly

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Dallas is opening the door to more gates at Love Field airport. Two things stand in the way. Federal law caps the airport at 20 gates, and Dallas has already negotiated a lease that would offer most of the additional gates to Southwest Airlines if the law were to change. Southwest already controls nearly all of the gates there. More room for adding Southwest flights is different than more competition for Southwest. Dallas needs a larger airport and to open itself to competition. The Dallas City Manager raised the prospect of additional gates at an aviation event last week. The airport is undergoing a $2.5 billion redevelopment that doesn’t include more gates. Former American Airlines Chairman and CEO Doug Parker defended the existing arrangement on this week’s Airlines Confidential podcast. His argument rests on the existing five-party agreement, federal law and investments made in reliance on both: “agreements are agreements.” That’s only why changing the rules will be difficult, not a reason to keep limits on competition forever. Why Dallas Can’t Just Build More Love Field Gates Southwest Airlines had to fight to operate from Dallas Love Field when it first launched. It flew its first flight in 1971 after being tied up in the courts. Existing airlines agreed to leave Love Field for DFW airport. Southwest wasn’t a party to that agreement. In 1979, the Wright Amendment was passed to limit competition from Southwest Airlines. Deregulation meant that Southwest could fy beyond the state of Texas, and other carriers feared that the close-in airport they operated from would be more desirable than DFW. The upstart Southwest that fought for the right to fly was mature and pivoted to using government to its own advantage by the time they succeeded in securing the Wright Amendment Reform Act of 2006. It was a compromise among Dallas, Fort Worth, DFW Airport, American Airlines and Southwest Airlines. Southwest gained the ability to sell connecting service beyond the old geographic limits, followed by nationwide domestic nonstop service in October 2014. Love Field’s gate count had to be reduced from 32 to 20. Southwest kept most of the gates. The airport couldn’t offer nonstop international passenger service. Basically Southwest got more flying from Love Field and a monopoly there, while American Airlines got a monopoly on international service from Dallas and a cap on how much service could compete from Love Field. Initially, Southwest effectively couldn’t even fly out of Dallas – Fort Worth offering direct competition with American. The five party agreement specified that any gate Southwest operates at DFW (or another airport within 80 miles) would require giving up a Love Field gate, up to 8 total gates. That restriction ended in 2025. The provision even covered affiliates and flights carrying Southwest’s code, with exceptions for existing DFW–Midway codeshare service. Southwest couldn’t even codeshare with partners who flew to DFW, and could set up a regional affiliate to operate there. Dallas Already Gate Southwest An Option On More Love Field Gates The city council approved Southwest’s October 1, 2028 – September 30, 2040 Love Field lease covering 18 of the existing 20 gates. One gate is under Delta’s control and the final gate is under city control and can (theoretically) be used for new service. Dallas can then seek or support the effort. If the law changes, Dallas can pursue four, eight or twelve additional gates without the usual review by major incument airlines (‘majority-in-interest’ airline review, effectively Southwest) as long as each new gate won’t cost more than $50 million in 2025 dollars. Southwest receives an option on three of four, five of eight, or eight of twelve new gates. Even after changing a law that protects incumbents, Dallas would offer most new gates to its dominant airline. Parker Explains Why American Would Fight This Parker attributed two percentage points of Delta’s margin advantage, in an internal analysis from his American tenure, to Atlanta’s lack of a competing airport like Love Field or Midway. American has a lot to lose if some Dallas travelers gain better alternatives to DFW. Parker acknowledges his bias. American has made enormous investments at DFW, and predictable rules matter when financing airports. But I’d note: The agreement is more than 20 years old. Investors who bet on government keeping out competition also take the risk that the government changes its mind. Twenty years of protection is a substantial benefit, not a guarantee of protection in perpetuity. American hsa benefited from other subsidies, too. Parker was a prime mover behind American’s receipt of $12.8 billion in pandemic payroll support, the vast majority of the second and third tranches displacing payroll the airline was going to pay anyway. American chose not to fly from Love Field. American ended service in 2009 and subleased its gates to Delta. Its later merger settlement required divestiture of the gates, but American had already stopped operating. American preferred concentrating its network at DFW but that shouldn’t give them a permanent veto over competitors serving a different airport. The Government Destroyed Gates To Make This Deal Work Parker correctly recalls the demolition of six gates at the former Legend Airlines terminal. The overall reduction was twelve gates from 32 to 20. Six of those were at the separate Lemmon Avenue facility. I covered the litigation over that terminal and its leases. The Court of Federal Claims awarded the owners $133.5 million when the city took it back through eminent domain. The Federal Circuit reversed in 2018 arguing that the fact the gates couldn’t legally be used meant it had no value. It might soon have had value. The terminal was turned into a Lincoln dealership. North Texas Has Outgrown This Bargain DFW is growing. Its new Terminal F is planned for 31 gates, all serving American, with the first 15 expected to open in 2027. Meanwhile, McKinney is preparing to launch scheduled commercial service on November 11. Different parts of the Metroplex support different airport choices. DFW, Love Field and McKinney aren’t interchangeable for a passenger near downtown Dallas. Noise and road access are real though anyone living near the airport knew there was an airport there. Additional gate construction would necessarily involve an operational, environmental and access plan. American Airlines certainly didn’t walk away from the Naples, Florida airport just because residents there don’t like commercial air service! Love Field should be allowed to grow. The additional preferential gates should be open to airlines that bring new competition, rather than committing ex ante to enlarging Southwest’s existing control. These lease commitment is an impediment to that, and actually gives American Airlines its strongest argument against expansion. Southwest built its business by fighting attempts to keep it out. American chose to leave Love Field. Neither of those things justifies keeping other competitors out of the Dallas market, or serving each of the local markets in North Texas separately. Southwest Airlines had to fight to operate from Dallas Love Field when it first launched. It flew its first flight in 1971 after being tied up in the courts. Existing airlines agreed to leave Love Field for DFW airport. Southwest wasn’t a party to that agreement.

In 1979, the Wright Amendment was passed to limit competition from Southwest Airlines. Deregulation meant that Southwest could fy beyond the state of Texas, and other carriers feared that the close-in airport they operated from would be more desirable than DFW.

The upstart Southwest that fought for the right to fly was mature and pivoted to using government to its own advantage by the time they succeeded in securing the Wright Amendment Reform Act of 2006. It was a compromise among Dallas, Fort Worth, DFW Airport, American Airlines and Southwest Airlines.

Southwest gained the ability to sell connecting service beyond the old geographic limits, followed by nationwide domestic nonstop service in October 2014. Love Field’s gate count had to be reduced from 32 to 20. Southwest kept most of the gates. The airport couldn’t offer nonstop international passenger service.

Basically Southwest got more flying from Love Field and a monopoly there, while American Airlines got a monopoly on international service from Dallas and a cap on how much service could compete from Love Field.

Initially, Southwest effectively couldn’t even fly out of Dallas – Fort Worth offering direct competition with American. The five party agreement specified that any gate Southwest operates at DFW (or another airport within 80 miles) would require giving up a Love Field gate, up to 8 total gates. That restriction ended in 2025.

The provision even covered affiliates and flights carrying Southwest’s code, with exceptions for existing DFW–Midway codeshare service. Southwest couldn’t even codeshare with partners who flew to DFW, and could set up a regional affiliate to operate there.

What is EMDR therapy, and does it actually work?

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

Maybe a friend swears EMDR helped her move past something she’d carried for years. Or maybe you came across it online while searching for ways to cope with a memory that still feels heavy. Whether you’ve heard of EMDR but don’t know how it works, or you’re familiar with the approach but skeptical, we’re here to break it all down.

If you’re uncertain, that’s understandable, because EMDR can sound unconventional: you briefly recall a painful memory while following side-to-side stimulation, like tracking a moving target with your eyes. But the research tells a more reassuring story. It’s a well-studied therapy for trauma, and it’s increasingly explored for anxiety and depression, too.

Here’s what EMDR involves, what the research shows, and what a session is like, so you have a clearer picture of whether it might make sense for you.

EMDR stands for eye movement desensitization and reprocessing. It’s a structured therapy that helps the brain reprocess distressing memories so they carry less emotional weight. Psychologist Francine Shapiro, PhD, developed it in the late 1980s after she noticed that certain eye movements seemed to ease her own troubling thoughts.

EMDR rests on Shapiro’s adaptive information processing model. The idea is that your brain normally files an experience away, so you can recall it later without feeling like you’re living it again. But a frightening or painful event can interrupt that process, leaving the memory raw and unfiled, still carrying the fear and detail of that moment. That’s why it can resurface later, as vivid as the day it happened.

According to this model, “processing” a memory means helping your brain finish the filing. You don’t forget what happened, and the facts don’t change. What changes is the emotional charge it carries, so recalling it feels less like reliving it and more like remembering it. To help that happen, EMDR uses a technique called “bilateral stimulation.”

EMDR uses a technique called bilateral stimulation. While you hold a difficult memory in mind, a trained therapist guides you through side-to-side stimulation.

Bilateral stimulation can take a few forms:

eye movements that follow your therapist’s fingers

gentle taps that switch between your hands or knees

tones that play in one ear, then the other

There’s a theory behind the side-to-side part, though researchers are still working out exactly why it helps. The leading explanation is about mental bandwidth. Your working memory, the part of your mind that holds what you’re focused on right now, can only handle so much at once. When you track side-to-side movement while recalling a hard memory, the two tasks compete for that limited space. The hard memory tends to feel a little less vivid, and with repetition it can lose its sharp edges, so it feels less overwhelming to hold.

EMDR has eight phases, though they tend to blend together in practice. The early phases cover your history and teach you coping skills, so you feel prepared before any hard memories arise. In the middle phases, you focus on a specific memory while following bilateral stimulation, which is meant to help your brain reprocess it. The final phases check that the memory feels less distressing and help you close out the work safely.

The evidence is strong for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), a condition that can develop after a frightening or life-threatening event. A Cochrane review (a gold-standard summary of research on a topic) found that EMDR and trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) both reduce PTSD symptoms. They also outperformed other therapies when researchers checked in after treatment ended. A 2024 review in the Journal of Traumatic Stress identified more than 30 randomized controlled trials supporting EMDR for PTSD in adults and children. Major health organizations, including the World Health Organization, recommend EMDR for PTSD.

However, some guidelines, including those from the American Psychological Association (APA), rate the evidence for EMDR as less certain than for trauma-focused CBT. Researchers also debate exactly why EMDR works.

The picture is more mixed outside PTSD. Early research suggests EMDR may help with anxiety, as one meta-analysis found reduced symptoms of anxiety, panic, and phobia, though the studies were small and varied in quality.

Evidence for depression is emerging as well. Another meta-analysis showed reduced depression symptoms, with the strongest effects in more severe cases. These findings are promising, but they don’t carry the same weight as the PTSD research.

After you and a therapist finish the early phases, you bring a specific memory to mind and notice the thoughts and body sensations that come with it. Then your therapist guides you through the bilateral stimulation while you let your mind go where it goes. You can ask to pause or stop at any time. Unlike many talk therapies, EMDR doesn’t require you to describe the memory in detail.

Sessions usually run 60–90 minutes. Your therapist pauses often to check in on what you noticed. Over several rounds of bilateral stimulation, many people find the memory feels less sharp, even though the facts don’t change. Between sessions, a mindfulness practice, such as slow breathing or a body scan, can help you ease feelings that surface.

EMDR differs from talk therapy because it doesn’t require you to describe your experience in detail. You’ll still talk with your therapist, especially early on, but the reprocessing happens while you hold a memory in mind and follow bilateral stimulation. Talk therapies like cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) focus more on examining and reshaping your thoughts out loud.

The main benefit of EMDR is that it can ease the distress tied to painful memories. For many people with PTSD, that can mean fewer flashbacks and less of that constant, on-alert feeling.

Much of the debate is about why EMDR works, not whether it does. The evidence is strongest for PTSD, with early and more mixed findings for anxiety and depression.

Some studies suggest the eye movements themselves add little. The benefit may instead come from giving your mind a second task while you recall a memory, which can make it feel less vivid and emotionally intense. Other tasks that split your attention may have the same effect.

EMDR is generally safe when a trained therapist delivers it, though you may experience some short-term effects. You might feel more emotional or tired between sessions, or have vivid dreams as your brain keeps processing the memory.

These reactions usually pass within a day or two. If they last longer or feel overwhelming, it’s a good idea to tell your therapist, who can adjust the pace with you. Serious side effects are uncommon when a therapist follows the full eight-phase process.

The number of sessions you need depends on the memory you’re addressing and how long it has affected you. Some people feel relief after a few sessions, while longer-standing experiences often take more time. A common range is 6–12 sessions, but your timeline may be shorter or longer. Your therapist can give you a better sense of what to expect once they know your history.
