59 Years Later, Peanuts' Best Quote On Happiness Remains Its Greatest Line Not From Charles Schulz

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Across nearly 50 years and 17,897 daily comic strips between 1950 and 2000, Charles M. Schulz gave Peanuts fans no shortage of memorable lines. Some captured the awkwardness and anxiety of growing up, while others delivered the kind of simple wisdom that sticks with readers long after the punchline. Schulz was famously hands-on with the strip, writing, drawing, and lettering every panel himself. So it's easy to assume that every famous Peanuts quote came directly from him.

When it comes to happiness, Schulz certainly left his mark. His April 1960 comic strip gave readers the now-famous line, "Happiness is a warm puppy," which later became the title of his bestselling 1962 picture book. It's one of the most recognisable quotes in the franchise, but it isn't the only line worth remembering. In fact, Peanuts' best take on happiness came from someone other than Schulz.

That line appears at the very end of You're a Good Man, Charlie Brown, the stage musical that premiered Off-Broadway on March 7, 1967, at Theatre 80 St. Marks in New York City. With Charlie Brown, Snoopy, and the rest of the gang brought to life on stage, the production captured much of the charm and dry humor that made the newspaper strips so popular. It went on to run for 1,597 performances, but its greatest contribution might just be its final line. Nearly 59 years later, that simple thought about happiness still feels right at home alongside Schulz's best writing.

Charles M. Schulz may have created Peanuts, but its best line about happiness came from songwriter Clark Gesner. Gesner wrote the book, music, and lyrics for You're a Good Man, Charlie Brown, though the musical wasn't exactly what he had in mind when he started. Back in the early 1960s, he was simply writing songs about his favourite newspaper comic strip.

He eventually sent a demo tape to Schulz, who liked the songs enough to give Gesner his personal blessing to release them as a 1966 concept album. That record opened the door to the 1967 Off-Broadway musical, with Gary Burghoff (yes, the future Radar from MASH*) playing Charlie Brown, Bob Balaban as Linus, and Bill Hinnant as Snoopy.

The musical's closing number is called "Happiness", and it's pretty much everything that makes Peanuts work packed into one song. Charlie Brown and his friends take turns singing about the little things that make them happy: learning to whistle, finally tying a shoe, having five different crayons, sharing a sandwich, or catching a firefly just to let it go. There's no grand adventure or big life lesson here. It's just the gang enjoying the sort of small victories that Charlie Brown's world is full of. And then Gesner saves the best line for last:

"For happiness is anyone and anything at all, that's loved by you."

That's the line that gets to the heart of Peanuts. Schulz's "Happiness is a warm puppy" is wonderful, and honestly, it's hard to argue with Snoopy-sized doses of puppy love. But Gesner's lyric takes that same idea and gives it a little more room. Happiness can be a pet, a friend, a favourite crayon, or someone who makes even a miserable day feel a bit better.

Anyone who has spent time with Charlie Brown knows that his life isn't exactly short on disappointments. His baseball team keeps losing, his kites keep meeting trees, and Lucy is always ready to pull the football away at the worst possible moment. Yet Peanuts never feels completely miserable.

There's Snoopy on his doghouse, Linus with his blanket, and a group of kids who always find reasons to spend time together. That's the magic of the strip: happiness isn't about winning every game. Sometimes, it's just about having someone to share the day with.

A truly unforgettable sendoff to a legendary comic strip still hits home 26 years later with the final Peanuts Sunday strip.

That's also why You're a Good Man, Charlie Brown gets the characters so right. Snoopy turns dinner into a Broadway showstopper in "Suppertime", Linus celebrates his blanket in "My Blanket and Me", and Sally steals the show with "My New Philosophy", added for the 1999 Broadway revival. The musical understands what made Peanuts special without changing what fans loved about it. And perhaps that's why Gesner's final line still feels so right nearly six decades later.

Divers Spent 7 Years Hunting a Ghost Ship Linked to a Nazi Spy Plot. They Just Found It.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A citizen-led search found the SS Cornwallis—and revived a forgotten Nazi spy story in Maine.

For seven years, a group of volunteer divers scoured historical records and the coast of Maine in search of one of New England’s largest undiscovered World War II shipwrecks. Now the SS Cornwallis has been found. The Nomad Exploration Team located the Canadian merchant vessel, which a German U-boat sank off Mount Desert Rock in 1944. The discovery also helps tell the story of the 43 people aboard the boat who lost their lives in the attack and revives interest in the German spies linked to the sinking.

The team used side-scan sonar to map the ocean floor where they believed the Cornwallis might rest, and they nearly overlooked the 400-foot, 5,458-ton vessel. Some of the group’s most harrowing dives later confirmed that the sonar anomaly really was the lost ship. “It was exhilaration,” said Danny Allan, a founding team member, according to Seacoastonline.

The nine-member, New Hampshire-based team began the search in 2019 with a rough location about 10 miles southwest of Mount Desert Rock. By drawing on historical records, the ship’s blueprints, and local fishermen’s observations, they narrowed the search to three likely sites. Even so, the work wasn’t going to be easy.

“There are big tides up off the Mount Desert area,” team member Jeff Goodreau told the Portland Press Herald. “A lot of weather, a lot of fog … Any way you look at it, the challenge is just getting there, whether it’s a five-hour run from Portsmouth or trailering a boat up there.”

In fact, the team nearly missed the wreck twice. On a first pass within 300 feet of it, they didn’t recognize the sonar image. Later, while they were reprogramming the GPS, they almost skipped past it again when it showed up just 100 feet away. “We would’ve written off that whole section as ‘we already looked there’ and wouldn’t have found it,” Allan said.

Reaching the wreck, nearly 400 feet down, was just as tricky. Team member Don Ferrara called it the scariest dive he’d ever done, and Goodreau described the site as deep, cold, and full of current. Still, “We were over the moon,” Ferrara said of the find.

“You’re the first person to see and touch this wreck literally in 80 years, so you’re ecstatic,” Allan said. “But it’s also extremely sobering because this is a grave. 43 souls lost their lives on this wreck.”

The tragedy happened in the Gulf of Maine as the Canadian ship was heading home to Saint John, New Brunswick, after loading sugar and molasses in Barbados. At 6 a.m. on December 3, 1944, the German submarine U-1230 fired a single torpedo that struck the forward cargo hold, and the Cornwallis sank in less than 10 minutes, according to the team. Only five of the 48 people aboard, most of them Canadian, survived. Fishing boats picked up the survivors, and a U.S. Navy vessel then carried them to Rockland, Maine.

The Cornwallis wasn’t a target the U-boat had been hunting. “The U-boat wasn’t looking for them,” Ferrara said. “They were just there.”

The sinking alerted the U.S. to the U-boat’s presence and prompted an investigation into its mission. By interviewing witnesses in Hancock, the FBI learned about Germany’s Operation Elster (Operation Magpie in English). Just days before the attack, the same submarine had landed two German spies, Erich Gimpel and William Colepaugh, near Hancock Point. Their mission was to gather intelligence in the United States. The pair took a taxi to Bangor, a train to Portland, and then made their way to New York City. In the end, the plot fell apart from within: Colepaugh lost his nerve, confided in a friend, and turned himself in, which led to both men’s capture.

This wasn’t the first time the Cornwallis had been torpedoed. The ship was built in Vancouver, British Columbia, in 1921. In 1942, a torpedo struck it while it was anchored at Barbados, and it partially sank in shallow water. After repairs, it returned to service.

“For the team, finding the wreck was the culmination of years of persistence,” Allan said in a statement, according to MassLive. “For the families and communities connected to the ship, it brings renewed attention to a largely forgotten tragedy.”

The Cornwallis is the latest find for the team, which had already located the USS Eagle 56 and the SS William H. Machen. “I love history,” Allan said. “My father was a history teacher. Other people like to see pretty fish and reefs. For me, I love to go see wrecks.”

The border wall dispute isn’t fit for the emergency docket

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

In its latest request to the Supreme Court over President Donald Trump’s approach to immigration, the Justice Department turned to the court’s emergency docket to ask for fast-track permission to construct a border barrier system along a 517-mile stretch in remote west Texas. Stymied by a federal district court’s preliminary injunction issued in October, the Justice Department argues that Supreme Court intervention is needed to prevent irreparable harm to the country. But details of the government’s legal argument reveal that the Trump administration hopes to use the emergency docket as a fast track to victory regardless of the actual urgency of the legal dispute.

Early this year, the Department of Homeland Security began taking important preliminary steps to build physical barriers through Big Bend National Park, Big Bend Ranch State Park, and nearby property along the U.S.-Mexico border. Part of the Border Patrol’s Big Bend Sector, covering approximately one-quarter of the entire border, no artificial barriers exist there. Despite its massive geographic reach, the region is sparsely populated and at immense distance from large urban centers. Unlike other parts of the U.S.-Mexico border, few people or goods cross from one country to the other in the Big Bend Sector. Indeed, according to U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone, who issued a preliminary injunction blocking DHS from carrying out its plans to erect a border barrier in the area, this is the border region with “the lowest aggregate number of apprehensions [of migrants] and the lowest number of apprehensions per mile of border.”

In Mullin v. Friends of the Ruidosa Church, a group of individuals with personal or business ties to the region, along with two advocacy groups, argued that DHS had failed to comply with required environmental reviews. Pointing to a 1996 law signed by President Bill Clinton, the Justice Department responded that Secretary of Homeland Security Markwayne Mullin and his predecessor, Kristi Noem, waived application of those environmental laws. The government also argued that the district court lacked the power to decide the lawsuit in the first place.

Cardone disagreed with the government on both counts. Congress narrowed courts’ jurisdiction in border-barrier disputes, but left room for courts to consider constitutional challenges to DHS’s conduct, she concluded. The challenge to Mullin’s waiver of environmental laws raises a constitutional question, Cardone added.

Having concluded that she could consider the challengers’ lawsuit, Cardone then turned to the criteria that Congress imposed on the secretary of Homeland Security to waive environmental review. The 1996 law directs the immigration agency “to waive all legal requirements” that could slow or impede construction of border barriers and roads. But the same law also instructs DHS to build barriers and roads “in areas of high illegal entry into the United States.” Considering the government’s data about apprehensions of people and seizures of drugs in the Big Bend Sector, as compared to other parts of the U.S.-Mexico border, Cardone concluded that the region cannot be considered to have high levels of illegal entry. “[I]f the Big Bend Sector is an area of high illegal entry, so is every other sector,” Cardone announced.

Seven days after Cardone released her opinion, the U.S. solicitor general asked the Supreme Court to intervene. Much of the government’s argument turns on its disagreement with Cardone about what constitutes an area of “high illegal entry.” Mullin determined that the Big Bend Sector constitutes an area of high illegal entry because Customs and Border Protection, the DHS agency that enforces immigration laws at and near the border, apprehended 89,000 people and “seized enormous amounts” of illicit drugs from fiscal years 2021 to 2025, the government explains. In addition, failure to build a border barrier in this region “would act as a dangerous magnet” for additional illegal entries, the solicitor general claims. Despite Mullin’s assessment that the facts on the ground met the “high illegal entry” threshold required by Congress, “the district court nonetheless issued an unprecedented injunction blocking those waivers and halting associated border-wall construction,” the solicitor general wrote to the court.

Unsurprisingly, the challengers disagree. Opposing the government’s request for relief from the justices, they describe Mullin and Noem’s waivers as “unprecedented” and claim they “carry undisputed and extraordinary political and economic significance.”

The solicitor general’s claim is fundamentally a dispute about the proper interpretation of the text of the 1996 law. The government argues that courts cannot second-guess Mullin’s decision to waive environmental laws, and that Mullin’s view of what constitutes “high illegal entry” is all that matters. Cardone disagrees and contends that courts have a role to play here.

By turning to the emergency docket, the Justice Department has dispensed with the ordinary appellate process in favor of a fast-track path to, it hopes, final victory. Wanting an answer sooner rather than later is understandable, but a party’s interest in “line-jumping,” as Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson has described the emergency docket, isn’t reason enough to circumvent the slower course of the ordinary appellate process. Jackson would prefer to limit the emergency docket to situations in which an actual emergency exists. It’s hard to see what exigency exists in west Texas, however. The solicitor general argues that drugs and people will continue to enter through the Big Bend Sector. That is undoubtedly true, but, as that stretch of land has been unfortified since it became a border on March 18, 1848, nothing is likely to occur in the near future that is markedly different from past decades.

The government’s request for fast-track intervention by the Supreme Court also does not meet the standards set by Jackson’s ideological opposites. Explaining his view of the court’s emergency docket in Trump v. CASA, the court’s fast-track decision announcing that district courts are not authorized to issue universal injunctions, Justice Brett Kavanaugh wrote that its alternative pathway is appropriate when “Congress passes a major new statute, or the Executive Branch issues a major new rule or executive order.” Neither applies to the border-wall dispute. Congress enacted the contested statute 30 years ago and the waivers issued by Mullin and Noem are neither an agency rule nor an executive order.

Nor is the border-wall legal battle the type of dispute that only the Supreme Court can resolve. In a 2021 speech, Justice Samuel Alito compared the court’s emergency docket to a hospital’s emergency room, suggesting that, in his view at least, the fast-track process is proper only when no suitable alternative exists. But an alternative does exist to decide if Cardone overstepped. The government could have appealed Cardone’s decision to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit. Like other circuit courts, the 5th Circuit regularly reviews preliminary injunctions granted by district courts. As Kavanaugh explained in CASA, “The courts of appeals and this Court can (and regularly do) expeditiously review district court decisions awarding or denying preliminary injunctive relief.” Instead of asking the 5th Circuit to correct the mistake it claims Cardone committed, the solicitor general skipped the 5th Circuit and went directly to the Supreme Court.

The reason it did this is not difficult to figure out: The administration is hopeful that it will receive a friendly audience at the Supreme Court. But this is a fast-track procedure meant for exceptional circumstances. And there is nothing exceptional about the legal fight over the administration’s attempt to build new segments of border barrier in rural west Texas. If the court grants the government’s request to pause Cardone’s preliminary injunction, allowing construction to move forward where no barrier currently exists, it should, at the very least, explain how this case meets the criteria that the justices have previously provided and if the term “emergency docket” is a misnomer.

How Syrup Became a Coffee-Shop Culture War

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For some coffee shop customers, premade syrups are now a major red flag. It presents a new opportunity for small business owners.

In a TikTok video with over 82,000 likes, the person behind the camera zooms into the shelves at a Pennsylvania coffee shop, three rows stacked with an array of Torani syrups in flavors like blueberry, brown-sugar cinnamon, English toffee, and salted caramel. “I just know my latte isn’t going to be good,” the caption reads. Across social media, similar sentiments appear. “If I see these at a cafe, I’m walking out immediately,” says one tweet, co-signed by many commenters. In a multipart video series, another creator documents her journey of replacing all of her Torani syrups with ones from small businesses instead. Last year, the niche food newsletter Unsnackable published what was described as a “Torani tirade.”

It’s not just a Torani problem, though the brand — by virtue of its market dominance — tends to get the most flack on social media. While some people seem to prefer competitor Monin, it, too, draws backlash over allegations of tasting “like chemicals.” The problem, for some drinkers of dessert-like specialty beverages, is premade syrups across the board.

For a certain sect of consumers, they’ve become a coffee shop red flag, a sign that a so-called “specialty” establishment asking them to fork over $8 plus tip for a latte is unfairly cutting corners when it comes to quality or craft. For these consumers, mass-market syrups now symbolize exactly that: They’re synonymous with coffee chains and corporations, the kind of impersonal institutions that many people are trying to get away from when they patronize a small business. The flip side: For independently owned coffee shops across the country, house-made syrup now represents a major opportunity.

When Carrie and Dennis Medina first envisioned their coffee shop, Room Service, in Ventura, California, they thought they would take an approach similar to the one at Sightglass Coffee, where they’d both gotten their start. “Very purist, with no syrups, no specialty drinks,” Dennis says. They imagined eight kinds of pour-over coffee and an emphasis on single-origin beans, since the couple also runs a roastery. “We said we’re never gonna do cream tops; we’re never gonna do these crazy drinks,” Carrie says. But in the first week alone, every customer was asking for them.

Now, Room Service offers 10 to 12 different syrups at any given time (and six different cream tops), all of them made in-house. It offers drinks such as a cinnamon roll latte topped with a cinnamon roll, and a honey cornbread latte with an ube cream top. “I hear that question all the time,” Dennis says. “‘Hey, do you guys make your syrups in-house?’” To him, a shop that isn’t trying to do at least the basics — like vanilla — in-house is “falling behind what the times are pushing for.”

The demand for syrups and other embellishments is so intense that the Medinas have brought on a full-time prep person who works exclusively on “all these things that we do in-house that make us special,” Carrie says. Room Service’s PSL, for example, starts with kabocha squash. It’s roasted, blended with spices such as cloves and cinnamon, and cooked into syrup, a process that takes two days. Echoing a sentiment that seems common among coffee shop owners right now, Carrie describes their current approach as “not necessarily the coffee shop we wanted, but it’s more like the coffee shop we have to be.” And if the market demands syrups and cream tops, then the Medinas wanted to ensure that the intention and emphasis on quality that they applied to their coffee beans also carried over.

In 2025, Michael Smith opened Trust Coffee Co. in Bay Village, Ohio, taking over, remodeling, and rebranding an older coffee shop. He, like the Medinas, imagined a more “pour-over, third-wave” style establishment. “But I’ve been a business owner for a long time,” says Smith, who also runs a restaurant and a catering business. “I’ve realized over the years that your customers tell you more about who you are than who you actually think you are.” His customers, it turned out, wanted seasonal syrups in niche flavors like maple rosemary, caramelized banana, and strawberry rhubarb.

The purist is no longer coffee’s north star; the maximalist is.

Syrups like Monin, which the shop’s previous iteration used, “serve a purpose in the industry,” Smith says, but he wanted more control over the menu. The two full-time pastry chefs from Smith’s other businesses oversee the shop’s syrups. “It’s become such a huge thing,” he says: Every time Trust’s social media manager posts a new syrup, the line is out the door for a week. “It sets us apart,” he explains.

When Sabrina Kaylor opened the first location of Bizarre Coffee in Canton, Georgia in 2020, the shop used bottled syrups. She hadn’t worked in coffee prior, and she said that approach felt like the norm at the time. “It allowed us to hone our craft and learn what we were doing,” she says. Over time, Kaylor — who’s quite active on the company’s official social media platforms — noticed that the tenor of the conversation was changing: In the immediate post-COVID years, people were making more things at home and there was more pride to be gained in “scratch-made.” With her business becoming known for lattes in flavors such as blueberry, banana, and guava, Kaylor decided to shift toward more house-made syrups. Previously, her customers didn’t ask about their sourcing, but now, they ask all the time.

As much as there is a sense of valor in the entirely from-scratch approach, Kaylor retains some skepticism about the current torrent of Torani and Monin backlash. Today, 90 percent of Bizarre’s drinks use house-made syrups, though one of its best-selling drinks still relies on the bottled stuff; Bizarre’s menu is extensive and making everything in-house would be a significant investment of labor. On the part of coffee shops, “I think a lot of [the house-made syrup conversation] is marketing, quite honestly,” Kaylor says. As for the bandwagoning that is all the anti-Torani memes, “I think it’s part of consumer behavior,” she says. And that, she acknowledges, can be really “discouraging” for small business owners.

In a sense, that’s what the premade-versus-house-made-syrup conversation boils down to: the stand between small businesses and big ones. For more consumers, the economic and social conditions of the current moment have inspired increased skepticism toward the influence of corporations and private equity in the food and beverage world.

The Minnesota coffee shop Little Joy has become famous across social media for posting the recipes of its signature drinks in detailed DIY videos. It has since launched a membership program through which other coffee shop owners can purchase its recipes, scaled up for commercial usage; gain access to training videos; and sell its drinks without even needing to attribute them to Little Joy, though the ones that would like to can pin their use of Little Joy’s recipes to a Drink Finder map.

Yes, these might just be recipes for syrups and foams, but in an interview with Eater’s industry newsletter Pre Shift earlier this year, Little Joy owner Cody Larson described the system as a way to build solidarity among independent businesses. “The big coffee chains, they’re never gonna do house-made syrups, so I think that’s where independent shops can get a leg up,” he said. “Ultimately, the chains are our competition... If independent shops can, in any way possible, raise the bar across the board, that’s good for all independent shops, including us.”

It’s hard to look at the drinks coming out of specialty coffee shops today — fluffy with foam, flavored to emulate nuanced desserts like princess cake and tiramisu— and not think of the Starbucks Frappuccino. The combination of app-ordering and social media “hack” culture have mainstreamed super-elaborate beverage requests, allowing Starbucks and coffee chains like it to become purveyors of highly complicated customizable beverages, mainstreaming the mindset of the aforementioned “hyperflavor.”

When it comes to why consumers have moved away from the single-origin pour-over approach at shops like his, “my guess is that these are a lot of people who used to be Starbucks loyalists and they’re looking for something that’s more local,” Smith says. “It’s a trend across everything where people want local, and we’re able to provide what Starbucks did, but in a more high-quality, more local way.”

Intentionality is what people are gravitating towards, especially as prices rise. Taika Waititi’s Delusional ‘Klara and the Sun’ Instagram Post Exemplifies the Dangers of a Growing Divide Between Critics and Artists in the Digital Age

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I’ve seen enough: Taika Waititi is no longer riffing on a level that is frickin’ sublime. A quick update for those of you who don’t get your news from DiscussingFilm and/or obsessively listen to “Who? Weekly” in order to keep tabs on Rita Ora: On Saturday afternoon, writer-director Taika Waititi opened Instagram and posted — to his grid! — a less than equanimous reaction to the reviews of his new motion picture “Klara and the Sun,” which was in the process of making less money per screen during its second weekend than the average movie theater employee. It wasn’t the most embarrassing self-own of the pre-midterms weekend (that honor belongs to Michigan congresswoman Hayley Stevens, who committed political suicide on live TV with Anderson Cooper), but I’d argue that Waititi’s faux pas has more to say about how the reality distortion field of today’s media landscape has made it all too easy for highly visible people to misperceive the world around them in a crisis. Related Stories Before He Played Sylvester Stallone in ‘I Play Rocky,’ Star Anthony Ippolito Fought Hard for His Own Big Break ‘Okinawa Saudade’ Review: A Moving Documentary About the Okinawan-Brazilian Community Explores the History of Displacement

“I haven’t read a review of anything aimed at me or my peers in almost 15 years,” the director wrote under an image celebrating the 53-point mismatch between the film’s audience and critics scores on Rotten Tomatoes. “But I do listen to the people, so this Halloween, let’s spare a thought for those sad lost souls who couldn’t achieve their dreams of becoming artists, and thus became critics. The same people who also hated ‘Jojo Rabbit 😂 🤷🏽‍♂️.’ Irrelevant. Out of touch. Obsolete. In the bin.🗑️😢🫶🏽.”

When I saw that his post had been modified, I assumed that Waititi had deleted the part where he wrote “and another thing: I’m not mad. Please don’t put in the newspaper that I got mad.” In truth, he had simply tweaked the bit where it said that “sad lost souls” like me had settled as critics. Good note! Much as fallen angels plummet from the heavens before landing in the depths of Hell to anguish for all eternity, critics don’t settle for a life of press screenings so much as we pathetically free fall into it, plummeting from the sacred highs of making “Thor: Love & Thunder” to the wretched lows of watching it. It’s that natural eye for editing that explains why “Next Goal Wins” felt like it had been cut with a soccer cleat.

Of course, I don’t take any of this too personally. (Don’t you dare fucking put in the newspaper that I got mad.) For one thing, I was actually quite tolerant of “Thor: Love & Thunder,” if only because I believe that casting Christian Bale as a guy named “Gorr the God Butcher” in and of itself makes that movie better than most of what Marvel was pumping out at the time. For another thing, I haven’t even seen “Klara and the Sun” — not because I was busy when it screened here, or because I live in one of the many countries where its release has been canceled on account of the movie’s supposed awfulness and verifiable lack of public interest, but rather because I adore the Kazuo Ishiguro novel on which it’s based, and I was convinced that hiring the director of “Jojo Rabbit” to adapt such a poignant tale of loss and brokenness would be like hiring Peter Farrelly to remake “The Remains of the Day.” Except worse, because I’ve always felt like the Merchant Ivory version of that book could have used a few gratuitous jokes about Anthony Hopkins’ swollen balls.

The truth is that the perceived quality of Waititi’s film is irrelevant to what I found interesting about his Instagram post, in much the same way as its quality is seemingly irrelevant to everything else as well. And in much the same way as the perceived quality of Waititi’s other films is irrelevant to the hissy fit he decided to throw about this one. Are “the same people who also hated ‘Jojo Rabbit’” in the room with us? Because that Oscar-winning movie is sitting at 81 percent on Rotten Tomatoes (Certified Fresh™!). OK sure, I hated it, but, as we’ve previously established, I am quite literally not “the same people” who hated “Klara and the Sun.”

On the other hand, I would very much consider myself to be among the large cohort of critics who adored Waititi’s earlier features like “Hunt for the Wilderpeople,” “What We Do in the Shadows,” and “Eagle vs. Shark.” I suppose I could look up the aggregator metrics for those movies as well, but there isn’t any need — that most of the people reading this article have even heard of such modest Kiwi treasures is testament enough to the groundswell of critical support they received around the world. Perhaps, if he’d read a review in the last 15 years, Waititi might have been able to put that together for himself.

But that’s OK. As a public-facing person whose work offers considerably more fodder for mockery than praise (both by virtue of its nature, and also by that of the je ne sais quoi I bring to the job), I would never sincerely begrudge an artist for not wanting to read their own press. Especially now. Perhaps it used to be possible for celebrities to engage with such things in good faith (it was certainly easier in the days when pans were only found in newspapers, and personal attacks limited to their gossip columns), but the internet is a machine designed to make you hate yourself even more than you hate everyone else, and even our finest “anti-hate satires” have proven incapable of solving the problem.

For some folks, especially those with much greater profiles than my own, the only viable way to navigate the digital world without being fully consumed by self-loathing is to decide that all of the people who snipe at you from the sidelines aren’t really people at all. They’re more like ubiquitous wraiths — eyes looking at you from behind a painting, shadows that your subconscious might warp into an ominous shape, visions in the bog that almost lured Frodo to his death in “The Two Towers.” To let any of it in is to let all of it in, and there’s no amount of fame, money, or adulation that can fully inoculate sensitive people against the poison that seeps into their skin every time they pick up their phone (take it from me, someone who doesn’t have any of those things).

And so critics have gone from being a healthy part of the artistic ecosystem to something that artists feel like they need to be immunized against. Which isn’t to suggest that the animus between artists and critics is anything new. On the contrary, it’s as time-honored a cinematic tradition as popcorn at the movies or, say, the old trope of talented indie filmmakers who completely lose the plot once they become too successful.

If anything, that animus used to be a lot more personal, if only because it was expected that artists would know critics by name. Vincent Gallo famously called Roger Ebert a “fat pig with the physique of a slave trader” after Ebert panned “The Brown Bunny” at Cannes. Uwe Boll offered to box his biggest haters after he got sick of being a critical punching bag, and he beat the absolute shit out of all five of the people who took him up on it. Pauline Kael got so far under Clint Eastwood’s skin that he paid a psychiatrist to figure out what her problem was (the shrink naturally concluded that Kael just wanted to fuck Eastwood), while her review of “Ryan’s Daughter” caused David Lean such a crisis of confidence that he almost retired from directing altogether. And he made “Lawrence of Arabia”! (Which Kael also hated).

I’m not so bold as to presume that I — or any of my contemporaries — have a pen worthy of Kael’s poison or place in the public sphere, but the contrast is telling all the same. Instead of headline-making beefs that stand the test of time, today’s critics might be the subject of a petulant DM or a horde-summoning quote tweet. A prominent action director used to get drunk and send me crude messages about my wife via Instagram, but I think he’s sober now. Ron Perlman once called me a “cunt” on social media because I shat on some Christmas movie he made about furries, but I’d be shocked if he remembered that. Or really if he remembered “Pottersville” at all. I had a funny back-and-forth with a good-natured David Ayer after I trashed his Netflix debacle “Bright”; his promise to put my review on his fridge suggested a much less formal riff on Ridley Scott’s decision to frame Kael’s “Blade Runner” pan and hang it in his office. These days, film critics typically only get put on blast by name, at scale, when they make a major sociopolitical misstep of some kind, such as by arguing that a cartoon about red pandas somehow limits its scope by spotlighting Toronto’s immigrant community, or by fixating on the sexual possibilities that could be explored as a form of Mrs. Incredible’s superpowers. (Pixar movies are just irresistible rakes for critics to step on, I guess).

Today’s filmmakers and stars are certainly still aware of their alleged shooters (bracing for another “Dune” situation, Timothée Chalamet asked me to be kind to “Marty Supreme,” though I’m generally a fan, and he had nothing to fear in that case), but most publicly shared pushback is aimed at “the critics” writ large, if only so we can be reduced to cynical strawmen who exist in contrast to “the fans.” The marketing campaign for “Gotti” argued that critics “put out the hit” on the New York mob classic, and then asked people who they trusted more: themselves or “a troll behind a keyboard.” Ignoring the fact that this troll was behind his keyboard in a fucking tuxedo because that movie premiered as an unofficial selection at Cannes, the poptimistic notion that “real people love things” has become a go-to gameplan for an era where reality is easier to warp than it is to reckon with. In an ironic example of life imitating art, “Digger” recently adopted a similar approach.

I suspect that the systemic diminishment of arts criticism is both a cause and a symptom of this ever-worsening phenomenon. There was a time in this country when arts criticism was valued as a worthwhile pursuit unto itself, as opposed to a wonky and unreliable component of the promotional apparatus. The likes of Gore Vidal, James Baldwin, and Norman Mailer were regular fixtures on late night TV and panel shows; they were as famous as any of the people they wrote about, and when artists pushed back against their critics, it was seen as a debate between equals.

Because of that standing, the brightest minds of their generation were able to thrive as public intellectuals, and even invited to leverage their success into lucrative Hollywood careers of their own (as Vidal and Mailer both did before the resulting feud between them led to Mailer headbutting Vidal in the green room of “The Dick Cavett Show”). Today, the Vidals, Baldwins, and Mailers of the world would never belittle themselves by reviewing “Klara and the Sun” for $150, only for their work to be subsumed into a content aggregator that reduced it to a number in a larger set of numbers. In lieu of meaningful financial and intellectual compensation, any thinkers of such caliber today might understandably find arts criticism unworthy of their time and/or just share it to their Letterboxd for free.

That brings us to the reason why Waititi’s post was so excruciatingly tone-deaf, even though he’s hardly the first director to try the “Am I so out of touch? No, it’s the children who are wrong!” defense. The hierarchy of power in the discourse universe has grown as wildly lop-sided as the hierarchy of power in every other arena of public life. Even the most high-profile of critics are barely scraping by, while anyone successful enough to make news with a “the critics put out the hit” post is likely to be a millionaire several times over.

You’d have to be completely disconnected from reality not to recognize how gauche and myopic it is to call journalists of any kind “obsolete” for speaking truth to power and/or product, but I suppose that’s a side effect of spending 15 years in an ecosystem designed to protect itself at all costs. It’s an ecosystem supported by influencer screenings, celebrity interview podcasts, and so many other quality-laundering tactics that it feels like a desperate and radical act when a star like Tom Cruise dares to sit down with a real magazine writer. Only in an ecosystem like that could someone sharp and humane enough to direct “Boy” and the funny parts of “Thor: Ragnarok” become convinced that everyone who hated “Klara and the Sun” did so out of resentment for their own shortcomings. Only in an ecosystem like that could someone that sharp make a movie like “Klara and the Sun” at all. (Which is not to specifically denigrate the actual “Klara and the Sun,” which again I have not seen.)

Of course, it’s not Waititi’s fault that he’s a product of his time, and in light of Aaron Sorkin’s considerably more sensible and self-effacing response to the reviews for “The Social Reckoning,” it’s worth considering whether his relative grace has something to do with the fact that he first came to success during an earlier iteration of Hollywood, where perspective was easier to come by. Whatever the case, we’re all trying our best to protect ourselves in the face of a world where imitated humanity is of limitless value, and actual humanity is an Achilles’ heel — a world in which one of the great modern authors might be moved to write a fairy tale-like story that questions the distance that has grown between people as a result of our efforts to create external vessels for our souls.

Reading Waititi’s tactless Instagram post back for what I pray is the last time, I’m reminded of a resonant line from Ishiguro’s novel, a line that unsurprisingly failed to make it into Waititi’s adaptation: “What was becoming clear to me was the extent to which humans, in their wish to escape loneliness, made maneuvers that were very complex and hard to fathom.” Perhaps, in some abstract way, Waititi’s Instagram post captured the essence of “Klara and the Sun” better than his film ever could.
