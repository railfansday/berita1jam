Singapore settles $77.5M tokenized bill sale with wholesale CBDC

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

MAS issued the bills directly on its test network, which it plans to rename SG Digital Ledger and use for settlement beyond the trials.

The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) issued 100 million Singapore dollars ($77.5 million) in tokenized bills on Nov. 6, with banks using wholesale central bank digital currency (CBDC) to pay for them.

Five banks submitted bids totaling more than twice the amount on offer, MAS said Thursday. The four-week bills mature on Dec. 4.

MAS issued the bills directly on the Singapore Dollar Test Network. That gives borrowers an asset they could pledge as collateral for loans on the network, the central bank said.

Singapore began experimenting with blockchain-based bank payments in 2016. In 2025, banks used the test network and MAS-issued digital currency to settle overnight loans.

MAS now plans to turn the Singapore Dollar Test Network, where the bills were issued, into a settlement system for financial institutions, Managing Director Chia Der Jiun said Thursday at the Singapore FinTech Festival. The network will be renamed SG Digital Ledger.

“As the use of tokenized assets and digital money grows, trusted settlement on CBDC will be needed,” Chia said. “It underpins settlement finality and confidence, especially for large value settlement.”

In 2027, MAS plans to issue more tokenized bills, bring more financial institutions into the trials and expand the bills’ use as collateral.

iFixit found the HomeView nearly impossible to repair yourself, but smart displays don't face the same daily risks as iPhones.

Senior Editor / Home Security and Smart Home

Senior Editor / Home Security and Smart Home

For the past few years, Apple was looking almost DIY-friendly. The company had been moving toward smart speakers that you could take apart without damaging and iPads with accessible batteries. But the new HomeView smart display broke apart when iFixit tried to access components in Tuesday’s full analysis.

The good news? I don’t think it really matters, at least not for this particular Apple device. Here’s why.

DIY repairs are a big deal for Apple products, not just because they’re difficult, but because many people believe they have a right to repair their own phones or computers.

Making repairs more accessible also sustains a secondary market of iPhone and Mac repair services that Apple would rather tightly control — or squash entirely. That’s one reason the EU has required Apple to make iPhone batteries replaceable in 2027.

The HomeView moves sharply in the opposite direction, held together by the toughest adhesive iFixit has ever encountered in an Apple product. The glue is so stubborn that the team accidentally snapped a speaker in half during disassembly, and the logic board was bound too dangerously tight to the aluminum chassis to remove.

“Obviously, when you buy a product you don’t anticipate the accidents, and there’s no way of knowing when wear and tear will become a problem,” iFixit said in its report. “But you can be sure of one thing: The HomeView will not be an easy or pleasant repair when that time comes.”

A representative from Apple did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

iFixit also pointed out that if a HomeView device falls out of warranty, the repair typically costs as much as the $299 smart display itself.

I’m just not sure any potential HomeView users need to worry about the device’s repairability the way they would about an iPhone or Mac.

Smart displays don’t have the same DIY repair needs as the phone in your pocket. There’s no battery to replace, which is one of the most common reasons to open up an iPhone or MacBook. And while a shattered iPhone screen can easily be swapped out for a third-party replacement, no aftermarket alternatives exist for the HomeView’s custom 7-inch display. Since you can only get a replacement screen through Apple anyway, making the device easier to open wouldn’t actually help you fix it yourself.

Plus, smart displays don’t face the same environmental risks as phones and laptops, which are constantly on the move, encounter dust in pockets, accidentally dropped and exposed to potential heat and rain. In the HomePod subreddit, I couldn’t find any reports of speaker failures except from people already Frankensteining their Apple speakers with third-party components.

I wouldn’t let the fear of repair difficulties get in the way of buying a HomeView. A bigger concern is the HomeView’s compact 7-inch screen, which feels cramped for watching video, especially compared with the larger displays Amazon has rolled out across its Echo Show lineup.
