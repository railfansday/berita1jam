That suggests both are bargains as November draws to a close. Here's a closer look at why you should consider putting $1,000 into each one of these bargain stocks.

Novo Nordisk is a pharmaceutical company that is leaning into its Wegovy/Ozempic GLP-1 weight-loss portfolio. Wall Street is worried for two main reasons. First, it fumbled the ball after being the first to market with this new class of drugs. Second, it is shifting its business model to one driven by volume, which has required it to lower prices.

As to the first concern, Novo Nordisk's execution of its GLP-1 shot wasn't great, as it struggled to keep up with demand. It has been much better with its recent pill launch, which has seen dramatically higher demand. Moreover, there's likely room for more than one weight-loss drug given the size of the addressable market, so falling into second place in the GLP-1 space isnt the end of the world. Meanwhile, lowering prices has, indeed, hurt revenues and earnings. But the company upped its full-year 2026 guidance when it reported third-quarter 2026 earnings. That suggests that the volume-led transition is going well.

Novo Nordisk's 4.4% yield is historically high and well above the pharmaceutical industry average of 1.3%. Its roughly 10x price-to-earnings ratio is also well below the industry average of 27x. It looks like a bargain for more aggressive income investors, and a $1,000 investment will net you roughly 24 shares.

PepsiCo is one of the world's largest consumer staples companies. It is an industry leader in the snack and beverage areas, and it has a sizable packaged food business as well. It is more diversified than most of its peers, and it can stand toe-to-toe with any of them as a business, with strong brands, marketing, innovation, and distribution.

The problem is that PepsiCo's business isn't firing on all cylinders right now, with its organic sales growth notably lagging that of key peer Coca-Cola (KO -0.03%). Still, PepsiCo's 3.1% organic sales growth in the third quarter wasn't bad, even though it was less than half of Coca-Cola's 7%. But investors remain worried, thanks notably to weakness in PepsiCo's large U.S. operations.

That's fair, but PepsiCo is a Dividend King, with over 50 annual dividend increases. It has been through difficult times before and survived. And it is making the types of decisions you would expect during the current difficult period, including acquiring desirable brands and introducing innovative new products. It is highly likely that the company will get through this and thrive again over the long term.

If you buy it now, however, you get to collect the historically high 4.1% yield. And you are buying it while its 18x P/E is notably below its 25x five-year average. For reference, the average consumer staples stock yields 2% and has a P/E of around 26x. A $1k investment will buy roughly seven PepsiCo shares.

To be fair, neither Novo Nordisk nor PepsiCo is about to suddenly break out of their funk. So you have to go in with a long-term mindset. However, you'll get to collect a well above-market income stream while you wait for these historically well-run businesses to get back on track. And given their recent performance and historical results, it seems highly likely that they will eventually do just that.

A Very Serious Story About Falling Asleep While Belaying

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The East Face of Mount Temple, Canada, and the scene of this story's shenanigans. (Photo: Mitra Samavaki / Getty)

Big falls are commonplace while sport climbing and bouldering, but, in my opinion, the high alpine remains largely a safe space to take your brake hand off the rope. Why? With loose rock, aerated ice, and an often minimalist rack, falling off—even with a tight belay—usually spells disaster. A climber has every reason to follow the old “the leader cannot fall” ethos.

This old school state of mind deeply resonated with me earlier this month on a difficult alpine climb on Mount Temple in the Canadian Rockies. This 1,400-meter ice and mixed route began easily enough, with a hike up a hard-packed snow slope at 6 a.m. toward the deep cleft that marked the start. Neither my partner Rory nor I were burdened with rope management during the first hour of climbing, since our rope stayed neatly coiled in a pack.

Hundreds of meters higher, below one of the crux pitches, we flaked out our rope. Above us stood a terribly skinny pillar of ice no wider than my shoulders at its base. Blue light filtered through its entirety, a sign of its youth and untrustworthiness.

“I don’t think I should place a screw in that,” Rory decided.

I scanned the shattered quartzite behind the pillar, hoping to at least keep us attached to the mountain before Rory essentially soloed the pitch. Two nervous pitons and a shallow micro nut were all I could cobble together after 10 minutes of searching. I stomped out a waist-deep hole in the snow on the back side of the pillar and clipped into our wobbly anchor. My now four-piece anchor—plus Rory’s unshakeable climbing ability—gave us great odds of surviving his lead.

The runout pitch gave me ample opportunity to pay out slack as I pleased while munching on trail mix and rehydrating from our busy morning. After all, Rory was not allowed to fall, so he didn’t.

Falling has been a frowned-upon practice for most of climbing’s history, especially before sport climbing became popular in the 1980s. Only relatively recently, and only in some styles of climbing, have leader falls become no longer taboo—even acceptable and encouraged.

The crusty old-timer in me knows that, in any form of free climbing, it is always safer to climb to the top of a pitch without testing your equipment, be it a skinny rope, crashpad, or ice screw. This gut instinct evolved from a time when climbing gear was not completely trustworthy. Maybe you hand-stitched your quickdraw’s webbing yourself. Maybe you’d sourced your climbing protection from a hardware store. Or maybe you had tied your lead line around your waist because drop-seat harnesses hadn’t been invented yet. In this era—really only a few decades ago—your climbing ability was the first line of defense. Ropes and harnesses and removable protection served merely as dubious back-ups.

But the advent of regular whippers is a tragedy for one more reason: With newfangled belay devices, hang dogging, and constant pleas to “watch me!” belaying has become a real pain in the ass.

Nearly 12 hours later, Rory and I were still on Mount Temple. We’d topped out the ice climbing portion of the face and traversed across open snow slopes and through rock bands to a final serrated crux: the Black Towers. Countless turrets of dark limestone slice across the summit ridge of Mount Temple, creating difficult route finding even on a clear summer’s day. We were approaching midnight in November and a thick fog had choked the upper mountain. Visibility hovered around 10 meters. I had no idea where we were supposed to go.

Thankfully it was Rory’s lead again, and he was questing. Fifteen minutes ticked by as he wandered up a steep rocky groove, then another 15 of the rope unmoving. An hour passed. I clicked off my headlamp to save battery. More time passed. I figured I could tie off the rope and catch a few Zs, so I did. Sleep felt amazing. I was glad that even as we approached 24 hours on the move, Rory still knew the rules: The leader cannot fall. And the belayer gets to sleep.

I woke up to the sound of Rory’s voice. He’d been blocked by an overhang and was downclimbing. Thick, icy fog obscured our vision as I took the rack and traversed along a snow ledge looking for easier passage. The fog would sometimes lift for an instant—I’d spot a lonely star above—and I’d frantically flick my headlamp to full strength to catch a glimpse of the mountain. But before my eyes could adjust to the new sight, the fog would crash back in like a breaking wave, drowning us in uncertainty once again.

This story does not end in a climbing success. We did not ultimately find our way through the Black Towers, nor did we share a weary, satisfied hug on the summit of Mount Temple. We became lost and a little delirious on a large mountain in the middle of the night and needed to go down.

We stayed roped together as we retraced our steps through deep snow on the upper mountain. I was a little worried about triggering an avalanche, but mainly I was just exhausted. If I stumbled, I would prefer to slide 30 meters before being caught by the rope than well over a thousand into the next life.

Rory and I regrouped at an anchor I’d built in an outcrop of yellow rock. He led out across another snowfield which terminated at a gigantic notch in an otherwise sheer ridgeline. The notch marked the start of a classic ski descent, the 60-degree Aemmer Couloir, and our escape hatch. Rory trudged toward it and I paid out rope, nodding off again.

Suddenly, I was awake. I had not pooped all day, yet the kilogram of trail mix and sausage and cheese I’d downed was making a basement appearance.

“Rory! Do you need a real belay for this bit? I really need to poo,” I yelled into the fog.

I let the rope feed through the anchor, then unclipped it from my belay device and, without moving even a step away from our anchor, unloaded into the snow bank.

It would have been a disgusting mess had Rory fallen and weighted the anchor, dragging me into my own steaming waste. But he didn’t, and I made it back to civilization with both my dignity and my life.

Controversial Senate Bill Would Speed Up Development on Public Lands

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On November 19, the United State Senate passed a sweeping 400-page bill with big implications for America’s federally managed public lands. Called the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act—or BAJAA for short—the bill would ease permitting restrictions for all sorts of public-land development projects, from mining and drilling to large-scale solar farms and geothermal power projects. Supporters say it would open the door for more clean-energy projects on public lands while reducing utility costs for American consumers.

Co-sponsored by Sen. Martin Heinrich (D-NM) and Sen. Mike Lee (R-UT), BAJAA has drawn widespread criticism from conservationists, environmentalists, and public land advocates, with groups from the Wilderness Society to the National Wildlife Federation speaking out in opposition. According to the National Council of American Indians, it has also caused serious concerns in tribal nations across the country.

A large portion of the bill aims to reform or streamline the permitting process that developers must adhere to when applying for projects on federal public lands. It would do so by shrinking the scope, timelines, and legal challenges currently available under both the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) and the Clean Water Act (CWA).

In an October blog post, the Theodore Roosevelt Conservation Partnership (TRCP) said “permitting reform rules decide how a pipeline, transmission line, mine or dam gets approved, whether hunters and anglers have any say in where it goes—and what happens to elk winter range, trout streams or migration corridors in their path.”

While TRCP has stopped short of publicly opposing the bill, they did highlight several concerns in the post. For starters, they flagged a provision in the bill—Section 1401—that “would make it much more challenging to fix problems once a project is permitted.”

Titled “Project Certainty,” this section of the BAJAA bill would restrict federal agencies—like the Bureau of Land Management or the US Forest Service—from revoking previously issued permits during future presidential administrations. Critics say this section of the bill could lock in controversial projects—like a proposed copper-sulfide mine on the edge of the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness or the 211-mile Ambler mining road that the Department of the Interior (DOI) is pushing for Alaska’s Brooks Range.

In a separate blog post, Backcountry Hunters & Anglers (BHA) highlighted similar concerns with the “Project Certainty” provision in the BAJAA bill. “For BHA, this raises questions about whether federal land managers would retain sufficient flexibility to respond to changing conditions and protect public resources over the long term,” the BHA post states. “For example, if a mining project near Minnesota’s Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness received federal approval, these provisions could limit a future administration’s ability to revisit that approval in response to newly identified impacts on water quality or fisheries, depending on the circumstances and applicable legal exceptions.”

Aaron Kindle is the Director of Sporting Advocacy at the National Wildlife Federation (NWF). He tells Field & Stream that NWF opposes the BAJAA bill in its current form because it raises too many questions, is moving too fast without adequate public awareness, and it undermines key public-land safeguards.

“This is a hugely consequential bill happening during a lame-duck session with very little public understanding,” Kindle says. “[NWF] is pro-permitting reform. But when we do the analysis on this, and we talk to other conservation friends and partners, it’s just too fraught.”

Kindle says NWF shares both BHA’s and TRCP’s concerns around the bill’s project certainty guarantees for developers. “It shortens review timelines, it narrows what triggers a review, and it kind of locks it in once it’s done,” he says. “We’ve always wanted to be able to do adaptive management on anything that’s done. Anytime you narrow environmental review parameters like this, or make permits too concrete, that’s concerning for a broad swath of our most cherished public lands and waters.”

Kindle says NWF is also worried that the BAJAA bill could expedite data center construction on public lands. Up until now, no data center has been permitted on any federal public lands, though the DOI did attempt to approve one on BLM lands in Nevada back in August, before a federal appeals court revoked the permit.

“What we’ve understood so far is that [BAJAA] shields [data center developers] from future regulations and makes it hard, or maybe even impossible, to revoke the permits once they’ve been awarded,” he adds. “What if we learn at some future point that these things are incredibly harmful to water, air, wildlife, the grid, who knows—and then they’re just locked in place, and there’s nothing we can do about it?”

The Wilderness Society has been one of the most vocal opponents of the BAJAA bill since it first started making headlines in early November. Wilderness Society President, Tracy Stone-Manning, who ran the BLM during the Biden Administration, calls the bill alarming.

“Some of the places that hang in the balance are the Boundary Waters in northern Minnesota and the Brooks Range of Alaska, because of the proposed Ambler Road and Arctic Mine, the entire Alaskan Arctic really, and all of that caribou habitat,” Stone-Manning tells F&S. “You’ve also got places in the Lower 48, like the Roan Plateau in Colorado with its amazing mule deer and elk herds, which sportsmen and women fought so hard to protect, and the headwaters of Montana’s Bitterroot River, where they want to build a rare-earth mine up on Sheep Creek. Whatever the Trump Administration decides to do in these places in the next two years, that gets locked in if this bill passes. We won’t be able to undo it.”

The Wilderness Society supports expanded permitting for renewable energy projects on public lands, which have declined steeply during the first two years of the Trump Administration. But for Stone-Manning, the risks of the BAJAA bill far outweigh the rewards. “The bottom line is, this bill takes away environmental safeguards for habitat and gives developers certainty,” she says. “Those two things coupled together spell disaster for our country’s conservation heritage and for our public-land hunting and fishing traditions.”

Many conservationists who opposes BAJAA are confused by Sen. Martin Heinrich’s staunch support for the bill. Heinrich has been one of the Senate’s most stalwart defenders of public lands for years. He has helped secure Wilderness Area and National Monument status for thousands of acres of public lands in his home state of New Mexico, for example. More recently, he was a vocal and outspoken opponent of an attempt to sell off more than 3.4 million acres of Western public lands in the “One Big Beautiful Bill.”

Today, he’s defending BAJAA in the halls of Congress and in the op-ed pages of major news outlets like the New York Times and the Economist. In a recent interview with Field & Stream, Heinrich cited an urgent need to slow climate change as his primary motivator for helping craft and co-sponsoring the legislation.

“This will allow us to put green energy on the grid at a scale where we might be able to mitigate the worst future outcomes of climate change,” Heinrich says. “I’ve realized that I’m watching the world change at such a pace, that if we don’t get to work on this, I don’t know what kind of world my grandkids are going to inherit. The places that I fish in New Mexico, for example, are changing. We don’t have a lot of water. If these places go away, and we don’t have snowpack, we’re done. It’s game over.”

Heinrich says the bill address climate change by putting public land renewable projects—like wind, solar, and geothermal—on equal footing with oil, gas, and mining. “We want to bring renewables into the same regulatory permitting regime that the fossil fuel projects already have,” he says. “Right now, oil and gas in particular, and natural gas pipelines, have a sort of privileged position.”

Heinrich disputes claims from his critics that the bill weakens key public-land safeguards like NEPA and the Clean Water Act. And he says it won’t expedite or lock-in mining permits in the Boundary Waters.

“We don’t change or lower standards in this bill; we streamline the process,” he says. “If you’re a mine developer, you still have to get your Clean Water Act permit, and it doesn’t change the standard for that. When you look at the Boundary Waters, what I don’t see is that project being able to clear is a state Clean Water Act permit, because I just don’t think it will be possible to do mining open-air tailings at that site—having seen it with my own eyes—without the acid generated making it into the water next door.”

Heinrich also disagrees with BAJAA opponents who say the bill will open the door for data center development on public lands. “I don’t think that data centers belong on public lands, and I think any effort to build them on public lands is probably a legal stretch of existing authorizations and presidential authorities,” he adds. “This bill just makes data centers pay their fair freight for attaching to the grid, instead of passing that cost on to everyday consumers in the form higher electricity rates.”

The Trump Administration’s current policy around data centers is murky at best. But recent reporting from the Washington Sun revealed that the BLM was reviewing at least 12 data center proposals spread across 17,600 acres of public land in six western states. Wall Street analysts, meanwhile, are lowering their expectations, but not by much, and the gap between the bulls and the market is the crux of the debate.

Cantor Fitzgerald on Wednesday lowered its price target to $96 from $100 and kept its overweight rating. The firm said fears about CoreWeave's business model "continue to be overblown" and told clients to "be thankful for the discount the market has given them." Cantor said the cut reflects a higher expected share count, because raising equity at a lower stock price means issuing more shares.

A day earlier, Evercore ISI cut its target to $80 from $100 and kept its outperform rating. The analyst said the sharp correction in AI stocks has put more focus on pricing and downside risks, and assessed where the sell-off "might have overextended."

From Wednesday's closing price, Cantor's target implies upside of about 125%, and Evercore's implies about 89%.

CoreWeave's lenders are far less relaxed. Its notes due October 2031 fell nearly 2 cents to about 74 cents on the dollar on Wednesday, pushing their yield to 17.91%, Reuters reported, citing Tradeweb data. Its notes due June 2030 fell to a record low of 76.55 cents. The cost of insuring CoreWeave's debt against default has also climbed to levels usually seen at distressed companies.

A yield near 18% is a warning sign. It means lenders are demanding a return closer to what you'd expect from a stock, and bondholders get paid before shareholders. CoreWeave does have a cheaper way to borrow: its banks are preparing to sell a $4.5 billion loan backed by chips it is installing for Meta Platforms (META +3.40%) as soon as next week, Bloomberg reported. Because Meta is the customer, that loan is expected to be rated investment-grade and priced around 7% to 7.5%. But that kind of financing only works for capacity tied to a specific customer contract, not for the company as a whole.

Even Cantor concedes the market is "extremely focused" on one issue: CoreWeave's need for as much as $5 billion of "parent capital" in 2027, meaning money raised at the company level to support its build-out. Cantor thinks management should address the funding plan "sooner rather than later" and said a clear answer could act as a "potential clearing event" for the stock.

That's the crux. If CoreWeave raises capital through new shares at today's prices, existing shareholders are diluted. If it borrows, it pays rates the bond market has just pushed sharply higher. Either route makes it harder to reach the upside in those price targets.

For most investors, not yet. The analysts may well be right that the sell-off overshot, and Cantor says its review of how CoreWeave resigns customer contracts gives it more confidence in the model. But when the company's own bonds yield nearly 18%, the market is pricing in real risk that shareholders would absorb first.

Risk-tolerant investors who believe in long-term AI demand could start a small position, but the smarter move is to wait for the 2027 funding plan Cantor is asking for. If management lays out a credible way to raise that money without heavy dilution, the stock could rerate quickly. Until then, there are less leveraged ways to own the AI build-out among cloud stocks and semiconductor stocks.

More recently, Denver replaced Peyton Manning with Brock Osweiler midway through 2015 even with the club sitting at 7-2. Manning returned in the season finale and then served as a game manager in the playoffs as the Broncos’ dominant defense led the franchise to its third Super Bowl victory.

Manning walked off the field with the Lombardi Trophy in tow in his final game.

While the Steelers are currently in playoff position, their next three games are against AFC West-leading Denver, surging Houston, and AFC South leader Jacksonville. While they can probably withstand one loss or maybe even two, three could be catastrophic.

Enter Rudolph, who rescued Pittsburgh’s season and briefly earned folk hero status in 2023 by coming off the bench in December and authoring a surge to a playoff berth.

That didn’t stop the Steelers from moving on from him immediately, bringing in Russell Wilson and Justin Fields in 2024 and then Rodgers in 2025, a move designed to help Pittsburgh end a postseason victory drought that’s nearing a decade.

Rodgers’ first year ended with a blowout playoff loss at home to the Texans. His future seemed uncertain until the Steelers hired McCarthy to replace Mike Tomlin in January, a job Rodgers played a hand in helping McCarthy land, though how big a hand is up for debate.

Not that it mattered in the end. Rodgers opted to make one last run at a championship while reuniting with the coach he won a Super Bowl with in early 2011.

A storybook ending on the field seems remote. Pittsburgh is a woeful 2-4 on the road, where it figures to be when the playoffs start if the Steelers can’t track down Cincinnati in the race for the AFC North title.

And the team’s Jekyll/Hyde play in general has offered little tangible proof that it is a legitimate threat to have an extended postseason stay.

Rodgers’ play has mirrored that of his team. He tossed three touchdowns in three consecutive weeks, even outdueling Cincinnati star Joe Burrow in a victory over the Bengals in late September.

Yet he’s struggled to make an impact of late, often checking the ball down to running backs Jaylen Warren and Rico Dowdle or taking sacks behind an inconsistent offensive line when trying to push the ball down the field to a receiving group that has underperformed, particularly two-time Pro Bowler DK Metcalf.

Rodgers averaged just 0.5 air yards per attempt in the first half last week against Philadelphia, the lowest since ESPN began measuring the stat in 2006.

What happens next to a player who has always leaned into doing things on his terms at all times is unclear. Pittsburgh was supposed to be his third and final NFL stop.

He seemed content on Tuesday, spending time in the locker room trading jokes with Rudolph and longtime defensive tackle Cam Heyward after addressing reporters.

Barely eight hours later, Rodgers was no longer the starter. Shortly after that, he was no longer a Steeler, the latest and perhaps final twist in a journey that will end with him getting a gold jacket at the Pro Football Hall of Fame, even if this ends up being an inglorious off-ramp for all involved.

AP Pro Football Writer Rob Maaddi and AP Sports Writer Aaron Beard contributed to this story.

Capcom Is Officially Killing Its 'Jurassic World Meets Overwatch' Shooter

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Four years after the game's launch, Capcom is officially shutting down Exoprimal, the company's 5v5 action game about a futuristic dinosaur crisis. Capcom announced Tuesday that service for Exoprimal will be discontinued in September 2027 — meaning that Exoprimal will no longer be playable — and that the game will no longer be sold starting in March 2027.

While disappointing for stalwart Exoprimal fans, the writing has been on the wall since 2024, when Capcom all but confirmed the sci-fi action game's grim fate.

In an official notice posted to the Exoprimal website, Capcom confirms that players will lose access to the game next year. "We regret to announce that service for Exoprimal will be discontinued as of 8:00 PM (PDT) on Wednesday, September 29, 2027," the shutdown announcement reads. "We cannot thank you enough for the love and support you have shown over the years, and we sincerely apologize for the gravity of this announcement."

Capcom has already pulled the game's four seasonal Survival Passes from stores, and warns that Exoprimal, its DLC, and all related products will no longer be sold on digital stores as of March 30, 2027. The game will shut down completely on Sept. 29, 2027, as Exoprimal is first and foremost a team-based competitive multiplayer game. Capcom notes that it will still be possible to download the game after Exoprimal is removed from stores.

Exoprimal was released in July 2023 on PlayStation 4, PlayStation 5, Windows PC, Xbox One, and Xbox Series X. The game is a multiplayer third-person shooter in which players control an Exofighter — an exosuit-clad soldier — who is tasked with completing objectives while also fighting hordes of dinosaurs in an attempt to defeat the evil artificial intelligence Leviathan. Players are grouped into two teams of five and compete against each other while battling ancient life and futuristic AI.

Capcom announced in July 2024 that, after four seasons of content, Exoprimal players would subsist on recycled, rotating seasons — all but confirming that the publisher was ready to put the new IP behind it.

Exoprimal was released to mixed reviews in 2023. In Polygon's review of the game, which drew comparisons to Overwatch, Dino Crisis, and the Earth Defense Force games, we criticized the game's opening hours, which make "a terrible first impression." But, as reviewer Jordan Oloman wrote, Exoprimal has some surprises in store several hours into the experience.

"This paradigm shift changes Exoprimal considerably, introducing new maps, a healthy difficulty spike, raid bosses, and, at long last, some genuine narrative intrigue," Oloman wrote. "It is a profoundly confusing pacing decision where you’re abruptly playing a very different, arguably much better game several hours in, one that actually sets a fascinating precedent for future multiplayer storytelling shenanigans. In the game’s universe, this is canonized as a 'firmware update' — which makes sense, but I just wish it would have come much sooner, or was at least communicated better in the game’s marketing, as many players might not gain access to Exoprimal’s best assets before bouncing off of its boring early hours."

Exoprimal has a "mostly positive" user review rating on Steam, based on more than 1,700 English-language reviews. Recent reviews, particularly those submitted after the shutdown notice, have been much more negative.
