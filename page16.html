37 public leaderboard strategies, one screen: the arithmetic holds, the concentration does not

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I parsed every strategy on a public leaderboard this week - 37 ids from Collective2's server-rendered page, monthly tables only, no login - and recomputed each one locally before looking at any ranking.

What I recomputed: yearly monthly cells compounded against the printed YTD; annualised return as eq^(365/days)-1; the share of total log return carried by the best three months; annual return against reported max drawdown; record length; win rate against the break-even win rate implied by average win and average loss.

The arithmetic reconciles. Across 36 active strategies, every year's monthly cells compound to the printed YTD within 0.4pp, and the annual figure matches calendar-day annualisation within -1.6 to +0.2pp on the five strategies I checked by hand. That rules out "the platform cannot compute". Any criticism has to be about what the number means, not whether it adds up.

The limits, since a screen is not an audit: the leaderboard is a selection - it shows winners, and the strategies that disappeared are not measured; everything is hypothetical per the platform's own disclaimer; no broker statements were seen; the edge test assumes a fixed average win and loss.

Hugging Face’s 70k-Word MoE Handbook After 800k GPU Hours — Why Sparsity Still Wins for Local Real-Time Tools

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On 5 November 2026 Thomas Wolf and the Hugging Face team published The Ultimate MoE Training Handbook. It is roughly 70,000 words, 55 interactive figures, and runnable code, distilled from their own experiments that burned on the order of 800,000 GPU hours on the Alps cluster. The point of the handbook is not another theoretical survey. It is the operational answers teams actually hit: how many experts, what router, why half a step disappears into all-to-all, why a run can silently become 25× slower with clean logs, aux loss versus DeepSeek-style loss-free bias, and whether you can serve with fewer experts than you trained with.

Eighteen of the twenty highest-scoring open models on the Artificial Analysis index are MoEs. Dense is not dead, but the density that matters for local deployment is the active parameter count, not the total. Flint from Recursive is the recent public example: 39.8 B total, ~2.5 B active, Apache 2.0, claimed 125+ tok/s on an M4 Pro at short context once quantized. The handbook’s diagnostic sections explain the training side of why that ratio is achievable and stable.

These are the same constraints that show up when you try to run a local model inside a Windows process that is already paying the WASAPI capture tax, the audio engine mix, and the overlay draw.

A desktop tool that does live transcription, translation, or short reply generation has a hard latency budget measured from speech end to on-screen text. Cloud STT endpoints (Soniox RT v5 at ~47 ms time-to-final on recent Coval numbers, Qwen3-ASR 1.7B variants around 35 ms) look fast until you add the Windows capture path: device buffer, shared-mode mixing, communications enhancements, and your own client buffer. 20–40 ms of capture jitter is common; lower is fragile under load.

Local models help only if they fit and finish inside the remaining budget. MoE sparsity is the practical lever:

The handbook’s emphasis on router precision and load balance is relevant here too. A router that collapses to one expert under production traffic destroys the sparsity you counted on. The same “measure one flag at a time” methodology they used for training applies when you quantize and profile the inference path.

The MoE handbook does not solve the Windows capture problem. It does make the model side less of a mystery. If half the training step was communication, you already know that the inference router and expert dispatch will also be sensitive to memory layout and quantization. Measure it.

Keep the router in higher precision even if the experts are Q4. Log expert utilization. If one expert is taking 90 % of tokens, you paid for density you are not using.

On the capture side the equivalent is buffer sizing and mode selection. A 10 ms buffer looks great until the scheduler hiccups; 20–30 ms is the range that usually survives real meetings.

GPU passthrough and live CUDA snapshots remain hard (Agent Substrate + gVisor explicitly call this out). For pure CPU or integrated GPU paths the MoE density story is more useful today. Windows still owns the end-to-end number for any tool that has to listen to a call without injecting a bot.

The handbook is free, long, and grounded in measured runs. Read the sections on all-to-all and router precision first if you care about shipping something that has to finish inside a real-time budget instead of a leaderboard.

‘Triplets of Belleville’ Director Sylvain Chomet Fires Back at AI Accusations Over Dior Film Featuring Robert Pattinson’s Voice: ‘Everything Was Drawn by Hand’ (EXCLUSIVE)

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

French animation filmmaker Sylvain Chomet, the four-time Oscar nominee behind “The Triplets of Belleville” and “The Illusionist,” is pushing back against accusations that generative AI was used to create his animated short for Dior featuring a voice cast led by Robert Pattinson, Rihanna, Josh O’Connor, LaKeith Stanfield, Louis Garrel and Camille Cottin.

The whimsical six-minute film, “Christian Dior’s Night in Color,” produced by Aton Soumache (“The Little Prince”), has sparked controversy online, where some viewers have pointed to apparent visual glitches, including a character with six fingers, as evidence of AI-generated animation. Related Stories 'Beef' Creator Lee Sung Jin Sets Netflix Projects With Ali Wong, Gene Hong Under New Banner Lila Pictures Netflix Names Mattel Executive Roberto Stanichi as VP of Consumer Products and Experiences

The controversy flared because Dior positioned “Night in Color” as a celebration of traditional craftsmanship to launch its 2026 holiday season, drawing parallels between Chomet’s hand-drawn animation and the meticulous work of the house’s couture ateliers.

But Chomet and Soumache tell Variety that the film was created using traditional 2D animation techniques, with the characters, backgrounds and key poses drawn by artists. While they acknowledge using automated interpolation to generate some of the intermediate frames that make movement fluid, the pair insists that generative AI was never used to create the artwork.

“Some people are claiming Sylvain was contacted two weeks ago and that the entire film was made with generative AI, without a single drawing, but the reality is that this film took seven months to make and it was entirely drawn by artists,” says Soumache, adding that film employed roughly 20 people, including six animators, alongside storyboard artists, designers, background artists, compositors and editors.

“We used an automated interpolation system for a very small part of the process, but the film itself is traditional 2D animation,” says the French producer.

Chomet, whose latest film “A Magnificent Life” world premiered at last year’s Cannes and was distributed theatrically by Sony Pictures Classics, explains that the interpolation tool was only used to help create the in-between frames on “Christian Dior’s Night in Color.” “It’s a way of automating the repetitive, mechanical part of animation, not the creative work,” he says.

The filmmaker says the accusations are particularly ironic given how committed he has been to traditional animation despite pressures to branch into CGI after the huge success of “Toy Story.”

“Before I made ‘The Triplets of Belleville,’ it was the era of ‘Toy Story.’ I remember Disney declaring that traditional 2D animation was dead and that everything would be CGI, but I decided to continue making hand-drawn films,” Chomet says. “That’s why I find it so strange that people are attacking me today. I’ve remained faithful to drawing throughout my career. At the same time, I’m going to use the tools that technology offers us.”

The film draws on several chapters of Dior’s history, including the couturier’s friendship with Marlene Dietrich, his ties to Hollywood and the creation of the iconic Junon gown in 1949. It also takes inspiration from one of the earliest ideas behind Dior creative director Jonathan Anderson’s Cruise 2027 collection: cinema’s transition from black-and-white to Technicolor.

The story imagines Dietrich in Los Angeles, preparing to shoot a movie when the director suddenly decides to film in color. An urgent call is placed to Paris requesting a colorful Dior gown for the actress, sending Christian Dior and his team of seamstresses into a race against the clock.

Pattinson narrates the film, while Garrel voices Christian Dior and Cottin plays Dietrich. O’Connor voices a talking chair, Rihanna portrays Dior’s lucky star, and Stanfield plays the Hollywood director. Nine d’Urso, Nadia Tereszkiewicz and Greta Lee voice the three bees.

Soumache, whose credits include the Cesar-winning “The Little Prince,” has spent much of his career working in CGI before returning to traditional animation with films including Joann Sfar’s “Little Vampire” and more recently “A Magnificent Life,” a biopic of late celebrated French novelist and filmmaker Marcel Pagnol.

“Sylvain and I have been championing traditional 2D animation for such a long time, and Dior came to us because they wanted Sylvain’s very distinctive drawing style,” says the producer whose track record also includes the blockbuster French IP “Miraculous Ladybug.”

Chomet explains that the production process on the Dior short was rooted in drawing, even if artists now use digital tablets rather than paper.

“But we’re still drawing by hand, it’s just done digitally,” he said, adding that he found it frustrating to work on graphic tablets and planned to “go back to pencil and paper because (he loves) having original drawings.”

“I design the characters, we create the storyboards, the backgrounds, the colors. The entire creative process is exactly the same as when I started making animated films. What has changed is the technology,” he said.

Chomet also addresses the apparent six-fingered hands that have fueled speculation online. “It’s a movement where the hand is spread out like a fan. In that kind of drawing, you might see six or seven fingers because you’re trying to convey movement,” he says. He argues that he’s seen much worse mistakes in traditional animation. “I’ve seen a character turn his head and suddenly his nose changes shape completely over five frames. These things happen.”

The short was conceived as part of a broader collaboration with Dior, with Anderson originating the idea and Martin Latham helping develop the screenplay.

“I worked particularly closely with Martin. We developed the script together and then presented ideas for the storyboard, the story and the characters. There was a lot of back-and-forth,” Chomet says. The collaboration also extended beyond the screen, with Chomet’s illustrations inspiring Dior’s holiday collections and boutique windows around the world.

“Dior needed to see the designs quite early because they were simultaneously developing the Christmas window displays and physical objects,” says Soumache. “Many of Dior’s windows are based directly on Sylvain’s drawings. They created physical versions of the characters and objects from the film.”

Chomet describes Disney’s 1961 classic “One Hundred and One Dalmatians” as his “madeleine de Proust,” the film he keeps coming back to.

“Whenever I make a film, I ask myself whether it has that poetry, that beauty of drawing,” he says. “We can tell very different stories from the ones Disney was telling at the time, but we can still preserve that beauty. That’s what I’ve always tried to do, using technology to maintain something traditional.”

Along with showcasing France’s vibrant animation talent pool, the short film also reflects Anderson’s love for hand-drawn animation. While at Loewe, he collaborated with Japan’s Studio Ghibli on three collections.

A fossil crocodile skull missing its snout just like the Great Sphinx finally got its own name after a century

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A crocodile skull from the rock of the Great Sphinx was misnamed for a century. CT scans show where Herugavialis fits among the gavials.

The Great Sphinx is famous for its missing nose. Dug from the same Egyptian limestone, a fossil crocodile skull is missing the tip of its snout too. For almost a century, it also carried the wrong name.

The skull was long filed as a relative of the false gharial, a narrow-snouted crocodile that lives in Southeast Asia today. A fresh look inside it shows that it belongs somewhere else on the family tree. It now has a name of its own: Herugavialis cairensis.

Nothing new came out of the ground for this study. The team reexamined bones collected near Cairo about 100 years ago. The species is still known from one fairly complete skull and a few partial jaws and snouts. Those pieces may all come from a single animal.

Paul Burke of the Swedish Museum of Natural History led the work with Philip Mannion of University College London. Researchers in Germany and Egypt, including a team from Mansoura University, joined them.

The skull came from the Mokattam Formation, a limestone laid down in the sea about 45 million years ago. The Great Sphinx was carved from the same rock.

The shared rock and the shared missing nose tip gave the crocodile its name. Heru is the Egyptian god, also known as Horus, that is often linked to the Sphinx. The second half of the genus name points to the Indian gharial, Gavialis gangeticus.

“Given this coincidence with the famously missing nose of the Great Sphinx, we decided to name the new crocodile after the Egyptian god Heru, who was possibly the inspiration for this iconic monument,” said Mannion.

Only two members of this narrow-snouted crocodile group, the gavials, are alive today: the Indian gharial and the false gharial. Both live in fresh water in southern Asia, and the false gharial is listed as endangered.

More than 40 extinct species are known, and many of them lived along coasts and in shallow seas.

When the Egyptian fossils were first described in 1927, the skull looked enough like a false gharial to be placed in the same genus, Tomistoma.

In recent years, though, studies comparing many crocodiles kept finding that the Egyptian species wasn’t a close relative of the living one. Three other fossil species that were once filed under Tomistoma have already been moved to new genera.

The bones are kept at the State Museum of Natural History Stuttgart in Germany. Nobody had described their anatomy in detail since that first account.

The team used CT scans, stacks of X-ray images that can be assembled into a 3D model, to look inside the skull. That let them map the space that the brain once filled.

The cast of that space had a blunt bump on top that no other member of the group shows. The bony rims around the eye sockets were raised well above the rest of the skull. The snout made up about 64 percent of the skull’s length.

Next, the researchers placed the animal on a family tree of 152 crocodile species and their relatives, compared across 331 anatomical features.

The Egyptian crocodile didn’t group with the other African or Mediterranean species. It landed closer to the Indian gharial than to the false gharial.

Herugavialis also differed in many ways from two other species in the group known from fossils in Egypt, both from somewhat younger rocks.

Herugavialis had company. The region that’s now the Mediterranean held at least six gavial species in the era that produced Herugavialis, and at least six more much later.

“Throughout much of the last 50 million years, the Mediterranean region hosted a high diversity of crocodiles, including gavials,” said Burke.

“However, this diversity fluctuated over time, seemingly corresponding to changes in global temperature and sea level, before the loss of all crocodiles in this region as a result of the drying of the Mediterranean approximately 6 million years ago.”

Between those two groups, there’s a long stretch with no known species of this kind in the region. The authors suggest that global cooling lowered sea temperatures and sea levels, thus shrinking the coastal waters that these crocodiles needed. Warmer seas later may have let them return.

The family tree backs that idea. If the later species had descended from the earlier local ones, they should cluster together on the tree, and they don’t.

The authors say the empty stretch could partly reflect fossils that haven’t been found yet. More bones from that time would settle whether the crocodiles truly left.

Several other extinct Mediterranean species still carry the old Tomistoma label, including one from Portugal.

Burke is reexamining that species and another from Austria, work the authors say should clarify how the region’s crocodiles were related and why they disappeared.

The full study was published in the journal Royal Society Open Science.

Scientists had a computer predict earthquakes without telling it what to look for

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A machine learning program found a shift in small earthquakes before three large quakes but nothing before two others. It doesn’t give a date for the next big one.

Earthquakes are famously hard to predict, yet before some big quakes, the smaller earthquakes around them change. A machine learning program has now spotted that shift in old records, and no one programmed it what a warning sign looks like.

Researchers at the GFZ Helmholtz Centre for Geosciences in Potsdam, Germany, built it and tested the program on five large earthquakes that had already happened.

Sadegh Karimpouli led the work with Patricia Martínez-Garzón, Marco Bohnhoff, and colleagues in Germany, Italy, and the United States.

No date was presented regarding the next big earthquake.

Sadegh Karimpouli, a GFZ scientist and the lead author of the study, said “This does not mean we can predict earthquakes in a deterministic way, but it provides a powerful tool to recognize when a fault system is behaving differently than usual.”

For decades, scientists in many fields have looked for signs that a large earthquake is eminent. The authors of this study open their reference list with a 1975 paper on those signs, and they call predicting a quake’s timing, place, and size a long-standing challenge that may not be solvable.

In a 1988 paper, Keilis-Borok and his team wrote algorithms that searched earthquake records searching for statistical patterns. For some time, those algorithms led to claims of success.

Others track gases rocks release before they break, or use a recent method to flag where a quake is likely, not when.

Machine learning has worked in the lab. In some experiments on smooth, well-monitored rock faults, programs that learned from known outcomes have predicted when a fault would slip.

Karimpouli’s team also used its approach on lab quakes, but real faults are more complex, and field sensors record limited detail.

The team gave the program only the records seismologists already keep: when each earthquake happened, where it happened, and the size of the quake.

For the magnitude 7.8 quake that struck Türkiye in 2023, the records covered 5,686 earthquakes within 31 miles / 50 kilometers of the epicenter from 2017.

Next, the program grouped earthquakes that happened close together in space, time, and size into “families.” It described each family with about 100 measurements, such as how tightly the quakes bunched and how much strain they released. Then it sorted the families into four or five groups based on how similar they looked.

“Earthquakes are not isolated events, they influence each other, and the closer the rupture event gets, the stronger this influence becomes,” said Marco Bohnhoff, a co-author and the head of the geomechanics and scientific drilling section at GFZ.

The team also replayed each record in order, adding one family at a time, as if the big quake hadn’t happened yet. Each new family either fit a group the program knew or needed a new one.

A 2023 study by some of the same researchers had described a burst of small quakes in the eight months before the Türkiye quake.

The program found one group of families that appeared mostly across those months. Those families were unusually tight in space and time, and they released more strain than the rest.

The 2009 quake at L’Aquila, Italy, had magnitude 6.1, and earlier researchers dated its lead-up to about three months prior.

The program found two groups of families working at the same time during those months. One of them, made up of long-lasting families, appeared mainly before the big quake.

The 2014 quake in northern Chile reached magnitude 8.1, and the program’s most unusual groups appeared about two weeks before it. In a 2014 Science paper, others found a burst of small quakes and a slow fault slide in those weeks. The program picked up only that last stretch, not the years of weakening that other studies describe.

In the replay, the program needed a new group about a month before the L’Aquila quake and about two weeks before the Chilean quake, but In Türkiye, it needed one from mid-2022 forward.

The program found nothing before the other two. It found neither a magnitude 6.2 quake near Amatrice, Italy, in 2016 nore a magnitude 7.5 quake on Japan’s Noto Peninsula in 2024. In the replay, no rising trend appeared in either as well.

At Amatrice, no group with those traits appeared in at least the six months beforehand. In the final two months, one group did give way to another, but the same switch had happened in earlier months and years without a large quake following. The authors say that could reflect what the sensors missed, or simply slip on the fault that made no shaking.

Noto had its own long run of small quakes including a swarm that began in late 2020 and lasted until the big quake.

Scientists at the same Potsdam center recently built a model for swarms at Italy’s Campi Flegrei. At Noto, the program sorted 23,630 earthquakes from 2021 onward, and yet none of its groups before the big quake showed the traits seen at the other three.

“Some faults may fail without obvious seismic warning signs, which is a major challenge for forecasting,” said Patricia Martínez-Garzón, a co-author at GFZ.

Each record held relatively few families with 96 families for the Türkiye quake and 186 families for L’Aquila, and that may be too few for the program to find solid groups.

Choosing how many groups to use requires an expert’s judgment, as does deciding the presentation of a new group. The method also works only if clustered quakes come before the big one.

Why some faults give clear signals, and others do not, remains an open question. Martínez-Garzón said the next step is to build these approaches into real-time monitoring and determine why.

Italy’s early warning system for high-speed trains issues earthquake alerts in seconds, but it serves a different purpose. The authors say their approach shows potential for operational earthquake forecasting.

Until the program runs on live data, every result comes from an earthquake that had already happened.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.

All 8 Episodes Of Netflix's R-Rated Thriller Miniseries Deliver Peak Psychological Horror

Some of the greatest thrillers carefully transcend the screen and invade a viewer's personal space. They introduce audiences to such bizarre yet intimate scenarios that it becomes hard not to wonder if their hard-hitting terrors could someday happen in the real world. One of Netflix's better psychological thrillers is driven by the same principle, allowing it to stay with one long after the credits stop rolling.

Written by Son Ho-young, The Frog is relatively obscure compared to most mainstream thriller shows on Netflix. It follows a relatively simple premise where a mysterious visitor enters the lives of ordinary unsuspecting people before unleashing complete chaos. However, what starts as a simple psychological thriller soon turns into a tale of extreme obsession and manipulation.

The Frog's early appeal comes from its portrayal of quaint rural settings. It primarily unfolds in the brightness of the summer in seemingly safe and peaceful environments. This, however, is cleverly juxtaposed by the gripping Netflix K-drama's portrayal of how one can fatefully find darkness even during the brightness of the day and the peace of the night.

There is a famous Korean proverb that goes something like: "A stone thrown carelessly can kill a frog." The proverb is where the show gets its name from as it serves as the primary driver of its story. More often than not, in typical psychological thrillers, a game of cat-and-mouse unfolds between a protagonist and an antagonist.

Even the show's characters seem so realistic and ordinary that it becomes hard not to see yourself in their place.

For the most part of its run, however, the Netflix thriller does a great job of making up for its flaws with its compelling character beats, brilliant performances, and incredible atmospheric contrast. Not to mention, its brief 8-episode runtime makes it perfectly bingeable.

The meeting presents an important opportunity for both sides to strengthen their positions in the 2. Bundesliga table as the season progresses. Bochum will be looking to make home advantage count, while Karlsruher SC travel to North Rhine-Westphalia hoping to come away with a positive result.

Bochum head into Sunday's encounter with renewed confidence after ending their difficult run of league form with a 2-1 victory away to Energie Cottbus last weekend.

Uwe Rösler's side had endured a five-game winless run in the league before travelling to Cottbus, but Christian Rasmussen's late winner secured three valuable points and lifted the club to 11th in the standings.

The victory was particularly important given Bochum's struggles in front of goal this season. Having scored just eight times in their opening ten league matches, they currently possess the lowest-scoring attack in the division. However, their recent cup victory over Mainz and the comeback success against Cottbus suggest that Rösler's team are beginning to find some momentum.

Rösler is nevertheless expecting a difficult afternoon against a Karlsruhe side whose intensity and attacking movement could cause problems. Speaking ahead of the fixture, the Bochum coach described Sunday's encounter as a potential 50-50 contest and warned that the visitors are one of the most intense teams in the division.

The German side will need to pay particular attention to Marvin Wanitzek, who Rösler identified as Karlsruhe's standout player, highlighting his ability to find space and influence matches through both open play and set-pieces.

Karlsruher SC travel to Bochum looking to build on an encouraging 2-1 victory over 1. FC Heidenheim, which brought some much-needed stability to their start under new head coach Maximilian Senft.

The Baden-Württemberg outfit had endured a difficult spell before welcoming Heidenheim, having suffered a heavy 6-1 defeat away to Magdeburg immediately after an impressive 4-0 home victory against Darmstadt.

However, goals from Tim Civeja and Marvin Wanitzek secured a deserved victory over the recently-relegated side last weekend, with Wanitzek's superb free-kick proving decisive.

Despite that result, consistency remains a significant concern for Senft. Karlsruhe have collected 11 points from their opening ten league matches, leaving them level with Bochum, although their inferior goal difference places them two positions below Sunday's opponents.

Their away form is another area requiring improvement, with the visitors yet to record an away victory in the league this season, collecting just two points from their four matches on the road.

Senft acknowledged that his team must become more consistent when discussing their recent performances, explaining that the squad are still developing under his new tactical approach.

"We are coming out of the Heidenheim game strengthened, particularly because of the way we performed tactically and mentally," the Austrian coach explained. He also stressed his desire to deliver positive performances more frequently rather than continuing to experience such significant fluctuations.

Karlsruhe will therefore be hoping that last weekend's victory can provide the platform for a more convincing away performance, particularly against a Bochum side who have also struggled to find consistency this season.

Historically, Bochum have enjoyed the better of this fixture, recording 23 victories compared to Karlsruhe's 16 across 64 competitive meetings, with the remaining 25 encounters ending in draws.

However, the more recent record at the Vonovia Ruhrstadion offers encouragement for the visitors. Karlsruhe have avoided defeat on each of their last six visits to Bochum, who have not beaten them at home since a 1-0 victory in May 2014.

Interestingly, 13 of the 24 meetings between the sides in the 2. Bundesliga have ended in draws, highlighting how closely contested this fixture has traditionally been.

The previous meeting came on the final day of the 2025/26 campaign, when Bochum claimed a 2-1 away victory thanks to Moritz-Broni Kwarteng's winning goal.

Bochum will be without several players for Sunday's encounter, with Enis Cokaj, Jean Manuel Mbom and Oliver Olsen having returned to training but not yet being available for selection.

Babis Drakas and Philipp Strompf are also not ready to return, while Berkan Taz missed one training session earlier in the week. However, Rösler confirmed that the remainder of his squad had completed full training.

The Bochum coach also suggested that changes could be made to his attacking partnership, with Philipp Hofmann having played a significant number of minutes recently. Charlie Patiño and Jarne Steuckers are among those pushing for starting opportunities following encouraging substitute appearances.

Karlsruhe, meanwhile, will again be without defensive regulars Marcel Franke and Sebastian Jung, who are both unavailable through injury.

New signing Héliton has completed his first full week of training since the previous international break, although Senft indicated that he is more likely to be considered for a brief appearance rather than a starting role.

Paul Scholl is consequently expected to retain his place in the back three after impressing during the 2-1 victory over Heidenheim, while Tim Civeja will also hope to keep his place following his first goal in the second division.

Karlsruher SC (3-4-2-1): Bernat; Kobald, Scholl, Trémoulet; Pinto Pedrosa, Väänänen, Civeja, Ofli; Wiethaup, Wanitzek; Fukuda

Kepa Arrizabalaga set for emotional Athletic Club homecoming at San Mames as Arsenal backup goalkeeper is called up by Basque Country to face Venezuela

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Arsenal goalkeeper Kepa Arrizabalaga is poised for a poignant return to former club Athletic Club's San Mames stadium after earning a call-up from the Basque Country representative team. The shot-stopper has been named in the Euskal Selekzioa squad to take on South American visitors Venezuela during next weekend's international friendly in Bilbao.

Osasuna manager Jagoba Arrasate has included the Ondarroa native as one of the marquee names in his 22-man selection for the clash on Saturday, November 14. Currently serving as understudy to David Raya at Arsenal following previous loan stints with Real Madrid and Bournemouth, Arrizabalaga originally departed Bilbao for Chelsea in 2018. That €80 million move to Stamford Bridge remains the world-record transfer fee ever paid for a goalkeeper.

Joining Arrizabalaga in the squad as players plying their trade outside Spain are Udinese forward Unai Gomez and Bolton Wanderers attacker Gaizka Larrazabal, while Osasuna stopper Aitor Fernandez provides competition between the posts. Arrasate has once again leaned heavily on a familiar domestic core drawn from Athletic Club, Real Sociedad, Osasuna and Deportivo Alaves.

In defence, Athletic's Dani Vivian, Hugo Rincon and Johaneko Louis-Jean are joined by Sociedad trio Jon Pacheco, Igor Zubeldia and Aihen Munoz alongside Osasuna's Inigo Arguibide and Malaga's Einar Galilea.

Midfield options feature Athletic's Mikel Jauregizar, Sociedad's Benat Turrientes, Alaves pair Pablo Ibanez and Ander Guevara, Osasuna's Asier Osambela and Sevilla's Jon Guridi, while Athletic star Oihan Sancet, Osasuna's Kike Barja and Racing Santander duo Inigo Vicente and Asier Villalibre complete the attack.

Organised by Basque Football Federation president Xabier Goni, the fixture marks the representative team's first appearance since defeating Palestine 3-0 in front of a record 51,396 crowd on November 15 last year via goals from Unai Elgezabal, Gorka Guruzeta and Urko Izeta. Next Saturday's contest carries deep cultural weight as it celebrates the 90th anniversary of the first autonomous Basque Government formed under President Jose Antonio Aguirre in 1936.

Aguirre subsequently established the Euzkadi team in 1937 to tour Europe and the Americas during the Spanish Civil War. Venezuela also holds a special bond with the region after welcoming thousands of Basque political exiles fleeing Francisco Franco's dictatorship.

Arrrizabalaga will hope to earn rare starting minutes in familiar surroundings next weekend before returning to north London to resume his backup duties under Mikel Arteta after the international break. On the pitch in Bilbao, Euskal Selekzioa will look to preserve their 100 per cent historical record in what will be their fourth meeting against Venezuela. The hosts previously beat the South Americans 4-3 away in San Cristobal in June 2007, 3-1 at the old San Mames in December 2010 and 4-2 at Mendizorrotza in October 2018.
