The Qantas Airbus A321XLR That Has 3 Fewer Economy Seats

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In a move that puts traveler comfort ahead of scraping every shred of profit for a jetliner, Qantas pulled out three seats from its Airbus A321XLR that are requisite revenue-generating hardware for most shareholders. The aircraft, tail number VH-OGD, is the first delivery of a 48-seat subfleet that will include a fourth bathroom, using space that was reserved for the usual row of seats in the main cabin. The modification highlights a design tension inherent to the long-range narrowbody platform.

The cabin reconfiguration was designed in response to customer feedback that followed the first three deliveries of the type. The overarching tone from passengers, aviation commentators, and the media was scathing and derisive, often framing the issue as a ‘legacy stink’ left behind by the airline’s highly controversial former CEO, Alan Joyce.

Thus, instead of 180 economy and 20 business, the updated cabin of Qantas’ long-haul narrowbody now seats 197 travelers in ‘coach.’ The customer feedback Qantas received was immediate and highly critical of the airline's management, forcing a mid-stream redesign before the bulk of the fleet was even built. Consequently, the airline also committed to retrofitting the first three A321XLR aircraft already in its fleet with the extra toilet during future heavy maintenance checks.

Qantas ordered the new extra-long-range Airbus single-aisles in 2022 to replace aging Boeing 737-800s. In 2025, Qantas launched commercial A321XLR flights, beginning September service with Sydney and Melbourne. Sky TG24 noted that while marketing focused on cabin width and fuel efficiency, the media attention was honed in on the jet’s layout flaws. It was specifically highlighted that the first three aircraft of Australia’s flag carrier had fewer toilets in economy than its low-cost competitors, Ryanair, Scoot, and Jetstar.

For comparison, a standard Ryanair Boeing 737 carries 197 passengers with three toilets, like the new Qantas A321XLR. That ratio averages 66 people per facility. A Scoot Airbus A320 accommodates 180 passengers with three toilets, or 60 per facility. Meanwhile, a Jetstar A320 seats 186 with three units, averaging 62. In contrast, the original Qantas cabin provided just two toilets for 180 seats, at a 50% elevated ratio of 90 people per facility.

As Runway Girl Network reported, the airline stated that the first aircraft will be finalized in 2022 with three toilets to match outgoing 737 specifications. Management has since promised all 48 jets on order will feature four toilets following the updated design. The layout assigns three facilities to economy and one to business class. However, The Guardian has emphasized that the first three airframes will likely operate unchanged for approximately three years before undergoing a formal retrofit.

The first modified four-lavatory aircraft was currently scheduled for a December 2025 delivery. It didn’t ultimately reach Australia until late February of this year, according to Australian Aviation. The arrival resolved the initial cabin design crisis, shifting the industry conversation away from consumer complaints. Yet, analysts turned to the manufacturing choices and cabin design priorities that had led to the ill-equipped jets’ arrival in the first place.

Packing 200 passengers into a narrow fuselage turns cabin calculus into a test of passenger patience. While three total lavatories average one per 67-person full cabin, the forward facility is reserved for business class. That left only two rear toilets for 180 economy travelers, inflating the ratio to 90 people per facility. An arrangement that fell below the amenities relative to the legacy aircraft it replaced despite Qantas’ claims.

One Mile at a Time calculated that the outgoing Boeing 737-800 provided two toilets for a cabin of 162 economy seats, which equals 81 passengers each. The A321XLR added 18 coach seats compared to the 737 without expanding toilet capacity. Airline Geeks also reported that the legacy jet carried 174 total customers, increasing total flyer volume by 15%.

That design deviated drastically from the standard of the rest of the jets in the full-service airline’s fleet. While budget carriers maximize efficiency by allowing open passenger access to all facilities, AeroLOPA noted that Qantas' own standards typically maintain a ratio between 42 and 49 passengers per lavatory on long-haul airframes. This underscored the unusual deficit in the new narrowbody layout, which fell well behind the carrier's traditional passenger-comfort standard in its international widebody fleet.

General industry benchmarks added to the negative optics that resulted after the first three planes began service. Other prominent ‘legacy style’ operators of the Airbus A321 family, including Delta Air Lines, routinely equip their economy cabins with at least three dedicated lavatories on short and medium range routes.

What will the Australian flag carrier choose?

The A321XLR delivers a single-aisle operational reach of 4,700 nautical miles (8,704 km), supporting flight durations approaching 11 hours. This capability enables operators like Iberia to service long-haul routes like Madrid to Boston.

Many A321neos are equipped with Airbus' SpaceFlex module, which combines the rear galley and lavatory footprint which increases passenger seating capacity. Yet, according to AeroLOPA, mid-cabin restrooms were first rejected by all six of the original launch customers: Iberia, American Airlines, Aer Lingus, Air Canada, Qantas, and United Airlines. Due to that design choice, facilities were concentrated at the extremities of the aircraft, which caused serious bottlenecks during lengthy flights lasting eight to eleven hours.

The practical consequences immediately manifested during real-world operations for more carriers than just Qantas. Iberia’s configuration placed all three economy lavatories at the extreme rear of the fuselage, with one unit dual-functioning as a flight attendant jumpseat. This layout restricted active availability to just two units for 168 economy passengers. It resulted in persistent queues that routinely clogged the rear galley workspace, according to at least one Runway Girl Network review.

Even so, Qantas still implemented the most aggressive configuration among these initial operators at the outset. Deploying the dense 200-seat layout well exceeds the 182 seats found aboard Iberia and Air Canada variants or the 184 on Aer Lingus, 155 on American, and 150 on United jets.

Qantas will have one of the highest passenger capacities onboard its Airbus A321XLRs.

Tail number VH-OGD was the first A321XLR incorporating a mid-cabin product redesign driven by preliminary operational feedback. Eliminating a three-seat economy row contracted total cabin capacity from 200 to 197 seats, leaving 20 in business and 177 in economy. This adjustment reduced the economy passenger-to-toilet ratio from 90 to approximately 59, or a 34% reduction in demand.

The rear aisle congestion also caused significant disruptions to the crew's workflow. Flight attendants frequently had to pause meal services, back up their food carts, and manually clear crowds out of the aisle just to pass. Because the new front-economy toilet can accommodate a substantial portion of the cabin's passengers, the back aisle remains open and makes everything easier for the cabin crew, which also improves the customer experience in-flight.

While sacrificing three seats removes only 1.5% of the total aircraft inventory, the configuration change greatly impacted the amenity available on each flight. The cut marginally limits the maximum revenue potential on Qantas’ routes with high demand and slightly raises unit operating expenses per seat. On the other hand, the additional restroom reduces overall cabin congestion and even lessens the operational risk of a single component failure grounding a flight, and mitigates mechanical wear across onboard plumbing.

Larger fuel tanks and a lower passenger and cargo capacity are needed for Qantas' Project Sunrise flights.

Even after the cabin update for the vast majority of Qantas’ A321XLR fleet, the initial three airframes will remain with the original high-density layout for an extended period. The Guardian projected approximately three years of active service before modification. The remaining 45 aircraft within the 48-unit order book will arrive with four factory-installed toilets.

In February, Qantas also launched its highly anticipated Economy Plus product. The extra legroom addition heavily improves passenger comfort and recline dynamics on the Airbus A321XLR. The 36 Plus seats on the A321XLR feature a generous seat pitch of 34 to 35 inches (86.36 to 88.9 cm). Standard economy seats have a pitch of 30 inches (76.2 cm). When the traveler in front reclines, the space tightens significantly.

As the A321XLR flies transcontinental routes and mid-haul international segments, posture changes are essential to prevent fatigue. Qantas also designed the A321XLR economy cabin with a focus on streaming entertainment. The seats include built-in drop-down tablet holders with anti-slip mats.

Both Qantas and British Airways are reconfiguring their A380s to cut economy seating.

The Airbus A321XLR registered as VH-OGD arrived in Australia in late February. Although Qantas initially scheduled the aircraft's international debut from Brisbane to Manila for October 25, reports say its deployment was deferred to February 1, 2027.

The A321XLR's operational role runs completely parallel to Qantas's massive widebody modernization. The effort is internally split across Project Sunrise, which will debut in first-of-their-kind Airbus A350-1000ULR variants on what is projected to be the longest commercial flights in the world. The first locked-in route connects Sydney to London Heathrow starting in October 2027, followed shortly after by a nonstop link between Sydney and New York JFK in mid-2028.

Meanwhile, Project Fysh is in the works to introduce Boeing 787s and A350s intended to replace the retiring A330 and A380 fleets. The 787-10 fleet will fly medium-haul routes from Sydney and Melbourne to Singapore, Tokyo, Hong Kong, and Auckland. The standard A350-1000 will take over the long-haul routes that require maximum capacity but don't need the Sunrise range, such as Los Angeles, San Francisco, Johannesburg, and the traditional one-stop kangaroo route to Europe via Singapore.

Israeli strike on Gaza hospital kills six Palestinians, health ministry says

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

First responders from the Civil Defence inspect the damage at Nasser hospital in Khan Younis

At least six people have been killed and a large number injured in an Israeli air strike on Nasser hospital, the biggest hospital in the southern Gaza Strip, the Hamas-run health ministry says.

Eyewitnesses told the BBC that a single missile fired by a warplane hit a building containing the surgical wards and intensive care units overnight.

The health ministry said medical staff, patients and companions were killed. It condemned the attack as a "flagrant violation of international laws and conventions".

The Israeli military said it struck a senior Hamas figure and other operatives from the group's armed wing who had been operating within the hospital.

It alleged that they had violated international law by using the hospital as "a command centre for military and terrorist activities".

Hamas did not comment on the claim. But it accused Israel of killing patients and medical staff and perpetrating "a full-fledged war crime".

Under international law, hospitals have a protected status, but they have been repeatedly targeted during the Gaza war.

Israel accuses Hamas, which retains security control of parts of Gaza not occupied by Israeli troops, of cynically exploiting civilian infrastructure. It says the group stores weapons and runs command and control rooms in hospitals, which Hamas denies.

Israel does not allow international news organisations, including the BBC, into Gaza, making it difficult to verify what is happening in the territory.

In a video posted on social media, a paramedic from the Civil Defence agency who arrived at the building after the strike early on Thursday said the missile penetrated the five-storey structure and hit a room in the surgical department.

A spokesman for the Civil Defence, which operates under the Hamas-run interior ministry, initially said that five people had been killed and 20 others injured.

Videos shared by activists showed thick smoke inside the building and extensive destruction on the second floor, where medical equipment and beds had been scattered by the blast.

Local activists have also circulated claims that the strike may have targeted a senior Hamas security figure who was hiding inside the building.

Speaking to reporters in the aftermath of the strike, the director of Nasser hospital said the building had been hit in a vertical strike, causing extensive damage.

Nasser hospital was hit by a double Israeli strike in August 2025, which killed at least 20 people, including journalists and health workers.

Israeli strikes on Gaza have continued despite a ceasefire agreement in October last year.

More than 1,620 people have been killed since then, according to the territory's health ministry, whose figures are considered reliable by the UN.

Israel and Hamas have accused each other of violating the ceasefire.

The humanitarian crisis in Gaza has recently worsened as a result of heavy rains that reportedly flooded tens of thousands of tents housing displaced families.

The Gaza war was triggered by the Hamas-led attack on southern Israel on 7 October 2023, when about 1,200 people were killed and 251 others were taken hostage.

Since then, more than 74,650 people have been killed in Gaza, the health ministry says.

The MX Master 4 Special Edition takes Logitech's excellent black mouse and spruces it up with a new white colourway and red-and-grey accents. Inside, it's the same MX Master 4, and like the 2025 version, it has a tough act to follow. Its predecessor, the MX Master 3S, is already one of the best productivity mice you can buy, so Logitech never intended to reinvent the wheel. Instead, we get more customisation, haptic feedback, and a handful of design tweaks, and in this Special Edition, a more personalised colour choice.

These are all the developments I’d expect from a follow-up to the excellent MX Master 3S, one of the best mice you can buy, but they make this a nicer-looking version of a mouse that was a smart evolution rather than a must-have upgrade, unless, I'd argue, you're a creative; then the Actions Ring becomes a bigger thing than you may expect and is new to MX Master 4, Special Edition or not.

The shape is the same as the MX Master 3S, with its sculpted ergonomic design, deep thumb rest, and plenty of controls to keep your hands off the keyboard. It’s comfortable for long workdays, and the weight is spot on. It has enough heft to feel planted on the desk without feeling clumsy. It’s big, though, and it won’t suit everyone. This is a mouse for your desk, not your laptop bag.

The MagSpeed wheel is as good as ever. Flick it, and it free-spins through a long spreadsheet or a 40-page document, then ratchets back to click-by-click scrolling when you need precision. Switching between the two is automatic, which is why it’s so easy to take for granted.

The finish is where things have changed. This is the Special Edition of last year’s MX Master 4 release, so the design changes over the MX Master 3S remain. Logitech has swapped the soft, rubberised coating of older models for something harder, but it’s not slick or glassy. The cover has a nice bit of tooth, and different areas have subtly different textures, so your fingers always know where they are without you looking. It feels different from the old MX Masters. A colleague wasn’t keen on it when they tried [2025’s MX Master 4], but I got used to it quickly, so your mileage may vary, and existing owners may need a few days to adjust.

Those alterations aside, the Special Edition replaces flat blacks with a soft grey-white colour and red accents. The dark grey Actions Ring thumb rest pops in this colourway, and I far prefer it to the faceless black PC-mouse aesthetic of the base model. There’s no price difference between black and Special Edition.

The thumb rest gets the biggest rethink. It’s usually the most wasted area of a mouse, a bit of plastic to rest your thumb on. Here, it’s the way into the new virtual dial, called the Actions Ring, which makes it feel like part of the design rather than an afterthought.

The haptics are a lovely touch. They kick in when you make a selection and when you open and cycle through the Actions Ring, so I can keep my eyes on the screen, click and scroll, and still feel a little resistance and feedback. This also helps with gesture control, too, if you go as far as needing that kind of input. It’s subtle, but it makes the mouse feel more connected to what you’re doing.

As you may have guessed, the Actions Ring is the real star of the new features. It’s an on-screen dial that expands what the mouse can do, making it easy to open folders or switch between apps without dropping into the Windows menus. You can tailor the ring to the job, from something as basic as Google to Photoshop or DaVinci Resolve. Support isn’t universal, though. The best digital art and design apps like Krita, Affinity Photo and CorelDRAW are covered, but smaller apps like Escape Motions Rebelle aren’t a default option, so you’ll need a workaround: assign function keys in Options+, then match them to the app’s own shortcuts for undo, clear layer or brush toggles. It works, but you need to know your keys or set them up ahead of time.

This also means getting to grips with Options+ and its app features, which is a lot of learning for a mouse. I’d like it to work more without that software running in the background. But if you persevere, it adds real depth and makes the mouse faster to use and more flexible. Once it’s set up, you’ll love it. You can tweak everything from haptic strength to scroll speed, and I’d probably be moaning if I couldn’t fiddle.

The haptics, gestures, and Actions Ring are a step beyond the MX Master 3S, and creatives who map shortcuts to the ring will get the most from this mouse.

So, should you buy the MX Master 4 Special Edition? If you’re coming from a basic mouse, absolutely. The ergonomics, scrolling and customisation make it a brilliant tool for anyone who works at a computer all day, whether in creative apps or simply navigating menus and Google.

If you already own an MX Master 3S, it comes down to how you work. The haptics and Actions Ring are genuinely useful and, for creative apps, a new, easier way to work, so it’s worth it if you’ll map shortcuts to the ring or, like me, hate sorting through Windows menus and would rather one-click into the regular apps and files. For everyone else, they’re not enough to make a perfectly good mouse obsolete.

Your AI agent is only as good as your endpoint data

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

PARTNER CONTENT: Real-time endpoint data keeps AI-driven IT operations accurate, fast and under control

Every so often a flaw comes along that shows how fast the ground can shift under IT and security teams. PHP2Shell, a pair of critical flaws in PHP, was one of them. Details leaked before the planned disclosure, frontier AI helped researchers find the flaws, and PHP shipped fixes the next day. For many teams, though, the patch was the easy part. So what does it take to respond at that speed, and where should teams begin?

Picture a loop. Software finds where a risk lives and ranks what matters most. It proposes a fix, then, inside limits people define, carries it out and checks that it worked. Routine, low-risk actions can run on their own. Higher-impact ones wait for a person to approve. A few are never handed over at all. Each step leaves a record that someone can review and reverse.

Three problems keep coming up. The first is visibility. PHP2Shell reportedly sat undetected for more than a decade. For these two flaws, the fix was a version upgrade. The hard part was finding every copy of PHP, including copies bundled inside other software or extracted by hand onto Windows machines that never registered as installed applications. Then teams had to work out which copies the internet could reach and check for signs of earlier attack. An inventory built from a periodic snapshot can't answer those questions on the day they matter.

The second is tool-hopping. Answering "where does this run, and can the internet reach it?" often means pivoting across an asset inventory, a vulnerability scanner and an external attack surface tool, each with its own view. Every hop adds delay. Every console is another chance to disagree with the last.

The third is pace. Several security firms reported exploitation attempts before PHP released its fixes, and Cloudflare reported early attacks hours ahead of the patch. Frontier AI models helped find PHP2Shell, and in April Anthropic said its Claude Mythos Preview model had found thousands of previously unknown flaws across every major operating system and browser. With models like these in the picture, the old rhythm of disclose, patch and verify assumes a gap that keeps shrinking.

Put an AI agent on top of stale, fragmented data and these problems don't go away. They scale.

Endpoints are where work happens and where many attacks end up, so they are the ground truth for any decision about the estate. Four qualities make that data a sound foundation for autonomous action:

Accuracy: Live data from the endpoints themselves, not a snapshot that was true last week.

Speed: A short path from question to action, with no hopping between tools to reconcile answers.

Control: Approvals and an audit trail, plus the ability to roll back, so any automated action can be reviewed and reversed.

Shared truth: IT operations, security and the system of record working from one view, rather than arguing over whose data is right.

Matt Psencik, a threat research engineer at Tanium, put the visibility problem bluntly when PHP2Shell broke: "The installations that worry me most are the ones a security team doesn't know it owns."

AI sits on both sides of this. It helped researchers find the PHP2Shell flaws, and attackers can use it to build exploits too. For defenders, AI works best as the operator and the interface: it takes a plain-language question, works out what to ask of the data, and proposes or carries out the next step. But it is only as good as the data it is grounded in. A fluent answer built on outdated information is still wrong, just more confidently so. Keeping people in the loop where stakes are high is what makes automation safe to expand.

Teams don't need to hand over the keys all at once. Start here:

Find every copy: Don't stop at the installed-applications list. PHP2Shell showed that software bundled inside other products can slip past it. Compare your CMDB against what is really on your network and record exact versions.

Draw the lines: Rank systems by what the internet can reach, then decide which response actions run automatically, which need approval and which are never delegated. Write it down before an agent needs it.

Pilot one low-risk workflow: Pick something contained, build approval into it, and read the audit trail before widening the scope.

Teams can also shorten the pilot with a partner. World Wide Technology (WWT), a Tanium partner, works with many large organizations on technology rollouts and hosts a Tanium lab in its Advanced Technology Center, where customers can test an approach hands-on before moving it into production.

Access to Shanghai Grand Opera House staircase restricted to paid ticket holders

Photography by Kaia Wang, Yumeng Zhu, Tian Fangfang, Chen Hao

The Snøhetta-designed Shanghai Grand Opera House has introduced a paid, ticketing system for visitors wanting to access the signature spiral staircase and rooftop viewing platform at the recently opened building in Shanghai.

When Dezeen visited the site, we observed a temporary barricade that had been set up to separate the free-to-access, ground-level public from the spiral staircase leading up to the roof. Access to the staircase and roof now costs 50 yuan (£5.60) per person.

Since opening, access to the architectural feature has been restricted with visitors now required to pre-book tickets online, with the staircase open between 10:30am to 2:30pm.  According to the booking site, people with tickets to see a performance inside the opera house are still required to purchase a ticket and the fee includes accident insurance.

Asked about the apparent change in access requirements, Snøhetta said that demand meant that “visitor management is necessary” and that “access arrangements will continue to develop”.

“As with any major cultural institution, operational procedures continue to evolve during the opening period as the Opera House team gains experience with visitor patterns, capacity needs, and the building’s day-to-day use,” the studio told Dezeen.

“Given the exceptional public interest and the limited capacity of the roof and public stairs, visitor management is necessary to ensure a safe and enjoyable experience for everyone,” it continued.

“Access arrangements will continue to develop with operational experience, but the intention remains the same: to create a building that can be widely enjoyed by the public while balancing accessibility, safety, and the visitor experience.”

Following news that a fee had been introduced, the phrase “is it worth spending 50 yuan to visit Shanghai Grand Opera House” trended on Chinese social media.

Some commenters questioned whether charing to visit the attraction was justifiable, while others sympathised with the need for crowd control. Others shared tips on how to take the best photos of the building without paying the entrance fee.

Visitors Dezeen spoke to at the site were concerned about both the fee and the temporary barriers.

“I don’t think it’s worth the money,” Mr Qiu, who paid to climb the stairs, told Dezeen. “Basically you pay to climb a giant stairs. If you want good photos of the stairs, best to take from afar, which you can do for free,”

Ms Ye, who didn’t pay the entry fee, told Dezeen that she was really disappointed that the barricade was in her photos.

“I don’t know what the point of the barricade is – to separate the paid and unpaid? Or for better crowd circulation? But at least they need to put some effort in designing it to match the beauty of the building,” she said.

“It really is an eye-sore for the photos.”

The building, and its accessible roof, are often compared to Oslo Opera House, which was also designed by Snøhetta and is topped with a walkable roof. According to the studio, both projects were designed to “reduce thresholds wherever possible”.

“Buildings like opera houses inevitably create thresholds through walls, doors, and ticketed programs,” said Snøhetta co-founder Kjetil Thorsen at the time of the Shanghai Grand Opera House opening.

“We see it as our responsibility to challenge those thresholds and make the building as much a civic destination as a performance venue. People should be able to enjoy the building without attending a performance. It should become part of everyday life, a place to spend time, meet others, and experience the city,” he continued.

“A successful landmark is not only recognised by visitors, it is also embraced by the people who live around it and make it part of their everyday lives.”

Several other cultural projects in China have been built with publicly accessible roofs. However, the accessibility presented in concepts has often been restricted when the buildings open.

Also in Shanghai, the Start Museum, designed by French architect Jean Nouvel and opened in 2022, features a public roof that can be used for displaying large-scale art work. However, the space has rarely been used.

The Amateur Architecture Studio-designed Fuchun Cultural Museum Complex is defined by a continuous undulating roof to resemble the experience of climbing the local Fuchun Mountain. However, the roof didn’t open to the public for four years and is now only open to public visits for one hour a day, three days a week.

The constellation Perseus is famous for its bright Double Cluster, but these two open clusters aren’t the only groups of young stars in the Hero worth viewing. Sitting 5.2° west-northwest of 2nd-magnitude Algol (Beta [β] Persei), M34 spans about half a degree, roughly the size of the Full Moon. In physical space, astronomers believe the group spans about 14 light-years and sits 1,400 light-years away. It’s best seen under lower magnifications, such as in binoculars or small amateur scopes (4 inches or so). You’ll spot several dozen stars brighter than 12th magnitude, with its brightest suns spread throughout the cluster.
