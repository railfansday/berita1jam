Early humans may have kept souvenirs half a million years ago

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Early humans may have collected keepsakes 500,000 years ago, a habit researchers suggest could have helped shape memory and a sense of self.

Somewhere in your house there’s a stone from a beach. A shell from a holiday. A pinecone your kid picked up on a walk three years ago that you can’t bring yourself to throw away.

It does nothing but it means everything. And according to a new study, you’re not the first human to feel that way. Not by about half a million years.

The study was led by Ran Barkai and Yoav Ram at Tel Aviv University.

The team argues that early humans in the Levant were already picking up colorful pebbles, fossil-studded rocks, and old weathered tools and carrying them home for reasons that may have gone beyond practical use.

According to the scientists, the habit of anchoring memories to objects didn’t just reflect the human mind. It may have helped build it.

The most striking evidence comes from the Sakhnin Valley in Lower Galilee.

Out of more than 200 surveyed handaxes at this Acheulean landscape, roughly ten were shaped around fossil imprints or unusual geological formations embedded in flint nodules.

Those nodules were harder to knap. Toolmakers normally avoided them.

But someone chose them anyway, then went out of their way to position the fossil or geological oddity at the center of the tool’s face.

The researchers argue that these choices were deliberate.

At Qesem Cave, small round colorful pebbles showed up starting around 400,000 years ago and kept showing up for the next 200,000 years. Their practical purpose remains unclear.

Then there’s something stranger. At Revadim, Jaljulia, and Qesem Cave, people picked up old flint tools that had been lying in the landscape long enough to develop a visible patina.

They resharpened the cutting edges, but they kept the old surfaces: the scars, the color changes, the story written into the stone.

Under magnification, wear traces show these recycled tools were barely used in their second life.

The fresh edges made them functional; preserving the patina may have held personal meaning.

The researchers call them “memory objects.”

Most discussions of weird prehistoric objects land on the same explanations. Art. Ritual. Status symbols. Shared cultural meaning.

This study does something different. It argues these objects worked at the personal level first.

No audience was required and no shared convention needed. Just one person who remembers what the stone means.

The authors lay out four things that separate a souvenir from a decoration: it’s tied to a specific episode in someone’s life, it helps verify a real experience against an imagined one, keeping it takes deliberate effort, and its meaning is private.

That last part matters. A keepsake points to something that happened to you, and only you need to know its meaning.

So how does holding a rock help you remember? The mechanism the authors point to is source monitoring. That refers to the brain’s way of sorting real experiences from dreams, hearsay, and imagination.

The hippocampus reconstructs episodes from partial cues. The perirhinal cortex handles familiarity, and the parahippocampal cortex fills in contextual detail.

The authors propose that a keepsake’s physical features could engage these memory systems together.

That matters more than it sounds. In small societies built on reciprocity, people who could reliably distinguish real experiences from imagined ones may have had an advantage.

Over enough generations, routine souvenir-keeping may have created a “souvenir niche” that favored brains better wired for autobiographical memory and sense of self.

“The disappearance of large animals, particularly elephants, required early humans to develop new hunting methods and become familiar with hunting grounds inhabited by animals such as fallow deer and wild cattle,” Barkai said.

“Objects taken from these places may have helped them maintain a personal connection with the places that would henceforth provide their livelihood.”

The elephant vanished from the Levant around 400,000 years ago. So did Homo erectus and  the Acheulean tool tradition.

Everything shifted. Smaller, faster prey meant learning new territories from scratch.

Familiar landscapes couldn’t be taken for granted anymore. Portable pieces of those places became worth carrying home.

The authors are clear this isn’t proof. Not every odd stone was a keepsake.

But the pattern is there. Objects with no clear practical role were carried far from their source, curated over long use-lives, and found near hearths and living areas rather than tool-making debris.

Aesthetics and raw material shortage don’t explain it cleanly.

“When early humans brought a colorful stone or an old tool they had found in the landscape back to their cave, that object may have helped them remember a significant event or encounter for the rest of their lives,” Ram said.

“In this way, keepsakes may have become a kind of ‘external memory device,’ supporting the evolution of brain mechanisms responsible for autobiographical memory, which is so characteristic of us as humans and so important in shaping our identity.”

The study is published in the journal Evolutionary Anthropology.

What to expect during SC26: Join theCUBE Nov. 17–19

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

High-performance computing and artificial intelligence are converging, driven by advances in exascale computing, accelerated systems, data storage and high-speed networking.

The convergence of these technologies is a prime topic for discussion during SC26 in Chicago this month. Major industry players, such as Dell Technologies Inc., Google LLC and Nvidia Corp., have pursued a set of initiatives in the HPC arena, and SC26 will be an occasion to learn more about how AI and HPC workloads are evolving to write supercomputing’s next chapter.

“SC26 provides an important opportunity to examine how the convergence of AI, high-performance computing and application development are reshaping enterprise infrastructure priorities,” said theCUBE Research’s Paul Nashawaty. “Organizations must connect high-performance infrastructure, accessible data and modern application development to move beyond experimentation and accelerate production-ready AI deployments. For Dell and the broader HPC ecosystem, SC26 offers a platform to demonstrate how integrated compute, storage and AI infrastructure can address these challenges.”

TheCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio, will be covering the latest HPC news and announcements from SC26 in Chicago, Nov. 17–19. Coverage will feature on-site reporting and exclusive interviews with industry leaders and analysts as theCUBE explores how supercomputing’s future is being shaped at the intersection of exascale systems and AI infrastructure. Discussion will focus on ways that accelerated computing is transforming research environments, while quantum-HPC integration and agentic AI push scientific discovery toward greater autonomy. (* Disclosure below.)

Dell’s ongoing collaboration with Nvidia and other organizations has yielded several new developments in the HPC arena over the past year, particularly in the area of quantum computing.

The two companies have been working with the U.S. Department of Energy to build and deliver the Doudna supercomputer at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. The system will use Nvidia’s Vera Rubin CPU-GPU platform and Quantum-X800 InfiniBand networking inside Dell’s integrated rack systems and PowerEdge servers to support large-scale scientific computing and data-intensive research.

In September, Dell announced a collaboration with silicon-spin qubit developer Diraq to integrate quantum processing units with HPC and AI server infrastructure. The joint effort is designed to integrate quantum processors with classical computing infrastructure in commercial data centers.

Moves such as these point toward a convergence of AI and quantum technologies, which will likely propel both forward as new platforms are built and deployed.

“The market for high-performance computing is meeting the AI factory at the point of innovation,” said theCUBE Research’s Dave Vellante. “HPC has always led the most challenging data problems, so this trend makes perfect sense. Quantum will be the next big inflection point and we’re seeing that new technologies are often complementary to existing infrastructure. We expect that to be the case between accelerated computing and quantum, particularly in the area of error correction.”

Hyperscaler Google Cloud is also building systems to support HPC demands for industries like manufacturing, health care and life sciences, weather forecasting and electronic design automation.

At the forefront of Google’s HPC initiative is the H4D VM, the company’s high-performance computing virtual machine. Introduced last year, the H4D VM is powered by fifth-generation AMD EPYC processors and offers whole-node VM performance of more than 12,000 GFLOPS and memory bandwidth exceeding 950 GB/s.

An example of how HPC is making inroads in daily life can be seen in the use of H4D VMs by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. In July, Google Cloud announced that it had been selected as the primary provider of high-performance computing infrastructure for the Weather and Climate Operational Supercomputing System.

The adoption of HPC to enable meteorologists and researchers to develop future forecasting models is an example of how systems technology will play a critical role in both supercomputing and AI infrastructure moving forward.

“Systems are the key in supercomputing advances,” said theCUBE Research’s John Furrier. “Different architectures. Different bets. Same underlying problem: How do you keep the multiplication engines doing useful work instead of waiting for data? That’s why I think the next era of AI infrastructure won’t be defined by the ‘best chip.’ It will be defined by the best system architecture.”

While the field of HPC is being defined by major players such as Dell and Google, other firms are making contributions as well in areas such as cloud infrastructure and analytics.

Vultr Inc. is building high-performance cloud computing infrastructure for AI, risk management, compliance and fraud prevention. The company is developing its platform across 33 global regions by leveraging CPUs, GPUs and open, composable architectures to position itself as a cloud infrastructure specialist in the agentic AI era.

High-performance computing is also generating a need for consistent data sharing and access across platforms. Omni Analytics Inc. is building a data collaboration solution that can connect directly to cloud data warehouses without needing to preload or duplicate data.

SC26 will offer an opportunity to not only assess the progress of HPC in the enterprise but also examine how AI is continuing to transform the compute stack to generate meaningful business results.

“As organizations invest in increasingly sophisticated AI environments, the competitive differentiator will be the ability to optimize the entire technology stack from data movement and storage performance to application development and deployment,” Nashawaty said. “The central SC26 narrative is that the future of AI supercomputing will be defined not simply by how much compute organizations deploy, but by how effectively they translate that investment into measurable business and scientific outcomes.”

It’s an ordinary Monday; you’re buttering toast, answering emails, and negotiating with the dog about when it’s time to go for a walk. Underneath all of it, a question keeps surfacing in your psyche: What happened to me?

Maybe three weeks ago, on a psilocybin retreat, you dissolved into the universe and haven’t entirely come back. Perhaps during surgery, you watched the medical team from somewhere near the ceiling and had a near-death experience. When you finally tell someone, their reaction in those first few seconds may shape how you carry this for years.

If this sounds familiar, you’re in larger company than you might think. Meditation teacher Jack Kornfield named this predicament decades ago; after the ecstasy comes the laundry.1 The experience is over, and the work of figuring out what to do with it has just begun.

The good news is that people have been mapping this terrain for a long time.

Spiritual teachers have identified various pathways that experiencers take following psychedelic and other events such as near-death, out-of-body, spontaneous spiritual awakening, kundalini, alien or UFO, and similar exceptional experiences that alter consciousness and challenge concepts of reality.

Carl Jung, who described his own near-death experience after a heart attack in 1944, saw such moments as encounters with the deeper layers of the psyche.2 In his view, what surfaces in these states, including the shadow and archetypal images shared across cultures, needs to be gradually integrated into conscious life. He called this lifelong process individuation, which means becoming more fully and authentically oneself.

Rhea White and Suzanne Brown mapped five stages experiencers tend to move through following exceptional experiences.3 First, the initiating event itself, during which the experiencers’ ordinary worlds are disrupted by an experience that leaves them questioning the nature of reality. Then the search for reconciliation, during which experiencers try to make sense of what happened to them by seeking out others who have had similar experiences and looking for frameworks that explain the event. In the between two worlds stage, people begin bringing their new understanding of reality into daily life but often feel pulled in two directions: unable to fully embrace this new reality, yet unable to return to the beliefs they held before.

In the experiential paradigm, as the experiencer moves to the next stage, they begin to accept what happened during the initiating event and fully integrate their new perspectives into their daily lives. Finally, the experiencer finds a new way of being in the world, characterized by alignment to one’s purpose and a desire to be of service. Once a psycho-spiritually transformative experience has been woven into this new way of living, White and Brown call it an "exceptional human experience." Rather than a ladder to climb, these stages are more like landmarks on a winding path; people may begin anywhere, double back, or revisit a stage more than once.

Early findings from a recent preprint study point to more nuanced pathways people may take after a psychedelic or other exceptional experience. One of us (Joelle Adams), along with colleagues Pascal Michael and David Luke, found that experiencers tend to follow one of four pathways: challenging, what goes up, slow flow, or deep surrender.4

On the challenging pathway, people struggle to make sense of what happened and may experience depersonalization, derealization, depression and anxiety, sleep disturbances, and other symptoms that undermine their well-being. Those on the what goes up pathway report an initial "high" following their experience, a period of positive mood, energy, clarity, and alignment with the universe. This is followed by a longer "low" when they face psychological, physical, relational, and spiritual challenges. They may chase the initial high, seeking repeat experiences to recapture those early feelings. Eventually, with professional support and meaningful changes in their lives, they can find a new equilibrium, much like White and Brown’s new way of being in the world stage.

Adams, Michael, and Luke also found that some experiencers follow an iterative pathway: the slow flow. Following the initial experience, they take their time to process what happened and integrate new perspectives and insights into their lives, coming to a place of equilibrium. Only then do they seek out or initiate new events or altered states of consciousness. Finally, those on the deep surrender pathway report integrating their experiences with relative ease, accepting and surrendering to new insights and concepts of reality. They often change their life circumstances, such as jobs and relationships, quite quickly, and report few challenges in returning to homeostasis.

For those who find an experience difficult to integrate, the company of others who have walked this path can be invaluable. Sharing the experience with a trusted friend, or with a therapist who is open to non-ordinary states and approaches them without pathologizing, can provide the steadiness needed to make meaning of a reality-altering event. Reading, podcasts, and workshops can offer both frameworks for understanding and the quiet reassurance that others have travelled similar terrain, though some people find this material overwhelming and benefit from taking it in gradually. Solitude, time in nature, journaling, and creative expression are also frequently described as essential companions to integration.

Discernment matters too. Confiding in someone who dismisses or misunderstands the experience can deepen the isolation it so often brings. Likewise, rushing back into the demands of daily life without pausing to reflect can leave the experience unprocessed and, at times, psychologically destabilizing. Chasing a return to the original high, or clinging rigidly to any single framework for explaining what happened, can also keep people stuck. Meditation and other spiritual practices can sometimes aid integration, though for some they may reactivate difficult emotional states; it’s often wise to proceed gradually and with compassionate support.

In the end, integration doesn't fully happen on the idealized spiritual mountaintop. More commonly, people learn to let their insights shape how they live, work, and love, becoming, in Jung’s sense, more fully themselves. With support, time, patience, and conscious intentionality, these experiences can lead to greater well-being, deeper empathy, and a quieter fear of death. The toast still needs buttering and the dog still needs walking, but the difference is who is doing it.

Where does Kane rank among all-time England greats?

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Harry Kane is now England's most-capped men's player as well as his country's record goalscorer, but harder to quantify is where these achievements place him among the Three Lions' greatest ever players.

This is a tricky debate with so many variables and a timescale as old as the game itself and, to my mind, not merely a determination of a player's individual ability but also a measure of their impact upon the nation's psyche.

Ballon d'Or winners Stanley Matthews, Kevin Keegan and Michael Owen would surely have a shout if the criteria was such, not to mention genius technicians such as Glenn Hoddle, Chris Waddle and John Barnes.

Likewise, if any England boss managed to extract the best from Paul Scholes, Steven Gerrard and Frank Lampard they would be surely making a classic late dart from midfield.

But to qualify as one of the greatest England players of all time, you need the above ability combined with an impactful overall contribution - those memorable moments and an element of longevity - in a Three Lions shirt.

It might be controversial then to leave out Geoff Hurst, who produced the most recognisable performance in England history, while the man he replaced, Jimmy Greaves, would be a shoo-in were it not for injury earlier in the tournament.

It is also hard to rank the contribution of those whose best football came outside of major tournaments and before the dawn of continental competitions - see Eddie Hapgood, Tom Finney, Nat Lofthouse, Dixie Dean et al.

There are only 10 spots, after all, so here we go.

Spice Boy. Celebrity. Galactico. There is no argument David Beckham is one of the most iconic England players of all time, but he backed it up on the pitch, too.

Beckham was the face of the Three Lions for a generation, and to his credit managed to overcome the vicious fallout of his red card against Argentina in 1998.

At times he seemed to single-handedly carry England, most notably scoring a 93rd-minute free-kick against Greece to qualify for the 2002 World Cup.

Beckham's former team-mate Gary Neville called him the "most influential player out of England in transforming football".

It's true. On top of 115 caps, Beckham was part of Manchester United's 1999 Treble-winning outfit - finishing Ballon d'Or runner-up - before joining Real Madrid.

His profile was truly global with spells at LA Galaxy, AC Milan and Paris St-Germain.

David Beckham scored 17 goals for England, including five free-kicks

There's arguably no England star fans forged an emotional attachment with more than Gazza - crying with him in 1990, rejoicing in his spectacular effort against Scotland and desperately willing him to connect during that Euro 96 semi-final defeat by Germany.

Gascoigne won 57 caps and scored 10 goals. At his peak, he was captivating - transcending football as a cultural phenomenon during the summers of '90 and '96, having joined a star-studded Serie A between those tournaments.

Creative, unpredictable, vulnerable. He was a loveable but flawed genius.

He suffered long-term injuries as England missed out on Euro 92 and the World Cup in the USA, but helped the Three Lions qualify for France 98 before being dropped from the final squad, famously wrecking boss Glenn Hoddle's office.

Gascoigne, Poborsky , McCoist - Goals that defined Euro 96

Goalscorers often grab the headlines but I've tried to credit players across positions and eras. From a defensive point of view Ashley Cole, arguably England's best-ever full-back, was close to making it.

But instead it's the great Billy Wright, the first men's player to surpass a century of international caps, including a remarkable 70 successive appearances over eight years.

Wright captained his country on 90 occasions, a joint-record recently broken by Kane, taking in three World Cups with impressive longevity for his era.

He was a true one-club man, winning three league titles, the FA Cup and becoming unofficial "world champions" with Wolves in 1954 - helping inspire the birth of the European Cup - while finishing Ballon d'Or runner-up to Alfredo di Stefano in 1957.

Billy Wright captained England 90 times - a record shared with Bobby Moore until Harry Kane recently surpassed the pair

It feels a little premature to include a 23-year-old with the bulk of his England career ahead of him, but that shows the impact Jude Bellingham has already made for England and Real Madrid.

A Champions League winner, the midfielder has also appeared at four major tournaments for the Three Lions, becoming the youngest player to do so at the delayed Euro 2020, aged 17.

But his role in helping carry England to the final two years ago and a third-place finish at last summer's World Cup really make him stand out.

Bellingham has delivered those moments a nation collectively remembers. The last-gasp overhead kick against Slovakia in 2024, a quickfire double at the Azteca against Mexico this summer and 93rd-minute quarter-final winner against Norway.

His seven goals at this World Cup were more than any previous Three Lions player in a single edition, the solo effort against France a thing of beauty.

The former Birmingham and Dortmund man doesn't turn 24 until June but has already collected 61 England caps and netted 14 times. As Roy Keane put it, Bellingham has the potential to be one of the "greatest of all time".

Ten of Bellingham's 14 England goals have come at major tournaments

In terms of natural ability, Wayne Rooney is among England's greatest ever talents.

Bursting on to the scene at Euro 2004 as an electric 18-year-old, having become England's youngest player and scorer the previous year, convinced fans and media the teen sensation could carry the Three Lions to glory in Portugal.

He bounced World Cup winner Lilian Thuram around in England's opener against France in a glimpse of his precocious strength, before scoring twice against both Switzerland and Croatia.

Who knows how far England would have gone had Rooney not been injured against Portugal in the quarter-final?

He was unable to replicate the impact of his first Euros - a red card at World Cup 2006, suspension for the Euro 2012 group stage, seven goals across six major tournaments - but was an all-rounder who brought more than just goals.

Rooney also won it all with Manchester United, becoming the club's all-time top scorer, and finished his 15-year England career with 53 goals from 120 caps.

Rooney burst on to the scene as an 18-year-old at Euro 2004

There has to be a place for a goalkeeper, and Gordon Banks is the safest pair of hands.

A World Cup winner in 1966, his save from Pele four years later against Brazil is one of the most iconic of all time. Pele called him a "goalkeeper with magic".

Banks spent the majority of his career at Leicester and Stoke, winning a League Cup with each, and earned 73 England caps overall between 1963 and 1972.

His incredible athleticism and reflexes saw the shot-stopper named Fifa's best goalkeeper for five successive years between 1966 and 1971.

Banks and Pele became good friends following the goalkeeper's iconic save in 1970

Gary Lineker's early club career did not deliver the silverware as his goals deserved, netting 40 times but finishing league and FA Cup runner-up in his one season at Everton before joining Barcelona.

He scored a Clasico hat-trick and won the Copa del Rey and Cup Winner's Cup, however, before adding an FA Cup with Tottenham in 1991.

Lineker netted 283 club goals (and famously no cards) in total across spells with Leicester, Everton, Barcelona, Spurs and Nagoya Grampus Eight.

But as a player Lineker is most renowned for his contribution in an England shirt, winning the Golden Boot as they reached the World Cup quarter-finals in 1986 - finishing Ballon d'Or runner-up that year - before netting another four as the Three Lions reached the semi-finals in 1990.

Lineker equalised against West Germany before England crashed out on penalties.

However, having missed a spot-kick against Brazil, he finished one short of Bobby Charlton's elusive goal record when his international career ended after Euro 92.

Gary Lineker scored 48 goals in 80 games for England

Harry Kane is undoubtedly England's most important and influential player of a generation - it is hard to imagine how this Three Lions side functions once the skipper hangs up his boots.

The Bayern Munich striker, complete with Ballon d'Or, has been scoring goals for his country for almost 12 years, the first just 79 seconds into his debut after coming off the bench against Lithuania in March 2015.

Rooney's goalscoring benchmark is long in his rear view mirror - you wouldn't bank against Kane doubling his nearest rival's figure once his England days are over.

They have come on the biggest stage, too - 21 in 36 appearances from three World Cups and European Championships apiece, winning the World Cup Golden Boot in 2018.

Kane has come closer to winning a major tournament than any England men's captain since Bobby Moore - losing two World Cup semi-finals and back-to-back Euros finals. Team-mate Bellingham called him the "greatest player that has ever played for England".

"I think we'll appreciate him even more in another 10, 15, 20 years when you look back and see what he has achieved," said ex-England goalkeeper Paul Robinson.

In many ways, Bobby Moore would be the complete modern-day defender. Cool as you like under pressure, elegant in possession, a great passing range off both feet and, as his West Ham and England team-mate Geoff Hurst said: "Nobody out-ran him and nobody out-jumped him."

Moore's positioning and timing was perfect, that iconic tackle on Jairzinho at the 1970 World Cup the finest example of his poise and precision when defending.

He was also a composed and influential leader. A player built for the big occasions. England boss Sir Alf Ramsey called him: "My lieutenant on the field, a cool, calculated footballer I could trust with my life."

The Barking-born centre-back won the FA Cup and European Cup Winners' Cup with West Ham but his finest moment was leading England to glory in 1966. He was only 25 at the time, having become his country's youngest captain three years earlier.

In 1970, after the Bogota bracelet incident, Moore was imperious as England reached the quarter-finals before bowing out to West Germany.

It was, however, the final group game against Brazil, the most exciting side on the planet, that produced some of the most recognisable and renowned images in sporting history - not only Moore's tackle and Banks' save but the defender swapping shirts with Pele after the final whistle.

"The greatest defender I ever played against," was the Brazilian's verdict.

Bobby Moore is the only man to captain the Three Lions to World Cup glory

For more than a generation, Sir Bobby's England goalscoring record remained unmatched. It took 55 years from his 49th and final strike against Colombia in 1970 until Rooney surpassed him with his half century in 2015.
