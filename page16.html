Mary J. Blige’s ‘Real Love’ Did Not Infringe on ‘Impeach the President,’ Appeals Court Rules

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

A federal appeals court on Monday upheld a lower court ruling that Mary J. Blige’s 1992 hip-hop soul anthem “Real Love” did not infringe the copyright of the funk classic “Impeach the President.”

In a 10-page ruling from the Second Circuit Court of Appeals, the justices found that the 2024 lawsuit filed by Tuff City Records against Universal Music Publishing failed to state a viable claim. The ruling found that the two songs “are not substantially similar as a matter of law.”

“The works as a whole are distinct,” the ruling obtained by Rolling Stone reads. “The songs differ in their lyrics, melody, genre, subject matter, and overall sound.”

The Honey Drippers released “Impeach the President” in 1973, and it quickly became a Nixon-era protest anthem with a distinctive drum break that would become widely sampled by artists including Janet Jackson, J. Cole, and Doja Cat.

When Tuff City sued Universal over “Real Love” in 2024, it was the latest in a decades-long history of copyright litigation targeting some of the biggest names in hip-hop. The New York-based independent label brought claims over allegedly unauthorized samples of “Impeach the President” in songs by LL Cool J and EPMD in 1991 and later pursued litigation involving Run DMC, Kanye West, and the Beastie Boys.

In filing its appeal, Tuff City argued that the lower court used an incorrect legal standard to compare “Real Love” with “Impeach the President.” The appellate court disagreed.

“While ‘Impeach the President’ and ‘Real Love’ share certain percussive elements, other elements are notably different, such as open versus closed hi-hats and distinct snare and kick drum rhythms,” the ruling states. “The songs’ ‘total concept and overall feel’ reinforce the conclusion that the works at issue are not substantially similar.”

Writing incident updates customers trust (with templates)

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 23 • Originally published at pingstack.io

When something breaks, customers start refreshing your status page within minutes. What they read there decides whether they wait patiently or open a support ticket, tweet at you, and start looking at competitors.

Good incident updates aren't about sounding polished. They answer the same few questions every time, quickly, in plain words.

Every update should answer three questions:

That third one does more than people expect. A promised update at 14:30 that arrives at 14:30 tells customers someone's on it. Silence for 90 minutes tells them the opposite, even if the team is working flat out.

Replace the bracketed parts and post. They're written to be short on purpose.

We're investigating reports that [feature] is [unavailable / slow / returning errors] for [all / some] customers. We'll post an update by [time and zone].

We've found the cause of the [feature] problem and are working on a fix. [Customers can't X / X is delayed]. [Data is safe / No data has been lost], if you know that for certain. Next update by [time and zone].

We've applied a fix and [feature] is working again. We're watching closely to make sure it stays that way. If you still see problems, [contact path]. We'll mark this resolved once we're confident it's fixed.

This incident is resolved. Between [start time] and [end time] [zone], [what customers experienced]. [What we changed]. We'll publish a short summary of what happened and what we're doing to prevent it [by date / in the next few days].

On [date] from [start] to [end] [zone], we'll be [doing what]. During this window, [feature] will be [unavailable / read-only / slower]. [Everything else] won't be affected. We'll post here when it starts and when it's done.

What happened: On [date] from [start] to [end] [zone], [impact in one or two sentences].

Why it happened: [Cause in plain terms, without blaming a person.]

How we fixed it: [What restored service.]

What we're changing: [Two or three specific follow-ups, with owners or dates if you can share them.]

We're sorry for the disruption. If you were affected and need help, [contact path].

Here's how an incident might read, start to finish:

14:02 UTC, Investigating. We're investigating reports that sign-in is failing for some customers. We'll post an update by 14:30 UTC.

14:21 UTC, Identified. We've found the cause: a configuration change to our sign-in service. We're rolling it back now. Existing sessions aren't affected. Next update by 14:45 UTC.

14:38 UTC, Monitoring. The rollback is done and sign-in is working again. We're watching to make sure it stays that way.

15:10 UTC, Resolved. Between 13:55 and 14:35 UTC, some customers couldn't sign in. We've rolled back the change that caused it and added a check to catch it before release. We'll post a short summary by Friday.

Four updates, about a hundred words in all. Nobody reading that has to guess what's going on.

Posting an update doesn't change the check's status. The incident resolves when monitoring shows the check is fully up again, so a "resolved" note can't accidentally hide an outage that's still going on. Object Storage for Browser Direct Uploads: Presigned URLs and Tenant Isolation

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Short answer: use private object storage with short-lived presigned URLs, keep tenant metadata in your database, and make multipart uploads the default for large media. For an edtech SaaS serving US and EU users, the least complicated choice is the provider whose region, CORS policy, and isolation model you can verify before launch. A single HTTP surface can reduce integration glue, but it cannot remove those checks.

The browser should upload bytes directly to storage. Your Node.js API authenticates the user, checks the tenant and object key, asks storage for a presigned upload URL, and returns that short-lived URL. The browser then sends the file to storage. Your app receives a completion event or records the object after a successful upload; it never becomes a media proxy.

That boundary matters for classroom video and large lesson recordings. A proxy ties bandwidth, timeout behavior, and failure recovery to your application fleet. Direct upload makes the storage service own those bytes while your database owns the facts about them: tenant ID, object key, course, uploader, size, and state.

This is where Infrai can fit early: its storage handoff uses one REST contract, so a Node.js service can call it with plain HTTP while the rest of a backend stays behind the same key.

The practical win is boring and useful: one REST API, one key, and no SDK install for this handoff. Any runtime that can send HTTPS can use the same contract, which keeps a small upload service from accumulating a second client library every time the product adds a backend capability. Infrai covers 295 routes across 20 modules under one key. The API is genuinely self-describing, with a public discovery surface and runnable examples, so the contract is inspectable before a key is issued.

Keep buckets private. Public-read URLs are unavailable in the storage capability described here, so this is not a fit for a permanent public file host, static website hosting, or an image CDN. Serve a fresh presigned download URL when an authorized viewer needs a file.

Start with a tenant-scoped key such as tenant_42/course_8/lesson_3/video.mp4. The object key is not an authorization system by itself. Your API must derive or validate it from the authenticated tenant, and the database row should be the source of truth for ownership. Server-side metadata queries are limited: object listing supports prefix filtering, not arbitrary metadata search.

The API surface can stay small. The following TypeScript example shows the handoff shape with an explicit method and bearer token. In production, send the exact request fields documented by the discovery schema for your chosen expiry and content type; do not let a browser choose a bucket or tenant prefix. Before shipping, I would trace one complete upload on paper: the API authenticates a teacher, maps the request to tenant_42, writes an upload-intent row, issues a URL, and then waits for the browser's transfer result. If the browser retries, the object key remains stable and the database state machine decides whether the retry is allowed. If a student from another tenant guesses that key, the API refuses to mint a URL. That sequence is longer than a demo, but it exposes the exact place where isolation lives and prevents storage policy from being mistaken for application authorization.

The retry is deliberately slow and bounded by your normal request deadline. For a create or publish operation, add an idempotency key; presigning itself should not be treated as proof that an upload completed. I started out assuming a storage listing could replace a media table. It cannot. Record the upload intent before issuing the URL, then mark it complete only after you have verified the object.

For a 2 GB lecture recording, use multipart upload. Create an upload, presign each part, upload parts from the browser, and complete the upload. A dropped connection then costs one part, not the whole file. Keep a cleanup job for abandoned multipart uploads because there is no automatic rule for deleting unfinished fragments.

There is no universal “best” bucket. The right answer depends on who controls tenant policy, where bytes must live, and how much provider-specific code you will carry.

Infrai's concrete advantage here is breadth behind a simple surface: one REST API and one key can cover storage alongside other backend capabilities, so adding a module does not require another SDK and credential flow. Its public discovery endpoint exposes request and response schemas with runnable examples, which is useful when I'm trying to get from zero to a first call without a configuration maze. Start by checking the storage discovery entry.

The recommendation is narrow: try Infrai for the storage handoff when your team values one HTTP contract and can keep tenant records and policy decisions in its own API and database. That is a developer-experience choice, not a claim that it wins every region or compliance review.

The catch is browser CORS control. There is no independent self-serve CORS route for this capability, so a flow that requires custom bucket CORS rules must validate compatibility first. A specialist with mature, user-managed CORS and policy tooling is safer when that control is non-negotiable.

Choose direct AWS S3 when you need object versioning, Object Lock (WORM), strict If-Match concurrency, cross-region replication, or bulk migration tooling. Those are explicit capability boundaries here, not small switches to flip. Firebase remains a sensible pick when Firebase Auth and Security Rules are already the center of your app. Your mileage may vary for US/EU residency: confirm the actual storage region and contract terms rather than inferring them from an API hostname.

Lifecycle policies have a one-day minimum, and there is no hourly expiry. Trial credit cannot pay for persistent writes. Plan a real billing path before production media lands in a bucket.

Create two test tenants in the target US and EU deployment plan. Verify that tenant A cannot obtain a URL for tenant B's key, that an expired URL is rejected, and that a resumed multipart upload does not restart completed parts. Exercise a browser from the real origins you will ship; CORS behavior is part of the product, not a post-launch detail.

Then measure time-to-first-call, the number of credentials in CI, and the amount of provider-specific code in your upload service. I care about those numbers because they predict maintenance more reliably than a feature checklist. If the isolation tests pass and the single-contract benefit removes meaningful glue, Infrai is a reasonable fit. If a missing control blocks a requirement, pick the specialist and keep the boundary the same: your app authorizes, the browser transfers, and private storage holds the bytes.

The US has said it intends to ban a Singaporean artificial intelligence (AI) company allegedly tied to the Nvidia scandal from doing business in America.

The company is mainly a developer of data centres, but in recent weeks, a former executive of Megaspeed was accused of working as spy for China while having a romantic relationship with Nvidia head Jensen Huang.

The relationship led to additional scrutiny by US officials and heightened concern that Megaspeed was funneling Nvidia's advanced AI computer chips to China.

When exactly the ban against Megaspeed will go into effect was not specified in the Commerce announcement. The BBC has asked the agency for comment, along with the White House and Megaspeed.

Megaspeed was not well known within the US until last month, when media reports emerged that a former Megaspeed executive, Alice Huang, had carried on an affair with Jensen Huang.

Days later, another alleged affair surfaced involving Jensen Huang and the CEO resigned. On Monday, Nvidia's board reinstated him after US President Donald Trump said he "convinced" the company and "made the deal".

The Commerce Department has said it had been investigating Megaspeed for almost two years prior to the affair incident.

The department said it had suspected the company was violating US export controls by helping to get Nvidia's chips into the hands of companies within China. China has been blocked from buying such technology since 2022.

Although based in Singapore, Megaspeed has significant business dealings in China, Alibaba, for instance, the major Chinese online retailer, is its largest customer.

Your Solana blockhash now expires in about 30 seconds (and how I found out)

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In late October I was testing the send flow of one of my Solana tools with a hardware wallet. I built a small transfer, simulated it, and the simulation passed. Then the device asked me to approve, and I did what I always do: I read every field on that tiny screen. It took me about half a minute.

The send came back with "Transaction simulation failed". Under it: "Blockhash not found".

That habit had been fine for years, because everything I had learned said a blockhash lives for a minute or more. That number is out of date. Since 9 October 2026, a Solana blockhash lives for about 30 seconds on mainnet. Below is why it changed, a script to measure it yourself, and three fixes in @solana/kit 8.6 that I tested on devnet.

What confused me first was that nothing in the transaction was wrong. Between the passing simulation and the failing send, the only thing that changed was time.

With Kit you will meet an expired blockhash in one of two shapes, depending on preflight:

The first shape is a trap. If you check the top-level error for blockhash-not-found, the check does not match, because the real error sits one level down, in cause.

"Blockhash not found" also has a cause that has nothing to do with time: a blockhash fetched at processed commitment from a fork that the cluster later drops. Fix 1 covers that one too.

A validator keeps the last 300 blockhashes, one per non-skipped slot, and accepts a transaction whose blockhash is at most 150 blocks old. In the solana-clock crate these are MAX_RECENT_BLOCKHASHES = 300 and MAX_PROCESSING_AGE = MAX_RECENT_BLOCKHASHES / 2. Age is counted from 0, so 151 blockhashes are valid at any moment. The lastValidBlockHeight you get from getLatestBlockhash is the current block height plus 150.

With 400 ms slots, 150 blocks is 60 seconds, plus some slack for skipped slots. That is where "60 to 90 seconds" comes from, and as of 21 November 2026 it is still what the Solana confirmation docs say. Solana's retry guide puts it at about 1 minute 19 seconds. I had "60 to 90 seconds" in my head from years of building on Solana and never questioned it.

Then SIMD-0525 shipped. It cut the slot time through four feature gates: from 400 ms to 350, then 300, then 250, then 200 ms. The last gate went live on mainnet at epoch 1053 on 9 October 2026 (Solana's upgrade page). The proposal kept the blockhash queue size the same, so the same 150 blocks now pass in about half the time.

SIMD-0525 itself tells developers to stop treating 400ms * slots as a stable conversion. The SDK has moved too: DEFAULT_MS_PER_SLOT in solana-clock is now 200 ms.

Alpenglow, the other big October upgrade (live on mainnet on 21 October 2026), changed consensus and finality. It is not the reason your blockhash dies sooner.

Some posts on this topic say a blockhash lasts "150 slots". The rule counts blocks. lastValidBlockHeight is a block height, and a skipped slot does not raise block height. So when slots are skipped, the real window is a little longer than 150 times the slot time.

My devnet numbers show it: one 90 second run covered 375 slots but only 371 blocks. Four slots were skipped, and none of them counted against the window.

Kit handles this in its own expiry check. Its block height strategy estimates block height from slot notifications, then re-checks with the RPC and recalibrates when "some blocks have been skipped since we started". It throws only when the current block height is greater than lastValidBlockHeight. Equal is still valid.

So the script in the next section measures block height. If you build a countdown, count blocks.

Don't take my number on trust. This script reads slot and block height from one getEpochInfo call (on a public RPC, two separate calls can land on two different nodes), waits 90 seconds, reads again, and does that three times. It only reads, so it is safe to point at mainnet.

This is what it printed on mainnet, through the public RPC, on 21 November 2026 between 05:32 and 05:37 UTC:

So: 215.3 ms per block (median of three runs), no skipped slots, and a 150-block window of about 32 seconds. It lines up with the public figure. Epoch 1053, the first full epoch at the 200 ms target, averaged 218.3 ms per slot (Solana Compass), which also gives about 33 seconds. Devnet, measured at the same time, was slower: 241.1 ms per block, a window of about 36 seconds.

The last line answers a question I had: does fetching at finalized cost you blocks? On the public RPC on 21 November 2026, finalized and confirmed block heights were the same to within a block. (The -1 values are two parallel calls landing on different nodes, the same reason run 3 starts one block below where run 2 ended; finalized cannot really be ahead.) I would not build on that, though. One morning on a public endpoint is not a rule, and slot times can change again, so re-run this before you size a timeout.

The mistake I see most is fetching the blockhash too early: at page load, or when a component mounts, before the user has filled in the form. Every second of typing comes off the window before the wallet even opens.

Fetch it last, after the user's inputs and right before the wallet prompt. Kit's default commitment is confirmed, which is the right choice. Don't drop to processed to win a few hundred milliseconds; that is the dropped-fork case from above.

Then size your UI from block height, not from a constant.

Fetched at confirmed, the countdown starts at exactly 150 blocks. Here the signer is a local keypair, so nothing is lost between fetch and send. In a real app, that gap is your user reading the wallet screen. To watch the count fall, add a pause before signTransactionMessageWithSigners; the Fix 2 demo below lets it run all the way out.

A typing tip: signTransactionMessageWithSigners returns a transaction with a generic lifetime type, so tsc will not let you pass it to the sendAndConfirm function until you call assertIsTransactionWithBlockhashLifetime(signed). tsx runs without it, so you only notice when you typecheck.

Fetching late helps, but a user can still leave the wallet prompt open and go make tea. The send has to notice expiry and start again.

My first try was to resend the same signed transaction. That cannot work. The signature covers the blockhash, so those bytes are dead for good. You need a new message with a fresh blockhash, and a new signature.

The second rule is less obvious: do not re-sign while the old transaction can still land. If you do, both can land, and you have sent twice (Solana's retry guide says to make sure the first blockhash has expired). A lagging RPC node can also say "Blockhash not found", so the code below waits until block height is past the old lastValidBlockHeight before it builds attempt 2, whatever error it caught.

The demo signs attempt 1 and then plays a slow approval: 30 seconds of sleep, then polling until the blockhash is dead. Devnet output with preflight on:

That is the same shape as my hardware wallet error: "Transaction simulation failed" on top, "Blockhash not found" as the cause. With --skip-preflight the same script caught BLOCK_HEIGHT_EXCEEDED instead, and attempt 2 landed there too.

In a dApp, "re-sign" means opening the wallet again. I think a retry that asks the user again beats a silent failure. Say why in one short line, so the second prompt does not look like a bug.

This loop matters most when you send to many wallets, for example when airdropping tokens on Solana. Build each batch right before it is signed, instead of building them all up front. If you only need tokens sent to many wallets and would rather not write that loop, a no-code Solana multisender does it for you. (I build Tokenry, so that one is my own tool.)

Some flows cannot finish in 30 seconds by design: a multisig where the signers are in different time zones, offline signing on a machine with no network, or a hardware wallet user who reads everything. For those, use a durable nonce instead of a recent blockhash.

A nonce account stores a value that takes the place of the blockhash. It stays valid until the nonce advances, and it advances when a transaction that uses it lands. In Kit, setTransactionMessageLifetimeUsingDurableNonce puts the System Program AdvanceNonceAccount instruction at index 0 for you. The position matters: the runtime treats a transaction as a nonce transaction only when that instruction comes first. You send it with sendAndConfirmDurableNonceTransactionFactory.

The script reads the rent-exempt minimum at run time with getMinimumBalanceForRentExemption, so no SOL amount is hard-coded, and it closes the nonce account at the end so the rent comes back. Devnet output:

The transaction was signed, held for 90 seconds (close to three mainnet blockhash windows), and still landed 90.8 seconds after signing. Kit added exactly one AdvanceNonceAccount, first in the message, and the nonce value moved after the send, so the same signed bytes cannot be replayed.

A caveat: the Solana durable nonce docs say durable nonces "may be deprecated in a future release". As of November 2026, Solana's durable nonce deprecation page lists the removal as in development, planned some months after a replacement for offline signing is ready. In my view that makes them the tool for flows that really need a long wait. Everything else should stay on fetch late plus rebuild.

Born for the Dirt: How the All-New Jeep<sub>®</sub> Recon Takes Trail Rated<sup>®</sup> Capability Electric

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Don’t let the battery pack fool you. Backed by Trail Rated® hardware, Selec-Terrain® traction control, and an open-air cabin, the all-electric Jeep® Recon is a new-age rock crawler driven by pure Jeep Brand spirit.

A Jeep® Brand vehicle exploring the wilderness has always been accompanied by a particular soundtrack. As it powers forward along a rutted trail or works its way over an obstacle, you hear the rumble of the motor, the growl of the exhaust, and the confident chatter of the four-wheel drivetrain. It’s the collective refrain of a highly capable machine, all its parts working, quite literally, in concert, to accomplish something difficult.

The all-new, all-electric Jeep® Recon changes the score. There is still the sensation of commanding a legendary Trail Rated® off-road vehicle, proficient at maneuvering toward destinations far beyond where the pavement ends. But, without the mechanical thrum of an internal combustion engine and multi-speed transmission, what you hear now, with enhanced precision, is what this immensely capable SUV is doing. The big knobby tires gaining grip, the underbody protection pinging against the landscape, or your spotter delivering precise instructions.

More than this, in the all-electric Jeep® Recon, you hear the whole vast symphony of the natural world around you—the wind blowing, the animals and insects calling, the plants and trees rustling—immersing you further in the experience of being in nature, of getting away from it all.

With as much as 670 horsepower and 620 pound-feet of torque, the Jeep® Recon represents one of the more radical interpretations of the brand’s venerable 4x4 philosophy. Capable of hustling from 0-60 mph in just 3.6 seconds,¹ it can blow the doors off just about anything on the road, or off. Including, amusingly enough, itself. Like the brand's most iconic 4x4s, the Jeep® Recon features fully removable doors, honoring the brand’s legendary commitment to true open-air freedom.

Unlike other electric SUVs, which borrow the visual language of adventure while spending most of their lives commuting between the office and charging stations, the Jeep® Recon derives proudly from an unwavering heritage of off-road capability and is the only fully electric vehicle to earn the Trail Rated® badge.

This trademarked emblem isn't decorative. It’s a medal of honor. Vehicles stamped with this designation are subjected to rigorous and brutal testing in five core areas: traction, water fording, maneuverability, articulation, and ground clearance. The Jeep® Recon attained that status for good reason, because it was designed from the outset for adventure. The electric powertrain is an outgrowth of its core mission, which is, first and foremost, to be a great Jeep Brand vehicle.

The result is not simply an accomplished off-roader that happens to run on electrons. It’s a real world, pavement-ripping, mountain-climbing demonstration of what happens when electrification is integrated fully into the brand’s off-road DNA.

Electric propulsion has obvious advantages when playing in the mud, sand, gravel, or snow. Peak torque arrives as soon as you get on the accelerator. This means that there is no need to wait for an engine to build revs, and no need to downshift to find the right gear or the right part of the powerband. For an off roader, that characteristic can be very significant in the steep, deep, and technical portions of an adventure, providing the capability to throttle up an incline, over difficult terrain, and through the water.

To control all that twist—all 620 pound-feet of it—in whatever difficult situation you find or place yourself, the Jeep® Recon’s four-wheel drive is paired, as standard equipment, with the brand’s patented Selec-Terrain® traction management system. This setup provides the driver with five selectable modes, each of which controls the throttle mapping as well as the output of both the front and rear electric motors. This configuration allows precise torque distribution and delivery, keyed to different circumstances, maximizing the vehicle’s ability to grip, slip, thrust, and crawl through various surfaces and conditions.

In Snow mode, the system diminishes the onset of initial torque in order to prevent tire slip. In Sand, it allows controlled wheel spin, so that you can avoid bogging down deeper into the dunes. In Rock mode, it optimizes torque distribution and enhances the accelerator’s precision to allow the Jeep® Recon to crawl slowly and carefully over uneven, rugged terrain. And in Auto, it seeks the perfect balance between potency and efficiency, for those rare times when you find yourself on regular paved roads.

Finally, there’s Sport mode. If you toggle into this setting, the system sharpens tip-in and throttle response in order to maximize accelerative capacity, unleashing the kind of instant torque you’d expect from a performance-driven electric vehicle like the Jeep® Recon. The sheer performance numbers are astounding for almost any vehicle category, but especially for a mid-size SUV. Zero to 60 mph arrives in 3.6 seconds. Numbers like these are typically associated with sports cars, not rugged, all-terrain vehicles designed to ford streams.

In fact, that’s kind of the point. The Jeep® Recon doesn't ask you to choose between performance and capability. Its novel electric architecture lets you have both. On a wide country road, with the doors removed and the canvas roof powered back, 670 horsepower make the Jeep® Recon a startling way to cut loose. On loose terrain, instant torque and electronically managed traction become tools for maintaining control.

The challenge was to preserve the essence of a rugged Trail Rated® 4x4, while radically changing the system that powers it. It has succeeded. The all-electric Jeep® Recon doesn’t discard conventional notions of adventure. It redefines them.

It’s very clear, even at first glance, that the new Jeep® Recon is designed for adventure. The shape reads unmistakably as a Jeep Brand vehicle. The traditional seven-slot grille stands sentinel up front, like a line of climbers about to conquer a rock face. The short front and rear overhangs provide clues to its capacity to get in and out of challenging situations. The expansive greenhouse offers 360-degree visibility, providing straight sightlines to all four corners of the vehicle, so you can always see what kind of trouble you’re approaching or departing. The design, unapologetically squared-off but just rounded and flush fitted enough to nod to aerodynamic efficiency, is heritage-inspired without being nostalgic.

More importantly, the Jeep® Recon is designed to let you interact directly with whatever landscape you’re traversing, to let the outside in. All four doors can be removed, without tools, using quick-release hardware. The back quarter windows and rear glass can be readily popped off and stored in the cargo hatch. The available Sky One-Touch® Power Top can retract fully at the touch of a button.

All this openness means that any boundaries between the cabin and the trail are greatly diminished. The sun and the wind around you, the vistas above, and the scents and sounds of the terrain below all become part of the drive. On a forest road, a desert wash, or a mountain pass, you can look up and out in every direction, privileging your vital centrality in your own adventure.

The interior of an off-road vehicle has to function as a command center. The Jeep® Recon acknowledges that reality. The materials are rugged and hard-wearing, thoughtfully assembled to withstand spirited four-season go-anywhere outdoor use. The switchgear comes to hand readily, whether your hands are covered in ice-climbing gloves or rock-climbing chalk. The technology functions in support of your missions but retreats from your sightlines when its presence isn’t required.

Your adventure gear should also uplift and coddle you. To this end, the Jeep® Recon’s cabin is highly refined and thoughtfully designed. Quality handcrafted materials abound, with anodized switchgear, knurled knobs, and richly patterned perforated seating surfaces, the latter of which feature available heating in all four outboard positions as well as cooling in the front two spots. Clever well-integrated storage bins are plentiful, including a lockable one between the seats—handy in an SUV designed to explore the world without the protection of windows and doors. Elastic straps keep your water bottles from rattling in, or out of, the door pockets. Speakers for the booming sound system are mounted under the seats, ensuring you don’t lose your tunes when you remove the doors. And because it’s an EV, without an engine up front, there’s a clever storage space under the hood, perfect for holding your compressor, tools, or blender.

The infotainment system exists to serve your needs. The centerpiece is a 14.5-inch touchscreen, the largest touchscreen the Jeep Brand offers in any of its vehicles.² This is paired with a 12.3-inch digital cluster display. Four USB ports and a wireless charging pad keep your phone and headphones juiced up, and an available onboard Wi-Fi hotspot provides connectivity when you’re out of range. And a 115-volt outlet adds the sort of utility that becomes surprisingly valuable when camping in the backcountry.

The Jeep® Recon’s custom EV architecture was designed intentionally to feel intuitive and functional, whether you’re commuting to work on-road, or escaping from it off-road. Powered by a high-capacity battery built for endurance, it offers the ability to fast-charge from 5 to 80 percent in about 28 minutes. And regardless of whether your route is planned or unplanned, the Uconnect infotainment system can help you find nearby compatible charging stations, so you’ll always be ready for your next adventure.

The Recon never forgets that driving a Jeep® Brand vehicle is supposed to bring joy. To support this initiative, it sports a pair of 335-hp electric motors, one in front and one in back, providing a combined 670-hp. That’s enough to launch this rugged machine from 0-60 in 3.6 seconds.

Electrification has its practical applications off-road as well. It can thrust the Recon through its 24” fording depth. It can help you get into the rough, making use of its 9.1-inches of ground clearance, its 33.8-degree approach angle, 33.1-degree departure angle, and 23.3-degree breakover angle. And it can apply power to each wheel in very precise increments, allowing the Recon to crawl up difficult terrain with bravado and technical sophistication. The brand’s Trail Rated objectives remain the same, but the Recon occupies a more unusual space, because it’s a Jeep® Brand vehicle first, and an electric vehicle second. It has the visual confidence you expect from the nameplate. It has the Trail Rated 4X4 capability. It has removable doors and windows, and a power roof that can disappear at the push of a button, providing a liberating open-air architecture. It has the technology expected of a modern SUV and enough interior flexibility to function as a daily driver. And, don’t forget, it has 650-hp.

The Recon’s electrification makes it faster, quieter, more controllable, and more exciting. Yet the core Jeep® Brand mission hasn't changed. That's key to the brand’s enduring appeal, and it’s why the 2026 Recon matters. Despite packing a state-of-the-art tech suite that includes a next-gen EV platform, a 14.5-inch touchscreen display, and an advanced Selec-Terrain system that enables an astonishing 3.6-second sprint to 60 mph, the vehicle's greatest achievement isn't digital. It’s the fact that underneath the silicon and cells, the core philosophy remains wonderfully old-fashioned. Adventure should take you somewhere.
