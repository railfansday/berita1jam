The slightly dorky vehicle you see here can use Tesla’s Superchargers and so-called Full-Self Driving (Supervised) driver assistance system. And no, your eyes are not deceiving you—this is not a Tesla. At least, not at first glance.

It’s called the Hoffman Badger, and it’s the creation of a small Wisconsin startup that wants to turn old Tesla EVs into pretty much any type of vehicle. The first Badger made its debut at this year’s SEMA show in Las Vegas, driving on its own wheels all the way from its home base and using Superchargers along the way, just like any Tesla would.

Hoffman says it uses all of the hardware and software from the donor vehicle, which can be either the Model 3 or Model Y. The seats, dashboard, steering wheel, motors, and even the door handles are lifted from Tesla. Then, they’re installed in a new chassis that puts the high-voltage battery pack one and a half inches higher than in the original vehicles. This, along with the eight-inch ground clearance, improves the boxy EV’s breakover angle, making it better suited for rough roads.

The startup says it can build around 100 units per year, but exact details haven’t been ironed out yet. In an Out of Spec Review video, Geoffrey Hoffman, the company’s founder, said that he doesn’t have a precise plan for the short term, but that attending SEMA will help in identifying what people actually want.

Hoffman expects the pricing to be somewhere between the Slate Truck and the Tesla Cybertruck, so from $26,400 all the way up to $77,000, if the base dual-motor Cybertruck is the upper target.

The doors, the roof, and most of the seats are removable, and the vehicle is titled as an 11-seat bus. All of the structure and body parts are powder-coated aluminum, while the exposed fasteners are stainless steel or steel coated with 1,000-hour salt spray resistance. Inside, the fasteners are stainless or zinc-plated steel. All of the new materials are recycled, except for a few small 3D-printed parts.

If and when sales eventually kick off, the Badger will be available as a ready-made vehicle with whatever top hat the customer wants, or as a kit with a VIN. In the latter case, the customer will have to source a donor Tesla Model 3 or Model Y.

There’s no estimated range, but seeing how it’s shaped like a brick, it will most likely be less than the donor car’s original figure. That said, Hoffman said that its boxy EV may deliver more range around town compared to the Model 3 or Model Y, simply because the Badger is lighter. In the first quarter of 2027, the aerodynamics will be improved, according to the startup.

Hoffman’s main business is converting old British cars to electric. Its portfolio includes the DeLorean DMC-12, the classic Volkswagen Beetle, classic Land Rovers, and vintage Rolls-Royce and Bentley models.

2 Bank Stocks to Buy After Their Third-Quarter Earnings Reports

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

With the third-quarter earnings season well underway, most banks have now reported.

Largely speaking, bank profits have continued to climb, as most banks take advantage of higher interest rates and a steep yield curve, in which lower-maturity bonds yield less than higher-maturity ones, an environment that most banks tend to benefit from.

Banks typically borrow money at short-term rates and lend it out at longer-term rates, which is why banks benefit from a steeper yield curve. Still, it's not all roses. Banks are also grappling with higher funding costs, while investors wonder whether a real credit cycle will ever show up.

Here are two bank stocks to buy after their third-quarter earnings reports.

For investors just starting to look at bank stocks, or who simply want a little exposure, JPMorgan Chase (JPM +4.18%) is an easy place to begin. It's the country's largest bank, with more than $5 trillion in assets, and is run by Chief Executive Officer Jamie Dimon, whom many regard as the best leader in banking.

JPMorgan Chase is good at many different banking businesses, from credit card lending to investment banking to wealth management and payments.

So you've got a bank that can benefit from a steep yield curve like other traditional banks, but JPMorgan will also benefit from large initial public offerings like the one planned for artificial intelligence (AI) company Anthropic, which only a handful of the country's largest investment banks can say. This gives JPMorgan revenue diversity that few banks have.

JPMorgan actually missed Wall Street estimates for both earnings and revenue in the third quarter, which is rare for the bank. The undershot came from weaker fixed-income trading revenue and higher expenses.

However, JPMorgan raised its full-year outlook for net interest income (NII), the spread revenue the bank makes on its loans and bond holdings after covering its funding costs for the deposits and other interest-bearing liabilities that fund those assets.

Management provided a forecast of $107 billion for NII in 2027, representing roughly a 10% increase over its projected 2026 NII. While JPMorgan trades at a premium valuation of roughly 3 times its tangible book value (TBV), or its theoretical liquidation value, it has earned this premium during two decades of excellence.

Investors should ignore the third-quarter earnings miss and buy the stock for the long haul.

Raleigh, North Carolina-based First Citizens BancShares (FCNCA +0.41%) has total assets of roughly $241 billion, putting it at a fraction of the size of JPMorgan Chase, but still one of the larger banks in the country.

First Citizens has delivered a masterclass in growth through acquisition, particularly by purchasing distressed banks or those on the brink of failure.

For instance, First Citizens acquired the struggling CIT Group in 2022, which traded at a significant discount to its TBV. As a result, the deal was 38% accretive to First Citizens' TBV, which is a huge increase. Most bank acquisitions dilute TBV and take two or three years to earn back the dilution.

First Citizens struck again when it acquired all of Silicon Valley Bank's deposits, loans, and branches after the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) took over during the banking crisis in 2023, of which Silicon Valley Bank was at the center.

Shares of First Citizens soared about 40% after announcing the acquisition. Furthermore, First Citizens had a loss-sharing agreement with the FDIC on loans, as well as other protections that made the deal even more appealing.

After its third-quarter earnings on Oct. 23, First Citizens' stock rose nearly 8% after adjusted earnings per share of $55.37 easily topped estimates of $45.89, according to FactSet. The bank's board also approved a $1.2 billion share repurchase plan through next year.

First Citizens is a proven acquirer, and I suspect it will continue to grow in this manner.

Social Security Trustees Project 22% Benefit Cut in 2032. This Change Would Cut Benefits by Only 17% Instead

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

There's a potentially less bad outcome, but it's not so clear-cut.

There are millions of older Americans today who rely on Social Security for retirement income. And without those benefits, many would be unable to afford basic living expenses.

Unfortunately, Social Security is facing a major financial crisis that could come to a head in just a few years. In fact, benefit cuts are something all retirees might need to brace for.

One potential solution could reduce the extent to which Social Security has to cut benefits. But it's not guaranteed to happen.

The reason Social Security faces benefit cuts boils down to a shrinking workforce. The program's main source of revenue is payroll taxes. But as older workers retire and a smaller share of replacement workers enter the labor force, Social Security will be under strain. The program is not expected to have sufficient incoming tax revenue to keep up with scheduled benefits once there's no trust fund to tap.

The Social Security Trustees, in their most recent report, projected that the program's Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) Trust Fund would run out of money by the fourth quarter of 2032. Once that happens, Social Security may face a 22% benefit cut.

The OASI Trust Fund is where Social Security's retirement benefits are paid from. But Social Security has another trust fund called the Disability Insurance (DI) Trust Fund, which pays disability benefits.

If the two trust funds were to be combined, Social Security would be able to pay retirement benefits in full for almost an extra two years. And from there, those benefits would only face a 17% cut.

The problem, though, is that Social Security can't simply combine the two funds. Congress would need to vote for that change.

Also, combining the OASI and DI funds doesn't prevent benefit cuts. It simply reduces them while slightly shifting the timeline.

It also puts the DI Trust Fund at risk of depletion much sooner than it would otherwise run out. The current projection by the Trustees indicates the DI Trust Fund could last at least through 2099 if left alone.

Of course, the main reason Congress might hesitate to combine the two Social Security trust funds is that a 17% benefit cut would still be catastrophic to millions of older Americans, not to mention future retirees. There's a good chance that if lawmakers make a change, they'll focus on one that can prevent Social Security cuts entirely.

Top Wallet-as-a-Service Providers for Ethereum in 2026

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Disclaimer: The below article is sponsored, and the views in it do not represent those of ZyCrypto. Readers should conduct independent research before taking any actions related to the project mentioned in this piece. This article should not be regarded as investment advice.

How Aligned, Privy, Dynamic, Turnkey, and Magic compare

A small group of established providers leads wallet-as-a-service solutions (WaaS) on Ethereum, including Aligned, Privy, Dynamic, Turnkey, and Magic. All are non-custodial and primarily compete on four things: how they secure the key, how they handle accounts and gas, how much of the stack they cover, and who owns the company.

For a consumer app or a financial institution building on Ethereum, the wallet is the integration you most want to get right the first time. It’s what users touch directly and trust with their funds, and it signs every transaction they make. It’s also the decision that compounds: once people hold balances, switching providers means migrating live accounts, so getting it right early pays off most.

Today’s leading WaaS providers look almost identical on the surface. They’re all non-custodial, and they all spare users the crypto mechanics: no seed phrase, no gas token, no contract prompts to approve. The differences that shape an integration sit underneath: the key-security model, how accounts and gas work, how much of the stack comes from one vendor, and who owns that vendor.

The field of credible wallet providers is small but far from uniform. Each was built around a different priority, and they overlap enough to blur together. Understanding what each one is keeps the biggest name from standing in for the best fit.

Aligned: Independent, founded by the team behind LambdaClass, and the only provider in the group focused exclusively on Ethereum: it runs on Ethereum L1 and on ZK rollups built with ethrex, while the others prioritize multichain reach. Every account is an EIP-7702 smart account that requires two signatures: one from the user’s key on their device and one from Aligned’s backend. It ships as part of a larger stack, with the rollup, proof aggregation, and interoperability all coming from the same team. Sovra runs identity for 8M+ people on it.

Privy (Stripe): The most widely deployed of the group by self-reported counts, with 200M+ accounts and customers like Hyperliquid and Ramp. It splits each key into two Shamir shares, both held on Privy’s side (one inside a secure enclave), and reassembles the key only inside the enclave. Its default EOA wallet can be upgraded in place through EIP-7702, or act as the signer of a separate ERC-4337 smart wallet. It carries several independent audits plus SOC 2 Type I and Type II.

Dynamic (Fireblocks): As much a login-and-wallet-connection platform as an embedded wallet, with a wide range of sign-in methods and connectors to 800+ external wallets. That fits apps where plenty of users already have a wallet, alongside those who need one made. Its keys use threshold-signature MPC. Customers have included Kraken and Ondo.

Turnkey: The signing and key-management foundation other wallets build on. It generates and uses raw private keys inside secure enclaves, with a policy engine that checks every signature. Of the enclave-based providers, Turnkey is the one that lets you verify the enclave itself: reproducible builds and remote attestation confirm the exact code that touched the key. Independent, founded by an ex-Coinbase Custody team. Customers include Bridge and Polymarket.

Magic: The oldest of the group, launched in 2018 and known for email-first onboarding. It protects keys with a mix of secure enclaves, a cloud key-management service, and sharding, and it holds the widest set of compliance certifications in the group: SOC 2, SOC 3, and ISO 27001. Its wallet business is being acquired by Payward, Kraken’s parent company, in a deal agreed in July 2026.

Most top WaaS solutions today are non-custodial, meaning they can’t move users’ funds without their consent. But that label covers a wide range: how much you trust the provider depends on how it handles the private key, and that trust underpins what you can promise users about their money. The clearest divide is whether the full key is ever assembled in one place, and it splits these solutions into two camps.

All three bring the full key together inside secure hardware, and they differ only on when. Privy splits it into two Shamir shares (not MPC), both held on its side, and reassembles them in a TEE at signing. Magic does the same with a TEE, a cloud key-management service, and sharding. Turnkey skips reassembly, keeping raw keys inside secure enclaves full-time behind a policy engine. Either way, the enclave exposes the full key, so its integrity is the security boundary.

Each gives you a different way to trust that boundary. Turnkey makes the enclave verifiable through open-source enclave code, reproducible builds, and remote attestation, so you can confirm the exact code handling the key. Privy leans on outside audits from Cure53, Zellic, and Doyensec, and Magic on the widest set of certifications in the group, SOC 2, SOC 3, and ISO 27001.

Dynamic and Aligned take the enclave out of the picture: no single key can move funds on its own, and they get there two ways. Dynamic uses threshold-signature MPC, where independent parties each hold a share and sign jointly, so no machine, Dynamic’s included, ever holds a spendable key. Aligned uses two separate keys and requires both to sign. The user’s key stays on their device (a WebAuthn passkey, a P-256 key, or an ECDSA key), Aligned’s key stays on its backend, and the account contract checks both signatures on-chain before it executes anything. Passkeys are verified on-chain as P-256 signatures inside the WebAuthn envelope. A breach of Aligned’s backend doesn’t move funds, and neither does a stolen device on its own.

The trade-off to plan for is availability: a signature needs a second party online. The two handle that differently. Dynamic’s 2-of-3 option lets any two of the three shares sign, so one being offline won’t lock the wallet. Aligned’s 2-of-2 doesn’t have that cushion: every transaction needs Aligned’s co-signer, so access depends on its backend staying reachable. For lost devices, Aligned offers optional guardian recovery: the guardian is set per app, and the timelock per account. The guardian can add a new key to an account, but that key can’t sign until a timelock expires (1 to 14 days, fixed when the account is created), which leaves a window to cancel an unwanted recovery. Accounts created without a timelock have no guardian.

One more point of trust sits above the accounts themselves. Aligned accounts route through a beacon contract, one per app, and the beacon’s owner can rotate the signers and point accounts to a new implementation without touching each one. That makes upgrades and key rotation simple to operate, and it means teams should know who controls the beacon for their app.

On its own, an Ethereum account is bare: a user needs ETH to do anything, every action is a separate approval, and losing the key means losing the funds. Account abstraction lifts those limits by giving the account programmable logic, so a product can cover gas for users, batch several steps into one confirmation, and offer recovery. All of them do this. They differ in the underlying standard, and providers are split between them.

ERC-4337 is the longest-running route, and Privy’s smart wallets build on ERC-4337. It works entirely at the application layer: user actions become “UserOperation” objects that a separate mempool and “bundler” services collect, batch, and submit through a shared EntryPoint contract, with an optional paymaster covering gas. Because it needs no change to Ethereum itself, it went live on Ethereum mainnet in March 2023 and now has the deepest audit and tooling history of any account-abstraction approach.

That maturity comes with operational overhead. Each user needs a separate smart-contract account deployed and maintained, and much of the market reaches 4337 through shared third-party middleware such as ZeroDev. It’s a well-worn path, and it also puts a common external layer in the critical signing path for many apps at once, which is worth weighing at institutional scale.

Aligned builds on EIP-7702, a 2025 protocol change that landed in Ethereum’s Pectra upgrade. It lets an ordinary account (an EOA) point to contract code and run it directly, so a user’s existing address gains smart-account behavior without deploying a second account alongside it. Aligned uses 7702 on its own, with no 4337 EntryPoint, bundler, or paymaster: its backend submits each transaction and pays the gas, so users never need to hold ETH or the chain’s native token. The result is leaner: one address, nothing extra to deploy or fund, and no third-party middleware between the user and the chain. Because it depends on 7702, it runs only on chains that have adopted Pectra.

EIP-7702 and ERC-4337 aren’t mutually exclusive: Privy signs 7702 authorizations for any implementation, Dynamic and Magic now offer 7702-based smart accounts, and Turnkey, as a pure signer, handles both transaction types and leaves the account model to you. Which one fits depends on whether a team wants established 4337 tooling or a simpler account with fewer moving parts.

Providers also differ in how much of the wallet you build yourself. The options fall on three levels, from a bare signing primitive you wrap in your own code to a full stack that arrives assembled. Where a provider sits decides how many vendors you take on and how independent the pieces of your stack stay.

Turnkey sits lowest, by design. It hands you a signing and key-management foundation and leaves the account-abstraction layer, gas handling, and smart-account logic for you to build. That’s the most work and the most control. Your wallet logic stays independent of any one chain or vendor, and signing sits behind a boundary you can attest. Teams with their own engineers often start here for that reason.

Privy, Dynamic, and Magic sit in the middle, as full embedded-wallet SDKs. They handle onboarding, key security, and gas, while you bring the chain and settle wherever you like. Dynamic leans hardest into authentication and wallet connection, with 800+ external-wallet connectors, so it suits apps where many users show up with a wallet already.

Aligned sits at the other end and sells the most in one piece: the wallet plus a rollup and proof aggregation, with interoperability, all from one team. Its ZK rollups are built with ethrex, the Rust Ethereum client from LambdaClass, and settle on Ethereum. That folds several procurement and integration decisions into one, and removes the seams between those layers.

It also concentrates your dependencies. The wallet, chain, and settlement all come from one provider, so they move together on a single roadmap and a single uptime, and more of your stack rides on one vendor. An institution gets one supplier to vet, and one point of dependence to weigh in return.

A wallet provider is a long-term dependency, so the decision comes down to a few things each team has to settle: how much to trust a provider with key handling, how much of the account and gas experience to hand off, and how much of the stack to source from one vendor.

The demo won’t decide it for you, because they all deliver the same clean experience: no seed phrase, no gas token, a smooth login. Their real differences sit underneath, in the two choices hardest to reverse once users hold funds: how the key is secured, and how much of the product leans on a single company. Past that, the best fit depends on what you’re building and what stage it’s at.

Bluesky was right all along and will accept your apology any time

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bluesky is a niche Twitter-clone frequently accused of being an echo chamber for progressives. After the blue tsunami of this week’s midterms, however, its inhabitants don’t seem to be echoing themselves so much as the vast majority of American voters.

The sheer scale of Democrats’ victory is staggering. They won control of both chambers of the U.S. Congress, flipping Senate seats in seven states Trump carried in 2024—including Republican stronghold Texas, which now also has a Democratic governor and attorney general. All in all, this year’s midterms represent the biggest electoral shift in state-level politics to hit the U.S. in generations. They also represent a Texas-sized validation of the worldview Bluesky has become notorious for over the past two years.

Although it officially launched in the spring of 2023, Bluesky might as well have been born on election eve in 2024. That was the moment millions of disillusioned Twitter users decamped from a website owned by the largest single contributor to Trump’s reelection. Bluesky quickly became a haven for Twitter expats and grew at a frenetic pace. It went from 9 million users in September 2024 to 15 million by mid-November, eventually reaching its current 47 million.

Because many of Bluesky’s users arrived out of principled opposition to X, formerly Twitter, the two sites have arguably existed in opposition to each other. Both portray their counterpart as out of touch, with Bluesky alleging Elon Musk’s algorithm tweaks have skewed X’s political vortex too far to the right, and X suggesting Bluesky is a bubble of liberals who can tolerate anything except a difference in opinion.

The latter idea seemed to largely prevail. 

In 2025, Washington Post columnists, tech world figures like Mark Cuban, and myriad other pundits dismissed Bluesky as smug, self-righteous, and irrelevant. It was the fantasy sandbox where progressives went to play among themselves, protecting reality dwellers from having to hear their drivel. (“Like a containment dome over a nuclear reactor,” as the writer Josh Barro put it.)

So, how well did Bluesky’s users stay in touch with reality between elections, compared with X’s exponentially larger population? Consider the post-midterms perspective.

Rather than accept the narrative that 2024 represented a rejection of “wokeness” and a political realignment toward Trump, the manifestly trans-friendly Bluesky suggested the election was more a referendum on postpandemic inflation and global anti-incumbent bias. As an emboldened Trump then proceeded to antagonize U.S. citizens and the rest of the world, while making billions for himself in crypto, Bluesky meticulously chronicled the state of play. It became the premier destination for seeing radicalized older white ladies, abysmally attended pro-ICE rallies, and complaints about the lack of mainstream news coverage around massive No Kings protests. Any random day would find endless posts with evidence that, per the motto of the political philosopher and Bluesky power user Olúfẹ́mi O. Táíwò, “We are going to win.” 

Above all, Bluesky proved to be less a bubble than an expression of the multitude of ideological factions that had united under the umbrella of non-MAGAness . . . to argue with one another.

Meanwhile, X’s millions of liberal and progressive folks were often drowned out by an algorithm that seemed to favor right-wing voices and their preferred narratives. A bubble chart from Nate Silver, the political analyst, visually depicted the accounts with highest engagement rates back in April. Its many MAGA-red circles made it look like the blood-filled elevator from The Shining. As a May 2026 study from Northwestern University suggests, X’s algorithm rewards posts that provoke strong reactions, so the loudest and most emotionally satisfying political claims become disproportionately visible. That’s how we got viral fever dreams about James Talarico, the then-senatorial candidate, checking into a mental hospital, something that never in fact happened.

It should come as little surprise that many high-profile MAGA voices on X spent the days leading up to the election incorrectly predicting how it would turn out.

Who looks out of touch and stuck in an echo chamber now?

While X may have been more plugged into the political discourse online for the past two years, Bluesky now appears better attuned to what’s been happening on the ground. Its users were right about pretty much everything. 

They were right that anti-trans ads would have little or no impact on the 2026 election, and that their impact on the previous election had been vastly overstated.

They were right that Graham Platner wasn’t the only Democrat who could beat Susan Collins in Maine, and that Abdul El-Sayed’s association with a controversial Twitch streamer relatively few voters had even heard of was not as big a deal as X users seemed to think. 

And they were right that fleeing X’s algorithm helped them view the world with clear eyes.

If Bluesky is a bubble, then it is currently a champagne bubble awash in celebration.

The festivities will likely last only a matter of hours, though, until infighting explodes around just how the Democrats should spend their newfound political capital.

6 good, bad, and weird changes in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time for Switch 2

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You may have heard by now that Nintendo has remade mighty '90s classic The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time. The reimagining of this 1998 Nintendo 64 adventure game for Switch 2 includes many major and minor changes, not all of them created equal.

With the Ocarina remake on shelves now, I wanted to highlight a handful of differences that stood out to me while playing. Some of them are fun, others are frustrating, and some defy that sort of categorization entirely. Without wasting any more time, let's dive in.

I'm not going to be too specific about this change because the game literally just came out and I don't want to ruin the experience for folks.

That said, you should know going in that Nintendo chose to alter several of Ocarina's legendary boss fights with new attacks, animations, and more. It's one of the most exciting elements of the new game for me, as it adds a sense of novelty to the adventure that is otherwise lacking, owing to the game's status as a remake. Every time a well-known boss fight swerved and surprised me, I had a huge grin on my face.

One of the best puzzle-solving items in the entire Zelda series is the hookshot, a straight-shooting grapple gun that can easily zip Link between points. Ocarina was not the first game to incorporate the hookshot, but it did expand the tool's usefulness with an extended targeting range and a wide variety of surfaces to which the hookshot can attach, such as wooden treasure chests and the rooftops of Kakariko Village.

Unfortunately, that philosophy has not been entirely maintained in the Switch 2 remake. Most grapple points remain intact, but many have been changed from natural-feeling features like tree branches to clearly marked targets. I don't mind that change so much as I mind the fact that the hookshot can no longer grapple onto Kakariko's rooftops. This greatly reduces Link's ability to move around the village and just makes the game less fun for no discernible reason.

In 2005, Resident Evil 4 set a template for third-person, over-the-shoulder aiming cameras that pretty much every mainstream video game nowadays deploys, including the Ocarina remake. When aiming items like the hookshot, bow, or boomerang in the new Switch 2 release, the camera sits behind Link, slightly offset so his character model doesn't get in the way. You can also aim using gyro controls as a nice bonus.

This point of view works just fine and may be more familiar to younger Zelda fans used to the aiming functionality in 2017's The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, however, I do miss being able to zoom in with a first-person lens to examine Link's surroundings up close. It's kind of a bummer in the opening Kokiri Forest area, where you can no longer get a good look at the stick drawing on the trunk of Link's treehouse.

I wouldn't say that rupees (the currency in every Zelda game) are totally superfluous in the original Ocarina of Time. After all, you need 40 of them to bypass an early-game obstacle, and some important mini-games require small sums in order to participate. You can also use them to stock up on supplies like arrows and health potions, but frankly, the original version doled out those things generously enough to render that unnecessary.

While I would say Ocarina on Switch 2 is easier overall, the developers did add some new incentives to hoard rupees and collect Gold Skulltula tokens in order to expand the size of Link's wallet. Specifically, the Great Fairies who provide Link with game-changing upgrades now ask for sizable monetary donations before they'll hand over the good stuff. In the original game, the upgrades were free, but some of them cost hundreds of rupees in the remake.

I like this change because it gives players a reason to more fully engage with optional content and wring as much fun out of the reimagined game as possible.

I talked about this in my review, so I'll keep it brief here, but major story cutscenes in the Ocarina remake have been expanded with full voice acting for every character except for Link. The acting itself is a little cartoonish, which adequately fits the vibe, but also comes across as slightly cheesy at times.

More importantly, though, this makes some cutscenes last longer than they might have if they were broken up by text boxes, as they were in the original. Maybe someone can do a proper stopwatch comparison, but it at least felt like the Ocarina remake seized cutscene control for lengthier periods of time than the N64 version did.

Lastly, Nintendo has opted to lengthen the day/night cycle in the Ocarina remake. It was very short in the N64 original, as you could hardly cross Hyrule Field on foot without the sun going down and horrible skeleton monsters emerging to ruin your travels.

In the Switch 2 remake, it takes much longer for the sun to rise and set, which has benefits and drawbacks. On the plus side, you get more time to enjoy each distinct atmosphere, which gives music lead and sound director Masato Ohashi an excuse to craft new, chilled out nighttime versions of some of Zelda composer Koji Kondo's iconic area themes. Some downsides, however, are that the Hyrule Field drawbridge no longer goes up at night, and it's somewhat more tedious to wait for certain times of day in the few situations where that's necessary.

One last note is that the day/night cycle is now universal, rather than turning off in towns and cities like it did in the original game. That means you get to see NPCs actively move from their daytime positions to their nighttime states in real-time, which is pretty neat.
