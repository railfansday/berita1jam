10 Classic Rock Albums To Listen to Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

​​​​​​Joining the choir invisible. Shuffling off this mortal coil. Ceasing to be. Biting the dust. Wearing wooden pajamas. Vacating the earthly meat prison. No matter how it's said, it all means the same thing: death. Death is the great equalizer, the one thing that is inevitable regardless of one's race, religion, income or social status. Every day brings us closer to hopping the twig.

But hey, you're still on this side of the grass, right? Unless you're a well-read zombie, but that seems unlikely (but not impossible... discuss). So you have a choice to make: sit idly by, paralyzed by the thought you may have had your last Slurpee; or make the most of the time you have left. And if — hopefully — you've selected the latter, before kicking the bucket you've got things you want — nay, need — to do, see, and hear. Thankfully, your friends here at Collider have compiled a list that speaks to the latter: 10 rock albums you need to listen to before going belly up, ones that are not only awesome but offer guidance as you prepare for the inevitable. We're dying, literally, to share those with you now.

There comes a point in everyone's life where it's time to drop the charades and get real. That means raw, emotional honesty with others and yourself, and there is no better album that wears emotional honesty on every track like Fleetwood Mac's 1977 classic Rumours. The story behind the album is legendary at this point, recorded as the interpersonal drama among the group was akin to a soap opera, with separations, outright spite and heartbreak, making the atmosphere in the studio, shall we say, tense. A lesser band would have buried that under upbeat pop/rock songs, recording in separate sessions if needed. Not Fleetwood Mac: they owned that energy and put it all on vinyl, creating a timeless masterpiece that is relatable, real, and, in the case of "Don't Stop," even hopeful.

Getting real with ourselves potentially opens up a proverbial Pandora's Box of questions about life, death, time, money, or even one's mental health. While it may not provide any answers to these questions, Pink Floyd, with their iconic album Dark Side of the Moon, sure as all hell wax philosophical about those same themes. Like Rumours, the album has an emotional honesty that resonates only with the fluid cohesion that comes from being a concept album. As a result, Dark Side of the Moon is less a record than it is an experience, one that should be made before going up to the "Great Gig in the Sky."

Speaking of experiences, seeing KISS in concert is one that is an absolute must. Or at least was, given the band gave their last performance at Madison Square Garden on December 2nd, 2023. So if that's on your bucket list, you now only have two options: wait for the KISS virtual avatars to hit town, or listen to their 1975 album Alive! Opt for the latter. Their breakthrough album, consisting of cuts from their first three albums (KISS, Hotter Than Hell and Dressed to Kill), achieves something that other live albums have mostly failed to accomplish: the feeling of being there. Alive! captures the bombastic, take-no-prisoners energy of a KISS live show and puts it on vinyl. Twice, that is, with that raw power requiring containment on a double album. Good luck replicating that, avatars.

The "when" of kicking the bucket is assured. The "how," on the other hand, is another story. There are millions of ways one could go, from horribly tragic to an eternally looping end as a Darwin Award winner. You could, as alluded to in the aforementioned Rumours, even "Go Your Own Way." And if that's the case, as Van Halen cheerfully suggests on their 1984 album, you "might as well jump" (but in all seriousness, please don't — help is always available). The last album with long-time frontman David Lee Roth (until 2012's A Different Kind of Truth) is also, arguably, their best, with "Panama," "Hot for Teacher," "Drop Dead Legs" and "Jump" the standouts. Really, though, the album is so good it doesn't really matter where you "jump" in.

It's ironically fitting that the last album the Beatles recorded before their demise, one that includes "The End," would show up on a list like this, no? As Collider explains, Abbey Road is arguably the greatest record of the 20th century. It's high praise, and even if you may disagree — you shouldn't, it's pretty damn good — then it still stands tall amongst the crowd. Noted as much for its iconic album cover as its tracks, Abbey Road has contributions from all four of the Beatles, with George Harrison and Ringo Starr each providing their strongest entries ("Something" and "Here Comes the Sun" for Harrison, "Octopus's Garden" for Starr). Even the much-maligned "Maxwell's Silver Hammer" is far better than its reputation. The album has a blend of genres that work as a whole, serving as a snapshot of the band at the apex of their creativity.

The Beatles fell apart after recording Abbey Road, while Australian rockers AC/DC could have done the same after the death of original frontman Bon Scott, and even toyed with the idea of disbanding. Instead, they stayed together, with Scott's father encouraging them to do so. They recruited Brian Johnson and set out to record what would be the definitive AC/DC album: Back in Black. It was created as a tribute to Scott, with the all-black cover a sign of mourning, while simultaneously launching a new era for the band. That new era came with a commitment to elemental, arena-ready guitar riffs and straight-forward rock, with a frontman who brought his own energy instead of being a Scott-lite. To say it worked out pretty well is an understatement: Back in Black hit the top of the UK album chart, became the best-selling album that never reached number one in the US charts, and is currently the second best-selling album of all time in music history. There is legitimately not a single track that sucks, from opener "Hells Bells" (which, hopefully, aren't what you hear on the other side) to "Rock and Roll Ain't Noise Pollution."

These classic rock albums are masterpieces from beginning to end.

Another album that showcases the band's mastery over a wide range of genres is Led Zeppelin's Led Zeppelin IV. "Black Dog" and "Rock and Roll" are straight bangers, "Going to California" has an acoustic folk vibe, while "When the Levee Breaks" takes the original blues song by Kansas Joe McCoy and Memphis Minnie from 1929 and reinterprets it as a slow-groove, blues-rock masterpiece. Every member of the band is at their best, which comes through the album's most prominent track: "Stairway to Heaven." The delicate acoustic softness of its opening melds seamlessly into its hard-rock crescendo and a fiery solo from Jimmy Page that arguably stands as his finest, with Robert Plant owning the track with his uncanny vocal range. And hey, when you do say goodbye to this world, it's nice to know you don't need to waste your time finding the elevator to Heaven.

And if you make that trek to the top of the stairway, you'll find "Paradise City," which is also a track from one of rock's all-time greatest debut albums, Guns N' Roses' Appetite for Destruction. Neither the album or the band entered the music world quietly, but rather with a raw, explosive danger. They took the makeup, tights and hairspray that marked the hair-metal era and made it irrelevant overnight, ditching the sexualized, party-time dynamic in favor of a gritty, punk realism. The album balances radio-friendly(ish) tracks like "Sweet Child O' Mine" and "Welcome to the Jungle" with darker cuts like "Mr. Brownstone," making it an album of all killer, no filler. Why waste the time you have left listening to crap?

Knowing that this life is fleeting, you may want to get familiar with the fella that'll be hosting you for eternity after you've bought the farm: God. To that end, you'll want to throw on Stryper's game-changing 1986 album To Hell With the Devil. With it, the holy-roller band did the unimaginable by making Christian rock music actually good. Like, MTV good, where they were in heavy rotation with the likes of Mötley Crue and Poison. They had all the bombast and flash of their secular hair-metal peers, even better than most, with a message that was radically different. "Honestly" is as good a power ballad as you'll ever hear, while standout tracks "The Way" and "To Hell With the Devil" showcase the blistering fretwork of Oz Fox and vocalist Michael Sweet's uncanny pipes. In a word, To Hell With the Devil is heavenly.

As we learned from It's a Wonderful Life, every time a bell rings, an angel gets their wings. Assuming the bells you hear on side B of life aren't one of Hell's, then congratulations, you get your wings! But why wait until you take the big Adios, when you can pick up Aerosmith's second album, Get Your Wings. First, a little context: Aerosmith's self-titled debut in 1973 was not a success, unaided by Columbia's promotional efforts being diverted to tout Bruce Springsteen's debut album Greeting from Asbury Park, N.J. That, and as Tom Hamilton points out, "a lot of people took a look at us and said, 'They're like a Kmart version of the Stones.'" They were given one more chance: "Unless your next album is really, really good, you're not going to be recording artists anymore."

Suffice it to say, Get Your Wings was, is, really, really good. The biggest sin of Aerosmith was that it simply didn't sound like Aerosmith, and Get Your Wings reflected the band's emergence from the blues roots that dominated the first album into their own hard-rock identity. Joe Perry and Brad Whitford's dual-guitars were accentuated by producer Jack Douglas, who also helped frontman Steven Tyler hone his songwriting to reflect the now-iconic, swaggering persona he was developing as a frontman. The album would define the distinct Aerosmith sound from that point on, with tracks like "Same Old Song and Dance," "Seasons of Wither," "Train Kept a-Rollin,'" and the playful sleaze of "Lord of the Thighs" particular standouts.

Steve Sarkisian vs. Lane Kiffin is a fun undercard to the main event of Texas at LSU

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AUSTIN - Steve Sarkisian matches wits with Lane Kiffin on Saturday, which offers an intriguing subplot to the main story on the field.

It could be argued that these two polarizing coaches are the top narrative, and the game itself is secondary. Kiffin, as the declared King of Social Media until he got off it, might not be opposed to that.

The LSU coach does like attention. No, wait. He demands it. Sarkisian is fine with it and, given some of his offseason quips, it seems some of his good buddy's antics have rubbed off on the Texas coach.

Sarkisian and Kiffin might be mirror images of each other. That especially holds for the LSU coach with the boyish charm because he has never met a mirror he didn't like.

Sarkisian also has no shortage of self-confidence - should we say arrogance - which is yet one more common denominator for these two premier head coaches in college football.

The two will go head to head as head coaches at their current jobs for the first time Saturday night in Baton Rouge.

Not sure there has ever been two head coaches playing in the same conference that share such similar backgrounds and common ground - or uncommon ground, considering their paths - than these two men barely in their 50s.

Sign up for Kirk Bohls' new newsletter for news, exclusive analysis and insights on University of Texas sports.

Both are head coaches in the SEC making $11 million or more. Kiffin somehow gets by with $13 million.

Both were quarterbacks in college, but Sarkisian actually played. Kiffin didn't play a snap.

Both called plays under Pete Carroll for USC.

Both got fired as the head coach at USC, but Kiffin got the axe barely after he got off the airplane from a road game.

Both have spent abbreviated time in the NFL before being fired.

Both coached for the Oakland Raiders, Kiffin as head coach and Sarkisian as quarterbacks coach.

Both are really good friends who share insults and insights on the same group text threads.

Both coach at programs with near $50 million rosters.

Both have been panned for less than perfect seasons since they've lost a combined five games.

Both have overcome alcohol addiction. "On a personal level, a major change in my life was getting sober almost six years ago, and he was a big part of that," Kiffin said of Sarkisian. "I owe him a lot for that and will always be appreciative of that."

Sarkisian is a native Californian. Kiffin is Nebraska-born but California-cool. No truth to the rumor he came out of the womb tanned and wearing sunglasses.

In fact, these two have so much in common that they rehabilitated their reputations and repaired their careers in Nick Saban's halfway house for wayward coaches.

They're both front and center in the Saban docuseries although Kiffin got the most verbal spankings.

"Lane and I do go way back," Sarkisian said Monday, noting he was a grad assistant at USC when Kiffin was the tight end coach. "I think Coach Carroll had a big impact on both of us. Then there's this influence of Nick Saban on both of our careers. You love competing against your friends, and there's nothing more gratifying than beating your friends."

Lane Kiffin's first season at LSU is off to a 6-3 start after last week's loss to Alabama. (Gus Stark/LSU/University Images via Getty Imag)

Sarkisian has to beat Kiffin to ensure a spot in the College Football Playoff. Kiffin has to beat Sarkisian to keep a For Sale sign from being planted in his front yard.

But they also have their differences, too.

When I asked Sarkisian about them Thursday, he demurred, "I don't know. I know this. I have a ton of respect for Lane and the job that he does. He's always had very talented teams. They play well. He's a really bright football coach. Thinks outside the box."

Thinks it? No, Kiffin invented out of the box.

No coach has angered more fan bases at his exits than Kiffin whether it was from Tennessee after one season to return to USC or from Ole Miss in the middle of the season. That was a new one, even for him, but it added to the growing list of fan bases who hate him. He might be leaving Bobby Petrino in his wake.

Both were unceremoniously fired as USC head coaches although Kiffin wins for drama since he was let go on an airport tarmac on the very night after giving up 62 points to Arizona State.

Both were quarterbacks, but Sarkisian actually played. He was a record-setting star at BYU while Kiffin was a backup at Fresno State and quit the team during his senior season to be a student assistant coach. Wise move.

Steve Sarkisian was the USC offensive coordinator/quarterbacks coach at USC when Lane Kiffin was assistant head coach/quarterbacks in the 2005 season. (Erich Schlegel)

The duo combined to call plays for a 2004 Trojans team that put up half a hundred points in almost half its games and obliterated Oklahoma for a national championship. Then-USC offensive coordinator Norm Chow, for whom Sarkisian played at BYU, claimed in Monte Burke's "Men of Troy" book that Sark undermined him with Carroll and wrote that Sark and Kiffin were young, cocky and living high.

Kiffin spent a season and a quarter as a 31-year-old head coach of the Oakland Raiders before going 4-12 and getting fired after a 1-3 start by owner Al Davis, who called him "a flat-out liar." Sarkisian called plays for the Atlanta Falcons for two years before getting fired for his red-zone failures. Hmmmm.

The two are very tight and don't mind exchanging barbs. They share a lot of group thread texts that Kiffin always starts.

"I do appreciate that Lane has the humor and ability to do that," Sarkisian said. "He's humble enough to joke about himself, honestly, and all of us. And so I think that part's kind of fun."

Both are super smart, maybe too smart for their own good. Kiffin at least trusts his offense to play-caller Charlie Weis Jr. Sarkisian, analyst and former Longhorn Rod Babers told "On Second Thought," has "nobody on his staff he trusts to call plays, and that scares me."

Steve Sarkisian and Lane Kiffin shared the sidelines at Alabama under Nick Saban. (Bob Rosato/RVR Photos-USA TODAY Sports)

Alabama owns Kiffin, who is 0-6 against the Crimson Tide, while Sarkisian is 1-1 against Alabama and would be 2-0 if not for a missed sack and a last-second field goal at DKR.

Saban hired Kiffin as his offensive coordinator, only to fire him and promote Sarkisian in the middle of the playoffs when Lane took the Florida Atlantic job.

Sarkisian has a very strong personality but is extremely guarded about his private life. Kiffin has a personality that's out of control. Kiffin doesn't do subtle.

Lane Kiffin doesn't mind a little showmanship such as having Kevin Costner join the "Victory Walk" before last week's game vs. Alabama. (Gus Stark/LSU/University Images via Getty Imag)

Did you see who joined him on the Victory Walk to the Tiger Stadium last week? None other than Kevin Costner, Shaq and Angel Reese. Who's joining him Saturday? Taylor Swift? Beyonce? The ghost of Billy Cannon?

"It doesn't have to do with winning or losing games and who walks in. We'd won every game at home so far, so who walked in wasn't the reason we didn't execute in the red zone," Kiffin said. "Obviously, recruiting is the number one part of the whole thing that you create a program with. People watch from far away, and not just 12th graders, but eighth graders, and they see it and go, "That place is cool. I want to go there."

Sarkisian, however, doesn't feel the need to draw as much attention to himself. He understands the Longhorn brand doesn't need to oversell.

"Sark is great and very transparent," SEC Now host Peter Burns told the "On Second Thought" podcast. "He's not going to rock the boat a whole lot, and they're winning ballgames. So are you trying to win the social media war? Or are you trying to win football games?'

Kiffin doesn't mind whom he trolls. Sarkisian rarely said an unkind word until this offseason when he poked fun of Texas Tech's schedule and promoted Ole Miss's basket-weaving curriculum.

"Lane tries to convince himself that all of the headlines help the branding," said Burns, a Baton Rouge native who's attended more than 50 games at Tiger Stadium. "You probably had to do that at FAU. You probably had to do that at Ole Miss to get on everybody's radar. LSU is like Texas. You're already on everybody's radar. So the question is is the juice worth the squeeze? So you probably can't ask a tiger to change his stripes."

It's definitely what LSU signed up for. Its 6-3 record, not so much.

Lane Kiffin's USC team beat Steve Sarkisian's Washington team in 2012. (Otto Greule Jr/Getty Images)

Another thing these two coaches have in common is that neither has won a national championship as a head coach.

Sarkisian's definitely come closer and might have played for one in 2023 but could never get a hand on Michael Penix in the Sugar Bowl and in 2024 because of a stupid blitz before halftime and a more stupid toss sweep in the red zone against Ohio State in the Cotton Bowl.

Kiffin has a lacking record against Top 25 teams. He's 15-27 versus ranked teams, including 0-3 this season. Sark's 25-35 but 18-7 in his last five years as well as 4-0 this year.

Kiffin has a better sense of humor. He once joked at Ole Miss that the Rebels were coming for Alabama and fans had "better get their popcorn ready." The Tide smoked Ole Miss. The next week, Ole Miss offered free popcorn at its game.

Sarkisian almost certainly could have had the Alabama job and succeeded Saban but didn't want to live in Tuscaloosa. Or follow the legend.

Kiffin almost certainly has coveted the Alabama job, but the Tide wanted no part of the Kiffin circus. (See: Saban butt-chewings.)

The simple truth is both are very good at recruiting. Both are damn good coaches. Both have egos that can land them in trouble but gets enormous clicks.

Neither really minds being in the spotlight, but just one absolutely craves it. Maybe almost as much as he does wins.

Da Brooklyn al Milan, Cardinale nomina Brett Yormark ad di MSE: chi è il nuovo dirigente

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Dai Brooklyn Nets alla Big 12 Conference, passando per Roc Nation e NASCAR. Adesso una nuova esperienza a Milano, al fianco di Gerry Cardinale. È Brett Yormark il dirigente scelto da RedBird Capital Partners per guidare Milan Sports & Entertainment (MSE), la holding che riunirà gli investimenti del fondo americano nel capoluogo lombardo. Nominato amministratore delegato della nuova società, Yormark risponderà direttamente a Cardinale e si occuperà di sviluppare la strategia del gruppo, supervisionando le attività in portafoglio e valutando ulteriori opportunità di investimento. Non si tratta quindi di una nomina all'interno dell'organigramma del Milan, che resterà comunque il principale riferimento sportivo di MSE. Attualmente Yormark ricopre l'incarico di commissioner della Big 12 Conference, che lascerà alla fine di gennaio 2027. La scelta nasce da una conoscenza professionale di lunga data: "Conosco Brett Yormark e lavoro con lui da 25 anni, e non riesco a immaginare un dirigente migliore nel settore dello sport internazionale per contribuire a realizzare questa strategia", ha dichiarato Cardinale.

Il nuovo CEO di MSE ha oltre trent'anni di esperienza nello sport e nell'intrattenimento. Una parte importante della sua carriera è legata a Brooklyn Sports & Entertainment, società di cui è stato presidente e amministratore delegato per quattordici anni, supervisionando le attività dei Brooklyn Nets e del Barclays Center. Ha avuto un ruolo centrale nel trasferimento della franchigia NBA a Brooklyn e, nel 2007, nella conclusione dell'accordo ventennale per i naming rights dell'arena con Barclays. Dopo l'inaugurazione del 2012, l'impianto ha ospitato circa 200 eventi e accolto oltre 2,1 milioni di visitatori nel primo anno, con circa 11.000 abbonamenti stagionali venduti e l'80% delle suite assegnate. Sotto la sua gestione sono cresciute anche le attività commerciali dei Nets, mentre il portafoglio degli impianti si è ampliato con strutture come il Brooklyn Paramount e lo storico Webster Hall di Manhattan. Prima di questa esperienza, Yormark aveva lavorato per sei anni in NASCAR; successivamente è stato per tre anni Chief Operating Officer di Roc Nation e Co-CEO di Roc Nation Unified.

Più recentemente, alla guida della Big 12 Conference, Yormark ha seguito l'espansione della lega universitaria americana attraverso l'ingresso di Arizona, Arizona State, Colorado e Utah. Ha inoltre concluso un accordo per i diritti televisivi con ESPN e Fox valido fino alla stagione 2030-31, lavorando sullo sviluppo commerciale e sulle partnership della conference. Un curriculum particolarmente significativo rispetto alle ambizioni di RedBird, che con MSE intende ampliare le proprie attività milanesi oltre il calcio. Tra i progetti figurano infatti la gestione di stadi e arene, una possibile franchigia nella futura NBA Europe, lo sviluppo di un polo per sport e intrattenimento a San Donato e nuove opportunità nel tennis internazionale. "Nel corso della mia carriera ho contribuito all'evoluzione delle organizzazioni sportive, ad ampliarne la portata e a creare nuove opportunità. Non vedo l'ora di mettere a frutto questa esperienza per realizzare la visione di RedBird per MSE", ha dichiarato Yormark, che sarà chiamato a trasferire a Milano il modello di sviluppo commerciale sperimentato nello sport americano.

Microsoft outage brings fax machines back into action across Japanese offices

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ongoing Microsoft outages have led companies across Japan to dust off fax machines, with employees now using the old-school tech to complete tasks during the widespread blackout.

A widespread Microsoft 365 outage has disrupted email and workplace services around the globe, leaving many without access to the everyday tools they need for work.

While some services have started to recover, local governments and firms across Japan have turned to a familiar sight in offices during the 1980s and 1990s: fax machines.

These chunky devices are used to send copies of documents over telephone lines, allowing staff to communicate and confirm transaction details while email remains unavailable.

So, despite the tech being outdated compared to more modern devices, it turns out that sometimes relying on old-school equipment can be the perfect modern solution.

On November 13, companies across Japan began using fax and other alternatives to keep communications moving. Disruption has also reached government offices, with some asking people to contact them by telephone or fax while their email systems remain unavailable.

Since then, Japanese users across social media have been praising fax machines and other analogue tech for enabling them to continue communicating throughout the outages.

“Unbelievable, faxes are saving the day yet again…it reminded me of when faxes were getting renewed attention after the recent cyberattack. When you’re in a bind, you can always count on analog,” wrote one user on X.

“Our company wasn’t affected by the Outlook issue, but since other companies were, emails were only going one way to our phones. We used to exchange photos by texting them to each other, but in the end, even in this day and age, fax was just unbeatable,” added another.

IDFA Artistic Director Isabel Arrate Fernandez On Festival’s Competition Lineups, “Huge” Interest In Elizabeth Holmes Doc, And AI In Documentaries

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The 39th edition of IDFA – the world’s biggest nonfiction film festival – is underway in Amsterdam, gathering the documentary field’s top talents, Oscar seekers and emerging filmmakers with dynamic work.

“It’s hectic, all the last-minute things,” artistic director Isabel Arrate Fernandez told Deadline just before the kickoff. “From the lineup competitions to also the industry program we have, there are many exciting things going on.”

The opening night slot Friday evening goes to White Tiger Column, a world premiere directed by Robert Mentov, Dwight Jantzi, and Bryan Dickie that’s set within the resistance movement opposing Myanmar’s military-dominated regime. The festival calls it “a striking example of documentary cinema… The film offers a moving portrait of a courageous generation.”

White Tiger Column is programmed in International Competition, along with films from Morocco, Madagascar, Burkina Faso, Brazil, France, Germany, the Republic of Georgia, and other countries. An Incomplete Day in the History of Georgia 10.06.93, directed by Salomé Jashi, examines a critical moment for Georgia in 1993 when the country, recently freed from Soviet domination, debated measures to replace the Russian ruble. Considering Vladimir Putin’s attempt to reassert Russian dominance over much of the old USSR – including Ukraine – it’s a film with immediate relevance. Watch on Deadline

A baker’s dozen of features is programmed in the festival’s Envision section, which platforms films with bold stylistic choices. “The way we look at both of our competitions,” Arrate Fernandez notes, “they are of equal value… In Envision, we are really looking at filmmakers that are challenging documentary, are challenging the form.” Arrate Fernandez adds, “If we don’t have that, then how does a genre, how does cinema evolve?”

One of the Envision titles, Ragazzi al fiume, examines the experience of three young Afghan immigrants to Switzerland. The IDFA program notes the documentary directed by Pablo Briones “gradually evolves into an observational portrait of isolation, loss, and the longing for a peaceful future.”

Fellow Envision Competition documentary The Black Box Is Orange focuses on the Israeli town of Shlomi that “was built on land belonging to the destroyed Palestinian village of Al-Bassa, near the Lebanese border,” according to the IDFA program. “Beginning with the Nakba mass displacement of Palestinians in 1948, its residents were forced out and citizens of the newly established state of Israel were settled there. All traces of the previous inhabitants were to be destroyed, including all Palestinian archives.”

That film is directed by Yuula Benivolski, an Israeli Jewish filmmaker born in Canada and raised in the village of Shlomi. (Two years ago, she posted a video on YouTube discussing her decision to renounce her Israeli citizenship. “It wasn’t a decision I made lightly… Yes, I did it in response to Israel’s horrific assault on Gaza,” she explained, before adding, “Whether Israel realizes it or not, all this death harms its own people as well.”).

More than 250 films are programmed across the festival’s sections. Among them are some of the strongest contenders for Oscars, including To Hold a Mountain, the Sundance winner directed by Biljana Tutorov and Petar Glomazić; Once Upon a Time in Harlem, directed by David Greaves and based upon material shot by his father, William Greaves (this festival is separately screening a large selection of William Greaves’s work); Musk, directed by Alex Gibney; Closure, directed by Michał Marczak, and You Can See Everything, the box office sensation about Elizabeth Holmes that’s directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim. Do they know the imprisoned Theranos founder in the Netherlands? Maybe so, maybe not, but enthusiasm for the film has certainly crossed the Atlantic.

Interest in You Can See Everything “has been huge,” Arrate Fernandez affirms. “The festival screenings sold out very quickly… It’s definitely generated some attention, to put it mildly.”

IDFA has become an increasingly important stop for filmmakers in Oscar contention, in part because of the plethora of Academy documentary branch voters who are expected to attend. (“It’s difficult to count them, I can tell you,” Arrate Fernandez notes. “Let’s see.”). Last year’s winner in IDFA’s International Competition, A Fox Under a Pink Moon, has become a leading contender for Oscar consideration this year. (That film’s protagonist and co-director, the young artist Soraya Akhlaghi, designed this year’s IDFA’s poster and contributed artwork used in the festival trailer. Watch it below).

Arrate Fernandez mentions other films in the 2026 IDFA lineup making a strong bid for Oscar recognition.

“The winner of Cannes, Rehearsals for a Revolution; Traces, a Ukrainian film talking about sexual assault as a war crime against women; Closure, NAZA… there are many interesting films there that I hope will make it,” she says. “Even though they are not the films with the bigger budgets and everything for their campaigning, I really hope they get far.”

14th, another top Oscar contender that will screen at IDFA, is among the documentaries that make use of AI – in the case of Ava DuVernay’s film, to animate vintage photographs. For the first time ever, the artistic director tells us, IDFA asked filmmakers to complete a form indicating if they had incorporated AI into their film’s production.

“Last year, we noticed that sometimes we were wondering while watching films and now we have this, we can go back and see what they filled out just to understand,” Arrate Fernandez comments. “But also, it’s part of the conversations that are taking place with regards to how to use [AI], what is acceptable? There are so many questions, so I think that really [speaks] to conversations that are being held within the industry.”

To that end, IDFA will be hosting two panel discussions on AI, part of a robust lineup of industry talks. A panel titled “Fighting Back – Cinema as an act of resistance?” will feature Oscar winner Laura Poitras, who is bringing They’re Here to IDFA, a short film she directed with Rachel Lauren Mueller that examines the fatal ICE surge in Minneapolis. Fellow panelists include Sofía Bordenave, director of Forest Up in the Mountain, and Mohanad Yaqubi, director of Revolutionaries Never Die. Fernandez is slated to moderate a conversation with this year’s IDFA Guest of Honor, filmmaker Tatiana Huezo.

This is the second year of Arrate Fernandez’s leadership as artistic director. Last year, she faced questions after IDFA revealed it would not invite to the festival what it considered to be Israeli state-supported organizations, such as DocAviv. On its website, IDFA spells out its position under a section titled “Principles and guidelines” governing the 2026 festival.

“For films, makers, and organizations from countries where freedom of expression is under pressure or human rights are violated, it is essential to assess and investigate their independence carefully, alongside the artistic selection process,” IDFA writes. “In this way, we aim to prevent governments and organizations involved in and/or suspected of serious human rights violations from gaining a platform at IDFA.”

The IDFA policy statement continues, “Films, makers, and organizations are never excluded on the basis of their origin and/or nationality, but rather on the basis of any possible relationship with regimes or organizations held internationally accountable for serious human rights violations.”

Arrate Fernandez tells Deadline, “We also really looked at what we did last year, how we’re implementing things, what are our internal processes. And basically, for… a film work or a film project, everything just went through the regular route of being evaluated by our different teams of programmers on the creative criteria. And the moment that films make it through the different phases and end up on a short list, then in cases of countries… that are known for human rights violations, we check the independence of this film or of this project in the sense of also going about checking credits, who are financiers. At the same time, it’s not a blanket approach in the sense of, ‘Oh, this financier, it’s out.’ We also look at the film, so we still have our case-by-case approach.”

She continued, “Then in terms of accreditations… we have the similar line in the sense of having this case-by-case approach.”

American Israeli filmmaker Rachel Leah Jones (Advocate) will be presenting her new project, Podium (You Have Three Minutes), at IDFA Forum, the event’s pitching, co-financing and co-production market for nonfiction and new media projects. Logline: “What does resistance sound like when delivered from the parliamentary podium? Since Israel’s founding, Palestinian lawmakers have taken the mic to appeal, demand, denounce, and warn — articulating a powerful counter-history of the state itself.”

Avi Mograbi, described in the IDFA program as “one of Israel’s most distinguished filmmakers,” heads to the festival for the Dutch premiere of his short documentary Wild Asparagus (Halayon), which bowed at the Venice Film Festival. In Collapse, Israeli director Anat Even returns to Kibbutz Nir Oz, where she lost her home and friends in the Hamas sneak attack of October 7, 2023. She films what remains there and also attempts to enter Gaza, documenting “a gathering of settlers who plan to build in Gaza and proclaim this by chanting, ‘Occupy, expel, settle,’” the festival program reads. “An introspective and revealing film essay on loss, grief, revenge, dehumanization and indifference.” (The program description includes this note: “This film contains content on potentially sensitive topics.”).

The production company for Collapse is listed as France.

“You won’t find films that have as a production country Israel” in this year’s festival, Arrate Fernandez indicates, “but there are Israeli filmmakers. The thing is also that we don’t ask people for their nationality… We have several films that are taking place in Israel and others that are taking place in Palestine or in the Occupied Territories.”

Just about every corner of the globe is reflected in the festival lineup. IDFA runs through November 22, a chance for documentary filmmakers and lovers of the form to gather in person in one of the world’s great cities.

Says Arrate Fernandez, “In a world where storytelling is being formatted by decision makers, AI, algorithm, numbers, I think it’s important to treasure these spaces.”

Many were therefore sceptical of United's investment in the player. While there was hope that the 22-year-old could develop into an effective ball-winning midfielder in the Premier League, there was an acknowledgement that a fee of £50 million incurred risk.

In a stunning report detailing Carrick's downfall at Old Trafford, the Times have revealed that, after enacting the deal with Chelsea, the United board accepted that they'd overpaid for Santos.

While the Red Devils' chiefs had unearthed some promising underlying defensive data on the player, there was little evidence in his numbers and the scouting that suggested he could step up as an in-possession force.

Andrey Santos' Statistics (Premier League Only)

Six Premier League appearances into his United career, and it's become apparent that the United hierarchy were right to have reservations about spending £50 million on Santos.

After playing his part in dismal defeats to Atlético Madrid, Como and Bournemouth, Santos has been relegated to the bench in the last two games, since Kobbie Mainoo has returned from injury.

Still just 22, there is certainly ample room for Santos to improve and establish himself as a dependable squad member at United.

From Mikel Arteta to David Moyes, all current Premier League managers have been ranked from best to worst.

However, at the moment, the decision to splash such a significant sum on an unwanted Chelsea player looks extremely dubious.

With Carrick, there was good reason to hand the coach the permanent job, even if it ultimately turned sour. United were one of the better teams in the English top flight after the former midfielder was appointed.

With Santos, there was no reason to bow to Chelsea's excessive demands based on what the player had produced in his career up until that point.
