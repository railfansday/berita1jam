Scientists Discover a Hidden Weakness in One of the Deadliest Cancers

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Researchers have identified another potential approach to treating pancreatic cancer, with early experiments suggesting that blocking a protein could slow tumor growth and strengthen immune defenses.

Pancreatic tumors can be surrounded by tissue so dense that it compresses blood vessels, making it difficult for nutrients to reach cancer cells. Even under these conditions, the cells continue growing. How they obtain the materials needed to survive has remained an important question.

Researchers at Fox Chase Cancer Center have identified one way pancreatic cancer cells obtain cholesterol and other fats, known as lipids. Their study, published in Nature Cancer, also identified a protein that helps control this process and could offer a potential target for cancer treatment.

The research focused on pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), the most common type of pancreatic cancer. These tumors contain large numbers of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), cells that produce and organize connective tissue. As CAFs and their collagen fibers expand, they can compress nearby blood vessels, restricting the tumor’s blood supply.

Despite contributing to this nutrient shortage, CAFs also help cancer cells overcome it.

“We found that CAFs can act as a source of these nutrients,” said Edna Cukierman, PhD, a professor and co-leader of the Cancer Signaling and Microenvironment Program at Fox Chase and co-senior author of the study. “Using laboratory and animal models, we discovered that CAF units acquire cholesterol from the bloodstream and transfer it to nearby pancreatic cancer cells through a process called trogocytosis”

Trogocytosis occurs when one cell takes small pieces of another cell’s membrane during direct contact. In this case, pancreatic cancer cells acquire pieces of CAF membranes containing cholesterol and other lipids that support their survival and growth.

The researchers discovered that this transfer depends on a protein called anoctamin 6 (ANO6), which helps prepare the CAF membrane for the exchange.

“These findings help explain how pancreatic cancer cells obtain essential nutrients despite the unusual structure of the pancreatic tumor microenvironment,” said Igor Astsaturov, MD, PhD, a professor at Fox Chase and co-senior author of the study. “Cancer-associated fibroblasts are not passive bystanders. They actively support tumor growth by transferring lipids to cancer cells.”

When researchers reduced or eliminated ANO6 in CAFs, the cells became less effective at supporting pancreatic cancer cell survival. In mouse models, tumors supported by CAFs with functional ANO6 grew substantially faster than those without functional ANO6.

The same nutrient transfer process also appeared to interfere with the immune system.

Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) are immune cells that recognize and kill cancer cells. When these T cells come into contact with CAFs, they can acquire membrane material containing cholesterol. Excess cholesterol in the tumor environment can contribute to T-cell exhaustion, making these immune cells less effective at fighting cancer.

“With Serge Fuchs, professor at the University of Pennsylvania and co-author on the paper, we were surprised to find that the same cellular mechanism can benefit the tumor in two ways – by supplying cancer cells with lipids while also weakening cancer-fighting T cells,” said Astsaturov. “Targeting ANO6 could potentially disrupt both of these tumor-supporting functions at the same time.”

In mouse models, blocking ANO6 reduced cholesterol accumulation in T cells within tumors and improved markers of their activity. Laboratory experiments also showed that T cells exposed to CAFs with reduced ANO6 were better able to kill cancer cells.

The researchers then tested whether drugs that interfere with ANO6-dependent activity could slow tumor growth. In mouse models, treatment with niclosamide or clofazimine significantly reduced tumor weight. Niclosamide also reduced cholesterol delivery to pancreatic cancer cells.

These findings suggest that targeting ANO6 could both restrict a nutrient source for pancreatic tumors and improve the immune response against them. However, the results remain preclinical, and further research is needed to determine whether selectively blocking ANO6 would work as a treatment in patients.

Trogocytosis is also only one of several ways pancreatic cancer cells may obtain lipids. The researchers suggest that the drugs tested could potentially help treat other difficult cancers, although that possibility requires further investigation.

“We believe our findings identify ANO6 as a potential therapeutic target that could simultaneously disrupt the nutrient supply supporting pancreatic cancer cells and reduce the immune-suppressive effects of the tumor microenvironment,” said Astsaturov.

The findings also build on nearly a decade of research into how the tissue surrounding pancreatic tumors supports cancer growth.

“Excitingly, reducing ANO6 expression also disrupted the stromal synapse-mimicry circuit we previously identified, which coordinates tumor nutritional support, CAF-unit metabolism, and immune suppression. These findings position ANO6 as the missing mechanistic link that unifies nearly a decade of discoveries by our team,” said Cukierman.

Ripple-Backed Evernorth Plunges 40% as Insiders Move to Sell 73.7 Million Shares

📅 2026-11-11  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ripple-backed XRP treasury company Evernorth Holdings (XRPN) is under extreme pressure as its shares have declined by over 40% while big investors like Ripple Labs move closer to potentially selling millions of shares.

Earlier this week, Evernorth filed a prospectus with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), indicating that the XRP-focused company has registered a whopping 73,688,162 Class A shares for potential resale by existing shareholders.

Evernorth's prospectus comes as the XRP-focused company is enduring a brutal stock market debut. XRPN began trading on Nasdaq on Oct. 12.

On Nov. 10, Evernorth's stock price dropped 23.54%. XRPN ended the day at $7.96, compared to $10.41 in the previous trading session. Moreover, about 1.39 million shares were traded, which is a significant jump from about 369,000 shares on Nov. 9.

So far, Evernorth's share price has declined by around 48% from its closing price on Oct. 28 ($15.26). The magnitude of the decline is even greater if we consider the high share price on its debut day ($19.39). XRPN's stock price is down by around 59% from that high.

Some of the biggest names and companies linked to the XRP ecosystem are shareholders who have registered shares for potential sale, as per the prospectus filed with the SEC.

For instance, the biggest shareholder is Arrington XRP Capital Fund, which was a sponsor of the merger between Evernorth and Armada Acquisition Corp. II. It has about 33.82 million shares registered for possible resale.

Similarly, the second-largest shareholder is Ripple Labs, which has about 18.14 million shares registered. Then there are various entities linked to SBI Holdings, a big Japanese financial institution. They have 11.79 million shares registered, as per the prospectus filed with the SEC.

The Larsen Lam Children's Remainder Trust, which is linked to Ripple co-founder Chris Larsen, has about 7.15 million shares registered for possible sale.

Other names mentioned in the filing include Pantera Capital, GSR Markets Switzerland and Kraken parent firm Payward.

It is important to understand that the fact that they've registered shares for sale doesn't mean that these investors have sold their holdings or necessarily plan to sell them.

For example, Ripple, the Arrington-sponsored entity and a Larsen-linked entity are all subject to six-month lock-up agreements following the completion of Evernorth's business combination on Oct. 9.

These lock-up agreements are typically set to expire on April 9, 2027, with some exceptions.

Average New Car Price Hits New Record High (And Even The Good News Doesn't Sound So Good)

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

Great news everyone! We just set another new record, and this time it has nothing to do with diesel prices. Sadly, though, it isn't nearly as cool as the Chevrolet Corvette ZR1X destroying the Pikes Peak production car record over the summer. No, unfortunately for everyone who still has to work for a living, Kelley Blue Book reports the average transaction price for new cars hasn't just crossed the $50,000 mark. In October, the average transaction price rose to a level never before seen in the U.S. So that's pretty exciting. Aren't you thrilled to be alive right now to experience it?

Using data from its parent company, Cox Automotive, KBB says the average price consumers paid for new cars in the U.S. hit $51,109 in the month of October. That's 1.3% higher than it was in September and a 2.1% increase year-over-year. Higher prices aren't usually a good thing, especially when those higher prices are on things most people need to use every day (cars) instead of luxuries that you don't have to own to participate in society. The average MSRP of new vehicles is also up, reportedly hitting $52,933 in October, up 1.4% versus September and 2.4% higher year-over-year.

That said, it isn't all bad news. There's always a silver lining, and this time around, KBB says, "The Kelley Blue Book ATP estimate marked an all-time high even as vehicle price inflation in October was below long-term averages." Yes, you read that right. The average price people are paying for new cars hit an all-time high in October, but at least the rate of inflation isn't as high as it could be. Don't you feel grateful for that? I know I do. From the sound of it, dealers are also perfectly happy to let customers pay those high prices, as opposed to chasing faster turnover:

The higher ATP – an estimate of the average product prices paid in October before all available incentives, taxes and fees are factored in – was driven in part by both price inflation and strong sales of expensive midsize and full-size SUVs and full-size pickup trucks. Incentive spending increased modestly in October but remains below long-term averages, suggesting automakers and dealers are not aggressively pursuing sales and inventory reduction, despite record prices.

KBB also found that incentive spending accounted for 6.8% of ATP in October. With September 2026 at 6.7% and October 2025 at 6.5%, incentives are trending upward but it's been slow and doesn't appear to have contributed significantly to October's ATP record. Plus, not every car costs the same, and some segments will naturally cost less to buy than others. Once you break pricing out by segment, only one really feels like an outlier. Here's how KBB's breakdown looks:

As you can see, midsize SUVs were number one in sales volume and had an ATP of $50,570, but the people buying compact cars only paid $28,000 for them on average. That probably seems high for a compact, but remember, a 2027 Nissan Sentra without any options has an MSRP of almost $25,000 with destination, and an extra $3,000 to drive something nicer than a stripped-out, base-model Sentra sounds pretty fair to me.

Meanwhile, full-size pickup trucks placed third behind midsize and compact SUVs, but the average transaction price is close to double what people are paying for Honda CR-Vs and Hyundai Tucsons. Yes, on average, full-size truck buyers pay $67,912 for their trucks. Some Ford F-150s will cost more, some Chevrolet Silverados will cost less, but on average a full-size pickup truck buyer spent more than $65,000 to buy their truck.

That said, it's also worth reminding everyone that if you adjust for inflation, the 1997 Toyota Camry cost more than a brand new 2026 Camry does today. Truck buyers' obsession with expensive trucks skews the average, but if raises had kept up with inflation, it wouldn't be nearly as scary to hear the Camry now starts at $30,995. But while many had their eyes on larger tech figures closer to the president, like Meta’s Mark Zuckerberg or on-again-off-again presidential ally Elon Musk, Palantir CEO Alex Karp beat them to the punch.

In a sit-down interview with Politico, Karp soft-launched his avowed support for the Democrats, and voiced concern over the current administration’s policies.

“I’ve been a Democrat my whole life. I grew up in a far-left environment. I know more about being a progressive than almost anybody on the planet, and I absolutely believe Palantir—all of our products are essentially progressive,” Karp told Politico.

While in the past, Karp has focused on supporting the military industrial complex through technology, he turned his focus onto economic injustice, suggesting Democrats have an advantage over Republicans when it comes to developing a solution.

“I’m really not pitching Palantir. I’m actually pitching, ‘What are you gonna do about economic justice in this country?’,” Karp said. “For many Republicans, there’s an orthodoxy that capitalism solves all problems. Maybe that was once true. I never thought it was true. It’s definitely not true now.

Past controversies, lucrative Trump-friendly contracts

The statement has undoubtedly raised some eyebrows on social media, particularly as the technology company—which Karp once referred to as “anti-woke“—has gained heat in the past years for its lucrative contracts with the Trump administration.

Perhaps its most notable—and controversial—is the $30 million contract for ImmigrationOS, a software made to help ICE track and deport undocumented immigrants. Karp addressed the controversy during the interview, commenting on trump’s approach to immigration enforcement.

“I was really happy to hear the president be honest that—you know, his words, I believe, were something like, ‘Yeah, we kinda went too far,’” Karp continued in the interview. “And I’m glad he’s saying in public now what I’ve been saying in private.”

“Pure survival grift”

A metal detectorist found Finland’s largest known silver coin hoard, dating back to the Viking Age

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A metal detectorist in Finland uncovered the country's largest known Late Iron Age coin hoard.

On September 3, 2026, metal detectorist Kalle Lappinen found two birch-bark containers buried between stones near the village center of Sysmä, in southern Finland. Together they held about 9.9 pounds (4.5 kilograms) of silver.

The hoard is the largest known coin hoard from Finland’s Late Iron Age, according to Lahti Museums, the regional museum that recovered it. It contains thousands of silver coins.

The previous record holder, found in Nousiainen in 1895, contained about 1,700 coins and other objects. It weighed about 4.9 pounds (2.2 kilograms).

The find spot was inspected the next day. Esko Tikkala, an archaeologist at the regional museum responsible for the area, directed the recovery.

The two containers lay about 18 inches (45 centimeters) deep, between stones and roughly 39 feet (12 meters) apart.

Besides the coins, they held a piece of hacksilver, plus a bracelet, a mount and a bead, all made of silver.

The coins have not yet been cleaned, sorted or counted. Their exact weight, number, origin and dating will be set by further study.

The site lies on Ohrasaari, in Sysmä. A new sand-surfaced path now leads to the find spot, and a sign about the discovery is on the way, the municipality reports.

Even before cleaning, Tikkala could date some of the coins to between the 1000s and the 1050s.

The coins come from several regions, including Sweden and the areas of present-day Britain and Germany. The museum has not yet said how many coins come from each region.

The most distant coins in the Sysmä hoard are dirhams, Islamic coins from the area of present-day Turkey and Iran.

The coins should help show how wide the era’s trade networks were. Many similar finds have already been made near Sysmä, but this hoard stands out for its size.

Viking Age coins circulated freely, and their value came mainly from their silver content rather than from where they were minted.

The Finnish Heritage Agency made that point about a smaller hoard found by detectorists in Mynämäki, in southwestern Finland, in 2022. Agency archaeologists excavated that site and recovered the remaining pieces.

That hoard included about 100 silver coins and about 30 pieces of hacksilver, the chopped silver used as currency.

Its coins were minted in places as far apart as England, Denmark, Germany, Iraq and Central Asia. Twelve were struck for the Danish king Harald Bluetooth, who died in 986.

The Mynämäki coins span roughly 250 years. The oldest is an Umayyad dirham from 743 or 744, while most date to the final decades of the 900s.

The makeup of that hoard closely matches hoards from Poland, and the agency concluded that it very likely came from Pomerania. It was apparently buried soon after it reached Mynämäki.

The label “Viking Age” refers to the period, Lahti Museums points out. It does not mean the hoard belonged to Vikings or that Finland was Viking territory.

The discovery has also prompted discussion of why such a large fortune was hidden in two places.

That work is expected to show more about the role of Sysmä and the surrounding Päijät-Häme region in Late Iron Age trade and cultural networks.

“Museums do important work in researching, recording and preserving cultural heritage for future generations,” said Tuulia Tuomi, museum director of Lahti Museums.

She added that the wide attention the discovery received in Finland and abroad shows how broadly archaeological heritage interests people.

In Finland, objects more than 100 years old that are found in the ground belong to the state. Finders must report them to the Finnish Heritage Agency through its online notification service.

Finders should not clean the objects or dig further, and they should record the exact find location. Finds from a known ancient monument belong to the state without compensation.

The agency may redeem a find or pass that right to another museum. For finds containing precious metal, the minimum price is the value of the metal plus 25 percent.

Redemption currently takes about three years, and selling such objects is illegal, the agency’s guidance for finders states.

Tuomi also credited responsible conduct by hobbyists with helping to preserve valuable information about such finds.

The information was obtained from Lahti Museums.

Rand Fishkin’s Seattle game studio serves up first trailer for ‘The Snackbar at the End of the World’

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

by Kurt Schlosser on Nov 11, 2026 at 11:21 amNovember 11, 2026 at 11:21 am

If the eventual release of “The Snackbar at the End of the World” is a full-on meal for video game fans, news this week of the game’s first trailer should at least suffice as an appetizer.

Seattle-based Snackbar Studio, co-founded by SparkToro founder Rand Fishkin and his wife, writer Geraldine DeRuiter, unveiled its debut title and Steam page on Tuesday. The hand-drawn narrative adventure RPG puts players in the role of a chef trying to save a struggling family restaurant in a magical version of 1960s Italy.

The studio now plans to launch the game late next year, after originally targeting the second half of 2026. Snackbar raised $2.15 million from 38 angel investors two years ago, and the project began four years ago, according to Fishkin. The team includes five co-founders (Fishkin, DeRuiter, game designer Nicolas Kraj, lead programmer Miriam Cabrera and art director Francesco Mazza) plus about 10 contractors.

In the studio’s description on Steam, players forage for ingredients in the “forbidden wilds” outside town, battling explosive tomatoes, chaotic zucchini and vendetta-driven poultry. They then cook dishes back at the restaurant before a tyrannical mayor known as the Sindaco can shut it down. The game mixes combat and stealth with cooking mini-games based on real-world recipes.

The trailer is set to “’O surdato ’nnammurato,” a classic Neapolitan song performed by professional musicians recruited by the studio’s music director. Fishkin said he and DeRuiter both cried the first time they heard it.

Fishkin is best known in Seattle tech circles as co-founder of SEO software company Moz and audience research startup SparkToro, and he more recently launched Alertmouse, a tool for tracking online mentions. DeRuiter, a writer and author, is Snackbar’s head writer.

In a LinkedIn post this week, Fishkin recalled telling DeRuiter during the COVID lockdown that he wanted to fulfill a childhood dream of making a video game. Her reply: “Don’t wait. Do it now. I’ll do it with you.”

In the same post, he said the game’s themes include found family and fighting fascism “even when there’s no hope you’ll win.” He called it a love letter to DeRuiter’s small family village in the mountains outside Naples, to that era of Italy, to video games, and to the many people both of their families have lost in recent years.

Moments like hearing that song come often in the work, Fishkin said, such as when a new feature clicks or a new animation brings DeRuiter’s characters to life. He has always loved art without being good at any form of it. “I may not be able to create art, but I can enable it,” he said. “What a wonderful feeling.”

There have been lows, too. The team spent its initial 18 months on the project with a contract agency that didn’t work out, losing money with nothing to show for it. Fishkin is glad they kept going, “even when it felt financially irresponsible.”

The game is entirely human-made, and Snackbar’s no-AI stance is firm. “Geraldine hates AI with the fury of a thousand suns,” Fishkin said. He once suggested, just once, that she use Google’s AI Mode to find a synonym, and she was mad at him for the rest of a six-hour flight home from Philadelphia. Even if players, the rest of the team and the games media were fine with AI (and they’re not), DeRuiter alone would make it a no-go, he said.

Still ahead are 10 to 12 months of finishing the game’s content, playtesting, bug fixing, marketing and launch prep. Fishkin called it daunting, but said team members have done it many times before, “even though it’s our first rodeo.”

Fishkin is still juggling Snackbar with SparkToro and Alertmouse, and describes his role at the studio as mostly oversight and management. SparkToro is growing again, and Alertmouse recently hit a big milestone in annual renewals with high retention. He and his Alertmouse co-founders are putting more money into the company to fund growth.

Balancing the three roles is a challenge, he said, but only rarely completely overwhelming. “Like this week.”
