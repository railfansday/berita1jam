Blade Runner 2099 might not be getting a second season, but the show’s final episode provided a tidy ending for everyone involved. The final episode sees the show’s main characters, Cora and Olwen (played by Hunter Schafer and Michelle Yeoh, respectively) reaching the Tyrell Launch Complex on a quest to find out where Tyrell’s memory is located.

The journey isn’t without its twists and turns: One person who is the key to obtaining Tyrell’s memory winds up dead, and Olwen’s future hangs in the balance as she gets closer to the end of her lifespan. Cora’s life as a Replicant also has a few complications of its own, too.

Here’s what happened in the season finale of Blade Runner 2099.

Olwen and Cora spend a large portion of the final episode searching for Tyrell’s memory at the Tyrell Launch Complex, which belongs to replicant creator Eldon Tyrell and serves as documentation about Nexus-1 replicants Julius and Helena.

Inside the complex, Olwen and Cora split up. Olwen finds Clotilde and they talk about how Julius killed her. When Clotilde suffers a fall, Olwen doesn’t allow her to die again; in exchange for saving her, Clotilde shares where Julius is within the complex.

Meanwhile, Julius discovers Cora’s whereabouts in the complex. Shortly after, Julius and Cora fight, and he inflicts a serious stab wound on her. He also destroys Tyrell’s memory and insists that he’s the only one who can extend Olwen’s life, but Olwen finds the pair, jumps in, and kills Julius with a gunshot.

Olwen spends the majority of the series aware that she’s at the end of her lifespan. As a Replicant, she was given a fixed limit to how long she can live and has accepted her fate—but she’s offered a second chance at life by the end of the series.

After saving Clotilde, she later reveals that she saw Tyrell’s memory before he died and knows how to re-create Olwen as a Nexus-1. Clotilde re-creates Olwen in exchange for Olwen having saved Clotilde’s life. By the end of the episode, Olwen emerges from a birthing bag, and she later leaves Earth on a spaceship.

The series ends with Cora and Greta understanding each other. Cora, a human posing as a Replicant, heads back to LAPD and is given a Replicant test by Greta to confirm her identity, but this time, Cora turns the questions on Greta and asks about her life.

The pair talks about Greta’s doll, leading Cora to come to the realization that the girl is still capable of understanding what it means to love and be loved. Cora becomes emotional at the realization, leading Greta to comment that Cora has high levels of empathy. This means Cora technically fails the Replicant test, but Greta allows her to continue hiding her identity and her work as a Replicant.

You can deduct only the medical and dental costs above 7.50% of your adjusted gross income, your income minus a few adjustments, and only if you itemize. Count the health premiums you pay yourself, Medicare’s included. If you’re self-employed and can’t join an employer’s plan, deduct your premiums separately, with no threshold or itemizing, up to your business’s profit.

For a retiree with $60,000 of adjusted gross income, the threshold is $4,500. With $9,000 of medical costs, Medicare premiums included, she can deduct the $4,500 above it if itemizing beats her standard deduction, which is bigger for anyone 65 or older.

Count what you paid out of pocket for doctors, hospitals, dentists, prescriptions, glasses, and hearing aids, plus the miles you drive to appointments at the IRS’s medical mileage rate. Premiums count too, including Medicare Part B and Part D, coverage you buy yourself, and qualified long-term care insurance up to an IRS limit set by age.

Nonprescription medicine other than insulin, most cosmetic surgery, and gym dues for general fitness don’t count. Neither do premiums your employer takes out of your pay before tax, or bills you pay from a health savings account, since that money was never taxed. IRS Publication 502 lists the rest.

If you’re self-employed, premiums for health coverage you buy on your own go on Schedule 1 instead, where they lower your adjusted gross income without a threshold or itemizing. Medicare premiums count there too, but the deduction can’t exceed your business’s profit. Any month you could join a subsidized plan through your job or your spouse’s doesn’t qualify, and premiums the deduction doesn’t cover can count with your other medical costs.

Costs count in the year you pay them, and a credit card charge counts in the year you make it, even if you pay off the card later. Say the retiree’s income stays at $60,000 and her costs run $6,000 a year. That clears her $4,500 threshold by $1,500 each year, or $3,000 over two years. The same $12,000 paid in one year clears it by $7,500, and that larger total is likelier to beat her standard deduction.

Once this year’s costs get close to your threshold, have the dental work, hearing aids, or eye surgery you’ve been putting off done before December 31, and pay the bill by then.
