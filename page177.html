Microsoft's SideWinder Strategic Commander was a failed attempt to reimagine RTS controls. 23 years later, why are some players still using it?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

How an unconventional peripheral became an equally unexpected survivor

PC players have been conditioned for decades to accept the keyboard-and-mouse setup for RTS games. One hand has to handle movement and commands through a keyboard, while the other points, drags, and selects with a mouse. Peripheral makers have, usually with the addition of more buttons, made many minor variations to this time-honored arrangement, but there's only ever been one real attempt to rethink it entirely for the PC: the Microsoft SideWinder Strategic Commander.

Unveiled on March 1st 2000 at Microsoft's Gamestock event, the Strategic Commander was one of three additions to their SideWinder line, with a scheduled release that October at a pricey $64.95. At E3 later that May, Microsoft demonstrated the Strategic Commander for the first time with SimCity 3000, while promoting its potential for other such games as Diablo II, Warcraft III, and Baldur's Gate II. This was all taking place during the so-called 'Golden Age of Real-Time Strategy Games,' which spanned roughly from the mid-1990s to the mid-2000s. It was when the RTS genre was at the peak of its mainstream popularity, and we fans were devoted to mastering its intricacies in any way we could to have a competitive edge.

Microsoft, all the while, were taking notes. "The team conceived the Strategic Commander in the late 1990s," Carl Ledbetter - then Industrial Design Manager for Microsoft's PC Hardware Group, now Head of Design for Xbox Devices - recalls to me. "As RTS games grew in popularity, we saw an interesting opportunity to design a new physical interface around a new interaction model. The idea was for it to be used left-handed in concert with right-hand PC desktop mouse control. We wanted to enable players to perform faster with more fluidity, comfort, optimised control placement for multiple hand sizes, simplicity of set-up, and ease of programming."

For Ledbetter and co. to realise this ambition, the Strategic Commander was designed as a unique combination of mouse, joystick, and gamepad. To manage the increasing complexities of the RTS games of the day, it came equipped with six programmable buttons, three shift buttons, and a three-position profile switch. Back then, these atypical features provided an unprecedented 72 possible key combinations across various configurations, all at the tip of our stubby little fingers. Better yet, it allowed us to toggle between three pre-configured profiles at a time, so that we could switch up our configurations on the fly depending on which stage of an RTS match we were in.

On release, contemporary reviewers acknowledged its originality, with critics like ActiveWin's Julien Jay describing it as "probably the most innovative (with the GameVoice) and revolutionary" peripheral designed specifically for strategy players. Others, like Neoseeker's Arnel Lim, suggested that it might’ve gone overboard with its customisation options, saying "you won't use anywhere close to all of them," in reference to the controller's programmable key combinations. Either way, the Strategic Commander failed to supplant conventional peripherals in the hands of most RTS players, and it was discontinued in 2003. As was the rest of the original SideWinder line, owing to declining sales and the industry-wide shift toward standard M+K setups for all PC games and genres.

However, unlike so many of its Microsoft-made siblings, this was not the end for the Strategic Commander. Niche as it was, it went on to enjoy a surprisingly long post-shelf life, one that was stretched out by a dedicated cult following - and that continues to this day. But why? Why, after all these years, is this forgotten peripheral from a discontinued line maintaining such a enthusiastic fanbase, years after the industry around it had written it off as a failure?

The answer lies in software and OS compatibility, as much as the hardware's own appeal. Andreas Weis was the developer of one of the first unofficial patches for the Strategic Commander, allowing players to keep on using it even after the original software stopped being compatible with newer versions the Windows. By that point, the device was a decade old, and well into 'official' obsolescence, yet Weis still answered the call of the Strategic Commander's righteously frustrated - if numerically few - fans. "There was a lot of interest back in the early 2010s in software patches to keep the Strategic Commander functional when the original software for the Strategic Commander stopped working," he explains. "Users were asking around on internet forums about how to get it to work on Windows 7 and Vista machines. Since I was studying computer science at the time, I was quite interested in reverse-engineering, and I hacked together a simple tool that would cover my use cases for the device."

In the years that followed, other patches were released to keep the Strategic Commander operational, the most notable of which was Philip Merwarth’s Strategic Engine software in 2013. Unfortunately, they weren’t open-source. Worse was that the protocol for programming the Strategic Commander wasn’t documented anywhere online in a way that was accessible for users. This is why Weis realized that he could make a unique contribution.

"I polished my old prototype code for the Strategic Commander software patch and released it as a library, so that other people could use it to write their own applications for programming the tool," he says. "As for me, I released one application that made use of the library publicly, the vJoy feeder application, which allows the device to be used with games that support joystick inputs." Curiously, Weis, by this time and like many others, had found other applications for a device with such a specific selling point, and even continues to use the Strategic Commander sparingly in 2026.

"The production quality of Microsoft hardware at the time was just top-notch, and I love the design of the old SideWinder devices, which is why I did not just throw the thing out when the software stopped working," he tells me. "It was worth keeping around to find new uses for it over the years, and I eventually realized that it makes quite a nice addition to a HOTAS (Hands-On Throttle And Stick) setup, where I could put actions that aren’t time-critical and don't fit well on the throttle on the Strategic Commander instead. For example, when I’m playing Elite Dangerous in VR, I’d put actions like deploying the landing gear on the Strategic Commander to free up buttons on the throttle for actions that were more relevant for combat."

When looking back at the history of Microsoft’s SideWinder line, it’s easy to call the Strategic Commander a failed experiment, given the fact that most of us are still using the same standard mouse-and-keyboard setup to play RTS games. However, the central premise behind its design philosophy was that the interface between a player and a game could be redesigned to cater to specific genre quirks, and decades following its release, it’s still maintained a coalition of loyal users: some RTS players who believed the Strategic Commander really pulled it off, and those found other uses for the device in other flavours of PC game. And with the issue of modern compatibility, a roadblock for so much retro hardware, bypassed by homemade patches, both camps can continue twisting and clicking their Strategic Commanders unimpeded.

All the same, I wonder if it might have basked in more conventional success, had Microsoft made more forward-thinking decisions. The Strategic Commander has proven over the years that its unprecedented customisation had applications far beyond what Ledbetter’s team had envisioned, and once the popularity of RTS games settled down, other, similar click-heavy subgenres like MOBAs and Real-Time Tactics got big as well. Would a more officially maintained, potentially refined Strategic Commander model have served those crowds? I think it might. The narrow focus on RTS games may have also hurt the device's since-demonstrated broader appeal; if Microsoft had fully capitalised on this customisation selling point by communicating its benefits to everyone, not just those playing the single most popular PC genre at the time, it could have provided the blueprint for a proper, long-lived alternative to controllers, mice, and keyboards.

Alas, gaming history is littered with revolutionary peripherals that failed to actually revolutionise the industry surrounding them. The Strategic Commander is no different, but for the fact that it’s had this surprisingly long post-shelf life - and, as a result, the opportunity for insight into what could have been done to salvage the its failures. Still, that unexpected longevity can provide come comfort to the Strategic Commander's creators, as well as its lingering users.

"I think the design made sense for its time, especially with the rising popularity of RTS games," Ledbetter reflects. "Looking back, I would have liked more time to test and refine the Strategic Commander. I’m proud of what we brought to market, and I’m glad to know that it remains beloved by many players to this day."

An Ethereum exploit case has brought renewed attention to transaction-ordering risks as the SEC allows tokenized stock venues to set their own safeguards.

A federal judge on Nov. 16 acquitted Anton and James Peraire-Bueno in a $25 million Ethereum maximum extractable value (MEV) exploit case because prosecutors had failed to prove a material misrepresentation.

The decision leaves a separate question for tokenized US stock markets about what protects investors when transaction ordering works against them.

The SEC had already opened a conditional route for those markets in September on its framework, which preserves applicable securities antifraud and manipulation laws, but requires venues to disclose their policies for MEV even when they have none.

MEV concerns the value captured through transaction ordering. Four operators' notices show how much can differ within that framework, from transaction privacy and planned sequencing controls to the prospect that investors bear their own losses.

In her 46-page opinion, US District Judge Jessica Clarke found insufficient evidence to sustain wire fraud and the related wire-fraud and money-laundering conspiracy charges. The missing element was a material misrepresentation in the brothers' Omakase operation.

The opinion describes a 2023 operation that used lure transactions to attract sandwich-trading bots and exploited a relay-code vulnerability, producing about $25 million in 12 seconds. The targeted bots themselves sought to profit by placing transactions around someone else's trade, a practice that can leave that trader with a worse execution price.

The case was more specific than a simple claim that someone profited from transaction ordering, since prosecutors had to establish the charged fraud.

Clarke found that the trial evidence did not establish a material misrepresentation in the Omakase strategy, and frustrating MEV-Boost's intended design could not supply that missing element.

The court also distinguished precedents involving spoofing, where orders create a misleading impression before being canceled. Here, the lure transactions executed and also were not placed on a regulated market with rules prohibiting the conduct relied upon in those precedents.

That reasoning does not establish a universal requirement that wire fraud occur on a regulated market, nor does it determine whether all forms of MEV extraction are lawful.

In a March 30 letter, the Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association (SIFMA) argued that exposing pending trades to sandwiching and front-running can conflict with securities-market expectations. It called for technical protections or fair-sequencing rules when automated market makers handle securities.

An automated market maker (AMM) lets participants trade against assets committed to a pool using software-defined pricing. The ordering of those trades can affect who receives a favorable price and who captures the resulting profit.

Keeping an investor's pending instruction private and deciding who processes it first are economically significant choices.

The SEC's Sept. 17 order created temporary, conditional relief from the definition of an exchange for qualifying tokenized National Market System stock venues using permissioned AMM pools. Certain liquidity providers trading proprietary capital received separate relief from the dealer definition until Sept. 17, 2031.

The order retains applicable securities antifraud and antimanipulation provisions, including Exchange Act Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. Its conditions include permissioned access, public notices, records, and specified trading stoppages.

A venue must describe policies and procedures designed to address it, or state that it has none. It must disclose known material risks, explain any mitigation and compensation arrangements, and describe trading-abuse monitoring or acknowledge its absence.

A venue's published account of its protections can be detailed while leaving investors exposed to risks the operator expressly acknowledges.

The SEC's public list, reviewed Nov. 16, contains four notice filers. Their submission dates range from TSV LLC's Sept. 23 notice to Formation Securities' Nov. 9 filing.

TSV LLC's current notice says Canton's architecture generally avoids broadcasting readable pending transaction contents to unrelated participants, aiming to reduce opportunities for conventional public-mempool front-running and sandwich attacks.

The operator still acknowledges risks of information leakage, sequencing, compromised infrastructure, and collusion.

OKXICE's Nov. 5 notice disclosed that it has no policies and procedures designed to address MEV. That sits alongside general protections, including permissioned participation, participant minimum-output settings, surveillance and the ability to revoke access for manipulative trading. OKXICE says those features mitigate, rather than eliminate, MEV risk.

Its planned pools use Uniswap v4 on XLayer, where the notice says the sequencer generally orders transactions according to offered network fees.

Perpetuals.com's notice proposes generally first-in, first-out sequencing, with priority for trading-halt instructions and signed arrival receipts intended to make ordering verifiable. Yet its affiliated sequencer would receive nonpublic transaction information.

A private pending-transaction route can reduce exposure to outsiders while leaving a concentrated information advantage to manage.

Its October notice describes safeguards that were not fully implemented and surveillance intended to precede trading. Formation's notice likewise describes planned safeguards for its Solana venue.

Preventing an abusive trade, detecting it afterward, and compensating its victim are different functions.

TSV says participants generally should not expect compensation for market or impermanent losses, compromised participant systems or losses caused by unaffiliated third parties, unless it expressly agrees otherwise or the law requires it.

Perpetuals.com reports no established compensation policy for the risks described in its notice. Formation specifically excludes compensation for lost or compromised private keys or wallets, which does not establish a general recovery guarantee for other losses.

Faith-based addiction recovery groups don’t often get federal funding. RFK Jr. wants to change that

While campaigning the week before the midterms, health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. visited Sheepgate, a Christian addiction recovery center in Colfax, Iowa. Sheepgate, Kennedy said, is a model for the type of faith-based center that doesn’t receive federal funding but that he believes should.

“We took a wonderful tour today and it was really impressive and really touching,” Kennedy said in a video about the visit.

The Trump administration has repeatedly emphasized the promise of faith-based addiction recovery programs, including in its 2026 National Drug Control Strategy. The Great American Recovery Initiative, co-chaired by Kennedy and Kathryn Burgum, extols the power of faith. And in late August, Kennedy tapped Monty Burks, who leads the Center for Faith at the Department of Health and Human Services, as interim executive director of the initiative. Like Kennedy and Burgum, Burks is in recovery from addiction.

Treatment and policy experts largely agree that faith communities — and a belief in something bigger than oneself — can play an important role in a person’s recovery. But some are concerned that federal grants will be directed to faith-based initiatives that don’t provide evidence-based treatment.

Sheepgate, for example, explicitly prohibits those who join its yearlong programs from using methadone, a drug approved by the Food and Drug Administration for opioid use disorder. They can only use another addiction medication, Suboxone, if they’ll be fully tapered off of it within the first three months, according to the center’s student guidelines — a quick timeline that doctors generally recommend against. It’s not alone: Research has found that faith-based treatment centers are less likely than secular ones to offer medications for opioid use disorder, and more likely to refuse clients who may be taking them.

Refusing to accept people on FDA-approved medications “is so irresponsible,” said Ayana Jordan, an addiction psychiatrist at NYU Langone who works with faith organizations on recovery efforts. “I would not want to provide federal funds to that type of recovery center because they’re actively working against things that we know can save lives.”

But Michael Hunsberger, Sheepgate’s regional president, said the issue isn’t so black and white. Sheepgate would accept people taking addiction medications, he told STAT, if the center received more funding and was able to build out the medical staff. He argued that faith-based organizations are often excluded from public funds regardless of the level of care they provide.

“We could literally allow multiple of those prescriptions and bring on different medical staff and still be excluded from certain dollars due to our faith-based beliefs,” Hunsberger said.

Medications for opioid use disorder are just a few on a long list of drugs that Sheepgate doesn’t have the capacity to distribute or monitor the use of, Hunsberger said. When somebody isn’t a good fit for Sheepgate due to any medication use, the staff tries to find another organization where they could seek help.

While the Trump administration has continued to broadly support medications for substance use disorder — which can be highly effective at preventing overdose deaths — Kennedy has repeatedly cast doubt on existing systems for treating addiction. He’s also expressed support for unconventional (and scientifically dubious) approaches to treatment, including “wellness farms” where people reside in nature and engage in manual labor. Kennedy’s own path to long-term recovery from drugs and alcohol leaned strongly on groups like Alcoholics Anonymous and his Catholic faith.

But what worked for Kennedy won’t work for everyone, experts say. As Trump’s tax-cut bill goes into effect and more people lose health coverage, “what really concerns me is that there seems to be an expectation from the Trump administration that these faith-based groups are going to fill those gaps,” said Libby Jones, associate vice president of the Global Health Advocacy Incubator’s Overdose Prevention Initiative.

The administration has not made official regulatory changes to the federal grant process, but it has gestured that the bar may be lowered for faith-based groups, Jones said. A “Dear Colleague” letter from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration stressed that faith-based groups should receive “equal treatment” in the grants process. The Justice Department has deemed unconstitutional Biden administration regulations that aimed to prohibit the use of federal funds for “explicitly religious activities” and protect people from discrimination on the basis of faith.

Experts believe there should be clear stipulations that faith-based organizations receiving federal funding follow evidence-based care, and that federal agencies should ensure that funding goes to a diverse array of religious groups, not only to Christian organizations.

“I am always heartened to know that there is money that is being put into faith-based institutions,” Jordan said. “But we have an obligation to taxpayers — this is taxpayers’ money — to say what actually works.”

Sheepgate would welcome any oversight and regulations that may come with federal funding, Hunsberger said, as long as it does not interfere with the organization’s core identity.

“If those federal dollars change our identity as a faith-based organization, then that would change us accepting those dollars,” Hunsberger said. But, “the faith community — the majority of it — is not against the medical side. It’s just, a lot of times, we’re pitted against each other.”

The Department of Health and Human Services did not respond directly to STAT’s questions about how it may assess the efficacy of faith-based recovery organizations for future funding opportunities. “For generations, these organizations have provided hope, accountability, mentorship, housing, workforce opportunities, and the relationships that help people sustain long-term recovery, often with limited resources and little government support,” HHS deputy press secretary Lizzie Harvey said in a statement.

This year, HHS has given supplemental funding awards of $500,000 each to Alabama, Kansas, and West Virginia to pursue faith-based prevention work. Kennedy has also said that SAMHSA’s STREETS grants — which send $12 million over four years to communities in support of work on the intersection of homelessness and addiction — will also have a “new emphasis on faith-based recovery,” according to the Iowa Capitol Dispatch.

Both of those grant notices included language directing recipients to employ evidence-based practices. But another “Dear Colleague” letter written by SAMHSA leadership earlier this year warned against relying on medications alone for addiction treatment, so as to avoid what it calls “a default sentence to life-long medication use.” That guidance, which has now been baked into SAMHSA’s terms and conditions for all grant recipients, created some confusion about the agency’s support of FDA-approved medications.

“There’s an ambiguity here that just needs to be clearer,” Jordan said, adding that the administration appears to be focusing largely on white, Christian organizations.

“The methods by which we define ‘faith-based’ and ‘evidence-based treatment’ need to be clear,” she said.

In the past four months, Kennedy and other HHS leaders have visited or hosted roundtables with at least half a dozen other Christian recovery centers. It’s unclear how many encourage or allow the use of FDA-approved medications for substance use disorders.

Without strict guidance from federal agencies, funding decisions may be left to states or more local jurisdictions. The Montgomery Alcohol, Drug Addiction and Mental Health Services (ADAMHS) Board, a county-level authority in Ohio, was tapped last month for one of eight federal STREETS grants. For years, the board has funded and coordinated with both faith-based and secular recovery homes, a housing agency, behavioral health facility, street outreach and medicine teams, and peer-support services.

The Montgomery ADAMHS board only funds recovery homes that are certified with the state, the board’s housing coordinator, Kaylyn Drodge, told STAT. That includes six Christian recovery homes and two secular ones. (They’re open to non-Christian faith groups, but have not received any such applications, Drodge said.)

Ohio law requires certified faith-based recovery homes to accept people with different religious backgrounds, as well as those who do not want to engage in any faith activities. But there are no requirements about accepting people on medications for opioid use disorder. Out of the six Christian recovery homes the board funds, two turn those people away.

Recovery homes in Ohio are not allowed to provide medical care, Drodge said. But in many places, those lines are blurred.

“For the general population, they are equating ‘recovery’ with ‘treatment,’” Jordan said. She believes organizations that don’t provide clinical treatment directly should still be eligible for federal funding, but the government should consider how they encourage or support patients to receive proven medical care.

Opinion: The problem with ‘zero tolerance for bad behavior’ signs at hospitals

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

By Autumn Fiester, Aliza Narva, and Stacy C. Smith

Fiester is professor of medical ethics and health policy at the University of Pennsylvania’s Perelman School of Medicine and director of the Penn Program in Clinical Conflict Management. Narva is the director of ethics at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania. Smith is a registered nurse, certified health care ethics consultant, and qualitative researcher.

Enter any hospital today and you’ll see signs warning patients and visitors that abusive language, disruptive behavior, or aggression will not be tolerated. Some threaten removal from the facility. Others warn that security or police may be called for infractions. Almost all of them declare “zero tolerance” for any of these behaviors.

The motivation behind these signs is understandable. Workplace violence against health care workers is a serious problem in U.S. hospitals, with research demonstrating an increase in frequency over the past five years.

In response to this widespread problem, both state governments and hospitals have made a concerted effort to demonstrate that they are taking this problem seriously. They have enacted a range of anti-violence measures that include reporting systems, de-escalation training programs, weapon-detection portals, increased security presence, and posted behavior signs.  Our own health system has implemented similar measures.

While these efforts are laudable in intent, not all of them are evidence-based with demonstrable efficacy in curtailing workplace violence. That includes the now-ubiquitous posted hospital behavior signs. Multiple studies that have tested these signs do not deter patients or families from committing acts of violence or reduce the frequency of physical threats.

If posted hospital behavior signs were merely ineffective at reducing workplace violence, they would be pointless, but not harmful.

But we believe that behavior signage is more problematic than it appears. The content of the signs sends clear messages to patients and families about their tenuous status and value in the institution. These signs telegraph to patients and their loved ones that they are on notice, warned that their care can be withheld and their behavior policed — even punished. In the urgency to reduce violence against health care workers, hospitals have failed to consider the message these signs communicate and whether that message aligns or conflicts with the core values of medicine: trust, care, compassion.

In our review of posted behavior signs, we found that they target only patient and visitor conduct. They rattle off a list of patient and family “do nots,” the hospital’s mechanisms of behavior enforcement, and the dire consequences of behavioral infractions. Even when the signs give a nominal nod to the institution’s commitment to reciprocal expectations for respectful communication between patients and the clinical staff, the list of “do nots” applies only to patients and families. They list no parallel behavioral standards for nurses or physicians. That makes behavior signs ignore the relational aspect of conflict. Research on lapses in clinical professionalism points to a more nuanced explanation for some of the challenging behaviors exhibited by families and patients. The Joint Commission has even named such clinical behavior as one of the causes of violence against health care workers.

Behavior signs also display from a type of institutional amnesia, forgetting why patients and families have come to the hospital in the first place: They are in pain, anxious, frightened, grieving. They may have or soon receive a life-altering diagnosis or a heartbreaking prognosis. They are vulnerable. Is this a group of people who should receive stern warnings and threats of removal as they enter the building?

When signs espouse “zero tolerance” for “noncompliant,” “disrespectful,” or “demanding” behavior, they signal to patients and their families that raising concerns about their care can be interpreted as misconduct. In a setting where patients and families already feel profound vulnerability, voicing a complaint about their clinical care can feel too risky.

That silence risks patient safety. Data show that patient and family feedback can reveal genuine safety concerns and quality issues that would fly under the radar if they remained unvoiced. Without patients and families feeling welcome to express concerns, hospitals can miss the exact information that would improve patient care and prevent patient harm.

Hospitals do not have to choose between protecting hospital staff and preserving patient/family trust in the institution.

Imagine signs that convey not threats, but shared expectations and a willingness to help when things go wrong:

“Our staff is here to help you during what may be one of the most difficult days of your life. We ask everyone, including staff, patients, and families, to treat one another with respect. If something is not going well, please let us know so we can help resolve it.”

Such messages still make clear that violence will not be tolerated. But they also clearly convey that health care is a partnership, that the experience of illness can be challenging, and that civility helps everyone. They express behavioral standards, while also indicating that the hospital’s response to challenging interactions is to help, not punish. Some hospital systems have already replaced their “zero tolerance” signs with civility signs that project care and concern.

Protecting health care workers from violence must continue to be an institutional priority, but that effort requires evidence-based prevention programs, including efforts to understand and address the root causes of conflict.

Messages about patient, family, and clinical staff behavior can be delivered in a way that acknowledges the concerns of hospital employees while also preserving patients’ and families’ sense of belonging and their ability to ask hard questions, voice concerns, and participate fully in their care.

The words we post on hospital walls must communicate more than rules. They must communicate medicine’s values.

Flavored nicotine tablets, the tobacco industry’s latest product, worry public health advocates

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This article was first reported by The Examination, a nonprofit newsroom that investigates global health threats.

The latest product from the tobacco industry is a flavorful nicotine tablet that melts in your mouth, allowing users to get a buzz as discreetly as popping a mint — and amping up concerns that products to replace cigarettes are too appealing to kids and teens.

In the United Kingdom and Switzerland, Philip Morris International recently introduced Zyn Melts, mint-flavored tablets that provide a nicotine hit as quickly as smoking a cigarette. Japan Tobacco International launched a similar product called Perlz in the U.K. in May.

And in the U.S., Nic Nac Naturals, a nascent nicotine company based in Spokane, Wash., has been selling flavored lozenges called Nic Nac since 2022; they’re now available in about 3,000 stores nationwide.

Users tuck these tablets next to their gums, similar to how they use nicotine pouches. Like pouches, the new products come in colorful packaging and flavors such as mint and berry. But with tablets, there’s no wet sachet to discard.

That makes them more appealing than pouches, said Erwin Henriquez, a global analyst for the marketing research firm Euromonitor. “It’s much more friendly to chew gum or a dissolving tablet than to put something in your gums,” he said. “You have vitamins and supplements that consumers are already used to.”

Public health advocates and researchers are concerned about nicotine tablets for a similar reason. Their resemblance to mints or lozenges “may make them appear harmless and more appealing to children and adolescents,” said Magnus Lundbäck, a cardiologist, nicotine researcher and professor at the Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm.

Tobacco companies have introduced e-cigarettes, heated tobacco, and nicotine pouches as less harmful alternatives to cigarettes. Tobacco-related diseases are the leading preventable cause of death worldwide, responsible for 7 million deaths a year.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration, which influences tobacco policy worldwide, has authorized these products because they’re less harmful than cigarettes and could help smokers quit.

But nicotine is tied to health problems even when it’s not inhaled through smoke, and the long-term health risks of these new products aren’t clear. Public health advocates say there’s little evidence that smokers are switching to pouches and heated tobacco entirely. More often, they say, people use those products in addition to cigarettes.

“The tobacco and nicotine industry is continuously developing new products that may attract new generations of users, rather than reducing nicotine dependence,” Lundbäck said. “The ultimate public health goal must be fewer people addicted to nicotine, not simply more ways to consume it.”

Henriquez, the Euromonitor analyst, said dissolvable tablets are a “natural evolution” of nicotine pouches and serve consumers’ growing appetite for stimulants as they “feel stress all the time.”

Nicotine pouch sales reached $9.2 billion globally in 2025, according to Euromonitor. The volume of pouches sold has grown 50% annually for the past five years and is expected to continue at 20.5% a year, according to a July report by Henriquez.

On its U.K. site, Philip Morris International tells consumers that Zyn Melts “offer a completely new way for adult users to enjoy tobacco-free and smoke-free nicotine moments on the go.” It compares tablets to pouches, saying tablets are for a “fast nicotine experience” while pouches have a “longer lasting nicotine and flavor experience.”

Japan Tobacco sells Perlz through Flavour Warehouse, a U.K. vaping company it controls. Its website states, “First came vapes. Then pouches. Now there’s Perlz — the ingenious new nicotine experience for existing adult nicotine users.”

Tablets contain less nicotine than most pouches, with Zyn Melts and Perlz available in 1 and 2 milligram doses.

Nic Nac lozenges are available in 3 and 6 mg doses. Nic Nac Naturals co-founder and Chief Executive Officer Nicco Magnotto said the lozenges are different from Zyn Melts and Perlz because they release nicotine over about 30 minutes, more like a pouch.

The most popular nicotine pouches, in comparison, range from 3 to 17 mg. Philip Morris International recently introduced a 1.5 mg pouch for users “new to nicotine pouches.”

Public health officials have been engaged in a yearslong balancing act with new nicotine products, weighing the potential benefits to adults looking for less harmful alternatives to cigarettes against the potential for youth to abuse or accidentally ingest nicotine.

Vapes exploded in popularity in 2018, causing the FDA to declare teen use an epidemic. Then nicotine pouches caught on, with surveys showing increased use among teens over the past few years. The industry, however, says the percentage of youth using pouches remains low.

Meanwhile, poison control centers in the U.S. reported that calls involving nicotine poisoning, many involving pouches, nearly doubled from 2024 to 2025. Three-quarters of cases last year involved children under the age of 6.

Nicotine tablets represent a new area of concern.

The industry says it’s not looking to sell to kids. In an email, a Japan Tobacco spokesperson said the company does not “design, market or promote Perlz to appeal” to minors and that the containers are child-resistant, requiring three buttons to be pressed simultaneously to be opened.

“We take steps to ensure that our advertising is directed towards adult tobacco and nicotine users only and does not have particular appeal to minors,” the spokesperson said.

Magnotto said his company does not advertise or market Nic Nac to youth and that the company’s research indicates they don’t find lozenges appealing. “It comes across as medicinal,” he said. “Pouches are the rage. Kids aren’t even aware of us.”

Asked about the potential for abuse by kids, Magnotto said his company’s container has a scientifically tested, child-resistent seal and will soon have a more secure mechanism.

Phillip Morris International did not respond to a request for comment. The company says on its U.K. website that Melts are meant for adults.

The U.K. doesn’t require regulatory approval for nicotine products, making it an ideal test market. The tablets were introduced shortly before regulations imposed by the Tobacco and Vapes Act 2026 became effective in October. That law imposes stricter rules on tobacco and nicotine products, including prohibitions on giveaways and big discounts.

Philip Morris International is looking beyond the U.K. and Switzerland. It has filed for trademark protections for Zyn Melts in the U.S. and Europe, both as a consumer nicotine product and a pharmaceutical aid to stop smoking.

The trademarks are among many the company has registered this year for Zyn, including “House of Zyn” and “Zyn Club” — signs that the company is positioning Zyn as a lifestyle brand.

Nicotine tablets have been available in the U.S. for years as smoking cessation aids, which requires a special FDA approval as a pharmaceutical product. The agency would need to do a separate review in order to authorize the sale of tablets to consumers.

Nic Nac Naturals submitted an application in 2023 to sell its nine mint and fruit-flavored tablets to the general public in the U.S.

Mitch Zeller, former director of the FDA’s Center for Tobacco Products, said nicotine tablets “could possibly be helpful” to smokers looking for alternatives in a “properly regulated market.”

First, he said, the product needs to undergo a complete scientific review, with studies showing how different users respond to it. He noted that a new FDA rule allows nicotine products to be sold while their application is pending, which means Zyn Melts and Perlz could be on shelves without any research on their appeal to adolescents.

That’s not how it should work, he said: “All of the intended and unintended consequences of something like this need to be sorted out on a pre-market basis.”
