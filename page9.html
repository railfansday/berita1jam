Nat Geo Explorer Bertie Gregory Deeply Cares About the Wolves He Has Followed for Seven Years

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Nat Geo Explorer Bertie Gregory Deeply Cares About the Wolves He Has Followed for Seven Years

In National Geographic’s newest television program, Wolves Up Close With Bertie Gregory, the eponymous British wildlife filmmaker, acclaimed photographer, and ardent environmentalist heads to Hudson Bay, Canada, to film wolves in their harsh Arctic habitat. Gregory chatted with PetaPixel about filming the new documentary.

While the focus of the new documentary is clearly on wolves, a specific wolf pack in particular that Gregory has followed for seven years, Wolves Up Close is a smorgasbord of Arctic wildlife in general. There are polar bears, red foxes, and moose, each beautifully filmed.

“One of the best parts about filming nature, and also the most annoying part, is that it doesn’t read the script,” Gregory laughs. “I always joke with the production team that we often go out with this make-believe script that we’ve written and then nature on day one is like, ‘Nope.’ And that’s the frustrating part, but on the positive part of it, it means that you never know what’s gonna happen.”

In one sequence, a pair of polar bears gets awfully close to Gregory and the rest of his small crew, which is admittedly a bit nerve-wracking for viewers. Gregory brushes off the dangers of wildlife filmmaking, thanks in large part to the local guides he worked with, including Mike Reimer and Albert “Butch” Saunders.

Gregory says he never felt nervous at all about the wolves, which likewise would saunter up to the crew to check things out, but what about the polar bears and moose?

“They’re potentially dangerous animals,” Gregory admits. “But humans are really dangerous animals. You know, statistically much more dangerous than a polar bear or a moose. Just like with humans, you’ve got to read body language to stay safe. But I work with great people, like Butch and Mike, our local guides, and they were really key to keeping everyone safe.”

Besides, Gregory might have been too busy feeling cold to feel fear.

“Any time during the episode, I was cold. I was feeling cold.”

The veteran filmmaker didn’t just experience physical closeness to some of his subjects — again, he’s a trained professional — but also emotional closeness, especially to the wolves. Bertie has followed this same pack of wolves for seven years, and over that time, he argues it’s impossible not to feel a bond.

In contrast to old-school wildlife documentary practices, which effectively demand that filmmakers remain entirely detached from the nature they experience and shoot, Gregory doesn’t fight the natural urge to feel something about his subjects.

“I mean, we’re trained not to,” he admits. “We’re told not to get attached because you can’t get involved, but I think that’s BS. I think there’s no way you can follow a pack of animals, an animal family, for seven years and not care about them.

I think if you don’t care about them, you’re pretty weird.

“I think if you don’t care about them, you’re pretty weird.”

Gregory says he isn’t actively trying to forge any bond, something he is fully aware would be a one-way street since the wolves “don’t care about us,” but he says, “I care about them.”

“There are two strategies when filming animals. You can either hide from the animals and they don’t know you’re there, or in the case of these wolves, you can have them know you’re there and be accepting of your presence. That’s an amazing relationship to have. They see us as kind of a part of the landscape.”

Viewers can truly feel through the screen how much Gregory cares about and loves these wolves. It shapes the way he films them, which I think is undeniably a good thing.

“You know when someone makes you like a bowl or a pot, or when you’ve got kids, they draw you a picture? There’s something intangible about the quality of the work and how much you like it when you know it’s been made with love,” Gregory explains.

There’s something intangible about the quality of the work and how much you like it when you know it’s been made with love.

He believes, rightly, that there’s something special too about wildlife documentaries that have been crafted with “real immense care and admiration for the animal.”

“I don’t think you need to try too hard to illustrate that. It just comes through naturally.”

For Gregory, wolves are easy to care about. He can’t help but wonder why such beautiful, intelligent creatures that are so much like the pet dogs many people love like they’re members of the family are treated so poorly and feared excessively.

As an example, the federal government is weighing a move right now to delist gray wolves and Mexican wolves from the Endangered Species Act, potentially removing the very protections that helped wolves rebound in the United States.

“That’s just so dumb on so many levels,” Gregory says. “So if you care about wolves, make your voice heard. Tell politicians, tell people with influence, tell them to shout about this stuff because it’s important. Not only are wolves important for wild places, but they’re representative of who we are as a species.

So if you care about wolves, make your voice heard.

“It’s so weird that we value their closest living relative, the domestic dog, in such high regard that we let them come into our houses and sleep in our beds, but yet we’re so horrible to the wild version of them,” Gregory says somberly.

On a much more positive note, Gregory and his crew got to experience something extremely special out in the field, filming a pack of wolves chasing down a moose from their drone. It’s moments like these that inspire wildlife filmmakers like Bertie Gregory.

National Geographic graciously shared a clip from the incredible sequence with PetaPixel.

“I realized I was seeing something very special and something that no human in that area had ever seen before,” Gregory says. “Mike says on camera, he’s been watching wolves for 30 years and never seen that.

“We combined amazing traditional knowledge with new technology. That’s the dream combo. Old school with new school, working together.

“I knew we were seeing something special.”

To make it even more special, it was a wolf Gregory has watched grow into a leader over the years, named Kona by “Butch” Saunders, that tried to take down the moose, latching onto one of its rear legs.

“I was like, ‘No one’s ever gonna believe this.’ It’s too good of a story,” Gregory says. “Then it dawned on me that I didn’t think Kona was going to survive it. The toll on his body. It just goes to show how tough these animals are.”

Kona survived the encounter, but there’s a poignant moment during the show when Gregory looks at the aging wolf and reflects on his age. Life in the Arctic is hard, and before you know it, the long, challenging winters have flown by.

Kona may not have many winters left, certainly not as the leader of the pack. But for a beautiful moment, Kona looked every bit the apex predator.

Wolves Up Close With Bertie Gregory premieres tomorrow, November 6, on National Geographic and will stream the following day on Hulu and Disney+.

CNBC's experts on whether the AI trade is ending and what investors should do next

📅 2026-11-05  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Amid a powerful rout in tech stocks, investors told CNBC on Thursday that something may be fundamentally breaking in the AI trade following the big success of Democrats in Tuesday's midterm elections and the threat they try to slow the data center buildout.

Democrats are more aggressively anti-data center than Republicans, and Wall Street is quickly redoing its math what any restrictions they implement could mean for the related companies' future growth.

"Right now, investors are questioning that $1.2 trillion of capex that we forecast for next year," Peter Boockvar, chief investment officer at One Point BFG Wealth Partners, told CNBC. "Well, maybe that's not going to happen."

Boockvar believes this could be the start of the end of the AI trade as we know it.

Whether it's completely over or not, investors need to be reassessing how they're planning to play the AI trade going forward, the analysts said.

The Nasdaq Composite dropped 5% on Thursday and fell into correction territory, off by more than 11% from its record high set last month. The iShares Semiconductor ETF dropped 11% on Thursday, its biggest decline since during the Covid breakout in March 2020. The Global X Artificial Intelligence & Technology ETF (AIQ) is down 6% on the day and down 15% over the past month.

"There has been a huge change in the outlook for AI infrastructure buildout," Eric Johnston, head of equities at Cantor Fitzgerald, told CNBC. "The entire economy and the consumer and earnings growth are all levered to that buildout."

The selling Thursday spilled over to non-AI stocks with the Dow Jones Industrial average down more than 1,600 points.

Josh Brown pointed to the 4.8% yield for the 10-year Treasury yield, saying a compelling risk-free rate is causing investors to reconsider taking the risk in these volatile AI stocks.

"You don't need to be at 80% U.S. stocks and have 60% of that exposure be tech," Brown said.

Many investors believe the AI trade isn't over completely, investors just need to get more selective.

"The AI buildout just got harder," said Rob Sechan, Newedge Capital Group Founder & Managing Partner:

"Nobody voted to make compute less scarce," Sechan said, citing Microsoft's Azure cloud business growing 48% year over year in its most recent third quarter. That level of growth suggests its capacity constrained, not demand constrained, he said.

"When new capacity gets harder to build, existing capacity becomes more valuable," Sechan said.

Richard Clode, portfolio manager at Janus Henderson, noted a few bright spots in tech on Thursday despite the highly correlated downward movements in the big tech indices.

"Motorola Solutions had some good results," he said. "We're seeing a little bit of rotation into some of the … internet names...Sony reported some good results, and again they were up strongly overnight."

"So there are some brief pockets of respite from this, but obviously pretty painful across the board today," he said.

Some experts believe the selling was overdone and investors could start nibbling.

This "indiscriminate selling" is a "buying opportunity," said Larry Adam, Raymond James chief investment officer. If investors "take a longer term perspective, which is what we do, I think the valuations at today's levels will tend to prove to be a good buying opportunity," he said, urging investors to look at the fundamentals.

Victoria Greene, chief investment officer of G Squared Private Wealth, said she saw some strong supports around the S&P 500's 200-day moving average and was on the lookout for a market bounce following Thursday's rout.

"Typically, the average return is plus about 180 basis points the next day," she said. "The question we have to ask ourselves: Is this a pullback in an uptrend, which is what I believe? Or is this the end-all, be-all [and] the wheels are coming off and everybody just needs to buy T-bills? I don't think we're there yet."

Google, Meta and TikTok are all suing California to block social media law intended to help kids

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Assembly Bill 1709 demands that platforms remove "psychologically exploitative features."

Google, Meta and TikTok are suing to block a recent California law that's intended to protect kids from some of the more harmful aspects of social media, according to Reuters. Assembly Bill 1709 is part of a larger package that Governor Gavin Newsom introduced earlier this year and it prohibits social media platforms from offering underage kids "psychologically exploitative features" that could lead to compulsive use.

This includes stuff like sending videos through autoplay and giving children under 16 personalized recommendations. The three social media giants say this is in violation of the First Amendment and Section 230 of the federal Communications Decency Act, which has often been used to shield tech companies from liability.

The three companies filed separate suits at various courts throughout California. "The state cannot, consistent with the First Amendment, override Meta's editorial judgments," Meta wrote in its complaint. Google and TikTok made similar arguments.

Newsom said when he signed the law that children's safety should always come first when considering social media platforms in California. The state "will not stand by while unregulated technology puts our children at risk," he noted.

Some organizations agree with the social media companies here. The nonprofit Electronic Frontier Foundation has called the law "a massive privacy and free speech nightmare" and unsuccessfully urged Newsom to veto the policy.

While many disagree on the potential solution, there's not much of an argument to be made regarding the severity of the problem. TikTok, Meta and Google are currently fighting thousands of lawsuits by individuals, school districts, cities and government entities that all claim that social media platforms addict children and have caused a mental health crisis. To that end, many states regulate children's access to social media.

Also, Meta recently agreed to pay up to $18 billion to settle a high-profile case over children's privacy and social media addiction. As part of this agreement, the company says it will make changes to its platforms to make them less addictive to young users. It still denies any wrongdoing.

Today's lawsuits are still in the early stages. We'll update you as they make their way through the California court system.

Iowa elected a Democrat whod dropped out of the U.S. midterms, and the internet has jokes

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The 2026 U.S. midterm elections delivered a decisive Democrat victory this week, with a blue wave taking control of both the Senate and the House of Representatives. It also unexpectedly swept up a Democratic candidate from Iowa who'd dropped out of the race, putting him in office regardless — and prompting numerous jokes online.

In September, Democratic nominee Eric Armstrong suspended his campaign for Iowa House District 20 due to health issues.

"Our District deserves a representative who can give 100 percent of their energy to this race," Armstrong wrote in a Sept. 7 Facebook post announcing his decision. "Right now, I must focus on healing so that I can continue to serve our community in other ways in the future."

With Armstrong no longer campaigning, Republican Keith Jones appeared to have a much clearer path to victory. But Armstrong's announcement came after Iowa's deadline for nominees to withdraw, meaning his name remained on the ballot when voters headed to the polls on Nov. 3.

As such, Iowans who took to the polls apparently looked at his announcement, looked at the ballot, looked back at Armstrong's withdrawal, and basically said, "Nah, we're not accepting that."

Despite having stopped campaigning in September, Armstrong defeated Jones with 55.2 percent of the vote, keeping the seat in Democratic hands. He will succeed fellow Democrat Josh Turek, who flipped a Republican-held U.S. Senate seat in Iowa.

The unexpected result has inspired jokes online imagining Iowa voters as particularly demanding bosses, unwilling to approve Armstrong's request for time off.

"Iowa said 'you are still going to work,'" wrote TikTok user @jalasherese. "We don’t care if we have to wheel you in there."

"Drink some ginger ale and we'll see you in January Eric," @mona_licia83 commented on TikTok.

For his part, Armstrong has indicated that he intends to serve, although his health remains a concern. In an interview with the New York Times, Armstrong said that he was still dealing with health issues but added, "I'm going to do my best to represent [the district]."

According to his campaign website, his priorities include expanding access to pre-K childcare, closing the minimum wage gap between Iowa and Nebraska, and improving benefits for Iowa educators to help schools recruit and retain staff.

Court leans toward narrow ruling on inmate’s use of habeas to challenge accelerated transfer to a halfway house or home confinement

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

The Supreme Court heard oral argument on Nov. 2 in Maxwell v. Dinis about whether a federal inmate may use a habeas statute, 28 U.S.C. § 2241, to contest the Federal Bureau of Prisons’ refusal to accelerate his transfer to a halfway house or home confinement.

Maxwell, a former attorney, was convicted at trial for his role in conspiring with a mob-related organization, La Costa Nostra, to take over a Texas-based mortgage-loan company, FirstPlus Financial Group, Inc., and drain it of its assets. As Maxwell neared the end of his sentence, he sought to contest the BOP’s calculation of good-time credits that would accelerate his transfer from prison to a halfway house or home confinement. The question for the court was whether Section 2241, which does not clearly spell out what claims may be brought but whose scope has largely been shaped by precedent, allows him to do so.

Maxwell’s attorney, Masha Hansford, pressed for a broad rule that habeas is available to an inmate like Maxwell who is asserting a right to a “distinct and lower level of custody.” But that position almost immediately encountered some pushback. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson questioned “whether we really need to use this case to define every circumstance in which habeas is appropriate.” Hansford agreed that the court need not adopt a test that allows inmates to challenge all changes to the level of custody and was content to have the court resolve Maxwell’s case by applying a narrower test endorsed by the government that would permit an inmate to use habeas to challenge the denial of an accelerated transfer to parole or, as here, a halfway house or home confinement.

Justice Samuel Alito pressed Hansford on whether an inmate may use Section 2241 simply to dispute how BOP calculated good-time credits or whether the inmate must also show that any error in calculating would also entitle the inmate to accelerated transfer to a halfway house or home confinement. After Hansford replied that Maxwell met all the requirements for the transfer and only the calculation of good-time credits was at issue, Justice Neal Gorsuch followed up by pressing whether the court needs to say that an inmate could bring a habeas challenge about the calculation of good-time credits alone. Hansford replied that the court did not need to say a dispute over good-time credits alone would suffice for a federal inmate to bring a claim in Section 2241.

Turning to Hansford’s level-of-custody test, Justice Sonia Sotomayor noted that, in a prior case, Justice Antonin Scalia had referred to habeas as covering a “‘quantum change’ in the level of custody,” but Hansford was asking the court “to create new language, ‘materially less restrictive custody.’ Why don’t we stick to what we have?” Hansford agreed that the court need not broaden its test and that Maxwell’s requested transfer to a halfway house or home confinement amounted to such a quantum change.

Assistant to the Solicitor General Kevin J. Barber, arguing for the government, contended that “there’s no need to adopt” Maxwell’s “amorphous level-of-custody rule.” He explained that the court “has always made clear that habeas is available when your claim is seeking discharge from actual physical confinement in prison to a different kind of custody,” such as a halfway house or home confinement. Here, the government relied heavily on Preiser v. Rodriguez, which held that habeas supplies the sole avenue for a state inmate to challenge the denial of credit toward release on parole.

When Gorsuch pressed Barber on the source of the government’s rule and why an inmate’s transfer to solitary confinement or maximum security would not be covered, Barber pointed principally to history and tradition in the court’s habeas cases. Barber explained, “we don’t have any tradition of claims being recognized as habeas claims challenging just transfers within prison systems.”

Despite agreeing that Maxwell could bring his claim in Section 2241, the government argued that no court could actually grant him relief. Specifically, the government pointed to 18 U.S.C. § 3621(b) and its terms barring courts from reviewing “a designation of a place of imprisonment under this subsection.” According to the government, service of a sentence in a halfway house or home confinement is “a designation of a place of imprisonment” governed by that statute.

But the government did not appear to have the votes to prevail on this argument, at least at the Supreme Court. Justice Brett Kavanaugh pressed Barber on whether that issue could be left for the lower court to decide on remand. Barber conceded that the court “absolutely could.” Sotomayor questioned Barber about whether the government is right that Section 3621(b) applies to Maxwell’s claim. But after Barber began responding, Justice Amy Coney Barrett reiterated Kavanaugh’s point that “we don’t have to get into any of this, right?” Barber again agreed.

Jackson pressed Barber on a separate point, that the BOP is expected to transfer Maxwell to a halfway house or home confinement in January 2027, which would soon make his claim moot. Jackson disputed why the court would revisit its decision to hear the case. Barber apologized for not pressing the Section 3621(b) issue or mootness in opposing certiorari, but said these factors spoke in favor of the court dismissing the case as improvidently granted.

The amicus, Brian W. Barnes, was appointed to argue in favor of the ruling by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit that habeas is unavailable for Maxwell’s claim. When pressed by Justice Elena Kagan, Barnes agreed that habeas is available to “encompass a move from incarceration to parole,” as Preiser held. Kagan pointed out that “parole looks an awful lot like what’s going on here.”

The justices’ questions suggest that they are unlikely to limit Preiser and reject an analogy between Maxwell’s requested relief and the parole in that case. The justices also showed little appetite for reaching the government’s Section 3621(b) argument. How they would handle Maxwell’s expected release to a halfway house in January 2027 was less clear, but that circumstance could result in a speedier ruling.

Richard D. Cooke worked for the U.S. Department of Justice for 23 years, first at the Antitrust Division for 5 years and then as an Assistant United States Attorney in the Eastern District of Virginia for 18 years.

Does the Oleshky ceasefire signal progress to come in Ukraine?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Hundreds of civilians were evacuated from the city of Oleshky after Russia and Ukraine agreed to a US-brokered ceasefire. Some observers have expressed optimism that more such humanitarian missions might follow.

Nearly 800 residents have been evacuated from the Russia-occupied city of Oleshky in southern Ukraine. This was announced by US first lady Melania Trump, who was credited with helping to mediate the ceasefire. The mission was coordinated by Ukrainian Human Rights Commissioner Dmytro Lubinets and his Russian counterpart, Yana Lantratova.

Most evacuees chose to be sent to Ukrainian-controlled territory, although some opted for transfer to Russia. Currently, more than 1,000 people remain in Oleshky, according to Kherson's regional administration. They were supplied with food, water and medicine.

Melania Trump hailed the mission as a success, taking to social media to thank Kyiv and Moscow for their "leadership and cooperation." She said the Oleshky ceasefire could go on to inspire a "lasting peace between the two peoples."

Dmytro Lubinez similarly wrote on the Telegram messaging platform that it is "important to maintain this cooperation mechanism," adding that "we are currently exploring options with Yana Lantratova to continue similar humanitarian efforts."

The Kremlin, however, was quick to dampen any such expectations, making clear that no further plans for humanitarian missions exist at this stage.

One day after the ceasefire ended, Ukraine's general staff stated that Russia had "used the humanitarian pause to fortify its positions." It also said Russia had carried out troop rotations, expanded its anti-drone defenses, and brought in ammunition.

British broadcaster Sky News aired drone footage, produced with the support of the Ukrainian army, showing the Russian army transporting heavy equipment, weapons, personnel and food to Oleshky and neighboring villages.

"It was clear to the Ukrainian government and international mediators that Russia could exploit the pause in this way. But it was the right thing to do to allow people to leave the country," Volodymyr Vyatrovych of the Ukrainian European Solidarity opposition party wrote on Facebook.

He believes Russia would do the exact same thing if Kyiv were to withdraw its troops from the Donetsk region, as Russian President Vladimir Putin is demanding.

"That moment hasn't yet come though," Greene told DW, adding that Russia was still trying to buy time by feigning a willingness to engage in dialogue with the US.

Kristen Bell Gives Sneak Peek at What's Thawing for Frozen 3

Kristen Bell is sharing a glimpse into the unknown.

1. An adaptation of Hans Christian Andersen's 1845 fairy tale The Snow Queen, Frozen was actually in development at Disney for almost a decade (and was briefly considered for a hand-drawn animated feature back in 1937).

The first modern attempt happened in 2002, and when 2003 also proved unsuccessful, more stalled attempts followed over the years. Frozen really didn't start coming together until Christopher Buck, who directed Tarzan, came aboard in 2008, but the film wasn't commissioned until 2011 following Tangled's box office success.

And in 2012, Jennifer Lee, who initially came on as a screenwriter, was named co-director, making her the first woman to direct an animated film by Walt Disney Animation Studios.

2. The movie was going to be titled The Snow Queen after the original fairy tale, but the filmmakers decided to change it to Frozen "because, to us, it represents the movie. Frozen plays on the level of ice and snow but also the frozen relationship, the frozen heart that has to be thawed," producer Peter Del Vecho explained to Bleeding Cool, denying it was inspired by Tangled. (The Snow Queen title was still used overseas.)

3. Idina Menzel and Kristen Bell auditioned and met with the producers of Tangled for the role of Rapunzel, which ultimately went to Mandy Moore. (Santino Fontana, who gave life to Anna's temporary fiancé Hans, originally auditioned for the role of Flynn in the 2010 film as well).

Bell, whose dream since she was a little girl was to voice a Disney character, was the "very first actress" to audition for Anna, with Lee saying, "We did audition a lot of people for that but she was the first and the best. We just fell in love with her."

4. Menzel's audition for Elsa, meanwhile, was actually a table-read with Bell before both had been officially cast, according to Lee.

"They read the whole script and at the end—we didn't have any songs written yet—and what they did was sing a duet and they sung 'Wind Beneath My Wings,' but they sung it like sisters and what you mean to me," she explained. "And there wasn't a dry eye in the house after they sang...that is when they knew, that was the whole potential and understanding the power of music and of what this emotional story can be."

5. In the early versions of the project, Elsa was actually the villain of the story (with one of the early concept art designs being inspired by Bette Midler and her "showgirl stage presence"), according to former Disney animation designer Claire Keane, who released the image on her web site.

"Originally, she was much more villainous," Buck allowed to Nerdist. "It's very easy to go in that direction for that type of character, but as the time went and we evolved the story, she became much more three-dimensional. Before, I thought she was one-dimensional but a very fully fleshed out character."

The moment the character really changed, though, was after they watched earlier cuts and realized it wasn't exactly working: "Someone came up with the brilliant idea...'what if the protagonist, Anna, and Elsa were related?'" art director Michael Giaimo revealed. "Because they weren't always siblings, they weren't always related, and that was the beginning of finding these characters."

6. The hardest character for the Frozen team to nail down aside from Elsa was actually Olaf, who was originally then-villainous Elsa's first guard.

"She created these snowman guards and Marshmallow was kind of a holdover from that because he was this scary snowman guard. Olaf was the very first guard she created," Buck explained, adding he was still funny at that time.

After changing the character of Elsa completely, it wasn't until the directors first sat down with Josh Gad that they found the character. "Josh adlibbed the line 'it's like a baby unicorn' and there was something about that innocence," Del Vecho told Kidz World.

7. One main character who wasn't originally in the movie was Hans, Anna's suitor-turned-villain, with Giaimo explaining, "Hans was never originally in the story in older versions...Kristoff always was in the story or a version of the Kristoff character and Anna. And then Hans was brought in at one point but he wasn't always a villain who turned initially, and then someone came up with the idea of him turning. He was always potentially a villain, but we gave it away much sooner."

8. Anna's oft-repeated "Wait what?" catchphrase? An ad-lib by Bell. She also slipped in one slightly off-color joke. "We slid it under the radar," she detailed to Vanity Fair of Anna's singing, "For years I've roamed these empty halls / Why have a ballroom with no balls?" The NSFW lyric almost got snipped, she admitted, "But then we were like, 'What are you talking about? That’s not what it means. Don’t be a perv.'"

11. After having a few of the first songs they originally composed for the film not make it in, songwriting couple Robert Lopez and Kristen Anderson-Lopez were feeling a little low, so they went for walk in Brooklyn's Prospect Park.

Listening to songs by Adele and Avril Lavigne and being surrounded by nature, Anderson-Lopez told Playbill, "We were in this kind of emo place of imagining what it would be like to be this perfect person who messed up once, and was chased away from everything she has known and all the people she has been sacrificing for."

Within a day, they would write the film's most iconic song that would go on to sell over 10 million copies in 2014 and win the Oscar for Best Original Song. All together now, "Let it go, let it goooooooo."

12. And after hearing "Let It Go" for the first time, Lee said she knew they had to rework the character of Elsa and her motivations completely.

"Half of us were crying. And then I just went, 'I have to rewrite the whole movie,'" she said on the Scriptnotes podcast. "I really, it was—I was just like I'm going to go lie down for a couple minutes. But it was the best thing. We knew we had the movie."

13. On the same podcast, Lee admitted there was "a lot of push and pull" when it came to Elsa's look after her transformation via "Let It Go" and how sexy to make her after this awakening.

"Everyone was seduced by her. And so there was this tug of war I think, a bit, of letting people have a little—people who wanted to have that a little and not be afraid of it, but not make it a sexual statement," the co-director explained. "It's more a moment of, for me, it was like you strut and you say nobody is looking, this is what I'm going to—I'm not going to be afraid of my sexuality. I'm not going to be afraid of who I am. 14. After eagle-eyed fans spotted one of many easter eggs in the film—Tangled's Rapunzel (post haircut) and Eugene briefly appearing in a scene during the morning of Elsa's coronation—a compelling fan theory that tied together Frozen, Tangled and Little Mermaid surfaced: Anna and Elsa's parents died when their boat sank...what if it was when they were on their way to the Tangled pair's wedding? Real Madrid's Nico Paz decision in focus after Como record

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Naim Beneddra | 05 Nov 2026 18:54

Real Madrid's decision to allow Nico Paz to remain at Como for another season has come under renewed scrutiny after the Argentine midfielder inspired another remarkable Champions League performance.

The 22-year-old scored once and provided two assists as Cesc Fabregas's side demolished Lens 9-1 on Wednesday evening, equalling the biggest winning margin in Champions League history and setting a new record for the competition's largest away victory.

Paz has now registered three goals and five assists in just four Champions League appearances this season, making him the competition's most productive player in terms of direct goal involvements.

His extraordinary form is particularly significant for Real Madrid, who agreed to let the midfielder continue his development in Italy during the summer but retained the right to bring him back in 2027.

With Como emerging as one of Europe's most exciting attacking teams, the decision to leave Paz under Fabregas's guidance is attracting fresh attention.

Como's astonishing victory in France featured a hat-trick from Anastasios Douvikas, but Paz once again demonstrated why he has become the creative heartbeat of Fabregas's team.

The Argentina international scored Como's third goal in the 40th minute, arriving in the penalty area to finish emphatically after being set up by Douvikas.

He then turned provider shortly after half time, releasing Lucas Da Cunha with a perfectly weighted pass for the visitors' fourth goal.

Ten minutes later, Paz supplied another assist for Douvikas, who completed his hat-trick as Lens's defence continued to unravel.

By the time the Argentine was withdrawn in the 58th minute, Como had scored six times, with their number 10 directly responsible for three of those goals.

His contribution followed an equally impressive performance against Manchester United on Matchday 3, when he scored twice and created another goal in a commanding 4-1 victory over Michael Carrick's side.

Those displays have propelled Paz to the top of the Champions League goal-involvement standings.

According to Opta, his eight direct contributions place him ahead of every other player in this season's competition, including Bayern Munich striker Harry Kane and Barcelona sensation Lamine Yamal.

Paz also leads the tournament's assist chart with five, having supplied two against Manchester United and another two against Lens.

Only two players have registered more assists in their first four appearances of a single Champions League campaign: Real Madrid legend Raul in 2002-03 and former Barcelona forward Neymar in 2016-17, who both recorded six.

The figures illustrate how quickly Paz has established himself among Europe's most influential attacking midfielders.

His outstanding performances have not been limited to continental competition, either.

Across Serie A and the Champions League, the Argentine has contributed six goals and eight assists in 13 appearances this season, underlining his importance to Como's increasingly ambitious project.

Fabregas's team currently sit fourth in the Italian top flight and occupy the same position in the Champions League standings, having recovered emphatically from their defeat to Feyenoord.

For a club competing in Europe's elite competition for the first time, that progress has been exceptional.

However, Paz's individual development also raises an intriguing question about the plans of the European champions who originally brought him through their academy.

Real Madrid faced an important decision over Paz's future during the summer transfer window.

Having sold the midfielder to Como in 2024, Los Blancos retained contractual mechanisms allowing them to regain control of his future.

Reports initially suggested that the Spanish giants were preparing to activate an existing buy-back clause, potentially paving the way for a return to the Santiago Bernabeu.

However, the two clubs eventually reached a different agreement.

On June 29, Real Madrid officially announced that Paz would remain at Como for the 2026-27 season, a decision made at the player's own request.

Crucially, the agreement also confirmed that Madrid would retain a unilateral option to buy back his registration rights for the 2027-28 campaign.

That arrangement allowed Paz to continue playing regularly under Fabregas while preserving a route back to the Spanish capital.

The financial details were not disclosed in Madrid's official announcement, although Spanish newspaper AS reported that the agreement involved a €60m (£52m) transaction and an €80m (£69m) buy-back option for summer 2027.

Madrid's decision was understandable given the competition for attacking midfield positions in Jose Mourinho's squad.

Jude Bellingham, Arda Guler and Brahim Diaz are among the players capable of operating in similar areas, leaving no obvious guarantee that Paz would receive the minutes he currently enjoys at Como.

His Champions League performances nevertheless strengthen the argument that he is developing into a player capable of competing at the highest level.

Real Madrid have not announced any intention to bring him back ahead of schedule, and a recent report from AS indicated that the club would not make a decision on its buy-back options before next spring.

For now, Paz remains central to Fabregas's plans, with Como preparing to host AEK Athens in their next Champions League fixture on November 24.

Madrid's decision to allow him another season in Italy has already helped the Argentine reach new heights. His continued progress will only make the question of what happens in 2027 more compelling.
