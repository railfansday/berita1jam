In reversal, American Cancer Society urges prostate cancer tests for all men

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Men aged 50–69 and at average risk should get PSA screening every two years.

In a significant update to its guidelines for prostate cancer screening, the American Cancer Society is now recommending that all men get routinely screened for prostate cancer with a PSA test.

Men at average risk should get a PSA test every two years from age 50 to 69, according to the new guidelines released Thursday.

Men considered at high risk due to race and family history should start testing earlier, at 45, and most are recommended to get tested every year. Specifically, yearly testing starting at age 45 is recommended for all Black men, who have higher rates of prostate cancer. Non-Black men should also get yearly testing starting at 45 if a close relative was diagnosed with prostate cancer before age 65, or if more than one of their relatives has had prostate cancer. On the other hand, if only one close relative was diagnosed with prostate cancer and it was diagnosed at age 65 or older, non-Black men in this case should start PSA screening at 45, but are recommended to get testing only every two years.

Men ages 70 to 74 should consult their doctor on continuing screening, while those 75 and up are not recommended to get screened.

The new guidance departs from ACS’s previous iteration, published in 2010, which made no universal recommendations for routine screening. Men at age 50 were recommended to use shared-decision making with their doctors on whether or not to get tested. The recommendation was similar to that from the US Preventive Services Task Force, a federal panel that regularly evaluates evidence and makes recommendations on such preventive medical care. (The USPSTF is currently working on an update to its prostate cancer screening recommendation, though it should be noted that the panel has recently been overhauled by anti-vaccine Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and new members are reportedly rife with conflicts of interest.)

In announcing the guidelines today, the ACS says its updates are based on new evidence since 2010 that shows screening saves lives, and that advances in case management can avoid the concerning harms from overdiagnosis and overtreatment. For many men with prostate cancer, their cancer will never grow or cause harm in their lifetime, while treatment can run the risk of erectile dysfunction and urinary incontinence.

In the new guidance, the ACS adds guardrails, firstly advising all men to be informed about the risks and benefits. If a PSA test comes back high—defined as 3 ng/mL or higher for men under 70 and 6.5 ng/mL or higher for those 70 and up—the ACS recommends repeating the test before taking any next steps.

If repeat testing is still high, then men should get either a prostate MRI or a validated biomarker test to assess risk. Only if there is a high suspicion of cancer—such as having a very high PSA test result—should men jump to a prostate biopsy.

For men diagnosed with prostate cancer, active surveillance should be the initial management strategy for men whose cancer appears to be at low risk of growing or spreading.

“By catching aggressive cancers with a simple PSA blood test, you open the door to highly effective treatments that protect your future,” Dr. Shanthi Sivendran, interim chief patient officer for the ACS, said in a statement. “Meanwhile, lower-risk findings can be safely managed through careful, active surveillance.”

Prostate cancer is one of the most common cancers in men and the second leading cause of cancer death in US men, the ACS notes. Nearly 334,00 men are estimated to be diagnosed with prostate cancer in 2026, and it’s estimated that over 36,320 will die from the disease.

Agent skills capture repeatable engineering work in files teams can review and reuse, while tools, project context, and permissions remain specific to the host that runs them.

How many times this week did you re-explain the same task to an agent? How many times did you ask your colleague with deep institutional knowledge how to solve a specific problem? That context shouldn’t be lost in a chat or locked in one person’s head. Agent skills let you capture repeatable instructions so you and your agents can reuse them. The skill you write for yourself tonight can later become part of a team’s pipeline.

An agent skill is a versioned directory centered on a SKILL.md file that describes when and how to do a repeatable task. You can share those instructions across agents, but the environment still needs to provide the tools, credentials, and permissions to carry them out. A skill can describe how to query a database, for example, but it doesn’t grant access to that database.

I covered this topic live with Shishir Tewari, Senior Manager, Data Engineering at Procore, during GitHub Universe 2026. Our session was titled “Build once, run on any agent: a practical guide to agent skills”.

A skill works best when it has a clear job, input, and output. For example, let’s say you’re building an agent that checks AI-generated changes against the original request. If the same agent assesses its own work, its explanation might influence the review. With Proof Agent, we use a separate verifier to examine the changes and provide evidence for its findings. At the center is a simple rule: self-verification is not verification.

The When to Verify section in Proof Agent’s SKILL.md says when to invoke the skill. The remaining instructions explain what the verifier reviews and what evidence to include in its findings.

When the judgment that makes a data pipeline correct lives in four different people’s heads, those four people are the roadmap. Each change to the pipeline depends on their availability and knowledge of the data. Data Forge is an example of how to capture parts of that judgment in skills that work together.

In the support-desk pipeline example, we start with a request for metrics that count incidents created and resolved. Before building the pipeline, the forge-init skill discovers details such as source tables and naming conventions, then writes them to a resolved manifest. The other skills read that manifest for the project context they need.

The forge-builder skill then coordinates the work from the issue through to a pull request. It uses forge-dbx to query the data platform and forge-lint to apply the team’s standards as it designs and builds the pipeline. A person reviews the proposed decisions along the way, and the resulting pull request contains the pipeline code, schema changes, and tests.

Each skill has a defined responsibility, and they share project context instead of rediscovering it independently. When a step changes, you can change one skill and leave the rest alone.

Skills fit bounded, repeatable work that benefits from reusable judgment, such as a checklist, release, migration, or metric pipeline. If a workflow needs a stable interface, shared state, an audit trail, long-running control, or permissions enforced outside the model, an application is a better fit.

We don’t want to hardcode every project’s names, database, or conventions into the skills. In the Data Forge support-desk example, a manifest describes the project, metric store, and platform so the skills can use the right project details without baking them into their instructions:

With the discovered details in the resolved manifest, the other skills don’t have to make their own assumptions about the project.

Keep discovered project details separate from team standards. The forge-init initialization skill can inspect existing schemas and code patterns; the forge-lint skill supplies the rules to follow before code is generated. A pattern found in a repository isn’t automatically a rule to preserve, so make discovered details reviewable and let people confirm or change them where business judgment is needed. To check portability, ask: could another team use these skills with its own manifest without rewriting them?

The forge-dbx skill gives the other Data Forge skills one way to access the data platform. It reads the manifest and selects a Databricks or DuckDB adapter, so each skill doesn’t need its own connection and query logic.

The manifest can choose DuckDB for the local support-desk demo, but it can’t install the DuckDB CLI or grant database access. Those remain setup responsibilities for the environment and its user. The manifest picks the engine; the host provides the access.

The Data Forge support-desk workflow has seven phases, with a human checkpoint at each one. The workflow overview shows how the skills fit together. Before a phase proceeds, the workflow checks that the previous phase is complete; before schema changes run, it asks for approval.

The Proof Agent example applies the same principle to code review. A GitHub Actions workflow gives the verifier the original request and code changes, but not the coding agent’s own assessment. The verifier returns PASS, FAIL, or PARTIAL and must cite evidence in the changed files. The GitHub Action does a static review of the diff; it doesn’t execute code or run tests. In the demo workflow, we set block-on-fail: false so findings were advisory. By default, a FAIL fails the check, which blocks the merge if the check is required. The aim is to keep test results and merge requirements in your repository’s CI checks and rules.

Supporting multiple clients can mean keeping several copies of the same instructions in sync. In the Data Forge example, we keep skills/*/SKILL.md as the source and use a packaging script to generate client-specific files: .claude-plugin/plugin.json for Claude Code, .cursor-plugin/plugin.json for Cursor, and .github/agents/*.agent.md for GitHub Copilot. Start with the task you’re tired of explaining. Write down what it needs, where it stops, and what a reviewer sees. Then try it with another agent and improve it as you learn what works.

The global population of cloud-native developers has topped 21 million, but the headcount tells only part of the story. Artificial intelligence is changing what those developers build and how open-source projects absorb their work.

Fresh research is putting numbers on that shift. The latest State of Cloud Native Development report from the Cloud Native Computing Foundation and SlashData Ltd. counts more than 21 million practitioners, or 41% of developers worldwide. Yet that figure likely misses many people who rely on cloud-native infrastructure without knowing it, according to Bob Killen (pictured, right), director of technical program management at CNCF.

“The big thing is it actually might be undercounting a significant amount of them,” Killen said. “Because there’s a very large amount of people that are essentially consuming cloud-native applications without even realizing it.”

Killen and Liam Bollmann-Dodd (left), principal market research consultant at SlashData, spoke with theCUBE’s Rebecca Knight at KubeCon + CloudNativeCon NA, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed the latest State of Cloud Native Development findings, AI adoption, the strain on open-source maintainers and the shift toward cost discipline. (* Disclosure below.)

The report’s most striking finding concerns AI. About 75% of cloud-native developers use AI in some form, compared with only about 55% of other developers, Bollmann-Dodd noted. The gap mirrors a broader trend of agentic AI pushing Kubernetes into a second act.

“It’s a massive difference,” Bollmann-Dodd said. “So somehow being cloud native enables you and your organization and your personal stack to do AI in all its forms.”

The same AI wave is straining the people who keep the ecosystem running. Sustainability dominated the Maintainer Summit ahead of KubeCon, as maintainers face a flood of pull requests frequently generated by agents, Killen explained.

“How do we as maintainers keep up with the flooding of PRs and issues and things like that?” Killen said. “Especially when many of them are frankly just slop. And the real frustrating part is they’ll be just good enough.”

Cost discipline is the next shift. Just as financial operations practices followed rising cloud bills, cloud-native developers will soon manage model and token spending closely, according to Bollmann-Dodd.

“I think token maxing is dead and it’s going to stay dead forever,” Bollmann-Dodd said. “And I think it’s going to become much more focused on how you spend your money and how you spend your tokens.”
