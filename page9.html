The "red line" behind Vitalik's updated vision is that the community should stop being fixated on the idea of ETH as "fee-burninng token". According to Yakovenko, the blockchain network must generate real value for the end users, not control the price of getting into the block.

Vitalik is absolutely right that you can’t focus on fees as the product. That’s absolutely meaningless. You need to pick a product, the thing that brings value to people, and do that really well.

There is lots of technical stuff and philosophy around, but what the missed deadline on the testnet and Vitalik's manifesto reveal is that Ethereum opts for long-term, secure, and even a bit boring stability of the basic layer. Infra doesn't try to flirt with the market through deflationary records.

Why Nvidia Stock Looks Like a Bargain Right Now

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia's shares have stalled, while its business hasn't. Here's the case for buying the dip.

Nvidia (NVDA +2.77%) has made investors a tremendous amount of money over the past several years. But lately, the artificial intelligence chipmaker has been heading in the wrong direction.

Shares have been essentially flat so far in 2026 and have fallen about 22% from their all-time high. That's quite a reversal for a company whose revenue more than doubled in its most recent quarter.

And I think investors are getting an opportunity with Nvidia stock.

Nvidia reports its fiscal third-quarter earnings on Nov. 17, and I'm expecting another blowout performance. The company's financial results and the extraordinary demand for AI computing suggest its growth story has plenty of room to run.

In its fiscal second quarter, which ended July 26, Nvidia generated $96.2 billion in revenue, up 106% from a year earlier. Data center revenue reached $89 billion, an increase of 117%.

Those are remarkable growth rates for a company operating at Nvidia's scale.

Management expects third-quarter revenue of approximately $108 billion, representing another 12% sequential increase at the midpoint. Nvidia also forecasts a non-GAAP gross margin of 74%, suggesting it expects to maintain substantial pricing power despite its enormous sales volume.

And there are good reasons to believe demand remains strong. SK Hynix, a major supplier of high bandwidth memory used in AI accelerators, has indicated that its advanced memory capacity is effectively sold out for years. That matters, because Nvidia's GPUs need that memory to handle increasingly demanding AI workloads.

Meanwhile, Nvidia's Vera Rubin platform is already in full production, with systems being deployed by major cloud providers and AI infrastructure companies.

Customers aren't waiting for AI computing demand to disappear. They're spending billions to secure more capacity.

The latest weakness in Nvidia stock reflects concerns about the enormous cost of AI infrastructure and the possibility that political opposition could slow data center construction.

Those aren't imaginary risks. If companies struggle to obtain electricity or construction permits, some GPU deployments could be delayed.

But there's a difference between a delay in building data centers and a collapse in demand for AI computing. Nvidia has already demonstrated that it can sell enormous quantities of increasingly sophisticated chips while maintaining exceptional margins. Its next earnings report will provide another opportunity to see whether that demand is holding up.

I'm expecting the results to exceed management's already aggressive outlook.

A 24% decline doesn't automatically make any stock cheap, and Nvidia still faces risks from customer spending cuts, competing chips, and restrictions on AI infrastructure development. But with revenue doubling and a new generation of products entering production, I see a stronger business than what the stock's recent performance suggests.

No clear winner in New Zealand election, as leaders prepare for weeks of talks

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

New Zealand Prime Minister and National Party leader, Christopher Luxon addresses party faithful in Auckland, New Zealand, Saturday, Nov.7, 2026. Alex Robertson/AP hide caption

WELLINGTON, New Zealand — New Zealanders will wait for weeks to discover who will lead the country after Saturday's election failed to deliver a decisive win for the right or the left, leaving fraught negotiations for a wafer-thin governing majority the only apparent path to victory for either side.

The tightest vote in years delivered a stinging rebuke to the major parties which have long dominated New Zealand politics, Christopher Luxon's center-right National party and center-left Labour led by Chris Hipkins, as voters threw more support behind minor parties on the ideological flanks than before. On Saturday's numbers, minor parties would enter negotiations for a governing deal with leverage to extract deeper policy concessions from a weakened National or Labour.

The near-deadlock was partly produced because voters, apparently weary of New Zealand's economic woes and apathetic about both Luxon and Hipkins, chose to propel a new entrant group to Parliament for the first time in years. Opportunity, led by 38-year-old business consultant and political newcomer Qiulae Wong, is unaligned with either bloc and Wong has said she could work with either side to form a government.

Wong's pledges of evidence-based centrism and an end to partisan bickering had on Saturday's results secured seven seats for Opportunity — enough to frustrate both left and right in a simple path to power. It was a staggering ascension for a party that made three failed attempts at Parliament before Wong became leader less than a year ago.

Other winners were harder to identify and on Sunday, "who won the election 2026" was the top trending Google search in New Zealand. So unclear was the result that parties won't begin negotiations to form a government until final tallies are released on Nov. 27, confirming the exact number of seats they've won.

With 2.8 million ballots counted on election night, the conservative incumbent government led by Luxon's National party appeared close to the slimmest possible return to power — by just one seat. However, Luxon didn't claim victory, instead striking a note of cautious optimism that he could negotiate a second term in power.

Speaking to reporters Sunday, Luxon blamed public dissatisfaction on global shocks which have buffeted New Zealand's trade-based economy, noting that other incumbent governments worldwide have faced voters' rancor over living costs.

Yet his opponent Hipkins, who was turfed from office in 2023 largely over discontent about the same economic issues, didn't appear to benefit on Saturday. The left-wing bloc appeared to fall short of a majority but was still hopeful it could triumph — though that seemed a more remote prospect.

Electoral officials were due on Sunday to begin counting nearly 400,000 so-called special votes, including from New Zealanders who live abroad, a process expected to take three weeks.

Those ballots have historically granted an additional seat or two in Parliament to left-wing parties, which could snatch the current razor-thin majority from the right, although such an outcome would likely leave the left struggling to form a majority too. If a government can't be formed, New Zealand could face a fresh vote.

Labour Party leader Chris Hipkins addresses party faithful in Upper Hutt, New Zealand, Saturday, Nov. 7, 2026. Mark Tantrum/AP hide caption

"I think everyone's over politics so let's all have a bit of a break for the next three weeks," Luxon told reporters on Sunday. He will lead a caretaker government until an outcome is reached.

Luxon suggested that National had a viable path to power and boasted that it had secured more seats in Parliament than any other group. However, Saturday night was bleak for his party.

Several of National's most senior lawmakers would exit politics on results so far, including the ministers of finance and defense, the speaker of parliament, and the attorney general. With the party's traditional governing partners winning increased support, Luxon faces a more strident list of policy demands to secure their backing.

Libertarian ACT won 13 seats and populist New Zealand First won 12. Both are led by canny veteran politicians, and detractors of Luxon, a former business executive who entered politics in 2020, said his dealings with them in the three years since he took office had highlighted his political inexperience.

The Opportunity Party leader Qiulae Wong poses for a portrait outside Parliament in Wellington, New Zealand, Friday, Oct. 30, 2026. Charlotte Graham-McLay/AP hide caption

New Zealanders don't vote for prime ministers. Instead they cast two votes, one for their preferred party and another for the candidate they want as their local representative.

The country has one house of Parliament and no states, so the central government holds ultimate sway over New Zealand's direction. Governments in the South Pacific nation of 5.6 million people are elected every three years.

National won just 29.21% of the preferred party vote as of Sunday, a collapse of its previous result, and secured its 42 seats entirely by winning local constituency races. Luxon, who unusually faced two separateconfidence votes among his own lawmakers this year, further slumped in popularity as leader close to election day.

However, Labour also won just 29.36% of the party vote, a result that appeared to suggest a second election defeat for career politician Hipkins. Despite winning about the same party vote percentage as National, Labour has secured only 36 seats on numbers so far because they won fewer local races.

Minor parties on the left saw bolstered support. The environmentally-focused Greens won 16 seats and Indigenous rights group Te Pāti Māori secured five.

The total number of seats in New Zealand's Parliament changes due to quirks in how seat allocations are calculated. There would currently be 131 lawmakers elected, with 66 seats required for a majority.

Luxon's conservative bloc would currently hold 67, a razor-thin margin likely to discomfit a leader managing a three-way government with two emboldened minor parties. However, the left bloc held only 57 seats before the historically left-leaning special votes were counted.

Even if the final tally boosted the left bloc's seats slightly, Hipkins would apparently need support from newcomer Wong to govern.

Before the election, Luxon repeatedly ruled out negotiating with Wong. He sidestepped questions on Sunday about whether his vanishingly slim lead had changed his mind.

Ruben Amorim explains the kind of ‘mentality’ he wants to always see from inconsistent Milan after their win in Genoa. ‘There is no day off. It’s feeling that losing is the end of the world.’

The Rossoneri remain completely unpredictable, as they have lurched from negative to positive results and back again, repeating the pattern at Marassi.

After the midweek round defeat to Bologna, victory over Inter in the Derby della Madonnina and 2-0 Europa League loss to Ferencvaros on Thursday, they again flipped for a 2-1 win away to Genoa.

Alphadjo Cisse and Christian Pulisic scored the early goals, hanging on under pressure following the Mikkel Ellertsson deflected strike, to end Genoa’s impressive run of three consecutive victories.

“I am proud again with my players, I said before the game the most important thing was to be competitive, because if we have this attitude in every game, we are closer to winning every game,” Amorim told DAZN Italia.

“There are moments when we change the game, we go for the second ball and play different types of football, that is the sign of a great team, who can play different ways during the same match.”

The problem therefore is managing to keep that going more consistently than just once per week, but meanwhile they are in fourth place and only five points off leaders Lazio.

“We can win any game, but I cannot tell you we are going to reach this at the end of the season, as I cannot feel the consistency is there yet. I don’t want to say anything more than we are able to win the next game, but then it depends on a lot of things,” added Amorim.

“If we have all the players available, we will be better not just in Serie A, but also in Europe. This cycle of games was hard for us because of everything that happened, but I can say that if we are competitive, we can win any game in the league.

“So let’s focus on that and go day by day.”

Now Milan go into the break for international duty and hope the injury crisis can calm a little, as they were already missing Strahinja Pavlovic, Fikayo Tomori, Matteo Gabbia, Samuel Chukwueze, Ruben Loftus-Cheek, Pietro Terracciano and Alexis Saelemaekers.

The coach had said that Milan were “not ready to be competitive every three days,” and it does seem as if they reserve all their determination, passion and energy for the weekends. What does he mean by competitive?

“It’s about the mentality. When you play for Milan, there is no day off. You cannot switch off on Thursday the way we did and then switch back on come Sunday. That is not possible. It’s not going to work,” insisted the coach.

“It’s not physical, it’s about having that panic of losing. That does not mean being afraid of losing, it’s feeling that losing is the end of the world. That is the feeling I want in my team, so it depends on that.”

Goncalo Ramos was again involved in the build-up to both goals, but does not contribute actual goals or assists.

“It’s hard for a striker when he doesn’t score a goal, but we don’t spend a lot of time in the final third. We have a lot of possession, but when we reach the final third more often, Goncalo Ramos is going to score many goals. It is a team problem, the way we play is not helping Ramos, whereas he is helping the team.

“It is a process, we need to have Ramos spending 60 per cent of his time in the opponent’s box, and we are not there yet,” concluded Amorim.

‘On par with what the DoD pursued during the Cold War’: Pentagon’s war stockpile plan hits a minerals wall

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Pentagon wants to stockpile enough critical minerals, fuel, and munitions on American soil to keep fighting a major war even if cyberattacks and sabotage knock out factories and power grids at home. One defense expert says the scale of the planned effort is “on par with what the DoD pursued during the Cold War.”

The minerals may be the hardest part. The U.S. produces less than 1% of the most common type of rare-earth magnet it uses, and China, which dominates the processing of many critical minerals, has kept American companies short even in peacetime.

The initiative, known as FORTRESS America, short for Federal Operations for Resilience, Troop Resources, Energy, Supply, and Survivability, was formally established on Sept. 30. It is designed to keep U.S. forces operating if a conflict disrupts domestic power grids and manufacturing supply chains, and it is the same program behind the Pentagon’s push for major bases to plan for 90 days without the grid.

Stockpiling materials isn’t unusual for the U.S. military, Bryan Clark, a senior fellow at the Hudson Institute, told Fortune. What distinguishes FORTRESS is its planned scale and its shift toward domestic reserves rather than overseas stockpiles.

During the Cold War, the Pentagon stored as much or more war matériel overseas, near anticipated battlefields in Eastern Europe or Northeast Asia, Clark said. FORTRESS instead focuses on protecting supplies inside the United States, reflecting growing concern that American factories could themselves become targets.

China could disrupt U.S. industrial production through cyber operations, drones, electromagnetic warfare, or attacks carried out by people inside the country, Clark warned. U.S. agencies have already warned that Chinese state-sponsored hackers had positioned themselves inside American critical infrastructure.

That concern has a recent precedent in Europe. On Dec. 29, 2025, cyberattacks hit about 30 renewable energy sites, a manufacturing company, and a heat and power plant serving nearly 500,000 customers in Poland. Polish officials linked the attacks to Russia’s Federal Security Service and said the attackers tried to destroy industrial systems, but the country avoided a major outage.

Building stockpiles on the scale Clark described runs into a stark industrial reality: America’s defense industry remains deeply reliant on foreign supply chains for its raw materials.

In 2025, the U.S. imported about $60 billion worth of critical minerals other than rare earths, including nickel, lithium, cobalt, graphite, and tungsten, according to the Peterson Institute for International Economics.

The gap is most acute in rare-earth magnets. U.S. demand for the most common type reached roughly 48,000 metric tons in 2025, while domestic sources supplied just 300 metric tons, according to Arthur D. Little data reported by Reuters. U.S. companies are on track to have capacity for about 5,000 metric tons by the end of this year, roughly a tenth of last year’s demand.

The shortage has already reached factory floors. Ford, No. 22 on the 2026 Fortune 500, idled its Chicago Explorer plant for a week in May 2025 when rare-earth supplies ran short.

China has repeatedly tightened rare-earth export controls, and American companies are still struggling to secure enough supply, Reuters reported on Nov. 6, citing U.S. officials. That day, U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer pressed Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng to fully carry out Beijing’s commitments on rare earths and other critical minerals.

A major war could further disrupt overseas production and shipping routes, making it harder for the Pentagon to replenish its stockpiles.

To reduce foreign dependence, Clark said, the Pentagon is pursuing a dual approach: building raw-material reserves while funding domestic mining and processing capacity.

Tungsten, a stockpiling priority Clark identified, illustrates why building reserves takes more than government spending.

The U.S. hasn’t commercially mined tungsten since 2015, when cheaper imports undercut domestic production, according to Scientific American. Its net import reliance for the metal exceeded 50% in 2025, according to the U.S. Geological Survey.

In September, tungsten manufacturer The Elmet Group won a Defense Logistics Agency contract worth up to $2 billion to supply tungsten ores, concentrates, and sodium tungstate to the National Defense Stockpile. Only $150 million of that is guaranteed. The Pentagon also committed $450 million to Elmet, including about $150 million earmarked to restart the Springer tungsten complex in Nevada.

Yet Elmet said it won’t begin deliveries to the stockpile until additional supply becomes available. To avoid diverting existing tungsten from American manufacturers, the company plans to wait until new production comes online from investments in Nevada, Australia, and Spain.

The deal illustrates a problem FORTRESS could face across the board: signing supply contracts doesn’t immediately put minerals into government vaults.

The cost of FORTRESS also remains uncertain. A senior defense official told reporters on Oct. 20 that officials are still assessing costs, Air & Space Forces Magazine reported. The magazine estimated the broader initiative could cost “scores of billions of dollars, if not hundreds of billions.”

To help cover the bill, the Pentagon is exploring public-private partnerships, Dale Marks, assistant secretary of defense for energy, installations, and environment, told Breaking Defense.

Even with capital flowing, analysts warn that supply chain independence can’t be rushed.

“The Pentagon is unlikely to meet the goals in the near term,” Clark said, pointing to the structural difficulty of rapidly stockpiling both finished weapons and raw minerals.

Chris Berry, a minerals industry analyst, told Reuters it will “take many more years” to build the infrastructure needed to compete with China’s dominant supply chain.

The first test comes soon. Starting Jan. 1, 2027, federal rules require defense contractors to stop buying rare earths, magnets, and tungsten from China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea. Washington has repeatedly granted waivers because domestic supply falls short, Reuters reported in July.

For the Pentagon, building the physical reserves may prove far harder than announcing them.
