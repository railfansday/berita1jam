SAG-AFTRA Boss Says AI Takedowns Matter More Than Damages To Performers

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In an era of deepfakes, performers whose likenesses are misused by AI mostly want that content taken down rather than to go after damages, according to Duncan Crabtree-Ireland, CEO of SAG-AFTRA.

“I would say the vast majority, 90-plus percent of the time, that’s what it’s about,” Crabtree-Ireland said at Web Summit in Lisbon. “It’s not someone wants to get into litigation and make money. They want it to stop because they don’t agree with it, they don’t want their image used that way. It’s wrong.”

The SAG-AFTRA topper was speaking at a Deadline-moderated session at Web Summit entitled ‘Hollywood’s Identity Crisis’. He recalled his own experience of having his likeness misused while his members were on strike in 2023, before foregrounding the proposed No Fakes Act, which would give people greater control over their own voice and likeness. Related Stories News Web Summit Cancels Day Two As Storm Charles Hits Lisbon News Hollywood Unions Wonder Whether Next California Governor Will Share Newsom's Commitment To Expanding Film Tax Credits

“People deserve the right to have a say in what words come out of their mouth and what they appear to be doing online. A system like the No Fakes Act would provide that. And to quell any fears, it’s really not about suing people and making a lot of money. What it’s really about is getting platforms to take stuff down when your image, likeness and voice is being used without your permission.” Watch on Deadline

AI was the main talking point at Web Summit, and Crabtree-Ireland was asked if he detected a shift in his members’ attitudes toward the technology. “I think that there’s been a sense that if we put the right basic rules in place, that there is a way to coexist with AI,” he said. ”There’s been an understanding and an acceptance of the fact that no one can stop it, no one can block it.”

He added that a portion of SAG AFTRA membership remains staunch AI refuseniks. “Some of our members would like us to block it. I know, I hear from them every day and they’re angry that we aren’t. But the reality is, if I were estimating, I’d say that’s something like 10%, maybe 15% [of members].”

There is also the portion of SAG-AFTRA that wants to use AI to augment their work. “Some of our members really want to work with AI tools for augmentation, but none of them want to be replaced by AI because they love the act of creation, that’s why they do this. What you want to do is have the chance to do it, and yes, have tools that help you expand your creativity. That’s the sweet spot.

“Frankly, we could settle on that and then send the rest of the AI resources toward curing more diseases or some other things like that.”

Crabtree-Ireland was speaking on Web Summit’s enormous main stage, where, earlier in the week, AI-generated performer Tilly Norwood had been a guest. The SAG-AFTRA boss was at pains to highlight the difference between fully synthetic characters and AI replicas of real performers.

“It’s important that we distinguish between humans and algorithmic outputs because an AI character, an AI asset, a generative AI-created character is not a person. It doesn’t have the life of a person; it doesn’t have the experience of a person. One of the bigger problems we have with AI in general is we are anthropomorphizing AI models and robots and blurring the lines between humanity and non-human, artificially created entities.”

Microsoft stock has another problem beyond its latest global outage. While the disruption to Outlook, Teams and Copilot has put cloud reliability under scrutiny, the company’s aggressive AI infrastructure spending is creating a separate challenge for investors. Microsoft is growing rapidly, but sustaining that momentum requires enormous capital commitments. With MSFT approaching a key technical resistance zone, investors have more than one risk to assess.

Microsoft’s latest global outage has exposed the risks of its growing dependence on cloud-based workplace services. The disruption, which began on November 12, affected Outlook, Exchange Online, Teams, SharePoint and Copilot across multiple regions.

The incident lasted more than 12 hours, disrupting businesses, government agencies and other organizations. Microsoft attributed the problem to server and infrastructure issues and said it had found no indication of malicious activity. Its recovery efforts initially struggled as some restored servers became unresponsive under returning traffic.

The disruption also follows another Microsoft 365 service incident earlier this year, bringing reliability concerns back into focus. For enterprise customers, consistent uptime is central to Microsoft’s competitive position. Repeated failures could encourage large organizations to reconsider their dependence on a single cloud ecosystem.

The immediate financial damage remains unclear, but the reputational risk is harder to measure. Investors will be watching Microsoft’s post-incident explanation and whether the company can demonstrate that its infrastructure is becoming more resilient.

The second concern is the scale of Microsoft’s AI investment. The company is expanding data-center capacity to support Azure, AI workloads and growing demand for computing infrastructure. Microsoft reported $45.2 billion in capital expenditure for fiscal Q1 2027, while management expects calendar-year 2026 spending to reach approximately $170 billion. It also indicated that quarterly capital expenditure could exceed $50 billion in the December quarter.

The investment is supported by strong demand. Azure revenue increased 48% in the September quarter, while Microsoft Cloud revenue rose 30% to $63.9 billion. Commercial remaining performance obligations also climbed 73% to $677 billion.

However, the central question is how quickly that demand translates into returns. Heavy infrastructure spending can pressure free cash flow and operating margins, particularly when new capacity takes time to become productive.

Microsoft’s own filings warn that slower AI adoption, underutilized infrastructure and rising computing costs could affect future returns. The company is therefore facing a demanding balance: invest enough to capture AI growth without allowing capital costs to outrun revenue generation.

Microsoft’s cloud business remains a major strength, but sustaining its current growth rate will not be straightforward. Azure grew 48% in the latest quarter, and management expects growth of 49% to 50% in constant currency for the December quarter. However, Microsoft also acknowledged that customer demand continues to exceed available supply, while some third-party GPU capacity deliveries have been delayed.

Microsoft stock price closed at $539.44 on November 13, gaining 1.08%. The stock is approaching a major supply zone between $550 and $560, where previous advances have encountered selling pressure. A sustained breakout above this region could strengthen the recovery and bring $575 into focus.

On the downside, $515 is the immediate support area, followed by $490–$500. The broader demand zone sits around $375–$390, although that remains well below the current trading range. The moving averages continue to provide support beneath the current price, but the recent rejection near the supply zone suggests buyers have yet to establish control. A breakout above $560 would improve the technical outlook, while a decline below $515 could weaken the recovery.

Microsoft’s long-term growth story remains supported by Azure expansion, enterprise software and AI demand. However, the latest outage has highlighted operational risks, while the company’s infrastructure spending raises questions about capital efficiency and future profitability.

The next phase will depend on whether Microsoft can restore customer confidence, convert AI demand into sustainable revenue and maintain cloud growth without sacrificing margins.

BBC Boss Confronted By Angry Staff Over “Banning” Of Trans Rights Symbols In TV Shows

EXCLUSIVE: BBC director general Matt Brittin has told staff that the UK broadcaster is “not an anti-LGBTQ+ organization” after facing angry questions about the removal of transgender rights symbols in TV shows.

During an internal town hall meeting on Thursday, Brittin was asked at least a dozen questions about the corporation’s approach to LGBTQ+ issues and content.

It follows the BBC blurring out the trans rights slogan, “Protect the Dolls,” on the T-shirt of Bethany Antonia, the House of the Dragon actress who competed on Strictly Come Dancing. Later in October, the BBC digitally removed LGBTQ+ pride flag pins being worn by contestants on University Challenge, the quiz show hosted by Amol Rajan. During the BBC town hall meeting, one employee contrasted the removal of the LGBTQ+ symbols with the broadcaster’s insistence on presenters wearing Royal British Legion Poppy badges, honouring members of the armed forces. Watch on Deadline

In the comment, which was reviewed by Deadline and received more than 100 upvotes, the staffer added: “Is this not hypocritical and suggests the BBC is an anti-LGBT organization (and particularly the T part of LGBT)?”

Per an audio of the meeting obtained by Deadline, Brittin responded: “We are definitely not an anti-LGBTQ+ organization, and we want to be inclusive of everyone.”

He continued: “There is a challenge, I think, for an organization which has editorial due impartiality at its heart, between how we do our journalism and reporting, and what the standards we hold our presentation of news in particular to, and the inclusive values of an organization that is one where everyone can thrive. And sometimes those are in conflict.

“We have very high bar editorial guidelines, which is where things like the poppy wearing comes from … But that’s something which we always need to kind of have a look at. We have to evolve our standards in line with how society is evolving.”

Brittin’s response did not seem to satisfy some insiders. “Given that the BBC isn’t an anti-trans organisation, why do so many trans people and allies think it is?” one asked.

Another added: “There are people aged 25+ who refuse to pay the BBC license due to the BBC’s stance on transphobia — can you explain what these guidelines are and why trans symbols of support are banned? The initial response did not seem to speak to a worry [that] the BBC silently supports transphobia.”

Others agreed. “How can we in good conscience expect LGBT audience members and their loved ones to pay for the ‘service’ of having their lives treated as a controversy?” an employee asked. Another added: “‘Protect The Dolls’ is not an editorial guideline to be followed or not. It is a fact, and anything less IS anti-LGBTQ+.”

During the internal meeting, Brittin referenced work the BBC is doing to roll out anti-semitism and anti-Islamophobia training for employees. This prompted one insider to ask: “When might we have anti-transphobia training at the BBC too? Trans people are being actively demonised and attacked, including by the BBC, although there is no focus on this at all.”

One employee questioned the BBC’s recent decisions to cancel RuPaul’s Drag Race UK and queer dating show I Kissed a Girl/Boy. They said it was “really damaging to LGBTQ+ members of the BBC.”

Another person commented on the BBC’s news output on transgender matters: “How many people in senior editorial roles believe that being trans is a political/debatable issue? I am seeing a growing dehumanisation in how trans people are referred to in news copy etc. We seem to have decided that readers are too stupid to understand the concept of a trans person.”

The removal of LGBTQ+ symbols is the latest flashpoint between the trans community and the BBC. In 2021, more than 20,000 people signed an open letter accusing the BBC of failing in its reporting on transgender matters. Trans rights advocates protested outside the BBC headquarters in 2021 and 2022, with demonstrators calling for a rethink of how the broadcaster represents the community.

Canada is threatening to sue Stellantis over Brampton plant sale

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Industry Minister Mélanie Joly said Stellantis has 30 days to address a notice of default tied to a federal funding agreement

Canada warned it may take Stellantis $STLA to court over the automaker's plan to sell its shuttered factory in Brampton, Ontario, giving it 30 days to resolve Ottawa's objections. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly told a Toronto news conference on Thursday that Ottawa had formally notified Stellantis it is in default.

"We sent a notice of default and, basically, they have 30 days — or else, of course, we will sue them," Joly told reporters, according to CNBC.

Joly said Stellantis broke the contractual promises it made to Ottawa and to Brampton's workforce when it revealed it had struck a deal to sell the facility to Roshel, which makes armored vehicles. She said the company is "on the hook" for several hundred million dollars.

Stellantis, the parent of Jeep, Dodge, Fiat, Chrysler and Peugeot, announced on Monday a deal to sell the plant to Roshel, though it did not disclose the price. The company said it recognizes what it owes the facility's employees and is ready to negotiate a settlement for union-represented workers in talks with Unifor, Canada's largest private sector union.

Stellantis Canada CEO Trevor Longley said on Thursday that the automaker will give back C$24 million in subsidies it received from Ottawa, according to CNBC. That figure falls short of what the government estimates Stellantis owes.

"We recognize the disappointment and uncertainty surrounding the future of the facility. While our decision to sell the plant represents a change from the original plans for Brampton, the agreement we have advanced with a Canadian manufacturer provides a path to return the facility to productive industrial use," Longley said, according to CNBC.

A Stellantis spokesperson was not available for comment on Friday morning, according to CNBC.

Prime Minister Mark Carney has called the situation at the plant “unacceptable” and a “betrayal” of its workers, according to CNBC.

Stellantis had dropped its plan to build the Jeep Compass at Brampton, moving future output to an Illinois factory as U.S. auto tariffs and other pressures in the market weighed on the company, according to CNBC. The U.S. and Canada are in a trade dispute, and President Donald Trump has urged carmakers to shift their vehicle manufacturing to the United States.

Mixing vampire mythology with Santa Claus – “and the sexual undertones that come with it” – has surprised some viewers, admitted Rautsi.

“It can be disturbing to people, but it’s not a disturbing film. It’s set in this absurd, outrageous world that hopefully allows me to get away with it. That’s the part of its charm: the grotesque and all the twists and turns in the story.”

After Fantastic Fest and Sitges, “Red Snow” is now heading to Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival and has been at the AFM. Hannu-Pekka Björkman, Anni Iikkanen, Tommi Korpela, Kari Hietalahti and Marja-Liisa Nguyen star.

It was important to still ground the film in some “recognizable emotion,” stated Rautsi.

“These are heightened characters, but there are also some glimmers of what they are actually going through. We don’t necessarily have to root for them all the time, but we’re along for the ride – despite all the madness.”

Christmas can be a stressful time, with or without vampires.

“For me, this juicy Christmas setting acts as a boiling kettle. We’ve all been there. We know the feeling of spending Christmas with family when you’re supposed to be jolly but it’s actually miserable,” he laughed.

“You can see this in all of Shane Black’s films that are set at Christmas but not about Christmas. I think they have affected me, too.”

Rautsi doesn’t hide his affection for horror. Known for shorts “Helsinki Mansplaining Massacre” and “The Night of the Living Dicks,” he has also written “Nightborn” and “Hatching.”

“I’m a nervous person. A good horror film is my comfort zone, though, because it validates the internal life of someone who’s afraid. Fear is such a taboo subject – people don’t talk about it. But then we go and see a horror film, and we celebrate the fact that it’s O.K. to be afraid. It can be fun to experience it in a safe setting,” he said.

Despite its wild premise, “Red Snow” captures how he perceives life. It can be scary, but it can also make you laugh.

“I’m constantly laughing about all the things I’m afraid of. The goal of the film was to express how I experience things. You can be going through something ridiculous and then notice something beautiful about the whole situation. Life can be so many things, all at once, within the space of five seconds.”
