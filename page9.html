Nintendo Switch 2 Patent Paves Way For DS/Wii U Games

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Scott Baird is a Contributor from the UK with over a decade of video game writing experience.

A new set of Nintendo patents has been discovered that have paved the way for DS, 3DS, and Wii U games to return on Switch 2, so long as the player doesn’t mind shelling out for extra hardware.

The Nintendo DS was a massively popular system, selling 154 million units worldwide. However, the system’s dual-screen design has made it hard for its titles to be rereleased on modern systems. Konami managed it with the Castlevania series, but few other companies seem interested in reviving their DS games on the Switch or Switch 2.

One possible solution to Nintendo’s second screen issue may have been revealed. According to RetroDodo, two patents have been discovered that will allow the Nintendo Switch and Switch 2 to play games from the previous console generations.

Video games YouTuber Jirard 'The Completionist' Khalil has collected over $23K worth of Nintendo 3DS & Wii U eShop games.

The first patent allows a game to be instantly transferred between two systems, while the second allows a different console to be used alongside one running a title, with the ability to use touchscreen controls. Taken together, these patents could emulate the Nintendo DS, 3DS, and Wii U GamePad.

The caveat is that it would require someone to own two Nintendo Switch 2 units, or one of each Switch console, to run old games. This would be fine in a household that owns both systems, but for those who don’t, it’s an expensive proposition just to be able to run DS, 3DS, and Wii U titles.

This isn’t the first time Nintendo has brought dual-screen games to consoles; DS games were available on the Wii U, with the GamePad used as the bottom screen. This isn’t the most elegant of solutions, especially as it forces the player to keep switching perspective between screens, but it gets the job done.

The caveat to these patents is that it would require someone owning two Nintendo Switch 2 units, or one of each Switch console, to run old games.

There’s also the question of what games fans would even want to play on such a setup. The obvious answer is the Pokémon games, as fans would crawl through broken glass for modern ports of Pokémon HeartGold & SoulSilver that don’t cost hundreds of dollars on eBay. However, considering Game Freak seems averse to making money, there are still easier-to-port Pokémon games that aren’t on the Switch 2, and the DS games would take a ton of work to update.

The Wii U also doesn’t have much, since pretty much all its best games are already on the Switch, and they received updated ports over the console’s lifespan. Sure, it’d be cool for Nintendo Land to return, but no one’s lining up for Star Fox Zero. The Wii U was such a bomb that Nintendo is unlikely to bring up its ghost, even through a costly hardware expansion.

The Nintendo 3DS had a stacked library of amazing games, but some of them would lose the system's big selling point: 3D visuals. Super Mario 3D Land was a tremendous game, but it requires playing in 3D to get the full effect, especially for its stage secrets. The lack of Streepass and Spotpass would also take away a lot of the extra functions present in Nintendo 3DS games.

It’s entirely possible that these Nintendo Switch 2 patents won’t come to anything. After all, companies patent ideas all the time that never get made. The ability to play DS, 3DS, and Wii U games on the Switch 2 would be incredible, but the price of entry might be a bit too high for most fans, even if the titles are released for free on Nintendo Switch Online.

Virginia S. Ct.: Felons Who Served Their Sentences Regain Second Amendment Rights Unless …

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

the government shows the felon "poses a credible physical threat to the safety of another."

From today's long decision of the Virginia Supreme Court in Ginevan v. Commonwealth, written by Justice Wesley Russell:

On January 24, 2023, police responded to a possible domestic disturbance where they encountered Ginevan in a dispute with a woman. The woman claimed that Ginevan had grabbed a shotgun and pointed it at her. Although Ginevan admitted to possessing the shotgun, he denied having pointed it at the woman. He told police that he had the shotgun for protection. After Ginevan confirmed that he was a convicted felon, he was arrested and charged with possessing a firearm as a convicted felon in violation of Code § 18.2-308.2.

A review of Ginevan's prior criminal history revealed various and sundry felony convictions. In 2011, Ginevan was convicted of possession with intent to distribute a Schedule I/II controlled substance. Over the following four years, he was convicted three times for felony probation violations. Then, in 2018, Ginevan was convicted of obtaining money by false pretenses as well as possession of ammunition by a convicted felon. Notably, pursuant to Code § 17.1-805(C), the General Assembly has classified possession of ammunition by a convicted felon as a violent felony, but none of Ginevan's other felony convictions are so classified.

The court held that the Virginia statute banning felons from possessing guns was unconstitutional as applied to Ginevan. Some brief excerpts from the long opinion:

Beginning with its decision in Heller, the Supreme Court has often included statements in its Second Amendment cases suggesting that bans on previously convicted felons possessing firearms were acceptable or, at the very least, that nothing in its decisions should be read as addressing felon-in-possession bans. For example, in Heller, the majority stated that

nothing in our opinion should be taken to cast doubt on longstanding prohibitions on the possession of firearms by felons and the mentally ill, or laws forbidding the carrying of firearms in sensitive places such as schools and government buildings, or laws imposing conditions and qualifications on the commercial sale of arms….

Critically, however, these statements about dispossessing felons all were made in cases where the question of whether the Second Amendment allowed legislatures to forever bar felons from possessing firearms was not an issue…. As such, the statements about felon-in-possession bans contained in these cases are properly understood as dicta and not holdings of the Supreme Court….

In Bruen, … the Supreme Court set forth an analytical framework that courts must apply in evaluating Second Amendment challenges to limitations on a person's right to keep and bear arms. In explaining the framework, the Supreme Court wrote that

we hold that when the Second Amendment's plain text covers an individual's conduct, the Constitution presumptively protects that conduct. To justify its regulation, the government may not simply posit that the regulation promotes an important interest. Rather, the government must demonstrate that the regulation is consistent with this Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation. Only if a firearm regulation is consistent with this Nation's historical tradition may a court conclude that the individual's conduct falls outside the Second Amendment's "unqualified command." …

Our conclusion that the Bruen framework and not the Heller dicta provides the rule of decision does not answer the question of whether or not, as applied to Ginevan, Code § 18.2-308.2 violates the Second Amendment. Rather, it requires that we apply the framework to Ginevan's conviction and circumstances as revealed by the record in this case….

[T]he first part of the Bruen framework requires that we determine whether Code § 18.2-308.2's prohibition conflicts with the text of the Second Amendment. To answer this question, the Supreme Court counsels asking three "subsidiary questions": (1) "[D]oes the law apply to 'the people?'"; (2) Does the law apply to Second Amendment "[a]rms, i.e., any weapon customarily used for offensive or defensive purposes?"; and (3) Does the law restrict a person's ability to keep or bear an arm that is protected by the Second Amendment?

In this case, there has not and cannot be any dispute that the second and third questions must be answered in the affirmative. The weapon Ginevan is charged for possessing is an ordinary shotgun, which clearly falls within the scope of a weapon in common use for lawful purposes. {Although there is evidence in this case that Ginevan may have pointed his shotgun at someone, he was neither charged with nor convicted for any use of the shotgun. He was charged only with mere possession, so our review is focused on whether the Commonwealth may ban his possession of a firearm.} Accordingly, it unquestionably is an arm for the purposes of the Second Amendment.

Similarly, because Code § 18.2-308.2 is a blanket ban on Ginevan even possessing such a shotgun, there can be no question that it restricts his ability to keep and bear arms. Thus, the only inquiry from Bruen's first step that could even be in dispute is whether Ginevan's prior felony convictions removed him from "the people" protected by the Second Amendment….

Starting with Heller and continuing in more recent cases, the United States Supreme Court has indicated that "the people" protected by the Second Amendment includes "all members of the political community[.]" … The fact that the phrase "right of the people" means the same thing in the First, Fourth, and Second Amendments is, by our reckoning, sufficient to resolve whether a felony conviction, without more, removes a person from the political community.

It almost goes without saying that no one would contend that, once convicted of a felony, a non-incarcerated person would fall outside the protections of the First Amendment, and therefore no longer enjoy "the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances." Similarly, no one maintains that a felony conviction forever forfeits "[t]he right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures[.]"Because a felony conviction does not remove a person from "the people" referenced in the First and Fourth Amendments, we conclude that, as a matter of language and logic, it does not remove a person from "the people" referenced in the Second Amendment.

Of course, our conclusion that Ginevan is not excluded from "the people" referenced in the Second Amendment represents the beginning and not the end of the analysis. As then-Judge Barrett observed, the conclusion that felons do not fall outside the Second Amendment "does not mean that the government cannot prevent them from possessing guns. Instead, it means that the question is whether the government has the power to disable the exercise of a right that they otherwise possess, rather than whether they possess the right at all." Accordingly, we now turn to Bruen's second step and whether the Commonwealth had the power to disable Ginevan's exercise of his rights under the Second Amendment….

Although governments are not required to prove the existence of an historical twin from the relevant historical period to justify a modern regulation, the existence or absence of one certainly is something to consider…. Statutes banning convicted felons from possessing firearms as a result of their prior felony convictions are a creature of twentieth century legislatures. When asked at oral argument, the Commonwealth could not identify a single American statute that banned all convicted felons from possessing firearms that predated the federal effort in the 1960s…

[2.] Felon punishment at the time of the Founding

The Commonwealth argues that a total ban on felons possessing firearms is permissible because, at the time of the Founding, felonies routinely warranted "punishments far harsher than disarmament, including execution, estate forfeiture, or life imprisonment. And if the greater punishment of death and estate forfeiture was permissible to punish felons, then the lesser restriction of permanent disarmament is also permissible." … Although this approach provides a reasonably clear rule of decision and has some facial appeal, we, for the reasons detailed below, do not believe it withstands scrutiny….

The argument has its genesis in the English common law and how felons were punished. In England prior to the Founding, felonies often were punished by death and estate forfeiture. Before the sentence of death was actually carried out or in the instances in which a sentence of less than death was imposed, the convicted person was subject to attainder. Attainder was the common law process "of extinguishing a person's civil rights when that person is sentenced to death or declared an outlaw for committing a felony or treason." It inflicted what has been called civil death or civiliter mortuus, rendering the person as devoid of rights as if he were actually dead.

Although the recognized process in England, not all aspects of the process were adopted in the colonies or in the nascent American republic. In his treatise on Blackstone's Commentaries, St. George Tucker noted that "[c]onfiscations, forfeitures, and corruption of blood, do not follow in case of conviction or attainder for any treason or felony either in the commonwealth of Virginia, or under the federal government." … By the late 1800s, multiple courts, including this one, explicitly recognized that, to the extent that civil death existed in America, it was temporary and ended when the convict had completed any period of incarceration….

Not only do we find that the historical practice does not support the Commonwealth's argument regarding Second Amendment rights, but we reject the logical endpoint of the Commonwealth's argument. If the greater potential punishment of death allowed for all lesser punishments, including the stripping of Second Amendment rights, it also allowed for the stripping of all other constitutional rights. After all, the Supreme Court has made clear that the Second Amendment right is not a lesser right and is of equal dignity as the other rights elucidated in the Bill of Rights.

Just as a dead man cannot keep and bear arms, he cannot freely exercise religion or speech (First Amendment), be secure in his papers, homes, and effects (Fourth Amendment), remain silent upon criminal questioning (Fifth Amendment), demand a speedy trial or have the assistance of counsel in a criminal case (Sixth Amendment), and so on. The Commonwealth has offered no example of a legislature at the time of the Founding, the ratification of the 14th Amendment, or anytime thereafter asserting that a felony conviction stripped a person of all constitutional protections or that a previously convicted felon only enjoyed those protections as a matter of grace that could be rescinded by the legislature as opposed to as a matter of constitutional law that could not. Because the Commonwealth has identified no such attempt for other protections provided by the Bill of Rights and because the Second Amendment right is a coequal right to those, we simply cannot accept, as a matter of logic, the Commonwealth's greater includes the lesser argument….

The Commonwealth next relies on the common law punishment of estate forfeiture as a historical analogue. The Commonwealth asserts that, in many of the colonies and then in some of the states of early America, complete forfeiture of one's estate, often but not always coupled with a sentence of death or life-imprisonment, was a common punishment imposed for conviction for many types of felonies…. [But] the historical evidence of which we are aware suggests that, once released from incarceration, convicted felons could acquire property because their rights were "merely suspended during the term of the sentence." …

Another historical analogue offered by the Commonwealth are laws that prohibited those loyal to the British crown from possessing firearms during the Revolution…. As others have noted, English laws from the late 17th century extending well into the 18th century "disarmed Roman Catholics …." Yet others have pointed to laws that … disarmed Native Americans …. [But e]ach of the proffered analogues sought to disarm people not because the groups were perceived to pose a heightened threat of criminal activity or as punishment for prior criminal activity, but because each of the groups were perceived as disloyal to the State and as posing a threat of rebellion against the State as a whole. Disarmament was a function of being an enemy of the State, not because one was a common criminal….

[4.] Dispossession of dangerous individuals

The Commonwealth next points to surety and "going armed" laws from the relevant time period. The Commonwealth posits that these laws demonstrate that, consistent with the Second Amendment, those who have been determined to pose a danger to others can be disarmed….

In advancing these analogues, the Commonwealth stands on firmer ground…. Regarding surety laws, the Supreme Court [has] noted that the regime had a significant common law pedigree, that such laws continued in the colonies and early America, and were applied to the carrying of firearms….

The question before us now is whether Code § 18.2-308.2's permanent ban on Ginevan possessing ammunition is sufficiently similar to the temporary disarmament that accompanied the historical surety and going armed laws that allowed a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8)(C)(i) to survive a Second Amendment challenge in Rahimi. Although there are many potential points of comparison between those proffered analogues and Code § 18.2-308.2, we believe the best and narrowest comparison that answers the question revolves around whether Ginevan's prior felony convictions represent a finding "by a court" that Ginevan "pose[s] a credible threat to the physical safety of another[.]" In short, do any of Ginevan's prior felony convictions represent a judicial determination that he is dangerous, i.e., he poses a credible physical threat to the safety of another?

The Commonwealth argues that the General Assembly's classification of the offenses is sufficient to require such a conclusion. Alternatively, it argues that the very nature of Ginevan's prior conviction for possession of ammunition by a previously convicted felon is sufficient to support the conclusion that Ginevan has been found to be dangerous. We address each argument in turn….

Although … the General Assembly sets the public policy of the Commonwealth and that courts normally must defer to those policy choices, that deference is not unlimited. As noted above, the very purpose of the Bill of Rights was to "take[ ] certain policy choices off the table….

[And a] review of the elements of Ginevan's previous conviction for possession of ammunition by a felon in violation of Code § 18.2-308.2 reveals that no proof or judicial finding of Ginevan posing a credible threat to the physical safety of another was necessary for the Commonwealth to obtain the conviction….

The evidentiary record developed in the trial court in this case is understandably sparse given that the matter was resolved by way of a guilty plea. The fact that Ginevan had prior felony convictions—including convictions for possession with intent to distribute, obtaining money by false pretenses, and possession of ammunition by a convicted felon—was established in the trial court. However, the record in the trial court (and properly before us now) reveals nothing about the circumstances surrounding those convictions. We do not know whether the circumstances involved any level of violence or threat, let alone a sufficient level to allow us to conclude that those convictions could represent a finding "by a court" that Ginevan "pose[s] a credible threat to the physical safety of another[.]"

The lack of such evidence in the record properly before us is fatal to the Commonwealth's position. The Commonwealth bears the burden of establishing that Ginevan's disarmament "is consistent with the Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation." To do so in this case, it needed to demonstrate that at least one of his prior convictions represented a finding "by a court" that Ginevan "pose[s] a credible threat to the physical safety of another[.]" …

[W]e emphasize that our decision today "is a narrow one." … A case dealing with different offenses, a defendant with a different criminal history, or a different evidentiary record may well lead to a different conclusion….

Europe's Hera mission returns to the chaotic scene of a cosmic smashup

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

“DART made a really, really large mess. However, we didn’t really know what kind of mess.”

Four years ago, NASA dispatched a small spacecraft on a suicide mission to show how humanity might deflect an asteroid on a collision course with Earth. The first-of-its-kind experiment worked beautifully as the solar-powered space probe slammed into asteroid Dimorphos, permanently altering its trajectory.

Using telescopes, astronomers measured changes in the orbit of Dimorphos around a larger asteroid companion named Didymos in the aftermath of NASA’s Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) mission. The spacecraft released some 10 billion joules of energy as it impacted Dimorphos in September 2022 at more than 13,700 mph (6.14 kilometers per second), shortening the asteroid’s 12-hour orbit around Didymos by about 33 minutes, 25 times the most conservative expectation.

But scientists could only learn so much from DART. The collision destroyed the spacecraft, as expected. DART recorded images as it took aim on Dimorphos, but only faraway telescopes and a small CubeSat that briefly detected the impact flash could see what happened next.

“We knew right away that the impact was successful, that DART made a really, really large mess,” said Sabina Raducan, a planetary scientist at the International Space Science Institute in Bern, Switzerland. “However, we didn’t really know what kind of mess. We were still missing pieces of the puzzle.”

The European Space Agency’s Hera spacecraft recently arrived in the vicinity of Didymos and Dimorphos after a two-year journey from a launch pad in Florida. On Wednesday, ESA released Hera’s first views of the scar left by DART on Dimorphos. The images show the car-sized impactor carved out a crater the size of a stadium, nearly as big as Dimorphos itself, a rugged chunk of extraterrestrial rock measuring about 500 feet (151 meters) across.

Mission planners selected Didymos and Dimorphos for the deflection experiment because they are similar to many near-Earth asteroids, but there’s no risk of them striking our home planet. Scientists weren’t sure what Hera would find when it got to the double asteroid, currently orbiting the Sun about 120 million miles (193.5 million kilometers) from Earth.

“I would say we had two kinds of predictions: either we made a crater, or we reshaped the asteroid,” said Patrick Michel, Hera’s principal investigator from Côte d’Azur Observatory in Nice, France. “In fact, we did both because the crater is so large that the shape has changed. Therefore, we are in a sort of a transition between just cratering and global transformation, and we are still not sure how this can happen.”

Hera found Didymos, the larger of the two asteroids, spinning on its axis just as DART observed on final approach in 2022. Dimorphos is no longer so stable.

“As Hera returned more images, it became clear that the DART impact has left Dimorphos tumbling wildly in its orbit,” says Michael Kueppers, ESA’s Hera mission scientist.

The Hera spacecraft will fly closer to Didymos and Dimorphos over the next six months to obtain more detailed observations. Hera will release two small sub-satellites to attempt landings on Dimorphos and collect data on the asteroid’s composition, internal structure, and mass.

Scientists will use this information to reconstruct the dynamics of DART’s impact four years ago and better understand how a future deflection mission might move an asteroid off a trajectory that actually threatens Earth. Near-Earth asteroids come in many shapes and sizes, with different densities and makeups, so knowledge of Dimorphos’s physical properties will anchor predictive computer models teaching scientists how best to deflect all types of asteroids.

“Once we understand, we have a breakthrough, which really solves planetary defense,” Michel said in an interview with Ars.

In its initial reconnaissance this month, Hera spotted hundreds of boulders surrounding the double asteroid. This was not entirely unexpected. A picture taken by the Hubble Space Telescope shortly after DART’s impact showed a cloud of material streaming behind Didymos and Dimorphos, but scientists were astonished by the extent of the debris swarm in Hera’s first images.

“As we approached Didymos and Dimorphos, we quickly realized the asteroids were not alone,” said Naomi Murdoch, planetary scientist at the Institut Supérieur de l’Aéronautique et de l’Espace in Toulouse, France. “In fact, they had a lot of company.”

An image released by ESA on Wednesday combines 11 long exposure frames from Hera’s hyperspectral camera. Image processors on the ground digitally removed the relatively bright Didymos and Dimorphos to reveal a panoply of dimmer streaks and dots, each of which is a rock fragment ranging in size from a football to a washing machine, Murdoch said.

“Hera came to visit two asteroids, and we found hundreds of companions,” Murdoch said.

One obvious explanation for the boulders is they were thrown off Dimorphos when DART slammed into it. But scientists cautioned they need more information before they can say that with surety. Hera is the first space mission to undertake a sustained study of an asteroid with its own moon. The debris around the two asteroids might be relics from the creation of Dimorphos some time in the ancient past.

“Hera will allow us to study that in more detail,” Murdoch said. “We really need to know more about these boulders and characterize them to understand if they’re linked to DART, if they’re linked to another process, and what the consequences might be for other asteroid systems in the Solar System.”

The boulder field will create challenges for navigating around the asteroids. The boulders are an added risk for the two small CubeSats Hera is supposed to deploy in January.

“We saw a couple of images that didn’t reassure me,” said Margherita Cardi, project manager for the Milani CubeSat. “There is a mix of excitement and panic.”

Claudia Balossi, spacecraft systems engineer for Hera’s other CubeSat, named Juventas, said her mission’s planned trajectory would take it right through the boulders as it moves within a couple of miles of the asteroids, then finally toward a landing attempt on Dimorphos, where it will measure the miniature world’s tenuous gravity field.

“We are right in the middle of it,” Balossi said. “The smallest ones are probably just as big as we are and the biggest ones are very big. In the worst case, a boulder will land on us. We will activate our gravimeter right away [if that happens],” Balossi joked.

The good news is the asteroids’ gravity is so weak that Hera, Milani, Juventas, and the hazards around them all move at snail-like speeds. Hera’s current trajectory takes it around the asteroids at a relative velocity of just a quarter-mile per hour, or about 12 centimeters per second. At that speed, there’s no danger of a sudden catastrophic hypervelocity collision between the spacecraft and the boulders lurking nearby.

Scientists have seen this at an asteroid before. NASA’s OSIRIS-REx spacecraft kicked up an estimated six tons of rock and dust when it gently moved in to collect a sample from an asteroid named Bennu in 2020. Low gravity and weak surface cohesion are a recipe for outsized effects.

“These types of bodies, because of their low gravity, have a very counterintuitive behavior,” Michel said. “Each time we interact with them, whether to take a sample or to make an impact, the response of the surface was not the one we predicted, simply because our intuition relies on what we experience every day on Earth. Even if the rules of physics are the same in the Universe, they express very differently depending on local attraction.”
