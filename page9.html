PNC lets businesses accept USDC without keeping the crypto

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Eligible corporate clients can accept Ethereum USDC payments and receive dollars in their PNC accounts. The limited Coinbase-powered pilot keeps the banking relationship intact.

PNC's incoming stablecoin pilot gives businesses a way to accept USDC without keeping their receipts in crypto. Under the limited program announced Nov. 9, eligible Corporate & Institutional Banking clients receive USDC on Ethereum, with payments automatically converted 1-to-1 into dollars and deposited into a designated PNC account.

The payment travels over stablecoin infrastructure, but the receiving customer's balance ends up at the bank. That design keeps the deposit account in the payment relationship, even as the way of paying changes.

Coinbase supplies the underlying payment infrastructure through its Payment APIs, while PNC provides banking and treasury-management services.

The offering is a route for incoming USDC to become dollars in a banking relationship. It does not announce a PNC-issued stablecoin or put customer deposits on a blockchain.

10 Most Creative Traversal Abilities in Metroidvanias

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Ronald is a video game journalist with more than 10 years of experience in the industry, working on several of the most important Spanish websites.

Movement abilities in Metroidvanias are a fundamental part of their appeal, determining both the pace of exploration and our ability to interact with the surrounding world.

The genre is built on understanding spaces, their composition, and how to traverse them, making movement a crucial variable regardless of the game's perspective, mechanics, or philosophy.

As a result, the possibilities a game enables for movement along the Cartesian plane largely determine how satisfying, tense, or rewarding a campaign will be, depending on its intended purpose.

Therefore, in order to pay homage to those video games that make the best possible use of the philosophy of moving from one place to another, here are the ten most creative traversal abilities in Metroidvanias.

One of the main problems games in this genre face when creating spectacular movement abilities lies in their limitations, as they have to be balanced so that they aren't too powerful.

For this reason, Silk Soar from Hollow Knight: Silksong is a masterpiece of design. Among all the powers in Metroidvanias that let you fly upwards, it's part of the few that properly contextualize the limits of its capabilities.

Not only do you acquire it in the game's last third, unexpectedly opening paths out of nowhere, but its use is also tied to your needle, requiring the presence of a ceiling to be used, so you can't just break the map's traversal in its entirety.

In terms of creativity, it's on the spectrum's simpler side, yet it solves a major verticality imbalance by granting you a great deal of ease in exploration without making you overpowered, which is a tremendous feat.

Going in the opposite direction from the previous entry, Pipistrello and the Cursed Yoyo is a constant display of reinterpreting Metroidvanias' most common forms of movement, with Walk the Dog being the crown jewel.

The game plays with yo-yo tricks to establish its mechanics, adapting them with thematic coherence to make you feel that, more than just having powers within a video game, you're learning new maneuvers to show off.

In this particular instance, it's as simple as being able to move orthogonally across the map at a greater speed, but this is maximized thanks to the bounce pads distributed throughout all areas that change your direction and make the ability the key to numerous puzzles.

Pipistrello and the Cursed Yoyo is brimming with ideas magnificently adapted to its setting, making it by far one of the most inventive Metroidvanias to come out this decade.

Well Dweller breaks one of the genre's cardinal rules by not including a double jump at any point in its story, though it compensates with a Slingshot mechanic that is simply mesmerizing.

The title allows you to throw your weapon and embed it in any surface to use it as a springboard and jump higher than usual, encouraging inquisitive and creative exploration in a truly compelling way.

Most of the game's movement is based on the biomes you find yourself in, whether in the form of bubbles, air currents, or elevators, and it works precisely because this direct yet versatile ability adapts to any situation.

Of course, it leaves you without any means of defense, but, like Kratos's Leviathan Axe, Well Dweller's matchstick flies back to you when you need it, which also leads to spectacular and almost cinematic moments when transitioning from platforming to combat.

If you're looking for games that subvert your expectations, try these eight Metroidvanias that totally reinvent themselves halfway through.

In terms of inventiveness, Animal Well is a couple of notches above the rest of the genre, with all its progression tools being as unique as they're rewarding.

Since the title is more puzzle-based than platforming-focused, most of its abilities are proportional to said distribution, but the Disc is a versatile component that you can use both to solve environmental puzzles and to traverse in seemingly impossible ways.

Throwing the frisbee and timing its trajectory to hop on and soar through the air never gets old, both because of how mechanically satisfying it is and because of all the paths mastering this skill opens up.

Animal Well is a sandbox where most of its castles are built through iteration and experimentation, but fortunately, you have the Disc on your side to make the journey faster, smoother, and more enjoyable.

Despite my reservations about Constance due to its short length and limited side content, its movement mechanics are some of the smoothest I've ever seen in the genre, with Paint Dive being the standout.

It functions as both a dash and a dematerialization of the character, allowing her to blend into the environment and pass through obstacles and hazards as a graceful mass of purple paint that seamlessly integrates with the surroundings.

In its abstract form, it's simply a directional boost, yet its execution is elevated by the environments, which force you to use the ability in increasingly diverse ways, ensuring it's more than just a regular push.

Given that platforming is Constance's forte, Paint Dive plays a prominent role throughout the entire campaign, making it a proportionally more important element of the game than many of the other abilities on this list.

From classic Resident Evil to Dark Souls, these are eight Non-Metroidvanias that heavily influenced the genre.

Returning to what I mentioned about Silk Soar and the problems of having very powerful abilities, the Crystal Arrows in Aeterna Noctis take the conversation to another level by granting you the power to teleport to wherever you aim them.

Certainly, they're used in many of the most excruciatingly difficult platforming sections the genre has ever seen, but also in one of the deepest movement systems.

Being able to chain together consecutive teleportations while fighting and traversing Aeterna's world quickly becomes a delight, especially once you get used to them and feel you've mastered their immeasurable offensive and defensive potential.

While Aeterna Noctis' structure doesn't fully capitalize on the Crystal Arrows, they remain magnificent and satisfying, surprisingly without ever breaking the game's sequence.

Castlevania: Symphony of the Night dares to take its most unusual ability and only make it necessary for a limited number of sections, yet the Mist Form remains an unforgettable memory for anyone who plays the game.

Becoming an incorporeal entity to pass through bars is the kind of power fantasy that only a character like Alucard can materialize, with the added significance of having achieved something so astonishing thirty years ago.

Although there aren't many mandatory instances where you must use it, the ability is a major surprise when you unlock it, primarily when it becomes an incredibly powerful toxic weapon that allows you to both move freely and eliminate all enemies in your path.

Years have passed, and I don't think I've ever been so impacted by unlocking a new ability, which only speaks to Castlevania: Symphony of the Night's timeless greatness.

Of all the innovations that Ori and the Blind Forest brought to the genre during the formative years when its heirs were still establishing themselves, Bash is undoubtedly the most prominent.

The ability to bounce off both enemies and projectiles to move omnidirectionally and reach new heights and locations is absurdly incredible from the very first minute to the last.

The mechanic was significantly improved in the sequel, yet its stellar and unprecedented appearance in the franchise's first title is a core memory of the genre, particularly due to its use in combat to stylize every fight sequence.

I must admit Ori and the Blind Forest isn't among my favorite Metroidvanias, though that doesn't stop me from acknowledging that, in terms of uniqueness, there's no other ability that truly compares to Bash.

By 1994 standards, the existence of a video game of Super Metroid's caliber seems absolutely insane, as years have passed and the game remains so fresh and enjoyable that it's astonishing how ahead of its time it was.

People usually praise the Shinespark or the Morph Ball, which are masterful abilities by any measure, but I have to admit that what truly amazed me was the Grapple Beam and how wonderfully it utilizes Zebes' layout.

You spend much of the journey wondering what those strange cross-shaped blocks are for, and when you discover that they allow you to aim a beam that lets you swing freely, spin, and propel yourself at top speed, you can't help but be blown away.

Whether you played it back then or today, Super Metroid surprises you with its mastery at every turn, and there are so many ways I could have included it on this list that all I can do is stand up and applaud.

I'm going to lean towards modernity to define the top spot on this list and praise the work of MIO: Memories in Orbit, a contemporary masterpiece of the genre whose scale is truly breathtaking.

There are many reasons to say this, but unlocking the Striders, tendrils into legs and arms that can grab any surface to walk on walls, was the moment in its campaign when I realized I wasn't just playing a good Metroidvania.

Even though their use is limited by a stamina bar, thinking about the titanic effort it must have taken to craft each room of its vast map based on the eventual access to this omnipotent movement tool makes me dizzy.

Not only does it have fluid and pristine animation with perfect sound design, but it also blends seamlessly with exploration and platforming mechanics to make everything related to movement a true gift for players.

I've had many surprising moments with movement mechanics in the genre, yet the Striders in MIO: Memories in Orbit are unique while also being functional, powerful, and visually appealing, which is an elevated balance that's extremely difficult to achieve.

From the best bosses to essential lore explanations, these are eight Metroidvanias that hide their best content behind the most unexpected paths.

How ‘Godzilla Minus Zero’ Dominated the Box Office: GKIDS Chief on IMAX’s Impact and the Growing Market for Foreign-Language Movies

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

“Godzilla Minus Zero” almost pulled of a Godzilla-times-three on its opening weekend, earning $31.6 million in North America to nearly triple the domestic debut of its 2023 predecessor “Godzilla Minus One.” With that Godzilla math, it destroyed the record for the biggest North American debut for a live-action foreign language film.

It was a crowning moment for the speciality distributor GKIDS. Founded in 2008, the label primarily built itself as a distributor of foreign language animated features, most notably with its acquisition of the North American distribution rights to the Studio Ghibli library. As a day-one employee of GKIDS, and its president since 2017, Dave Jesteadt has helped expand the stateside audience for subtitled features. After a 2025 acquisition by Toho, the Japanese film industry giant behind “Godzilla,” the reach of GKIDS is wider than ever with “Minus Zero,” which drew a box office debut that some studio tentpoles this year would envy. “Anecdotally, so many people I know are like, ‘I took my dad this movie and he doesn’t go to any subtitle movies,'” Jesteadt told IndieWire. “Godzilla brings us together. If we’re spreading the gospel of foreign cinema, and Godzilla is a vehicle to get people to engage with great films and deep themes, then I’m all for it.”

After a flashy premiere at the New York Film Festival and a historic cume from IMAX and other premium large-format auditoriums, Jesteadt digs into how GKIDS helped “Minus Zero” come to America.

The following interview has been edited and condensed for clarity and length.

IndieWire: “Godzilla Minus One” was seen as a breakout success in 2023, with $57 million in North America. What lessons did GKIDS take away from Toho‘s release of “Minus One” in building toward this big opening for “Minus Zero”?

Dave Jesteadt: GKIDS joined the Toho Group in October 2024, but our story of working together on this began before that, back in 2023. GKIDS had “The Boy and the Heron” from Hayao Miyazaki and Studio Ghibli in theaters just a week after “Godzilla Minus One” opened, which was being handled by Toho’s L.A. office at the time. We got to know that team because we had these two films in theaters, sort of competing for similar audience profiles.

In terms of lessons that both sides took, it’s that you can really drive conversation with theaters as a source of celebration and community. When we joined the Toho Group, we took in the team that worked on “Minus One.” We re-released “Shin Godzilla” in 2025 as a test for bringing modern Japanese “Godzilla” films to theaters. Then the planning for “Minus Zero” really began in terms of how we could build and improve on the audience that “Minus One” already found.

Did “Minus Zero” get a bigger marketing spend stateside? How did you consider which people to reach to expand the audience for a Japanese “Godzilla” film?

I can’t give a specific number, but I’d say that we’re very efficient and very mindful of how we can reach the audience in the most direct way possible. For this film, we have the great benefit of our colleagues at Toho International owning and operating Godzilla.com, overseeing merchandise licensing. They have direct communication with the “Godzilla” fan as they exist in America.

But the other goal is to reach, as “Minus One” did, an audience that hadn’t engaged in Japanese “Godzilla” films. We also balanced reaching that core “Godzilla” fan and making sure that they turn out with this desire to continue the story from “Minus One,” reminding people that these films from director Takashi Yamazaki really speak to broader feelings today. They have prestigious bona fides through playing film festivals and great critical reviews. We’re leading with the quality of the film, making people feel that this is a very safe choice — not just if you like spectacle, but that this is going to be one of the best films of the year.

The New York Film Festival premiere played as a flex, especially after a stretch where Hollywood would premiere summer blockbusters at Cannes, draw mediocre reviews, and then have to weather weeks of bad buzz for their expensive tentpoles. With the NYFF premiere, “Minus Zero” was positioned as a prestige object. Is that the aura you were trying to cultivate around it?

I think so. Godzilla has been one of the most famous characters in the world. There’s a big audience that you can potentially address. But the “Minus” franchise takes it into more realistic, human-centered stories. That differentiates it. It is a risk to go to a film festival; I think that has been pretty well-argued. It is a risk anytime you want to share something with the world early before you open, especially when you have such high hopes about how it’s going to go. On the other hand, this was a really important way to demonstrate confidence.

“Minus Zero” opened in more than 3,200 theaters, roughly 1,000 more theaters than “Minus One.” How much did you expand its presence in premium large-format auditoriums?

“Minus Zero” was the first Japanese production filmed for IMAX. That was a central part of the very first announcement and it was a defining part of the marketing campaign. IMAX mentioned it was the highest-indexed opening gross on IMAX screens for an opening weekend in the U.S. We had Godzilla Day on November 3, with IMAX early access screenings globally. So, PLFs definitely played a big presence: Imax, 4DX, Dbox, and others. That’s very unusual for a subtitled foreign film.

It speaks to the expectations for high quality that audiences brought. The PLF story is a way to reinforce production quality. Everyone is saying, “You have to see it in the biggest screen possible with the best sound.” I’ve seen many, many beautiful foreign films. They often end up playing circuits to audiences that are very passionate, but don’t have access to those kind of screens. So it’s a privilege to be able to play a film on the kind of screen that’s typically reserved for the biggest mainstream titles.

How long are you staying in IMAX and PLFs?

It depends on the market. We’re definitely here for the rest of this week. But as “The Odyssey” and other titles have demonstrated, there’s such draw to these moments. It can even create this quest to see something in multiple formats. But as we head into the Thanksgiving frame, the Hollywood competition is obviously huge. We’re realistic about that, even while we’re trying to encourage people to see it as soon and as often as they can.

The reported statistic that more than 70 percent of grosses came from PLFs seems a bit of a double-edged sword to me. It speaks to a high regard for the film among consumers, but it also might indicate that it has a specialized audience that may only want to see the movie in a premium format. Is that a consideration GKIDS takes in, particularly when other titles are taking those auditoriums in the coming weeks?

Obviously, PLFs are time-sensitive. It’s not just about performance, but also about new titles coming in. We’re up against the biggest studios in the world. I’m obviously thrilled that we were able to get so many and I’m thrilled that people saw it that way. I also encourage everyone to just see it in regular theaters too. The way that it was mastered and the way that director Yamazaki made the film is so you won’t have a bad experience in any house watching it. But with any of these core fandoms, there’s a lot of interest in seeing something “the right way.” I’m happy for that, but then the hope is they tell their friends and we continue the growth from there.

You’ve been with GKIDS since its inception and have played a major part in growing the American audience for foreign language cinema. What do you think has changed in the marketplace that has allowed a Japanese “Godzilla” film to now find a much broader audience in the West than it could find before?

There’s no doubt that the quality of the films really helps. “Minus One” was a fantastic film. It won the Oscar for visual effects and became an audience darling. But really, the thing that’s changed the most is that younger generations are much more open to subtitled and foreign cinema than they would have been 10 or 20 years ago. We’ve entered an age of opportunity where what can constitute a hit film is really broadening. That gives me a lot of optimism compared to negative stories about the overall economics of the industry. GKIDS has been bringing subtitled animated movies and anime to audiences for almost 20 years and seeing that audience grow naturally. At first, dubs were essential; now, they’re not.

Though GKIDS isn’t producing these films, I’m curious if, as a distributor, you think that Japanese productions like “Minus Zero” are now being developed and produced with more of a global audience in mind.

The films are still intrinsically Japanese and are basically impossible to separate from the cultural tradition of “Godzilla” in Japan, as well as the overall Japanese film industry. On the other hand, there’s no doubt that the property has the ability to reach a global audience. The popularity with the “Minus” series is really a focus on quality, and how its storytelling is rooted in a time and a place that feels authentically Japanese. Although it deals with a post-war moment in Japanese history, it is incredibly contemporary in terms of the thoughts and preoccupations of a global audience.

So I don’t think there’s necessarily a change in focus. I would say that the audience and the director are in a much more direct conversation. On social media now, everything is auto-translated. You can see real-time active conversation between Japanese fans and viewers commenting about the film with American or Spanish or European audience. Everyone is able to share their thoughts or opinions about some of these themes in a way that brings local perspective to the table. By creating something that feel like it’s created for a specific audience, ironically, you end up making it feel more universal, just because it feels more lived in.

Do you think we are headed to a world where a Japanese “Godzilla” film can outgross a Western “Godzilla” film in North America?

I don’t really think about that. I’m very competitive against a lot of people, but the “Godzilla” franchise has benefited from having a lot of flavors to it. By building that big tent, it ends up being a character that has endured.

Dads want to do more caregiving. But here’s how workplaces hold them back

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The tension between caregiving obligations and work responsibilities is often framed as a women’s issue. But it’s not just working mothers who find themselves in a bind: Plenty of men say they not only value caregiving but want to do more of it. So what’s holding them back?

A new report from the Better Life Lab at the think tank New America—which publishes research on workplace equity and family policy—indicates that men face some of the same hurdles that working women do, from limited flexibility or autonomy to a lack of structural support. 

Compared to 2019, men are taking more time off from work or reducing their work hours due to caregiving responsibilities, especially those who earn less or have children with special needs. At the same time, they continue to grapple with the kind of cultural stigma that can keep them from taking paid leave even when they’re entitled to it. 

The report’s findings suggest that many men actually want to spend more time on caregiving: In fact, a surprising number of fathers—six in ten—expressed an interest in staying home full-time. Men who helped care for adults or children with special needs were even more interested in being home. Over two-thirds of the men surveyed claim to think caregiving is as important as paid work, and that both parents should contribute equally. But the reality is quite different: Only about 40% of respondents who are caregivers and parents said they actually split those responsibilities equally.  

There are a number of potential explanations for this disparity. Over a quarter of dads said there are barriers to being the kind of parent they want to be, from their job to their financial situation; male caregivers of adults or children with special needs reported feeling this at similar rates. Men with higher-paying jobs—earning over $100,000—were even more likely to report experiencing those barriers. 

As the Better Life Lab points out, however, the data seems to suggest that men are actually underreporting the extent to which work obligations actually impact their ability to show up as a parent or caregiver. 

A third of fathers said they have “no control” over their schedules; when men reported having more control over their hours, that usually meant they couldn’t dictate which days they worked, and vice versa. The majority of parents and caregivers—74%—said they would prefer to work remotely or with a hybrid arrangement if they could, but only 40% of them currently do. Whether they were parents or caregivers, only about a fifth of respondents expressed a desire to work out of an office (though men are more likely to have a preference for the office.) Nearly two-thirds of fathers also said work obligations interfered with their family time. 

Perhaps the most interesting data point is that men are missing work or slashing their work hours at higher rates—a sign that men may be facing the same pressures that women have for many years. Among men who care for adults or children with special needs, the share who missed work due to care obligations rose from 64% in 2019 to 76% as of this year. About half of men also reported cutting back on their work hours, up from 42% in 2019.

As Fast Company reported recently, there has also been a noticeable shift in how men take time off when they become parents. Men are finally taking more parental leave, in part due to the rise of paid leave policies at the state level. Until recently, the data indicated that men took little time off, maybe a week at most, and often through some combination of sick days or vacation days or unpaid time off. 

But recent research shows that dads are filing just as many leave claims as moms in states like California, Colorado, and Washington, which have robust paid leave programs. In states that have had these programs for many years, leave claims from men have only increased, while states that introduced paid leave more recently have seen higher uptake from the start. 

In other words, it seems clear that when men have good access to paid leave, they’re more likely to take it—a pattern that also lines up with the Better Life Lab’s findings on how workplace culture can shape the caregiver experience. When fathers took leave, they overwhelmingly cited a supportive workplace as a reason (72%); many also pointed to a paid leave policy through their employer or state policy (63%). 

Still, even as men say they want to be more present, it is still women who bear the greatest burden of caregiving strain. Women are more likely to sacrifice their careers due to caregiving responsibilities, and this happens at higher rates when they are mothers. As the report’s findings suggest, women are also more quick to self-identify as caregivers and place more value on care work. 

While the culture around caregiving has shifted to some degree—especially with more men now taking leave and stepping up to care for aging parents—it’s clear that both men and women aren’t happy with the status quo.

How many SpaceX Starship rocket tests have failed?

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In late September 2026, SpaceX's Starship entered orbit for the first time, deploying 26 Starlink satellites in what was heralded as a major step for SpaceX's cosmic ambitions. However, the celebration was cut short when a failed engine forced the mission to end shortly after reaching orbit. The malfunction prevented the intended six-revolution trek through Earth's orbit, falling well short of its 10-hour goal.

This result is typical for Starship, as the company's "fail fast, learn fast" mindset recasts the program's failures — anywhere between four and 11 of 14 total launches, depending on how generous you are — as opportunities for growth. This has been true since its first test flight in April 2023, which exploded 65 miles short of its intended climb into Earth's atmosphere. As Musk tweeted after the fiery test, "Congrats @SpaceX team on an exciting test launch of Starship! Learned a lot for next test launch."

Given SpaceX's success to date, it is hard to argue with the company's logic. According to analytics firm BryceTech, SpaceX launched 85 percent of all spacecraft in 2025, including over half the world's orbital launches. To boot, Starlink's 9,800 active satellites currently account for over half of the world's total, according to satellite tracker Orbital Radar.

The largest and most powerful rocket ever assembled, Starship has a cargo capacity five times larger than SpaceX's Falcon 9 rocket. This makes it essential for SpaceX's grander ambitions, including its million-satellite constellation, NASA's lunar missions and Musk's Mars colony dream. With another launch set for November 13, 2026, stakeholders will soon see whether the September launch represented progress or foreshadowed further additions to SpaceX's growing list of frustrations.

Gauging the success of the Starship project is difficult. Although some missions may fit within a binary success-failure matrix, most land somewhere in between. And while it has yet to deliver a fully-reusable rocket capable of missions to "Mars and beyond," progress was always going to be incremental. As such, its only fair to judge each mission against its own standards of progress.

Starship's start was turbulent. After its initial test combusted within minutes, its second flight saw the first-stage booster separate before the booster and vessel met fiery ends. Five months later, Starship's third test flight completed many of its objectives, reaching an altitude of 145 miles before disintegrating on its return. SpaceX then had two back-to-back successful launches, culminating in its launch tower successfully catching Starship's first-stage booster.

Unfortunately, another troubled period followed. Test six saw SpaceX call off its lauded mechanical arm before crashing the Super Heavy booster into the Gulf of Mexico. Two months later, Starship 7 exploded over the Bahamas just eight minutes after takeoff, sparking investigations by both the FAA and the Turks and Caicos government. The FAA launched another investigation after Starship's eighth test exploded in space, grounding planes across Florida. Starship 9 achieved minor improvements before spinning out of control, forcing SpaceX to cancel its planned deployment of 8 mock satellites.

Starship followed the disastrous run with a pair of its most successful tests. In August 2025, Starship released its first cargo of Starlink satellites. Two months later, SpaceX successfully launched and landed its 11th Starship rocket, fulfilling all major objectives.

Starship's twelfth test launched its upgraded Starship V3 rocket. While it accomplished several objectives, engine failures caused it to crash into the Gulf, triggering yet another FAA investigation. In July 2026, Starship 13 successfully deployed 20 of Starlink's new high-powered satellites before its first-stage booster failed to stick the landing. Considering its scattershot history, Starship 14's semi-successful run is par for the course.

Judging each mission against its stated objectives, most Starship launches have failed to meet expectations. SpaceX, however, would likely claim that its willingness to learn from mistakes puts its true failure rate closer to zero. The better question is whether the program is learning quickly enough to keep pace with its own meteoric ambitions.

If one takes Musk's proclamations as a measuring stick, Starship has underdelivered. In 2025, Musk predicted there was a 50 percent chance Starship would land on Mars in 2026. And with the once-every-26-months Mars launch window closing this November, you can guess which side of the coin SpaceX landed on. Of course, Musk is notorious for overpromising, so this is business as usual at Starbase.

Earlier this year, NASA announced that the program had fallen behind schedule by at least two years since the Agency selected SpaceX's rocket to carry its Artemis moon lander in 2021. With China aiming to reach the moon by decade's end, the pressure is mounting. NASA hopes to return to the moon in 2028; to do so, Starship must meet the Artemis III deadline in summer 2027.

Beyond being Musk's conduit to Mars, SpaceX's most ambitious projects depend on Starship to slash costs. SpaceX's proposed 1 million-satellite datacenter constellation Starmind, for example, is impossible without a high-capacity reusable rocket. The November 13 test should provide another data point to judge SpaceX's progress, especially given its ambitions. In a Q3 earnings call, Musk stated that SpaceX will attempt to catch both the rocket and its booster, making it the  "first time that an orbital launch vehicle has been fully recovered." How Do Borderline and Narcissism Differ?

Posted November 11, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Although they may appear superficially similar, borderline personality disorder and narcissistic personality disorder are very different from each other. Although both develop similarly—a child with a particular inborn temperament tries to adapt to a parenting style that doesn't fit their needs—they diverge. The borderline adaptation focuses on getting love and nurturing and avoiding abandonment, while the narcissistic adaptation focuses on getting admiration and high status and avoiding being seen as flawed.

(In this post, I am using BPD and NPD, adaptations, and the terms borderline and narcissist as shorthand for someone who qualifies for a full diagnosis of borderline personality disorder or narcissistic personality disorder.)

Both lack whole object relations and object constancy.

This means that people with BPD and NPD cannot form an integrated, realistic, and stable sense of themselves and other people. They can only shift back and forth between seeing themselves and other people as either all-good or all-bad.

A three-part unit forms when someone with a personality disorder interacts with others; however, it only shows one side of itself. Masterson called this a “split object relations part unit.” It comprises only good feelings or only bad ones.

Part 1—How the person with the PD feels about themself and acts.

Part 2—What the person with the PD believes the other person feels and thinks.

Part 3—The emotional atmosphere between them.

Carol has borderline and severe abandonment issues from her childhood in foster care. Her husband, Bill, is leaving on a regularly scheduled business trip. Her old abandonment fears return when she sees him packing his suitcase. She knows he is traveling for business and will be back in three days, yet she becomes furious with him. She accuses him of not caring about her feelings and keeps him up half the night arguing.

Part 1-Carol’s View of Self: Unwanted and unlovable.

Part 2-Carol’s View of the Other: An abandoning, rejecting parent.

Part 3-Atmosphere: Poisoned by Carol’s hurt and anger.

Dave is a very controlling narcissist married to Callie. They divided their responsibilities. She gave up work to have their children and run the house, while he focused on earning enough money for their lifestyle. But Dave keeps picking fights over trivial household details. He kept Callie up half the night fighting because he felt disrespected when she rejected his suggestions of how she should load the dishwasher.

Part 1-Dave’s View of Self: Disrespected.

Part 2-Dave’s View of the Other: Acting superior to shame him.

Part 3-Atmosphere: Poisoned by Dave’s hatred and desire for revenge.

People with BPD or NPD often sabotage important intimate relationships when they get emotionally triggered. The trigger is usually something associated with traumatic childhood experiences. Their reactions become more understandable when we look at their rage and splitting through an object relations lens. They are not really fighting with the actual person in front of them. This person has become an unconscious symbol of someone from their past who hurt them.

Once you understand the limitations that a lack of whole object relations imposes on intimate relationships, it is easier to understand why these relationships fail or are filled with ugly and unnecessary fights (Masterson, 1976; 1981). Here are some different versions one might encounter.

BPD Form of All-Good: I am lovable, and you love me, and we are feeling great right now. You are my good mommy or daddy. You will never do anything bad to me.

BPD Form of All-Bad: I am ugly and unlovable, and you are rejecting and abandoning me. You are my bad mommy or daddy.

NPD Form of All-Good: I am perfect, special, and entitled to the best of everything. You are my admiring audience who mirrors my perfection back to me. I feel great.

NPD Form of All-Bad: I am worthless, defective garbage. You are attacking and humiliating me. I feel deep shame and fear public humiliation.

If we have object constancy, we do not get as mean during a fight. We can remember things we like about our partner and our past good history together. If we lack object constancy, we lose touch with everything good when we feel hurt, disappointed, or angry with our partner.

Even if your lover with NPD or BPD declared their undying love for you 10 minutes ago, once he or she gets angry, their love turns to hatred.

Part of Carol’s problem with Bill leaving on a work trip is that she lacks object constancy and cannot feel her emotional connection to Bill (or his to her) when he is not at home with her. And her lack of it made it easier to abuse Bill because everything good about him was out of her awareness when she split on him the night before he left.

When they feel good in your presence, they project all good things onto you. When they feel unhappy, they blame you. Sometimes the all-good role is projected onto one person and all the split-off bad things are projected onto someone else.

BPD Example: Jane sees a psychiatrist for medication and a psychologist for psychotherapy. She splits and sees her therapist as her hero and her psychiatrist as her enemy.

NPD Example: Catherine treats her son as her “golden child,” while making her daughter into the family scapegoat.

People with BPD or NPD can get overly caught up in their current emotional state. They do things they later regret because they act impulsively and do not stop to think through the likely consequences.

BPD Example: Tanya got triggered by her new lover not texting her “good night” and impulsively blocked him on social media because she became convinced that he was cheating on her. He decided he did not want drama and broke up with her.

NPD Example: Married Harry ended up in therapy trying to save his marriage because his wife caught him having an affair with a pretty intern at work. Her attention had made him feel special.

BPD: Get unconditional love and nurturing from a combination lover/parent figure.

NPD: Gain high status, be acknowledged as special, all-knowing, and always right.

BPD: Being abandoned or engulfed by the emotional needs of the other person.

NPD: Experiencing a humiliating public failure that exposes them as flawed.

People with borderline personality disorder and people with narcissistic personality disorder sometimes behave in a similar fashion. But when you look closely, it is obvious that they are very different diagnoses. The disorders create different types of vulnerabilities, and people with BPD or NPD want different things from their partners and get triggered by different types of events. Think of a pear and an apple. They are both fruit, but most people can easily tell them apart if they have experience with both.

The Best Ways For Star Trek Fans To Play Magic's New Set: A Guide For Beginner Players

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When I sat down at my friendly local game store to participate in a pre-release drafting event for Magic: The Gathering's new "Star Trek" set, several players saw the Enterprise tattoo on my forearm and asked me the same question. If a longtime Magic player wanted to sample "Star Trek," what was the best place for them to start?

Ironically, I was there to ask the Mirror Universe version of that same question: If a longtime "Star Trek" fan wanted to sample the new Magic set, what's the best place for them to start?

I am new to Magic, having only picked up the game a few months ago to prime myself for the new "Trek" release. And one thing Magic and "Star Trek" have in common is that they're both absolutely daunting at first blush. One is a collectible card game with over 30 years of history and dozens of ways to play. The other is a sci-fi universe with nearly 1,000 episodes of television and 13 movies, with dozens of possible entry points.

I can answer one question immediately. "Trek" newbies should start with "Star Trek: Strange New Worlds," with its glossy production values and modern streaming sensibilities, before backtracking and sampling the various other shows and finding the one that connects with their tastes. (This is a sometimes controversial opinion, but I stick by it.)

But the other question involved some legwork, a lot of time spent at a gaming table, a great deal more time sorting through cards, and even a question proposed to the lead designers of the set itself. So, what is the best way for a "Star Trek" fan to sample the new Magic: The Gathering set? Here's what I learned.

One thing I will not do here is explain the rules of Magic: The Gathering, which are intuitive and easy to learn, but challenging to master. There are countless online resources to learn before you try anything I recommend below. I'm writing this piece under the assumption you intend to seek those out on your own.

The best way to truly dive in headfirst is to visit your local gaming shop and participate in a sealed or drafting event. Naturally, this is dependent on your location and whether your local gaming shop is an inviting and safe space (shout out to Mothership Games in Austin, Texas for playing host to my first sealed event). If such a place exists for you, check the calendar and see what's on offer. The good news is that the "Star Trek" set is brand new and there should be plenty of opportunities.

At events like these, players are organized into one-on-one matches and construct their decks from scratch, tearing open booster packs, examine their cards, and trying to build the most effective strategy possible around a 40-card deck. While experienced players will have an edge, they can't bring finely honed and powerful decks from home to immediately wipe the floor with you. Everyone starts at square one. And "Star Trek" fans should take very easily to this format, since the characters you'd expect to be powerful tend to have cards that are powerful. It also leads to some amusing team-ups, like how my winning deck involved an unlikely alliance between Deanna Troi and Gul Dukat.

In a friendly shop with a friendly crowd, this is Magic at its purest: a direct, competitive game where tactics at the table are as important as the strategy behind your deck construction. And the mechanics in the "Trek" set are intuitive and mesh well, meaning that creating a deck that functions as needed is fairly straightforward, and going with your gut choices (i.e., building around characters you like) often leads to strong results.

I can hear your next question: You don't have access to a local shop, or you don't feel comfortable going to one, so what's the best way to play at home? Once again, there are options.

You and a friend (or more) could always buy booster packs, tearing them open and building personal collections and constructing 60-card decks for standard play. However, I would recommend booster packs being the start of your collection, and once you have bought a few, track down single cards to complete your strategy rather than buying more and blindly hoping for strong pulls.

Another option is for you and a friend to buy a pre-release kit, as there are always plenty leftover after the initial week of special event. Use these to construct imperfect but fun starter decks to learn the game, and then grow and expand those decks as you see fit, doubling down on the characters and strategies you're enjoying.

However, the best at-home experience would be to pick up one of the set's Draft Night boxes, which are pricy but offer bang for your buck: enough cards for four friends to craft decks and go head-to-head for an evening, leaving everyone with enough cards to build upon in the future.

However, all of these options require tinkering, and suggest that you'll enjoy the process of building a deck as much as playing one. These options require additional legwork, additional financial investment, and even a bit of luck (although that can be mitigated by buying single cards from reputable online and brick-and-mortar shops). If you want to truly just dive into the game itself and skip the rest of the process, there is another way.

When I had a chance to speak to the key designers behind Magic: The Gathering's "Star Trek" set during a launch event in Los Angeles, I asked if the new set was friendly for "Star Trek" fans with no gaming experience, and if they had a recommendation on where to start.

First, they noted that the set was designed with new players in mind and puts narrative first. A card will generally behave like its counterpart in the "Star Trek" universe, so a fan knows that a Captain Kirk card will be a bit more headstrong and aggressive while Spock will allow for more wily, thoughtful strategy. This means a "Trek" fan should be able to grasp the new cards fairly quickly, using their existing knowledge as a crutch.

Second, they highlighted their recommendation for the best way to sample the game: the "Star Trek" commander decks.

Commander is the most popular format in Magic, where players utilize 100 card decks in four-player games that are slowly paced, socially driven, and generally take an entire evening to play. Think of them as more of a board game experience — or a nerd poker night — than a standard Magic set. Many Magic sets are accompanied by a few pre-constructed commander decks, which players can purchase, open, and play immediately without any prep. The "Star Trek" set has four of them: Federation Fleet, We Are the Borg, Klingon Fury, and Landing Party.

Specifically, the designers recommended the Federation Fleet commander deck as their choice for a "Star Trek" fan hoping to play the game.

Naturally, the next step was to test the designers' suggestion. I rounded up three friends and assigned them each a different Star Trek commander deck, taking Federation Fleet for myself. It's easy to see why this one was recommended: With Picard on the cover and a deck full of '90s "Trek" staples, it's the most popular era of the franchise in one 100-card deck.

Our four-player game took over three hours, and frankly, it was some of the best Magic I've ever played, with all four decks interacting in surprising and wild ways that left everyone at the table screaming and laughing. The "Trek" fans were impressed by the thematic touches, and how the mechanics relate to character personalities and motives, while the Magic players simply relished in the new design elements. I squeaked out a win, but only after the Borg player staged a comeback that essentially wiped out the table. My victory told a story in of itself: Dr. Bashir threw himself at the Borg player, perishing, but in the process, activated his ability to resurrect Ro Laren from the dead, activating her ability and ending the Borg threat once and for all.

After seeing the commander decks in action, I'm inclined to agree with the designers' recommendation. The Borg deck is scary and powerful, but makes them an immediate threat the other players will team up against to defeat. The Klingon deck is pure aggression, which may work against a new player's inherent shyness. The Landing Part deck, with its focus on Original Series characters, is fairly straightforward, but has a lot of moving pieces to consider. The mechanical simplicity of the Federation Fleet deck, and its arsenal of beloved characters, makes it easy to grasp and understand quickly.

Much like with "Star Trek" itself, there are countless ways to get into Magic: The Gathering, and no incorrect way. Ultimately, I agree with the designers: the pre-constructed commander decks, and specifically the Federation Fleet deck, are the best point of entry. Ideally, you'd buy all four and learn with friends for the best possible experience.

But like with "Star Trek" itself, you should be warned: once Magic has sucked you in, be prepared to be sucked in for the long haul.
