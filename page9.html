AI stocks climb after trillions wiped from market values

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Shares in artificial intelligence and technology firms have recovered slightly from an enormous sell-off that wiped trillions of dollars off stock values.

The tech-heavy Nasdaq stock index closed in positive territory following a market rout that began in the US on Thursday before spilling over to Asian and European markets into Friday.

Investors had been selling off tech shares in part due to concerns the Democrat victory in the midterm elections would mean slower artificial intelligence development.

However the latest US employment figures added to a mixed picture for American markets on Friday morning, with an unexpectedly strong 165,000 jobs added to the economy in September.

On Thursday, businesses closely linked to artificial intelligence development took a hammering on stock markets, particularly chip and semiconductor makers.

But the Nasdaq Composite bounced back slightly on Friday, ending the day up 1.1%, partly reversing some of its 5.5% fall.

The S&P 500, which tracks the share prices of the largest publicly-traded US companies, closed up 0.3%, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average was flat through the day before ending slightly down 0.4%.

Companies that lost hundreds of billions in market value recovered some ground on Friday, including Nvidia, SpaceX and Tesla.

However, stock markets in Asia and Europe fared worse.

The Kospi in South Korea closed down 6% on Friday, with shares in chipmaker SK Hynix plunging more than 10%. European markets also fell, with the main indexes in Germany, France and Spain ending the day down.

In the UK, the FTSE 100, which tracks the largest companies on the London Stock Exchange, declined 1.01%.

In addition to the recent market volatility, UK traders were also digesting the latest Bank of England decision to raise interest rates and the signal that another rate rise could be on the cards to curb rising prices.

The market fluctuations also come after the sweeping Democrat victory in the midterm elections.

A number of Democrats campaigned for new rules and regulations for artificial intelligence (AI) and data centres, and the party has since won control of both houses of congress as well as key contests in states that have been part of the AI data centre race.

US President Donald Trump on Friday reiterated his supported for what he calls "super intelligence", saying only "foolish leadership" would "say no to the riches they bring".

However, there have also been concerns over whether the enormous amounts of money being spent on AI will pay off.

Russ Shaw, founder of global tech advocacy firm Tech London Advocates, said the election result was sending a message that a rethink was needed on AI.

"The incoming Congress is going to really focus on AI in a very dramatic way," he told the BBC's Business Today.

"I'm a big fan of AI, but I'm watching what these companies are doing, and they're not communicating effectively at all, particularly in the US, about what the benefits of AI are all about."

Pierre BaquÃ©, chief executive of AI platform Neural Concept, said the share movements were a "necessary market correction" that reflected a legitimate concern about the amount of money being poured into AI infrastructure.

"The industry must move beyond unchecked infrastructure investment and demonstrate greater financial discipline," he said.

"We are entering a critical transition phase, a market purgatory where raw tech must prove its widespread economic value."

Dan Coatsworth, head of markets at AJ Bell, said it had been a brutal week for markets, and the key question was whether the jump in the S&P 500 and Nasdaq would be sustained.

"Once investors have had the weekend to catch their breath, next week could give a clearer indication of whether the tech trade is going down the pan or fighting back."

As well as the fallout of the midterms and the plunge in artificial intelligence stocks, US traders will also be digesting the latest jobs figures.

And those numbers were better than expected: US employers added 165,000 new jobs in October, well above the average monthly increase and more than double what analysts had expected.

Overall the unemployment rate, which counts the number of people who are out of work but actively looking for a job, eased slightly to 4.1% from 4.2% in September.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) said the unemployment rate has remained in the narrow range of 4.1% to 4.3% since March.

Neil Birrell, chief investment officer at investment firm Premier Miton, said while the jobs figures may feel like second billing to the market movements they were still important.

"The US economy is running hard which may mean the risks the markets are worrying about at the moment are not such a risk to the real economy as is being feared," he said.

Treasury Cuts Crypto Exchange MEXC Off From US Financial System

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Treasury Cuts Crypto Exchange MEXC Off From US Financial System

The U.S. Treasury Department has moved to sever crypto exchange MEXC Global from the American financial system, accusing it of facilitating at least $16 billion in illicit transactions tied to Iran, North Korea and Russia.

In an order issued Friday through its Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, Treasury said MEXC, one of the world’s 20 largest crypto exchanges, served as a major conduit for sanctions evasion and cybercrime.

The action, part of a broader effort dubbed Operation Economic Outcast, bars U.S. financial institutions from processing any funds transfers to or from the exchange. It takes effect 30 days after publication in the Federal Register.

“Financial institutions cannot have it both ways: Either you support the Iranian regime, or you have access to the U.S. financial system,” Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in a statement, vowing to keep severing the regime’s “economic lifelines” and its attempts to use digital assets to dodge sanctions.

Founded in 2018, MEXC processes more than $1 billion in daily transactions, holds around $5 billion in reserves, and offers more than 3,000 tokens, according to FinCEN. Between May 2021 and May 2026, FinCEN said, the platform moved at least $16 billion connected to illicit activity, including nearly $1.5 billion tied to Russian sanctions evasion, with $232 million in direct transactions with sanctioned entities. The agency named MEXC a “primary money laundering concern” in connection with Russian illicit finance.

Despite publicly claiming anti-money-laundering controls and barring users in places like Iran and North Korea, MEXC’s compliance was riddled with “severe deficiencies,” FinCEN said.

The exchange was still transacting with sanctioned Russian exchange Garantex and its successor Grinex as recently as June 2026, more than four years after Garantex was first designated. It also processed roughly $200 million linked to the A7A5 stablecoin and about $22 million benefiting Iran’s central bank and its Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps.

Those connections place MEXC squarely within a sprawling sanctions-evasion ecosystem that Treasury has been dismantling piece by piece.

The department last month designated Russia’s A7 Network a transnational criminal organization and proposed cutting its front companies off from U.S. finance, flagging more than $179 billion in transactions through its ruble-backed A7A5 token, much of it routed through Garantex and Grinex.

The same campaign has reached into Iran’s crypto sector, where Treasury gained sweeping authority to sanction anyone operating in it and blacklisted the Iranian exchange Nobitex over its role in funneling crypto to the IRGC.

The order uses a provision of the Combating Russian Money Laundering Act rather than a standard sanctions designation, a tool FinCEN has increasingly reached for to cut off crypto infrastructure that operates outside the correspondent banking system.

Bence "Beadyboy" Hamos reflects on 8 years at Crytek helping build Hunt: Showdown's community.

The Hunt: Showdown community received a sucker punch in September when it learned that Bence "Beadyboy" Hamos, a beloved community-facing figure for the long-running extraction shooter, was unceremoniously let go in a wave of Crytek layoffs that affected at least 30 people.

For years Hamos was one of the faces of Hunt: Showdown, hosting livestreams, vlogs, and social media videos that kept players abreast of their favorite game's happenings. Through the highs and lows of Hunt's updates, events, and occasional controversies, he was a steady presence—calm, exuberant, and in touch with the experience of average players.

Hamos began at Crytek in 2018 in a social media role, eventually climbing to senior broadcast manager. As someone who absorbs the work of community managers constantly for work, I believe Hamos was particularly good at it—which is why his sudden departure hit Hunt's most dedicated players hard.

Hamos grew up in Budapest, Hungary. He'd looked up to Crytek since he was a teenager playing Crysis with his brother. He moved to Germany to find work and jumped at a job posting at the famed studio in 2018. He worked there for nearly nine years, focused entirely on Hunt: Showdown.

I caught up with Hamos last month, soon after his last day at Crytek, to learn what it was like to cultivate Hunt's community over nine years and talk about his unexpected departure.

"I really looked up to Crytek," Hamos told me. "[Getting hired] was such an unbelievable moment in my life, which obviously completely changed my whole career. It was nothing I never imagined I'd do. I was super happy."

Hamos said he had never worked in games when he interviewed at Crytek, but managed to charm the studio with his experience managing his rock band's social media, interacting with its community, and running livestreams on Ustream.

"It was right before the launch of Hunt, so in 2018," Hamos, who had recently moved to Germany from his home in Budapest to look for work, recounted. "I saw an opportunity at Crytek for a very entry-level social media position, and they really needed something.

"Somehow, by huge luck, I got hired in two weeks."

When Hamos arrived at Crytek in early 2018, the industry was in the middle of a major shift toward live service games. Fortnite had yet to become the biggest game in the world, battle passes didn't exist outside mobile and MOBAs, and Crytek was taking a big chance on an FPS format so early in its infancy that it didn't have an established name yet: the extraction shooter. There was no playbook for communications in a game that's always changing, so Hamos did what came naturally.

To deal with a community and manage a community, you have to be part of a community

"I always had this principle that to deal with a community and manage a community, you have to be part of a community. So that's why I played a bunch of Hunt," he said. "I played over 2,000 or 3,000 hours just in my personal account, not even my testing account. I streamed it in my personal time.

"I think it's super important in a position like that to not just understand what the company wants, and the vision for the company, but also the other side of the equation—what players are feeling. I would understand their frustration. I would see the issues firsthand."

Hamos says his extensive hours in Hunt put him in a sometimes "weird" position of representing the interests of both the players and devs, which did not always align.

"You're the voice of the community, and you have to push that to the dev team and let them know about feedback and issues people are talking about. But at the same time, you also push the vision of the team and answers to the community from the dev side. It's a weird mixture."

I was curious what that process actually looked like, behind the scenes. How would a community manager package up player sentiment and deliver it to devs who aren't as deeply embedded in Reddit or YouTube?

"So we would collect reports on a weekly or bi-weekly basis, depending on the time period, and we would have to actually measure and collect it. Like, 'Hey, this many players are positive about this subject. These are the mixed ones. These are the negative ones.' And we list them out and say '200 people are saying this is bad.' That's a high priority from the community side, and you want to look at different platforms too, like socials, Steam, Reddit, Discord, and even your personal DMs, because not everyone has an X or BlueSky account. Those are all counted in, but it has to be accurate."

Hamos also explained how being the go-between for players and devs sometimes means taking a popular idea to the team and getting a no.

"There may be things that the community is really asking for, but the team is definitely saying no to. Whatever the reason: It may be not in line with the vision of the game or simply technical reasons, it may take too much time, and you really have to prioritize things because live service games always need updates, and people always want the big shiny things," he said. "When that happens, you just have to be transparent with the community and not ignore it."

The other side of that coin is calibrating feedback (especially balance feedback) from platforms that are prone to hyperbole.

"When you talk about a game like Hunt, you have to understand that the vocal part of the community may be veteran players that have been playing for a long time, and may be the highest-skill six star players, but that's actually a minority of all players. So you cannot necessarily balance the game around 1% or even less than 1% of the player base when your numbers show that it's not actually a problem for the community," he said.

"If you're talking about balancing a weapon [for example], designers look at the percentage of people using it in every skill level. Sometimes it may look very loud on Reddit that, say, the weapon is way too overpowered, but when you look at the data, it may be actually a super low percentage of people using it. They would then decide to make a small change or even show the data to players.

You cannot necessarily balance the game around 1% or even less than 1% of the player base...

"It has happened before where we said, 'Hey guys, here's the usage rate, this is why we are not planning on changing it, and it's not actually as big of a deal as you may perceive it," he said. "I think the community appreciates it when they can see the numbers behind things."

But Hamos also offered an example of an adjustment made with high-skill players in mind that didn't really affect the rest of the community: Making Spitzer ammo, a special bullet type that increases bullet velocity, a scarce lootable item instead of something you can buy from the store whenever.

"Today, Hunt has so many layers and so much depth that you can really make small adjustments that may not affect lower-skill players," he said. " I always had a lot of trust and respect for the designers. They really know what they are doing, so I think Hunt and the community are in good hands in this regard."

Hamos had nothing but amazing things to say about his coworkers and experience working at Crytek, even if the way he left was, in his words, shocking and sad.

"It came out of nowhere. And after nine years, I did feel like a part of my soul was ripped out because it was my day-to-day. I kind of lived and breathed the game. I would wake up and think about Hunt and work on Hunt. It has been a part of my entire adult life, basically," he said. "So I was shocked and sad, and I'm still sad. I still get a bit emotional about it because it's painful, but I don't have any hard feelings."

His firing was part of a wider round of layoffs at Crytek, the second the studio has been through since February 2025. Hamos understands that the game industry is a "pretty rough space" right now, with tens of thousands of developers displaced in the last three years, and showed no sign of bitterness—in keeping with this jolly demeanor in countless Hunt update videos. On his last official day, Hamos posted a farewell message on the official Hunt subreddit—it quickly became the top post on the page with thousands of upvotes and hundreds of supportive comments. The response was "really special," as he recalled.

"To get faced with the fact that your work meant so much to people is something really special. I'm trying not to get emotional here, but when you work day-to-day, you don't really feel that. But now when it's over, it is kind of a payoff for all of that, which really hits like a truck," he said. "It's not something that I expected or maybe even deserved, but something that meant a lot to me."

Unlike countless colleagues now left in the lurch by layoffs, Hamos was fortunate enough to quickly land a new gig, thanks in large part to his recognizable community work on Hunt. In October, he became head of community and social at Good Fun Corporation, a studio formed by former Hell Let Loose developers. They're making Hunger: an old-timey extraction game with distinct similarities to Hunt.

It came out of nowhere. And after nine years, I did feel like a part of my soul was ripped out because it was my day-to-day. I kind of lived and breathed the game.

"Of course there are similar vibes to Hunt in Hunger, though it's an extraction RPG, which is very different. It has a lot of MMO elements to it and progression and questing, which appeals to me a lot."

Hamos is starting over from scratch, and his highest hope is to cultivate a community as friendly, passionate, and sturdy as Hunt: Showdown.

"I think [Hunt] is one of the least toxic communities. Of course there's some toxicity, but the people are more mature and understanding compared to most. And since it's a bit smaller overall, you also have this little family kind of vibe where we're talking to people who also understand the game and live the same hunter life."

Hamos told me the end of his time at Crytek doesn't mean the end of his own Hunt career. He still loves the game and believes its best days are ahead.

"Many games enter with a bang and then fade out quickly with fewer players unless they have a major update. Hunt, especially among live service shooters, is really an exception to the rule. You see growth forever—like it's still going," he said.

"I just wish the best for Hunt and hope it will stay for another 10 years at least."

With the ‘New’ Yeezy, Ye Says He Wants to ‘Let the Clothes Do the Talking.’ But Who’s Listening?

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“I don’t have a lot of words tonight,” Ye told me, not long after concluding his Yeezy Season 01 runway show in Los Angeles on Wednesday evening. “I’ll let the clothes do the talking.”

You might read that quote and be surprised to hear that Ye said it warmly, with an all-32-bared smile. But for those still paying attention, his relatively upbeat demeanor tracks with the tame-not-turnt persona that Ye has performed through most of 2026; the one that’s been slowly-but-surely trying to reamass the cultural capital he’s spent most of the last decade either annihilating or reclaiming. Legions of fans have proven they’re still all in; the culture at large remains justifiably dubious. The question this week is, how will Ye’s return to fashion affect the calculus?

Upon arrival, a typical Hollywood warehouse exterior turned into a Frank Herbert-esque interior as soon as you crossed the threshold. Inside, a hundred or so guests sat on two rows of unbroken benches that encircled a large white mound in the center, lit by a heavenly white glow from above. Inside was a mix of the usual cool kids: models (including the runway-favorite teen rapper Nettspend) and recurring characters in the overarching, enduring Kanye Universe. There was Fear of God founder Jerry Lorenzo, who made conversation with Don C, while 2 Chainz was patiently posted up in a hooded black leather coat like ’98 Wesley Snipes. Big Sean, rebuffing the sweltering indoor temperatures in a shearling jacket over a crewneck sweater, caught up with Hit-Boy.

Models on the Yeezy Season 01 runway, which featured a characteristically minimalist set piece.

The 19-year-old rapper Nettspend walked in the show.

Once everyone was seated, the lights dimmed as the unmistakable opening piano note of “Runaway” set the show into motion, with one lone model striding out in a killer black trench. From there, two more models, men this time, walked out in the opposite direction; what followed was a bit of organized chaos with the number of models and paths alternating at random intervals to the tune of a playlist that zipped between new Ye music (UK garage vibes, it would seem) and a gothic orchestra not unlike the scores Danny Elfman used to chef up for Tim Burton.

The fits were crisp, practical and tactical. Most of the menswear was ready-made for basic maneuvers, while the women’s looks were sleek and casual, with a few Milla Jovovich-in-Fifth-Element skimpy ensembles thrown in because Ye can only restrain himself but so much. The most notable thing, about the guys especially, were the relatively streamlined silhouettes: close-cropped coats, bombers and vests, fitted pants and sweats. Where past Ye lines championed and emblazoned the path for the baggy wave, with oversized boots and pants you could swim in, Ye seemed to be brazenly planting a flag back on the opposite end of the spectrum.

Nearly 12 years, and a dozen lifetimes, since his debut Yeezy Season 1 runway show in 2015, the notion of a “Season 01”—his first formal showing since his damning 2022 show in Paris, the one with the “White Lives Matter” shirts—is a conspicuous attempt to clear the slate. All of his previous shows had been produced in collaboration with Adidas, who severed ties with Ye four years ago, after he tweeted a tirade of antisemetic remarks. This time around, to let the clothes tell it, Ye is locked in. I don’t have an image of every single Yeezy Season line seared into my brain, but I feel comfortable saying Ye’s latest was the first time I looked at one of his collections and objectively thought, I could wear that. Most of the gear is quite solid—and familiar Ye's running the same playbook with Season 01 that he’s run with his music, and to some degree, his persona. It’s the Yeezy gear you know and love(d), refined to make it that much more palatable, similar to the way a song like “Whatever Works” puts him back over chipmunk’d soul samples. And true to the title, it does indeed; the fact that it does is maybe a testament to the brilliance that got lost in the insanity. But is playing the hits (quite literally, with “Runaway” opening the show) what we want from one of the most paradigm-shifting—and absolutely chaotic—creative minds of our time?

Historically, Ye’s designs are so focused on an idea of utilitarianism that they ultimately come off as a bit drab, if not downright dystopian. Essentials like hoodies and beefy leather jackets are still central to his vision, but as the models strode around in his usual earth-toned palette—save for a handful of black looks to start, and a bright pop of reds at the end—it became clear that Ye was putting on the same show in a different kind of stadium. The same sci-fi silhouettes, tweaked to their cleanest shape and most pleasing hue, sans screenprinted hate speech. Ideologically, the runway show wasn’t far off from the experience of hearing his 2007 hit “Good Life” all these years later, ringing off stadium speakers, carrying 80,000 people along on a wave of wistfulness— even if Ye himself only performed approximately 75% of the lyrics against a backing track.

In keeping with all of his other moves this year, Yeezy Season 01 is Ye presenting a safer, rule-abiding version of himself: Ye with just art, no antics. Much in the way that his latest album, Bully, eschewed any lyrics that say, shouted out R. Kelly or Bill Cosby (which is more than can be said about 2024’s Vultures 1), it felt like a deep sigh of relief to see him present clothes that don’t feature appalling slogans, at a fashion show with Candace Owens nowhere in sight. But the compromise for a sanitized Kanye is also one that can’t help but feel anodyne as well. The New Ye doesn’t needle, but he’s lost Old Ye’s knack for trying something new. Of course, we’re also well over a decade into the Yeezy experiment—at this point, the present has caught up to the futuristic vision Ye first dreamt up back in 2015. There’s a whole new generation of aspiring designers creating in his image, kids who fancy themselves trailblazers whose paths just loop back to Ye: streamer Kai Cenat just launched his own clothing brand, Vivet, while N3on attended Wednesday night’s show, skipping the Yeezy space gleefully documenting every interaction. It feels as though all of this has happened before, and all of it will happen again.

Mystery. Intrigue. Chaos. This is the typical life cycle for a Ye project, be it music, fashion, or otherwise. An announcement materializes, hype ensues. Then, inevitably, the seams start to show and it becomes apparent that he isn’t inviting you on a pre-planned journey so much as building the plane in real time, seconds before takeoff. And then, through sheer force of will, or maybe even genius, the result ends up so captivating that the speedbumps hardly matter. He’s still a master showman, and all it takes is a strong yearning for the Old Ye to find yourself surrendering to it. The efficacy of that last factor, though, has undeniably waned over the years. For much of the 2020s, in the midst of many a manic outburst or worse, Ye’s messes have hardly cohered into something objectively beautiful. The intrigue curdled into perverse bemusement; for every unwavering diehard fan, there was a rubbernecker tapped in to watch the circus.

The 2026 version of Ye has been an exercise in proving how many people will show up when he shuts up, and whether he can make it back from the brink if he delivers the right spectacle.

The result is the most well-oiled the Yeezy Machine has ever been. An album that more or less dropped when he said it would, for once. A smattering of live shows across the world, each in a sold-out stadium boasting record-breaking rap concert attendance where he leans on his most enduring strengths: visual spectacle and a bulletproof catalog. The only area left to renegotiate is fashion…which brings us back to Wednesday night in LA.

These are the scenes you’d expect at a Ye event, but the first sign that the overall vibe was different and more relaxed was that the man himself was at the door, tucked off into a hallway, immaculately dressed in a fitted all-black leather fit offset by a ridiculously large and ornate gold waist fixture—every bit the Dune-caliber fit as the space we were standing in required. Ahead of the show, he posed backstage flanked by his wife, Bianca Censori, and the four kids he shares with his ex, Kim Kardashian (including 13-year-old North, who was sporting her unmistakable blue-raspberry locks). At Yeezy events past, you’d expect to see harried staff scrambling, while Ye was somewhere out of sight but exuding stress and anxiety out into the atmosphere. But on Wednesday, the 49-year-old beamed as friends made their way past him to their seats. Even a three-piece-suited Kid Cudi came over to say hello.

Ye backstage with his children, from left: North, Psalm, Chicago, and Saint West.

Fifteen years ago, Yeezy Season 01 would’ve caused a massive sea change in the menswear world almost instantaneously, with the wave starting in SoHo then slowly emanating out into the beyond. In 2026, does Kanye still move that needle? The Year in Ye has been one big display of his ability to Don Draper the culture at large, and shock us all by “how much it never happened.” There’s no controversy big enough to keep him away for too long and very few fences he can’t mend.

For example, Sean and Cudi ceased their umpteenth falling out with their old label boss two months ago at a guest-packed, blockbuster homecoming show in Chicago on Ye’s tour stop; their show of support tonight only underlined how deeply the hatchet has been buried yet again. 📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The US Treasury bureau FinCEN on Friday ordered US financial institutions to stop processing transfers involving MEXC Global.

The US Department of Treasury on Friday issued an order to cut cryptocurrency exchange MEXC Global off from the US’ financial system after accusing it of facilitating at least $16 billion in transactions linked to sanctions evasion, cybercrime and other illicit activity.

The Treasury’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) asserted that MEXC handled transactions involving sanctioned groups in Iran, Russia and North Korea between May 2021 and May 2026. This included around $1.5 billion in transactions linked to Russian sanctions evasion, according to the official press release.

The order effectively bans US financial institutions from processing money transfers involving MEXC and will take effect 30 days after it is published in the Federal Register.

“Financial institutions cannot have it both ways: Either you support the Iranian regime, or you have access to the U.S. financial system,” said Secretary of the Treasury Scott Bessent. “Treasury will continue to sever the Iranian regime’s economic lifelines and thwart its desperate attempts to circumvent U.S. sanctions using digital assets.”

The action is part of Operation Economic Outcast, a US Treasury campaign launched in August with aims to cut off financial entites that help Iran evade sanctions.

According to DefiLlama, the exchange held about $4.48 billion in total assets as of Friday. Cointelegraph contacted MEXC for comment but had not received a response by publication.

He also added that people need to retain their cognitive skills instead of just ChatGPTing everything. Both creators and viewers should investigate things themselves, using their own human brains.

Rober’s team is using AI in its writing process, and a script is the foundation of long-form, educational YouTube content like his. Is the vibe just that viewers won’t care, so long as no fully AI-generated video makes it into an upload? Intriguing…
