InfoQ Homepage News Netflix Turns Operational Data Into a Knowledge Graph for AI-Driven Observability

Nov 13, 2026 3 min read

Netflix is developing an ontology-driven knowledge graph to accelerate production investigations and provide AI agents with a shared understanding of its operational environment. The platform connects information about applications, incidents, deployments, alerts, and infrastructure dependencies, addressing a problem that can leave engineers spending hours piecing together information scattered across different systems.

The company claims it handles more than 2 billion requests daily and upwards of 38 million real-time logging events every second. During the November 2024 Jake Paul-Mike Tyson fight, its infrastructure supported approximately 65 million concurrent streams. Yet investigating a typical application regression can take around four hours, with much of that time spent reconstructing relationships between systems rather than analysing the underlying data.

Netflix's solution is to make those relationships explicit through an operational ontology and knowledge graph, creating a foundation for automated root-cause analysis and, eventually, self-healing infrastructure.

Netflix already collects metrics, events, logs, and traces (MELT) across its applications and infrastructure. However, these signals are spread across systems such as alerting, incident management, deployment tooling, and engineering communication platforms.

An application regression might originate from a client release, an experiment, infrastructure load shedding, or a service several dependencies downstream. Although each system contains useful information, engineers must often connect the evidence manually.

Netflix identified the missing capability as a consistent way to describe not just individual components, but how they relate to one another.

Its Observability Ontology provides that common model, defining operational entities, their properties, and the relationships between them. It uses established standards including RDF, OWL, SHACL, and SPARQL, while the resulting knowledge graph is stored in QuipuDB, Netflix's distributed RDF database built on Rust's redb storage engine.

The graph is populated through a pipeline of components Netflix calls enrichers. These consume information from operational systems and transform it into structured relationships, represented as subject-predicate-object triples.

Some enrichment is deterministic, while other stages use LLMs and probabilistic techniques to interpret less structured information. Netflix constrains these outputs through strict validation rules, including consistent entity types, URI naming conventions, and checks against its software catalogue.

Netflix also captures information from incident discussions, including hypotheses, rejected explanations, and mitigation decisions. This allows human reasoning to become part of the operational knowledge base rather than disappearing when an incident conversation ends.

Its correlation engine examines 13 types of signals, including log errors, metric anomalies, traces, alerts, incidents, deployments, configuration changes, experiments, and code changes. It searches for potential causal relationships, evaluating evidence such as temporal proximity and severity to produce ranked hypotheses.

Netflix describes the process as a knowledge flywheel: Observe, Enrich, Infer, and Adapt. As investigations produce new insights, those relationships can inform future detection and analysis. The knowledge graph also underpins AutoRCA, Netflix's internal agentic AI system for root-cause analysis.

Rather than having specialist agents exchange loosely structured findings, they use the ontology as a common interface. Agents analysing traces, logs or deployments can contribute information about the same operational entities without needing to understand each other's internal implementations.

Netflix intends to use this foundation to produce ranked, evidence-backed explanations for incidents, with humans retaining approval authority over proposed changes and remediation.

Platforms such as Datadog, Dynatrace, and Splunk already correlate observability signals and increasingly incorporate AI into incident investigation. Netflix's approach emphasizes the semantic model behind those signals, making operational relationships persistent and directly accessible to both engineers and machines.

The company ultimately wants to extend this capability from automated root-cause analysis into automated remediation and self-healing infrastructure. It also plans to explore integration with its Unified Data Architecture, connecting operational information to broader business and technology domains

Duquesne’s Michael Buckley says young investors skip over learning these critical skills. They could prove to be invaluable in the AI boom

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As investors race to identify the next big winners in the AI boom, standing out requires more than just spotting the next breakthrough. Michael Buckley, managing director and head of equities at investment firm Duquesne Family Office, says young investors often overlook the fundamentals of corporate finance. In the age of AI, that could give them an edge.

Buckley has built a diverse investing résumé. He’s worked across private equity, venture capital, and public equities—and his early days in debt and credit helped set him apart. Right after graduating from Harvard, Buckley learned the ropes of accounting and corporate finance at Sankaty Advisors, the now-renamed credit arm of private equity giant Bain Capital. With credit as the focus, Buckley poured himself into capital and corporate structures, as well as bonds and loans. His responsibilities unwittingly set him up to be recruited for one of America’s top private investment firms.

“I learned the importance of things like capital structure and debt, which I think a lot of people—for example, that start out their career in things like equities—oftentimes will skip that stuff over,” Buckley tells Fortune. “It’s because it’s tedious and it can be complicated,” he added.

Early-career analysts who have their heads buried in stock charts, earnings growth, and revenue projections could get a leg-up by returning to those basics. There’s a simple reason: The AI boom hinges on debt. Buckley explains that in this new ecosystem, debt is suddenly very important not only to distressed debt companies, but also to public markets and venture firms. As AI companies and their infrastructure partners take on more borrowing to finance data centers, chips, and computing capacity, investors need to assess how quickly a business might grow—and whether they can afford to pay back what they owe. It particularly stands out for those involved in data centers or funding next-generation labs, Buckley adds; tech hyperscalers issued more than $100 billion in corporate bonds last year, according to the Bank for International Settlements.

Buckley recognizes that doors opened thanks to his debt and credit expertise. During his two-year stint as an analyst at private equity firm CPMG, a headhunter came calling; Duquesne was looking to hire a credit and equities wiz to work under the family office’s founder and leader, legendary macro investor Stanley Druckenmiller, whose claim to fame was helping George Soros make $1 billion betting against the British pound in 1992. He joined as an analyst at 27.

Today, at Duquesne, he’s steering some of the firm’s biggest wins. Over the past 11 years he’s leaned into investments in AI, as well as healthcare and biotech—which he believes will vastly innovate over the next decade. Recently, Buckley says the firm backed pharma company Formation Bio. As a self-proclaimed “non-dogmatic” investor, he’s continuing to keep his horizons open, a philosophy shared by Druckenmiller: Keeping a broad perspective on the market—and a set of skills to meet the moment—could mean the difference between spotting an opportunity or missing it entirely.

“Now, we’re seeing that in the AI venture world, these concerns about credit and interest rates suddenly have this big impact on AI infrastructure, private companies, and the venture community,” Buckley says. “I think it’s something I’ve learned from Stan, to always be thinking about the top-down and the bottom-up.”

The CEO's Real Job During An AI Transition

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

​Most conversations about AI leadership tend to sound alike, with transformation road maps and bold predictions about what the organization will look like in five years. That language sounds impressive, but it skips past the part of the job that keeps me up at night: how we get there without breaking what already works.

The tension few leaders admit is that you have to build the organization you’ll need 18 to 24 months from now while still running the one you have today. You cannot pause operations to retool. Customers still need service and payroll still needs to run. As a result, you really need to do two jobs at once, and most days they pull against each other.

I think of it as renovating a building while people still live in it. You can’t tear out the plumbing and tell everyone to come back in a year. You’re rerouting water while the taps still need to work. That means running temporary lines so nobody loses service and telling residents which faucet to use this week and why.

In an AI transition, the residents are your employees and your customers. The work still has to get done today, even as you change how it gets done tomorrow. The CEO’s job is to sequence that change and protect what’s working while it’s being replaced, while keeping people informed enough that they trust the renovation instead of fear it. It gets harder once you factor in the people doing the work, because they don’t all move at the same pace.

What surprised me most, even after years of leading organizations through change, is how unevenly adoption spreads across a company. Give the same tool, training and deadline to five departments and you will get five different response curves. Some teams run with it immediately, while others need more time. A few will quietly work around it and hope nobody notices.

The instinct, especially under pressure to show results, is to treat the slow adopters as a compliance issue: Send another email, set a harder deadline, loop in HR. But when someone with real skill and a track record of success is dragging their feet on a new way of working, often it’s because something else is going on: unclear expectations or a legitimate gap in the capabilities of the tool itself. Maybe it’s an unspoken fear, usually about what their job becomes once the tool works.

So I try to get curious before I get directive and ask teams what they see as the main barrier. Sometimes the answer is that the person needs more support to build a skill they don’t have yet. Sometimes they have the skill and the motivation and I just haven’t given them a clear enough reason to change something that already works for them. Those are different problems, and they require different responses. I’ve learned that neither gets solved by a companywide memo. Here is what I recommend leaders do instead.

Some leaders avoid or ignore the hardest conversation, which is what happens to people’s roles during AI tool adoption and transition. I’d rather have that conversation directly than let people fill the silence with worse assumptions. In my own organization, I’ve told people plainly that most of our jobs will look different within a year. I don’t mean they’ll disappear. But the work will shift from producing to reviewing, from doing the task to deciding whether the machine got it right.

That can be an uncomfortable thing to say to a team. It’s also more respectful than pretending it isn’t true. People can handle hard news. What wears them down is watching the ground shift underneath them while leadership insists nothing has changed.

Another part of the job that rarely makes it into strategy decks is deciding, in advance, what requires a human sign-off. Once you start giving AI real authority over workflows, whether that’s scheduling, communications or decisions that touch a customer, you need clear thresholds for when it acts on its own and when a human should intervene to stop and ask.

It’s better to over-define those boundaries early and loosen them as trust builds than to discover deep problems. More than anything, AI transitions are leadership and process decisions that need to be made by someone who understands both the operational stakes and the people affected by them.

When I think about what my job looks like during this AI transition (or any other major transition), a lot of it is managing friction day after day, and moving fast while keeping things stable. Some teams will be ready before others, and that’s okay.

We also must accept that there is often a difference between what technology can do and what people are ready to trust it with. A tool can be capable on day one, but adoption happens one workflow, one team, and one good experience at a time. Nobody can hand you a playbook for this because every organization’s friction points are different. The job is finding those and working through them without letting the taps run dry.

Navigating this well has everything to do with showing up week to week and having the uncomfortable conversations early, not worrying about five years down the line. It means treating every person’s resistance as its own puzzle instead of a compliance problem. So I keep checking the taps. Most days they work, and when one doesn’t, I want to hear about it early, and I want whoever tells me to know I’m glad they did. ​

Setting up the TV itself is largely a breeze, especially if you already have the Google Home app on your phone. Even though I’m an iPhone guy, I was able to scan an onscreen QR code and use the app to move quickly through the setup process. An account sign-in and Wi-Fi network selection later, I just had to wait on a software update before heading to the couch.

TCL’s unremarkable remote has sharp edges, the usual mishmash of Google TV buttons, and a pretty disorganized layout overall. On the plus side, it’s backlit and comes with six app-shortcut buttons.

One head-scratcher: The penultimate TV-setup screen notes that I can “customize the star button on the remote,” using it as a shortcut for this or that, which sounds great except that there is no star button.

Speaking of hallucinations (kidding!), Google Gemini comes baked into the Google TV UI, which is reasonably easy to navigate but not without a few annoyances. For example, the top menu includes a Live TV page—home to various FAST channels, simple enough—but also a TCL page that contains … other FAST channels.

As for Gemini, it’s nice to have but hardly essential. Just talk into the remote and you can ask for, say, movies about sharks, or learn who invented the Steadicam. The voice recognition is responsive, and the responses arrive quickly. Too bad it’s not hands-free; you have to press and hold a button on the remote to use it. Gemini also dishes up “watch now” options, and you can use it to create custom images and video clips, if you want. Unfortunately, while Gemini can accommodate basic TV commands (“Start Netflix,” “Mute the sound,” etc.), it can’t do things like open picture settings or toggling off motion-smoothing—a bummer because those commands would actually save you a little time. Instead, such requests turn into basic web searches, returning only information on how to access those settings yourself.

I was a bit disappointed with Google TV’s screensaver. It lets you draw from various sources, including an art gallery and your own photo library, but it doesn’t offer any “art mode” options like a mat overlay or automated brightness reduction. You also can’t adjust the amount of idle time before the screensaver kicks on. An art TV this is not.

Software quibbles notwithstanding, the QM7L is a joy to behold. Whatever you care to make of TCL's marketing lingo (SQD Mini-LED, the “S” stands for “Super”), the results can’t be denied: dazzling color and ocean-floor blacks that really do bring OLED to mind. In fact, I’d argue that this TV “pops” more than the aforementioned Roku OLED—not because the colors are more vibrant, but because there’s greater brightness behind them.

While watching Pixar’s Coco, for example, both TVs produced glorious hues, but my eye was more drawn to the TCL because the reds, greens, and blues just seemed to burst a bit more. (OLEDs, especially on the lower end, do tend to struggle with brightness, as detailed in my Roku Pro Series review.)

Even watching something like East of Eden on Netflix, with its decidedly muted color palette, it was the brightness that stood out; the QM7L revealed a bit more detail inside the Trask house. Interestingly, I also spotted a difference in the Netflix logo: On the TCL, it blazed a fire-engine red; the Roku version looked more subtle, almost orange in comparison.

Make no mistake, both TVs impressed me greatly. But seeing the two alongside each other, I liked the QM7L’s picture just a little better. I also watched sports and played Xbox games—both well served by the panel’s native 144-Hz refresh rate—and marveled at the smooth action and sharp lines.

Not everything was perfect, however. There were a few cases where contrast and brightness seemed a little lacking, like during an episode of the criminally underrated Halt and Catch Fire on the Roku Channel. Checking the settings, I found that the brightness had been dialed down quite a bit—but not by me.

The prime suspect? TCL’s AI-powered “smart picture optimization,” a common bit of marketing in modern TVs. If there was any behind-the-scenes intelligence at work here, I struggled to find it. Indeed, I had to manually adjust the brightness for that show.

Even more frustrating, where was motion-smoothing management? This scourge of modern television should be off by default. With the QM7L, it arrives in full ruinous glory—and the AI isn’t smart enough to disable it in situations where it doesn’t belong: namely, everything except sports and gaming. Luckily, it’s not buried 10 menus deep; you can toggle it off pretty easily. But this is one area where Roku proved superior, its Smart Picture Max adeptly adjusting motion-smoothing for most viewing.

As for sound, the QM7L is the rare TV that doesn’t straight-up suck, thanks to tiny, down-facing speakers buried inside the cabinet. This one has two Bang & Olufsen–branded cones you can actually see, and because they’re embedded in the backside, they face backward as well. That’s not ideal for a wall-mount situation, as the audio would be forced to kind of squirt out from the sides, but the speakers do sound pretty good when the TV is freestanding. TCL doesn’t specify speaker wattage, but I can attest that they get plenty loud, and do so without distortion. However, I don’t understand why they’re positioned just a few inches apart, as the cabinet appears to have room for much more separation. Consequently, the soundstage feels pretty narrow; there’s very little “surround” to be found here. Even with this above-average audio, you’re still going to want a soundbar—hence my complaints about the pedestal stand earlier.

But if my main complaints are a wide pedestal stand and so-so AI integrations, that's good news for the QM7L. The most important thing, picture quality, is stunning for the price. You buy a TV for your eyes, and with this one, they're in for a treat.

Nvidia Employees Push Board to Bring Back Jensen Huang: Report

Some Nvidia employees are calling on the company's board to bring back Jensen Huang, days after he resigned as CEO following reports of extramarital relationships, according to internal correspondence reviewed by Wired.

An internal "suggestion box" post, submitted by a longtime engineer prior to Huang's resignation, urged the board to take employee sentiment into account before acting, Wired reported. It has since drawn more than 1,600 comments, several of which asked the board to find a way to reinstate Huang. "If you believe in the mission, bring back THE boss," one anonymous commenter wrote.

The post also called on the board to share the findings of its investigation with staff and to formally seek their views on Huang continuing as CEO. One employee went further, sending a companywide email demanding transparency about Huang's exit and declaring: "I stand by Jensen."

In the hours following Huang's departure, some two dozen Nvidia employees posted supportive messages on LinkedIn, while Wired said it could not find any employee posts backing his departure. The engineer behind the suggestion box post declined to comment, and Nvidia and its new board chair, Harvey Jones, did not immediately respond to the outlet.

Huang resigned as Nvidia's president, CEO and a board member on Thursday, ending a 33-year tenure at the company he co-founded. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is serving as interim CEO, and Jones, a former CEO of Synopsys, became the board's first chair. Nvidia did not give a reason for the resignation.

Huang told staff on Sunday that he had been "unfaithful" to his wife, without naming anyone.

The New York Times had reported in October that surveillance of Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le, indicated that she had developed a "close relationship" with the Nvidia CEO. Per the Times, U.S. officials concluded she was a Chinese intelligence asset, while the Financial Times reported that a U.S. memo said the two had been romantically involved. China has denied that she is a spy.

Alice Huang was CEO and chair of Megaspeed, a Singapore-based Nvidia customer, and was investigated by Singapore police over possible false declarations relating to the company's server purchases, the country's Ministry of Home Affairs said on Thursday. She was not investigated for espionage, it said.

Police found insufficient evidence that she had committed any offenses, but concluded she had knowingly given them inaccurate information to downplay her role at Megaspeed, according to the ministry. She was issued a stern warning, left Singapore in February and was barred from returning. Investigations into Megaspeed and six other people are ongoing.

Alice Huang is now living in Beijing with the knowledge of security officials, Bloomberg reported, citing a person familiar with the matter. Attorney General Todd Blanche said on Thursday that the Justice Department had not yet concluded whether Huang was a spy, and that the FBI was investigating the reports, according to Reuters, which was unable to reach her for comment.

On Wednesday, the Times reported that Jensen Huang had also had a relationship with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of health care, who reported directly to him. Powell has not commented.

On Thursday, Reuters reported, citing two people familiar with the matter, that Huang had an intimate relationship with a second Nvidia employee, whom it did not name. Nvidia declined to comment to Reuters, and Huang and a representative for the board did not respond. Huang has not commented on the reports since his Sunday email, according to Wired.

The calls inside Nvidia echo criticism of the board's decision from tech and crypto figures. Binance co-founder Changpeng "CZ" Zhao called it a mistake for Nvidia to let Huang resign, while Elon Musk said hiring him would be "an honor."

Huang still faces scrutiny in Washington. Congressional committees have asked him to sit for a closed-door interview on December 1 and to testify at a Senate hearing on December 3, as lawmakers examine Nvidia's chip exports to China.
