‘NCIS’ Still Hasn’t Found the Perfect Replacement for Its Most Important Character

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Amanda M. Castro is a Network TV writer at Collider and a New York–based journalist whose work has appeared in Newsweek, where she is an Associate Editor, and The U.S. Sun, where she previously served as a Senior Consumer Reporter.

She specializes in network television coverage, delivering sharp, thoughtful analysis of long-running procedural hits and ambitious new dramas across broadcast TV. At Collider, Amanda explores character arcs, storytelling trends, and the cultural impact of network series that keep audiences tuning in week after week.

Born and raised in Puerto Rico, Amanda is bilingual and holds a degree in Communication, Film, and Media Studies from the University of New Haven.

NCIS has been bringing in new agents for quite some time, yet it still hasn't filled the gap left by a certain character. Cote de Pablo’s contribution as Ziva David dates back to 2013, but her departure has affected the characters around her. Even now, with Michael Weatherly back as Tony DiNozzo in Season 24, the series is finding reasons to bring her up, which is a pretty remarkable achievement for a character who hasn't been a regular cast member in more than a decade.

Ziva has had an unusual introduction to team NCIS. She arrived in the Season 3 premiere, "Kill Ari (Part 1)," after Kate Todd's (Sasha Alexander) death, and she happened to be the half-sister of the man responsible. Ari Haswari (Rudolf Martin) had murdered Kate, and Ziva was the one who ultimately shot him to save Gibbs (Mark Harmon). Understandably, nobody was thrilled to have her around at first. She had to convince an office full of suspicious colleagues that she belonged there, and the show gave her plenty of room to do exactly that.

Plenty of memorable agents have come after Ziva, and they don't need to be carbon copies of her to be worthwhile, but her departure left a specific gap for a reason. Her story connected the procedural's action to its family drama, while her relationships with Gibbs and Tony gave the series two very different emotional threads to follow.

When Ziva joined NCIS, she had already experienced more than most of her fellow employees could imagine. Her father, Eli David (Michael Nouri), headed the Mossad, and she grew up where discipline and responsibility took priority over everything else. Ziva served in the Israeli army before being recruited into the Mossad, lost her youngest sister, Tali, to an explosion, and learned how to survive in a world that demanded she be cruel.

From the start, Ziva clearly had the skills to succeed. She spoke several languages, was skilled at hand-to-hand combat, and performed well even in the most difficult situations, but what made her interesting was that she spent so many years learning to accept violence as a solution to everything that she had to learn how to live without it.

The change became especially important after her capture and torture in Somalia. Gibbs, Tony, and McGee (Sean Murray) helped bring her home in the Season 7 premiere, "Truth or Consequences," but getting her back was only part of the story. Ziva eventually resigned from Mossad and pursued a position at NCIS, choosing to build a life with people who had become her family.

She also had a personality that was entirely her own. Her difficulty with American idioms became a recurring source of comedy, particularly when Tony was around to make things worse. Ziva could be intimidating one minute and completely baffled by an expression the next. De Pablo understood that the humor made her feel like someone you actually wanted to spend time with.

The relationship between Gibbs and Ziva is one of the best examples of how much her character changed after joining NCIS. Their introduction wasn't exactly warm; Gibbs had every reason to question her loyalties, and Ziva wasn't particularly accustomed to explaining herself to anyone. Still, the circumstances surrounding Ari's death gave them a connection that neither could easily dismiss.

One of their earliest defining moments comes in "Kill Ari (Part 2)." After Ziva saves Gibbs by killing her own brother, she visits him in the hospital. When he struggles to remember what happened, she takes his hand and slaps herself with it, prompting him to remember that she saved his life. The moment ends with Ziva breaking down in his arms. It's a rare glimpse of how much the experience has affected her, especially since she's spent so much time trying to appear unaffected.

Their relationship continues to deepen in Season 7's "Good Cop, Bad Cop." After Ziva is cleared in an investigation involving the deaths aboard the Damocles, Gibbs kisses her on the forehead. It is a small gesture, but it matters to anyone with issues around her father. Eventually, Gibbs becomes the person Ziva is comfortable enough to show her vulnerability around, which is something she lacked growing up – unconditional acceptance and belonging.

This will make Season 17 especially significant. Ziva is understandably hurt that Gibbs never searched for her after she was presumed dead, and the two have to confront what that absence meant to them. They have to work through the resentment, which makes their eventual reconciliation feel earned.

When it came to their relationship, Tony and Ziva had chemistry from the moment they began working together, although neither was particularly eager to admit it. Their early dynamic was full of teasing and competitive banter, but Season 3's "Under Covers" gave viewers a memorable glimpse of what lay beneath. The pair go undercover as a couple, complete with a romantic rendezvous, and the episode helped establish the attraction that would become one of the show's longest-running storylines.

What made Tony and Ziva work wasn't constant flirting. They were colleagues who understood each other's instincts, challenged one another, and developed a loyalty that extended well beyond the job. Their relationship took years to evolve, and even when they weren't saying what they felt, the audience could usually tell.

Their eventual daughter, Tali, brought that story into a new chapter. After Ziva was presumed dead following an attack on her farmhouse, Tony left NCIS to raise their daughter. Ziva's return in Season 17 revealed she was alive, allowing the series to revisit a relationship left unresolved for years. The pair eventually reunited in Paris, and their story continued in the Paramount+ spinoff NCIS: Tony & Ziva.

The spinoff's cancellation after one season left Tony and Ziva's story in an uncertain place, but the flagship series hasn't exactly forgotten about them. Tony's return in Season 24 has brought Ziva back into the conversation, even though she isn't appearing on screen. Tony tells McGee and Nick Torres (Wilmer Valderrama) that she's in the Boston area, working at a language institute while keeping an eye on Tali, who has started boarding school nearby. He even suggests she could be available if the team needs her.

Showrunner Steven D. Binder has said Ziva won't appear in Season 24, so that update isn't confirmation of an imminent reunion. Still, it's telling that the show takes the time to establish where she is and what she's doing. Her life remains relevant to Tony's, and Tony's life remains relevant to the show.

Bishop (Emily Wickersham), Knight (Katrina Law), and the agents who followed Ziva have brought their own strengths to NCIS. The series doesn't need another Mossad operative to make the team interesting, and trying to recreate Ziva would probably be a mistake. What it hasn't found is another character whose personal history could influence so many corners of the show at once. Ziva's story was about more than becoming a better agent. It was about a woman trained to serve other people's expectations finally deciding what she wanted for herself. She found a family in the people who once questioned whether she belonged, and built a future that looked nothing like the one her father had planned. NCIS can fill another desk, but it can't manufacture that history overnight, and judging by how often the show still brings Ziva into the conversation, it hasn't stopped trying to find a way to bring her back.

If Parks and Recreation and The Office have one thing in common, it's that both series took a little while to find their footing. Of course, these two comedies have more than one thing in common -- both aired on NBC around the same time, both were created (or co-created in Parks & Rec's case, alongside Michael Schur) by Greg Daniels, and both debuted with big expectations from fans of an existing series. It's no surprise that Amy Poehler would find some very common ground with Steve Carell when talking about their experiences on their respective shows.

Not all of the best sitcoms of all time got off to an amazing start. The Office and Parks and Recreation are two perfect examples of shows that needed more than a few episodes to find their groove. In March, back when Steve Carell's new HBO series Rooster was just getting started, the Office star sat down with Amy Poehler on her Good Hang podcast. Among the topics that came up was how poorly their earlier sitcoms were initially received by viewers.

As Poehler tells it, there are two people that she's been told she's a "poor man's version of," and Carell is one of them. She went on to laugh about how terribly the debut of Parks and Recreation went, saying:

Parks and Rec had the worst launch ever. Everyone was like, 'This is not The Office, we don't like this.' I just remembered we're like -- 'You're not Steve and you're not -- we don't like it!' And I think I just dissociated and was like, 'well.'"

That viewers might have been looking for more of what they'd come to know and love from The Office -- and Carell's character, Michael Scott -- is not that surprising, given its creators and the fact that it was another single-camera mockumentary-style workplace comedy. In the case of Parks and Rec, the focus was on the office workers at the Parks and Recreation department of a small town in Indiana. That included Poehler's optimistic and determined Leslie Knope.

When Greg Daniels and Michael Schur's new series debuted in spring of 2009, it was around the same time that The Office was wrapping up its fifth season. Months prior to Parks and Rec's debut, The Office had its post-Super Bowl double episode, "Stress Relief," which was watched by millions (and is remembered as one of the best episodes of the series). The Office was firmly established with its fanbase, while Parks and Rec was just getting warmed up.

It's almost easy to forget that The Office had faced a similar challenge years earlier, as Carell noted in his conversation with Poehler:

Our pilot was the lowest casting pilot in the history, I think, of NBC. People really hated it ... They actively hated this show, and I don't quite know how it got legs after that.

Lest we not forget, The Office didn't debut to universal praise and sky-high ratings when it arrived in 2005, and it was nearly cancelled after its first season. The show arrived on the heels of the success of the 2001 Ricky Gervais sitcom that started it all, and to say that the U.S. version of the beloved UK comedy had big shoes to fill would be an understatement.

Personally, I didn't hate the first season of the U.S. version of The Office when it aired, but I also wouldn't argue with many of the criticisms of the show's first six episodes. That's a whole other conversation though. Getting back to the topic at hand, in his conversation with Amy Poehler, Carell called himself out as "a poor man's Ricky Gervais."

I don't think anyone would say that about Carell today, but he certainly had a high bar to meet when leading the U.S. version of The Office. And from what he said, he intentionally didn't watch more than a minute of Gervais in Office:

I watched like a minute of him, and he was so good, and so specific, and so funny, I thought, if I watch a second more, I'm just going to go on an audition with that. I won't be able to even imagine it a different way.

There really is only one David Brent, so it's understandable that Carell wouldn't want to try to recreate that character in Michael Scott. Ultimately, Carell's character became iconic in his own right, thanks in large part to the actor's comedic brilliance. Several seasons after he made his debut as Michael Scott, Carell had a say in how his character would exit the series.

All of this to say, I can see why Carell and Poehler would connect on this particular topic, as both of them saw success in their respective roles and shows, but it wasn't instant and both had to find their way out from under the shadow of other performances. Each show had to find their own way to success, and it's just really fortunate that both were given time and seasons to do so. I can't imagine a TV world without Michael Scott or Leslie Knope!

‘Summer School, 2001,’ the First Czech-Vietnamese Feature, Is Coming to America via Cinemas, Amazon (Exclusive)

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Two diaspora groups can hit Cinemark theaters from Dec. 11 for a limited run of the award-winning debut feature directed by Dužan Duong, courtesy of Reel Citrus, before two big studio tentpole releases hit.

In 2025, Summer School, 2001, the debut feature of writer-director Dužan Duong and dubbed the “first Czech-Vietnamese feature film,” made a splash at the Karlovy Vary International Film Festival and beyond. Now, it is coming to America, courtesy of Reel Citrus, an L.A.-based distributor of Asian and diaspora films.

The company will open Summer School, 2001 in a limited U.S. theatrical release on Dec. 11 at Cinemark cinemas, hitting screens before Dune 3 and Avengers: Doomsday opens a week later. The film will then stream on Amazon from Dec. 18 before rolling out globally. “At a time when immigration and belonging are front-page news in both the U.S. and Europe, the release brings two diasporas into the same theater,” said Reel Citrus.

Summer School, 2001 follows 17-year-old Kien, who returns to his family’s market stall in Cheb, a small town on the Czech border, after spending 10 years in Vietnam. It becomes a chaotic summer, during which the family must try to become whole again.

After its Karlovy Vary world premiere, the movie drew more than 20,000 moviegoers during its run in Czech cinemas. At the Czech Lion Awards, the Czech national film honors, Summer School, 2001 won the best screenplay award for Duong, Jan Smutný and Lukáš Kokeš, along with the best supporting actor honor for Dũng Nguyễn, the first Vietnamese actor to win a Czech Lion. The Czech Film Critics’ Awards named it the best screenplay and the Discovery of the Year.

At Viet Film Fest, the largest U.S. festival for the Vietnamese diaspora, the film won the Bronze Drum (Trống Đồng) Award for the best feature film, the best actor honor for Tô Tiến Tài and the Audience Choice Award for best feature.

Reel Citrus says it chose the theaters to target the communities that the film, which is in Vietnamese and Czech with English subtitles, speaks to. Most of the cinemas are in areas with large Vietnamese American populations, including Southern California, where it plays in Huntington Beach next to Little Saigon, the Bay Area, Houston, Dallas, Seattle, Las Vegas and the Washington, D.C. area. The rest of the theaters are in Chicago, Cleveland, Pittsburgh and Central Texas, “home to strong Czech, Polish and other Central and Eastern European communities,” the company explains.

Vietnamese American families and Czech and Polish moviegoers came out in droves for a screening of Summer School, 2001 at the Avalon Theatre in Washington, D.C. last March. Co-presented with the Embassy of the Czech Republic, it served as an early test for Reel Citrus, which says it releases films “in person, one community at a time.”

Reel Citrus acquired North American rights across all media, along with worldwide streaming rights, to Summer School, 2001 from AZN kru.

“Summer School, 2001 was an eye-opening film for us,” said Brittany Tran, founder & managing director of Reel Citrus. “Many of us grew up knowing the Vietnamese American story, but we knew far less about the Vietnamese families who built their lives in the Czech Republic and across Europe. Our diaspora is global, and when Vietnamese communities in the U.S. see the communities in Europe, we realize how much we share.”

She added: “We know we’re opening the week before Dune and Avengers, so we’re competing with the gods. But audiences who rarely see their own stories on the big screen are exactly who this release is for.”

And Duong, who talked to THR about his creative vision for Summer School, 2001 and his other creative work, shared: “Vietnamese families have been part of Czech life for decades, but our stories rarely reach the screen. I made this film for my community in Cheb and for every family that has had to find its way back to each other. Seeing it open in American theaters, in front of Vietnamese and Czech audiences together, means more than I can say.”

M. Night Shyamalan's The Visit: Now Free on YouTube

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Just don't leave your room after 9:30 P.M.

Though Halloween is over, that doesn't mean that horror movies need to be put on the shelf until next year. In fact, it could even be argued that November is a perfect time to continue watching them. Writer/director M. Night Shyamalan has no shortage of horror films that can work in the chilly, dreary days of November, such as 2004's The Village. But an underrated 2015 found-footage horror film manages to cement itself in Shyamalan's filmography as one of his greatest unexpected achievements.

The Visit was released in 2015, following a downward streak in Shyamalan's career. During this time, found footage was experiencing a boom, so Shyamalan's decision to capitalize on that public interest made sense, even if that wasn't the kind of horror film one would expect from him. It grossed $98.5 million worldwide on a $5 million budget, and it received widespread praise from critics. However, it seemed to slowly fade from public consciousness — now, eleven years later, there is a chance to revisit The Visit.

The Visit stars Olivia DeJonge and Ed Oxenbould as siblings Becca and Tyler. When their recently-divorced mother Loretta (Kathryn Hahn) goes on a five-day cruise, she leaves the kids in the care of her estranged grandparents, whom she hasn't spoken to in fifteen years because they disapproved of her marriage to her high school teacher. Becca and Tyler decide to use the opportunity to make a documentary of the visit with a handheld camcorder.

Nana (Deanna Dunagan) and Pop Pop (Peter McRobbie) meet Becca and Tyler at the train station. Initially, it seems as if everything is fine. They even wonder why their mom disliked them so much. Once they get to the house, though, they are told to never leave their room after 9:30 P.M. and to never go into the basement. This immediately sets the children on edge and creates the simmering tension that makes up the film.

Quickly, Nana and Pop Pop go from pleasant to unsettling. Each time Becca and Tyler witness something — especially at night — it carries a foreboding, sickening sense of anticipation. Pop Pop attacks a man that he thinks is following them, and Nana chases the children during a game of hide-and-seek. Tyler finds soiled diapers in the shed, and Becca sees Nana vomiting violently in the downstairs hallway. When challenged, the grandparents are each dismissive of the behavior of the other. As things begin to escalate, Becca and Tyler are on their own as they try to figure out what to do with two people they barely even know.

The film drew praise not just for its scary moments, but for the moments where it lets itself have a laugh or two. The terror it draws from lies within the everyday, which is a perfect setting for a director like Shyamalan, whose twists have become an integral part of his filmmaking signature. A single-house setting with limited boundaries adds claustrophobia and helps The Visit stand out from its peers.

With The Visit streaming for free on YouTube, it's the perfect time to give this film a chance and see for yourself what, exactly, is going on with Nana and Pop Pop.

If I Could Buy Just 1 Stock in November and Hold for 5 Years, It Would Be Alphabet

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Alphabet is already one of the world's most powerful technology companies. With AI creating another major growth opportunity, this could be a stock worth holding through 2031.

If I could buy just one stock this month and hold it for the long term, Alphabet (GOOGL +1.18%) (GOOG +0.73%) would be at the top of my list.

The reason is simple: I believe artificial intelligence (AI) could become one of the biggest investment opportunities of the next decade, and Alphabet is positioned to benefit from it in several ways.

But unlike some companies that depend almost entirely on AI spending, Alphabet already owns highly profitable businesses that generate billions of dollars in revenue.

That combination makes it an attractive long-term candidate.

Alphabet is much more than Google Search. It owns YouTube, Google Cloud, Android, and a range of widely used digital services. These businesses give it access to billions of users and provide multiple ways to monetize AI.

That Alphabet has multiple ways to win in the years to come is an important advantage.

Unlike most AI investments, Alphabet's investment case isn't based entirely on future promises. Even without including future opportunities, Alphabet is already a massive, profitable business.

For perspective, in the third quarter of 2026, revenue increased 25% year over year to $127.6 billion. Operating income rose 30% to $40.6 billion.

Google Cloud was particularly impressive, with revenue more than doubling to $30.7 billion. Its operating income also increased to $11.1 billion from $3.6 billion a year earlier. Meanwhile, Google Search revenue grew 12%.

These numbers demonstrate something important: Alphabet's AI opportunity is already translating into meaningful business growth, particularly in cloud computing.

Better still, its existing profitable businesses also provide a foundation to fund further investment in AI. That diversification matters over five years. If one part of the AI market disappoints, Alphabet still has other businesses capable of generating revenue and profits.

There is, however, a reason investors should not overlook the risks. Building AI infrastructure is expensive. Alphabet is investing heavily in data centers, servers, networking equipment, and other computing resources. In Q3 alone,capital expenditure (capex) reached $51.4 billion. That was enough to push quarterly free cash flow into negative territory, at $9.1 billion.

Management expects full-year 2026 capex of $195 billion to $205 billion, with spending increasing significantly again in 2027.

This is a substantial commitment. If AI demand continues growing and these investments generate attractive returns, the spending could strengthen Alphabet's competitive position for years. But if demand disappoints or computing capacity becomes less valuable than expected, the company could end up with expensive infrastructure that takes longer to pay off.

There is also a competitive risk. Companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic are challenging Google's position in AI. Alphabet has the resources to compete, but it is not guaranteed to win every battle.

For me, the attraction to Alphabet is the combination of an established business and a significant new growth opportunity. Alphabet already has products used by billions of people. It has a profitable advertising business, a rapidly growing cloud division, proprietary AI models, and the infrastructure to integrate these technologies.

Over the next five years, I would watch three things closely: whether Search maintains its competitive position, whether Google Cloud continues growing profitably, and whether AI investments translate into stronger free cash flow.

If it delivers on all three areas, the stock is well positioned to create enormous shareholder value in the next five years.

In The Ogre’s Mouth — John Muir Trail Day 12

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

I woke up in my little tent spot nice and early, around 5:30 a.m., and then it took me two hours to get on the trail.  During that time the sun appeared to light the mountain on the opposite side of the valley.

Early morning view from my camp site. I needed Geologist Sam to explain the stripes in the rock…

This time the biggest delay was that the bottom of my tent was quite damp, so I spread it out to dry before packing it and heading on my way.

While I was doing all that, along came Sally who greeted me warmly and then walked on by.  Here’s a little more information about Sally that I learned the previous day:  she was born in Canton, and she works in medical research.  The rest I already reported.

By 7:30 a.m. I started off and immediately started down the infamous Golden Staircase.  I could see how it got its name.  Oh my gosh, it was just a straight down drop of short switchbacks back and forth, and back and forth, and back and forth, seemingly endlessly.  It was enough to make your ears pop from the altitude change.  Going down was bad enough, but I really felt sorry for the poor people trying to ascend it!  I could imagine how it might be demoralizing and exhausting as well.

It’s a little hard to see, but there is a trail going down that near-vertical descent!

Finally, I got to the bottom and eventually I even caught up with Sally.  We stopped for a water break together and then I went past her onto what was the nicest, cruisiest stretch since I started the John Muir Trail.

After the river’s steep drop coming down, the landscape leveled out and the trail mostly followed the gentle contour of the river heading due west.  As a smooth quality trail, it meant I could make very good time.  In the lower elevation the lizards reappeared, and I finally got some pictures of them.

As a child in Mexico City I bred lizards, so I have a special place in my heart for them!

I also  took a picture of a deer, some very large pine cones, as well as shots of several waterfalls.

It feels like somehow they know I am not a predator…

Lower elevations also brought much bigger pine cones.

Mostly, though, I kept a fast pace for about seven miles down the Palisade Creek valley to the river juncture with the Middle Fork Kings River, where the trail turned sharply north and started climbing again.

I reached that juncture at about 10:30 where I rested and had a snack.  Along with the food I took my last dose of antibiotics from the hospital.  I had to admit the medication had been highly effective, but not perfectly so.  I slept through the night now but still coughed occasionally.  I was easily 95% better though and grateful for it!

This was also the spot for errands including filtering water, filling my storage containers, and washing my socks and bandanas.  It was a little challenging to do this as the river was flowing swiftly, but I got the errands done.

My pack resting against a log. (I was resting too…)

As I took stock afterwards, however, I came up one sock liner short and assumed it had washed down the river.  Rather than accept its loss, I went back and looked for it.  To my pleasant surprise, I found I had unwittingly dropped it on the trail before washing.  So, I returned to wash it, then brought it back to its lonely mate.

Looking at those sock liners, I was disappointed.  In the past I considered them indestructible, but now I saw holes forming around the toes and elsewhere, apparently from chafing.  They were still worth using, but I realized they would not endure beyond the end of the hike at best.

Sally arrived at the juncture shortly before I departed.  Today it seemed we were tag-teaming more than we were leapfrogging, but I enjoyed a snack with her and directed her to the water collection site before hoisting my pack.

As I started going upriver, I realized I was at 8,000 feet, my lowest point so far on the JMT northbound route.  The next pass, John Muir Pass, would be almost 4,000 feet higher.  I resolved to try to get more than half of that elevation gain on this day, before making camp.

I was pretty sure I could identify the 10,000-foot contour.  In the Sierras, open fires are not allowed above 10,000 feet.  For this reason, it seemed every time we crossed that elevation there would be a sign flagging the change in fire status.  Most hikers would thereby know whether they were above or below 10,000 feet.

This shot was actually taken on a different day, but it captures what I was talking about, so I inserted it anyway.

As I ascended slowly, I eventually reached the turnoff to Bishop Pass, an indicator that I had gone ten miles already.  I was feeling pretty strong.   Without resting, I drank some water to prevent dehydration and pressed on.  It was a boost of confidence to reach the point I intended to stop, and to realize I still had energy for several miles more.

At a bridge over the stream I looked down and saw three little fishes swimming in an eddy of the stream.  I began to sing “Three little fishies in an itty bitty pool…”  You know that one?  First recorded in 1939, so maybe not.  At that moment a hiking couple arrived and no doubt thought I was completely insane singing to the fishes!

Two of the three little fishies… the third was camera-shy.  I am fairly confident they are Golden Trout!

I continued climbing and climbing through woods that changed as I went higher up.  For a while a number of thorny bushes lined the trail.  In my shorts this was uncomfortable; though they didn’t actually break the skin, they left many surface scratches.

I stopped to fill up my water bottle again at an ideal camp site.  While tempted to stay there, I decided it was still too early to pitch my tent.  Just as I was leaving, a guy came and asked if I’d take his picture on his phone.  I looked across the river and saw a stunning mountain.  “How cool,”  I thought, “I’ll crouch really low, aiming upward, and it will make a great picture with this giant crag looming behind him!”

That was not his intention at all.  It turned out I had not realized it, but I stopped at an iconic trail location.   The man moved toward a giant boulder, and then I saw it.  It was a face!  A face naturally created  by the geology of the place, but with a huge gaping ogre’s mouth.  In that yawning maw, people had placed a row of small triangular stones to represent teeth.  They added a couple on top of his head as silly little ears.

The man entered the “mouth” and lay down between the “teeth.”  He said his girlfriend back in France had taken her picture there several years before, so now I took his picture to send back to her.   Of course, having seen “The Thing” I had to ask him to reciprocate with a picture of me!  How funny that I almost left the site without even noticing it…

The forest was reduced to occasional scraggly trees as I hiked higher, passing more waterfalls as the slope became steeper.

Waterfall catching the sunlight, with the wildflowers in front? Lovely!

Shortly after passing the symbolic 10,000-foot sign (“No Fires!”) I came to a lush meadow.  It looked like a perfect camping area, and I found what seemed like an ideal spot off the trail.

I dropped my pack and turned off my AllTrails app and prepared to settle in.  Then I realized the meadow was flat and green and there were no logs or rocks for me to sit on.  I thought, “Let me wander around here a little nearby.  I’m sure I can find a nice big stone to carry over by my tent to sit on.”  Indeed, I found just such a stone.  But when I picked it up, I found under the stone was… toilet paper!  Ugh!

I put it back down in the same spot quickly and reassessed my decision.  Suddenly that place felt like bad karma, so I turned AllTrails back on again, shouldered my pack, and continued looking for a place to spend the night.

It was late afternoon already, and I worried that the camp sites higher up might all be full.  I decided to poll the descending southbound hikers passing me to find out what I might expect.  My question was simple and uniform:  “How many people did you encounter higher up who had already made camp there?”

The first person who came by answered, “One” and I was relieved.  But the second person who came along answered, “Six.”  I grew concerned.  Finally, the third downhill hikers were two women; they looked like mother and daughter.  To my question, the mother answered, “Fifteen, I think.”   Fifteen!  Then, to my relief, the daughter chimed in, “Oh no, I don’t think so, maybe five?”

This was a quasi-scientific experiment proving how totally unreliable we humans are at reporting anything.  Yet it left me with the same dilemma.  Then the daughter continued, “there’s a pretty site just up ahead and there still seemed to be room there.”

I looked on the AllTrails app, and it did show a spot nearby with four tent sites.   I figured, “Well, even if there are a couple of people there, I don’t mind sharing  and it will give me a little extra elevation and a little extra distance that I won’t have to take on tomorrow.”

Upward I trudged.  Soon, there was the spot with three tents already pitched, but space for a fourth about twenty yards apart from them.  The owners of the tents seemed to be friends, or maybe a family, as they had camp chairs and were all playing cards together.

Late afternoon arrival, with the sky still a deep blue!

I took my spot and pitched my tent, feeling very tired.  As I took off my trail runners and socks, I realized my feet were well beyond filthy.  I wanted a bed and a shower; not necessarily right away, but soon.

That was when I broke down and texted my wife on my Garmin.  It was something like:  “If you can get me a cabin or a room at the John Muir Ranch for day-after-tomorrow, I will be very grateful.  It is probably all booked up, but just in case, please give it a try.”

It was deep twilight by the time I soaked my dinner and dug my cathole.  I wolfed down my repast and called it a night.  It felt like it might prove to be a cold one.

Personal Finance (Not Investing) • Re: Testamentary Trust - Making sure money goes to kids, not a new spouse

I cannot help you but maybe my anecdote can provide some insight as a cautionary tail.

My father, 3 children including myself, and stepmother, 2 children, were married 30+ years. It was a good marriage and I'm glad he found her.

The agreement, as stated by them dozens if not hundreds of times over the years, was that any inheritance would be split between the two families equally. 50% to her children, 50% to his children. A few years ago they paid for a grantor trust, primarily to avoid probate in California, and retitled their wealth into the trust with the exception of a few outlying annuities and such that just hadn't been cleaned up yet. The current wealth level is about 3.5 to 4 million.

She is now in the early stages of dementia and he passed from cancer about 6 months ago. We didn't discover until after his death how much he had been covering for her. As the annuities needed to be handled, and new paperwork filled out, it's become clear that she no longer understands the process or what it's supposed to do. When filling out paperwork, like car titles or beneficiaries on annuities, she is unwilling to put the trust. She wants to know why she can't leave her kids something, she says it makes her feel good, and we try to explain that the trust puts everything in a big pot and they'll get half when she passes but she doesn't absorb it. The effect of this is that she's slowly but surely been removing things from the trust and putting her own children as the direct beneficiaries. In the next few years she'll end up selling their real estate to go into care of one sort or another and move it into other accounts outside the trust. The major cash accounts are now being 'handled' by her daughters husband who is sure he can 'grow it'. It's likely that there will be little, if anything, actually distributed to his kids.

I want to be really clear that this is NOT her fault. There is no one to blame here. She has become unable to process higher level accounting and administration but not yet at a point where we can reasonably stop her or get her to accept input. We cannot protect her against herself at this stage, much less ourselves.

While I am well off, my two siblings and one her children as well could really have used the financial help while he was still alive. By delaying until after his death, he's guaranteed they will receive no help at all. I urge you to give with a warm hand, which is the only way to guarantee that the assistance goes to where you want it to go. If you cannot bring yourself to do that, then I also urge you to protect the inheritance against yourself. As we get older, there is no reason to believe that we'll be able to understand what we have put in place 20 years before. Good luck.
You have a more generous spirit than I do.

It seems that there is room here for your stepmother’s children to step in and stop their mother from effectively disinheriting you & your siblings. Especially given that they must know their mother is losing her cognitive abilities.

This situation may not be her fault, but her kids aren’t blameless.

I’m sorry you are going through this.
