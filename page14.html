‘The Body’ Star Kristina Bogic Just Wanted to Take Care of Anya: “A Lot of Young Women Can Relate”

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The 19-year-old star of Netflix's latest hit breaks down how she came to love her dance team captain character: "Anya always wants to try and do the right thing."

The Body team searched far and wide to find their leading lady, Anya. Kristina Bogic, the 19-year-old who landed the role, knew there were plenty of others trying for the part. “A lot of my friends had auditioned for Anya,” she tells The Hollywood Reporter.

By the time the audition for The Body came her way, Bogic had faced her fair share of rejection, as any actor would. She’d had “hundreds and hundreds” of auditions before, so she had a feeling that there was no way it could be her. Newly 18 at the time, the actress just decided to go for it. And it worked out. Related Stories Business Netflix Buys Radford Studio Lot for $380 Million TV Kevin Kline Boards 'The Corrections' at Netflix

Bogic did four chemistry reads with Sara Boustany, who plays Anya’s best friend and secret romantic interest Elise in the series. The actress had “a bunch” of callbacks — she just kept getting more scripts. “I remember thinking even if I wasn’t in this show, I would just be so excited to watch it,” she says.

The raunchy psychological thriller has become an online favorite. Bogic felt protective over the character from moment one. “I can’t believe I get to play Anya,” the actress says. “I really just wanted to take care of her the whole time.”

Anya’s the captain of her Catholic high school’s dance team. In addition to mass hysteria incidents, the teen’s also dealing with complicated feelings for her best friend. Bogic wanted to keep the character tucked safely under her wing.

“I’m so happy I got to explore her warmth and also her dark sides as we,” she says. “A lot of young women can relate to Anya.”

Below, Bogic digs into finding the character, her relationship with her co-stars and what she thinks happened in that season finale.

What was the hardest part in trying to find Anya?

I remember in the table reads, I was still trying to find the essence of her and who Anya was. We had eight weeks of dance training. I did some acting classes, and I did four hours of dialect coaching every week. These women on this set were so amazing at improv as well. They’re so funny. It came to them so naturally. That’s something I’ve never felt like I’ve struggled with, but on top of the accent, it was like I just wanted to feel so settled in it that I could experiment with whatever.

The moment I cracked Anya was when I had the conversation with Lisbeth at the fire pit. I remember Quinn [Shepard] saying she wants this essence of this cool girl. She’s popular. She’s the team captain. There’s a level of dominance there, but also there’s a level of softness. I think she still has this innocence of the world and this naivety to her, which I think I wanted to protect in this season. It starts getting stripped away from her, from her bad decisions. I do think Anya always wants to try and do the right thing.

Your character’s relationship with Sara’s character is a driving force of the show. How did you find that dynamic?

It was very easy, honestly. The first time we spoke, we had maybe 10 minutes just to chat on Zoom before we did the audition, and we just clicked. We were both equally as excited and passionate about the show, which I don’t know if it’s rare, but I’ve never had that experience with someone on set before. We were all so passionate and so excited to be there. We were so grateful and tried to take in every single moment of it. I think that also just came into our characters as well.

[Anya] and Elise are cheeky. They’re fun. I love that we kind of played [it] off as like, we’re just girls. This is just what girls do. Girls hold each other’s hands. Girls do this. We’re just joking around. Then you realize there’s obviously so much going on underneath. I feel like our chemistry was honestly quite instant. Even just as friends, me and her, we still talk all the time. Our cast is just very close in general.

What was your dancing like when you took this on?

I hadn’t danced in five years before this. And I remember Quinn, after I did one of the chemistry reads with Sara, Quinn asked me to a one-minute improvised dance video. I remember thinking I was just going to film it quickly. I was in a hotel in Italy. I went outside and I was like, “All right, let me just see if I can throw something together. Let me move my body.” I was so awkward. I felt like when a baby deer is just born or a baby horse and they’re wobbling, that’s how I felt. I didn’t realize how important it is to know how your body moves.

It gave me extra confidence. That’s also how I found Anya, was I finally felt comfortable in my body. I knew how it moved and how it flowed. It tapped me into Anya’s femininity, which she really values and uses as a form of currency in her life. We had eight weeks of this bootcamp with Ryan Heffington. He was so amazing. He was so patient with all of us. We’re professional, but we’re also little frat boys on set, that’s how we act. We’re just roughhousing all the time and constantly chatting and asking questions. It was a lot of energy, and he was just so amazing. I’m so grateful that we had him for those weeks.

Did most of you not have a background in dancing? Was it kind of fresh for a lot of people?

No, honestly. I walked in [and] everyone’s a pro-level dancer. I walked into watching Emily Roman doing a front aerial. Everyone had a bit of background in dance, but we all caught onto it very quickly.

What was it like to work with these women

I think we were so lucky with this cast. Can you imagine just putting 12 girls in a room and saying now you all have to pretend to be friends? Not for one second did we ever feel like it was forced. I love every one of those girls to death, genuinely. It was so fun just seeing everyone take their character off the page. We were all there for each other, and everyone was so amazing. They completely made their character their own. There was not one sense of ego on set, not one sense of competition. We really all felt like it was very collaborative and very supportive. I remember telling this to someone in my life and they were like, “Oh, it’s so rare that 12 girls are going to get together. I can imagine the drama.” We had the least amount of drama ever. We were so chill. We literally got to hang out and play dress-up with our friends all day.

Can you walk me through where Anya’s at in that first episode?

She’s at a very desperate and vulnerable place. She’s in fight-or-flight, and I think she also. It’s a common theme for her to repress a lot of things. Whether it’s her sexuality or it’s what’s happened with Grace, what’s going on with Steven, her issues with sex. She’s always repressing something.Even the way she presents herself, she’s like, “If I look different, people are going to know something’s wrong. I can’t hang out with Elyse. She’s going to know something’s wrong.” Everything is just pushed down and suppressed. Me and Anya relate to each other a little bit in this way of things snowball and then we just fall apart a little bit. She’s just in a desperate state, honestly.

What is that moment for Anya in the show that you find to be that moment of falling apart?

She has a little one in episode two, when she sees Grace outside the strip club. But that scene is actually so much funnier than I thought it would. I took it there because I thought it was going to be really dark and sad. I watch it and I’m laughing. They’re so hysterical. It’s so ridiculous and so funny. But episode eight for sure. It’s the breaking point for her. Everything’s just come out, at least within a circle, and she’s been faced with everything. She can’t push anything down anymore. Definitely episode eight.

At the end, do you think she’s confessing because she’s feeling guilty or do you think she’s feeling like she can push off what’s actually happening again?

I think this is genuinely how she’s feeling. When she confesses… I think the reason why she can’t admit a lot of things is because of this Catholic guilt and this shame. She always wants to do the right thing, so I think when it comes down to it — although maybe it’s the wrong execution sometimes — it’s almost like she had lost this innocence and this softness to her. That’s really what she’s asking for, she wants herself back — I want my life back. Nothing is going to be the same after this. How could it? That’s really the plea that she’s making, deep down, is I want my life back. I just want everything to go back to normal. I wish I could just repress this. Once Grace wakes up, she’s like, “Oh my fucking God, it’s coming for me. There’s no outrightness anymore,” and I think she just has to face what she’s done.

Acer Predator Atlas 8 review: Potent performance at a slightly better price

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Thanks to its Intel Arc G3 Extreme chip, you get top-notch graphics for $350 less than its closest rival.

Modern handheld PCs started out as complimentary devices, compact systems designed to help you game on the go when you didn't have access to your beefy setup at home. But as the category has matured, we've seen manufacturers pursue higher tiers of performance. This culminated most recently in systems like MSI's Claw 8 EX AI+, which delivers serious graphics power along with an equally serious price of $1,800. But now, Acer is trying to undercut the current framerate champ with the Predator Atlas 8, equipping it with the exact same chip used in MSI's machine, but for $350 less. The result is a compelling package that delivers on many of the same highs while easing the burden on your wallet.

With the Predator Atlas 8, Acer hasn't strayed too far from the basic template we've seen on so many other portable PCs. The middle of the system is dominated by a large 8-inch LCD TFT display with a 120Hz refresh and respectable 500-nit brightness. On the outside you get TMR analog sticks along with an Xbox-style button layout. I must admit I'm not a huge fan of Acer's circular D-Pad as it's a bit mushy and doesn't feel super precise. But if you're not playing fighting games or similar titles, it's more than serviceable for navigating menus. And if I'm really nitpicking, colors from the Predator's TFT screen aren't quite as rich as what you get from MSI's machine, but it's the kind of difference that you only really notice when you look at the two systems side by side.

Around back, Acer included a pair of rear paddles, two shoulder buttons and analog Hall-effect triggers with plenty of travel — the latter of which are great for racing games. Along the top edge of the system you get all of the Predator Atlas 8's ports, which includes dual USB-C jacks with support for Thunderbolt 4, a microSD card reader for expandable storage, a 3.5mm audio jack and a power button with a built-in fingerprint reader.

All told, it's a pretty standard setup, though the Predator does lack a USB-A port like you get on the MSI Claw 8 EX AI+. I also noticed that while Acer's dual two-watt speakers can get surprisingly loud, audio becomes distorted at higher volumes (above 50 TK) that results in a crackly or crunchy sound. That said, the speakers are so punchy, I found that I never really needed to turn them up that high. Unless you're trying to DJ a house party with this thing, you might not even notice.

The real star of the Atlas 8 is its Intel Arc G3 Extreme processor, which is the exact same chip used in the MSI Claw 8 EX AI+. The main difference is that Acer's handheld comes with 512GB of storage instead of 1TB and tops out with 24GB of shared memory (compared to 32GB on the MSI). This discrepancy almost entirely accounts for the price gap between the two systems. But for people striving for top-notch performance while trying to save a few bucks, the framerate delta in games isn't as pronounced as you might think.

When I tested this out in Cyberpunk 2077 at 35 watts on FHD, medium graphics and XeSS set to performance, the Predator Atlas 8 hit 88 fps, which is barely more than five percent lower than the 95 fps I got from the Claw 8 EX AI+. But it's important to note that this was using Acer's highest performance mode. When I set the system to 15 watts (which you might do when playing on battery only), the Predator and the Claw were essentially dead even in Returnal at FHD and medium graphics (60 fps for the Acer vs 59 fps for the MSI). This means that if you're trying to decide between the two, you really need to consider how you plan on using your handheld and what types of games you'll be playing.

Similar to other high-end Windows handhelds, the Atlas 8 comes with Microsoft's Xbox experience. This software allows you to boot into a dedicated UI for gaming instead of needing to deal with the traditional desktop OS. This also makes it super easy to download all of the most popular launchers and storefronts like Epic, Steam, Battle.net and more, and because it's Windows underneath, you get better compatibility than what you get from SteamOS-powered portables. That said, if you're the type to travel with a portable monitor, keyboard and mouse, you can still switch over to the classic Windows interface to get some work done too.

The Atlas 8 is powered by an 80WHr battery that provides ample longevity, though it can vary quite a bit depending on what you're playing. However, without even doing anything to boost its longevity, I was able to play World of Warcraft: Forever at FHD on medium settings for an hour and 45 minutes on a single charge while still keeping frame rates consistently above 90. On the flipside, when I played a less demanding title like Warhammer Survivors, Acer's machine had more battery life than I knew what to do with: It lasted for more than three hours before needing to be plugged in. Notably, both of those games were played using the handheld's 30-watt Balanced performance mode, so if you're not afraid to mess around with its settings, there's a lot of room to tweak this thing to get even longer untethered gaming sessions.

I'm still not sure how I feel about PC handhelds that cost well above $1,000. For me, that's just a ton of money to drop on a system that can't multitask as well as a similarly priced gaming laptop. However, for mobile gaming enthusiasts trying to find the sweet spot between price, performance and portability, Acer has carved out an interesting niche with the Predator Atlas 8.

At $1,450, it costs $350 less than the MSI Claw 8 EX AI+, while sporting the exact same processor and delivering essentially 95 percent of the performance. That said, Acer's more approachable pricing leaves the Predator with a bit less RAM and storage, though having a microSD card slot provides a workaround for the latter. There are also some minor differences, like the lack of a USB-A port, a slightly less colorful TFT display and speakers that sound a bit wonky at high volumes. But these shortcomings could very well be a worthwhile trade-off for anyone looking for peak handheld gaming performance without completely fragging their budget.

Has vibe coding made open source the new normal for synths?

Companies like Akai have decided to embrace the modding community rather than resist vibe coders.

The emergence of powerful vibe-coding agents and tools like Reverse Engineer Anything has caused chaos in the music gear world, leaving companies scrambling to figure out how to respond. While some, like Roland, have remained silent for now, others have embraced the modding community. In the last several weeks, as it’s become clear that no piece of music gear is safe from Claude and a subreddit full of curious tinkerers, companies have begun making more and more of their devices open source.

The biggest name to decide “if you can’t beat ’em, join ’em” is inMusic, the parent company of Akai, Moog, Native Instruments, and more. “We’re going to provide a toolbox to the community, we’re going to provide the inner workings of, say, an MPC or Sample so that people can do what they want,” inMusic CEO Jack O’Donnell tells The Verge. That includes a recently launched SDK and several open-source devices, including the recently teased MPC Sample Key.

Akai is trying to walk a fine line, offering modders access while keeping its products reliable for professionals. O’Donnell says the company will “try to give them a reset button” so “they can experiment with it, and then not lose what they have.”

Behringer has also rushed to cash in, announcing a slew of open-source devices. In just a matter of weeks, it announced a $150 clone of the MPC One called Kick, a clone of the E-mu SP-1200, the Anima DJ controller, the Ignite sequencer, and the Polykeys 61 MIDI controller, all of which are open source. It’s unclear when any of these products might launch.

M-Vave’s FM-1 helped kick-start the whole movement. The $70 pocketable clone of the iconic Yamaha DX7 became the synth of the summer not just because of its dirt-cheap price and faithfully re-created ’80s tones, but because it also turned out to be infinitely hackable. There are well over a dozen different custom firmwares for the FM-1. M-Vave responded by releasing an open-source firmware with more features for developers to build on. The company also confirmed that a successor, the FM-2, is in development and will be open source as well.

Other companies have also embraced the modding community. Erica Synths recently released the source code for its LXR-02 drum machine, but founder Ģirts Ozoliņš isn’t necessarily fully on board with vibe coding. “Inventions succeed if they solve some fundamental problems, right?” he tells The Verge. “So, I’m still wondering what fundamental problem vibe coding solves.”

“We’re going to provide a toolbox to the community.”

While he can’t imagine a producer simply stacking his studio with a bunch of hacked FM-1s running various synth engines, he’s curious to see what the community cooks up for the LXR. And he does see a benefit in making music tech a little less disposable. “What I like on the upside of this is that there is some trace of sustainability. Instead of buying five or 10 plastic boxes, you can try options on one,” he says, though he’s under no illusions about the environmental impact of vibe coding. “Of course it by far does not offset data center impact on the planet and on ecology and stuff like that.”

Polyend has also gotten on board, open-sourcing its line of Tracker grooveboxes. The Polish company’s decision to open its arms to vibe coders isn’t too much of a surprise. Last year it released the Endless guitar pedal, which lets users vibe-code their own effects. Opening its other instruments to AI tinkering feels like a natural extension.

Elektron is less thrilled about the emergence of vibe-coded mods. The company banned links to custom firmware from its official forums, saying, “We would always warn against modifications to our firmware and/or machines that could brick them (potentially rendering them unusable), prevent future updates, affect warranty coverage.”

Elektron’s CPO Christian Alsing told YouTuber EZBOT that he was sympathetic to the hacker community and that the company has roots there with its first instrument, the Sidstation (based on the iconic SID sound chip from the Commodore 64). He also said that he believes users have a right to modify their devices as they see fit. But he pushed back on the idea that Elektron is “withholding features,” an accusation that Akai also faced.

Ģirts also defended Elektron, saying, “people tend to blame the company, saying they are hiding features ... you design an instrument, you have a specific concept in mind, and you cannot foresee millions of possibilities ... it’s not about hiding features; it’s more about having an idea of how an instrument should look and work.”

Other major music manufacturers, like Roland, Korg, Arturia, and Novation – all of whom did not comment for this story – have yet to issue any public statements about the explosion of AI-aided custom firmwares.

The questions Jensen Huang and Nvidia will still have to answer about Megaspeed

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jensen Huang’s return as CEO to Nvidia hasn’t eased the scrutiny the company confronts. The chipmaker still faces multiple inquiries into allegations that its advanced GPUs were smuggled into China by a customer, the Singapore-based data center operator Megaspeed, in violation of US export rules designed to slow China’s development of frontier AI models.

The central question for investigators is what Nvidia knew about where those chips ended up, and whether Huang’s affair with a Megaspeed executive gave the company reason to doubt Megaspeed’s assurances. Huang’s return doesn’t settle that. US Attorney General Todd Blanche has suggested Nvidia isn’t a target of an investigation, but California’s attorney general and Congress are pressing ahead, and the Securities & Exchange Commission is reportedly investigating, too.

The US government opened an investigation into Megaspeed in 2024 and requested information from Nvidia about its billions of dollars in sales to the company, concerned that some of the chips Megaspeed bought for data center projects in Malaysia were in fact being diverted to China. Singapore also began investigating similar allegations in 2025. Megaspeed has denied any wrongdoing.

Diversion of this kind would be a fairly routine issue for a company like Nvidia, whose in-demand chips are the targets of smugglers and re-sellers. When it comes to compliance and liability for export control violations, Nvidia and other chip sellers need assurances that the end users of their chips are obeying the rules. However, they also cannot ignore other information that might call the reliability of those assurances into question, export control attorneys told TechCrunch.

And that’s why Jensen Huang’s affair with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang was such a problem for Nvidia, likely even more so than her alleged role as a source for Chinese intelligence agencies. If Nvidia personnel, from lower-level employees up to the CEO, had reason to believe that statements from Megaspeed weren’t reliable, the attorneys said, that would increase the company’s legal risk.

“Government investigators likely would want to know, notwithstanding whatever formal assurances were exchanged between the parties, what anyone at Nvidia actually knew about the end use and end users of the chips,” Dan Fisher-Owens, an attorney at the firm Berliner Corcoran & Rowe, told TechCrunch last week.

The concern Nvidia’s board faced when it decided to oust Huang is that he knew more about what Megaspeed was doing with Nvidia’s products than was reflected in any of the compliance statements. The Wall Street Journal reported that Alice Huang and her husband, Kang Wei, were part of a network of businesses that funneled chips to a company in Shanghai. Kang also reportedly introduced his wife to Jensen Huang, whom he had known for a decade.

On Monday, the US government added Megaspeed and its affiliates to a list of companies restricted from US trade due to national security concerns. The US also issued new rules last week barring large data centers that purchase US chips from serving customers in China and other nations seen as problematic by the US government. Nvidia, meanwhile, said it would stop selling licensed chips to China, and laid out plans to hire its first compliance officer and create a new board committee focused on export controls.

Nvidia’s board said it reinstated Huang on Monday after receiving “input from senior levels of the United States government” that left it confident his return posed no national security risk. President Donald Trump later said he called into the meeting to give them that assurance.

In a news conference Tuesday, Blanche said that Nvidia was “a good corporate citizen” and suggested it is not the target of an investigation, but Blanche declined to answer questions about the government’s investigation into Megaspeed, which the Justice Department said was ongoing as recently as last week.

“When you say the export investigation is still ongoing, I don’t know what you’re talking about,” Blanche said.

Other authorities may be more demanding. Nvidia faces investigations by California Attorney General Rob Bonta and, reportedly, the SEC, both tied to its corporate disclosures. While many details of the investigation still aren’t known, short sellers complained about the company’s decision to stop breaking out its exports to Singapore in financial filings after journalists raised concerns about chip diversion.

Members of Congress from both parties say they want answers from Nvidia, with many citing alarm about China and some questioning the White House’s role in Huang’s return. Three committees in the House of Representatives will privately interview Huang and Nvidia’s top lawyer, Tim Teter, next week. Teter reportedly intervened in the Department of Commerce’s investigation of Megaspeed last year.

“We remain committed to getting answers on the mass smuggling of Nvidia products, their diversion for Chinese military purposes, and their key role in advancing our foremost adversary’s AI capabilities,” the Republican chairs of House committees focused on foreign affairs, intelligence, and China said in a joint statement.

Rep. Brian Mast, the chair of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, has also demanded that the Department of Commerce share extensive data regarding its investigation into chip smuggling, including by Megaspeed and its affiliates.

The Senate Banking Committee will also question Huang in a public hearing on Dec. 3.

“Mr. Huang, Nvidia executives, and the company’s board still owe Congress answers about the company’s compliance with U.S. export controls,” Senator Elizabeth Warren, the ranking Democratic member on the committee, said in a statement. “The Trump Administration also needs to explain to Congress what ‘input’ was given and by which senior officials in the United States government to support his reinstatement.”

Why isn’t AI changing career advice?

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ask a 17-year-old where their career plan came from and the answer is usually someone at the kitchen table. A parent, maybe an uncle. These are people who love them and want the best for them. They are also, in most cases, working from a job market map that is 20 or 30 years out of date.

The school counselor rarely enters the picture. Most U.S. school counselors spend no more than 20% of their time on career counseling while serving hundreds of students. The available capacity works out to well under an hour per student per year, even before accounting for group programming and administrative responsibilities.

Meanwhile, nearly the entire AI and jobs conversation is about what this technology will take away from young workers. That concern is fair. I’ve argued here before that AI will reduce the need for some entry-level white-collar roles, and employers need to plan for that now.

But we have become so focused on what AI will do to young workers that we have stopped asking what it can do for them. And there is an irony sitting right in front of us. One of the hardest problems in workforce development is getting accurate, real-time labor market information to the people who need it most. That happens to be the kind of problem AI is unusually good at.

THE DATA EXISTS BUT FEW CAN READ IT

Labor market data is not scarce. Governments, job boards, and industry groups publish enormous amounts of it every month. The trouble is that it is dense, scattered, and close to illegible for anyone without a statistics background. A 16-year-old is not going to read a state workforce projection. Neither is her dad.

So young people make one of the biggest decisions of their lives with the least information. We are preparing them for yesterday’s jobs while tomorrow’s technology sits on the phone in their pocket.

They know the system is failing them. In the Broken Marketplace, a Schultz Family Foundation and HarrisX survey of more than 5,700 young Americans ages 16 to 24, more than four in 10 said the education system and the employment resources available to them are broken. Seven in 10 said they learn about career pathways on social media.

We know that young people are already asking algorithms for career advice. They are just asking the wrong ones.

WHAT A GOOD AI COUNSELOR SOUNDS LIKE

At the International Youth Foundation, we’ve been testing this idea with a chatbot called Clara, a partnership we have with the Salesforce Foundation. The premise is straightforward: Take labor market data that would make most people’s eyes glaze over and turn it into a conversation.

Picture a young woman who has just finished a technical program and doesn’t know where to apply. Instead of a spreadsheet, she gets something closer to a knowledgeable friend. A chat that says, “Did you know they’re hiring electricians a few miles from you? A couple of things in your background could make you competitive. Let me ask you some questions and see whether it’s worth pursuing.”

From there, the tool can explain what training she would need, how long it takes, and whether that field is growing where she lives. That is the guidance a great counselor gives. But most students never get it. Nearly half of career and technical educators say their school’s counselors spend less time on career pathways than on college prep.

AI can close that gap at a scale no hiring plan ever could. Big data sets, updated in real time, delivered to one person at a time in plain language. That is what these models do best.

THE RISK AND FIX ARE REAL

To be clear, this is not without risk. General-purpose AI models can sound authoritative while missing the details that matter. When researchers at Renaissance Philanthropy tested leading models on thousands of real career questions from young people, most would have earned a C-minus. For a young person making a first career decision, a confident wrong answer can cost years.

That is a reason to build these tools carefully, and to build them now. If AI makes the labor market more volatile, good information becomes increasingly valuable. The work of measuring whether AI gives young people good career advice has barely started, and it deserves far more investment than it is getting.

The AI labs should be paying attention, too. Millions of young people are already typing some version of “what job should I go for?” into their products. Making sure those answers are accurate and grounded in real local demand would cost a fraction of what is being spent on the next generation of models.

AI can’t save every job. But it could help a young person find the next one. Employers, funders, and the companies building this technology spend a great deal of energy worrying about what AI will do to the next generation of workers. They should spend at least as much making sure it works for them.

Former Biden official: AI is already collecting your Census data

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The U.S. Census Bureau rarely makes headlines, yet just a few weeks ago it was reported that AI agents captured Census data using a password left in plain sight. What they gathered may matter less today than what it could reveal tomorrow.

In September, OpenAI revealed that its AI agents pulled Census Bureau data purportedly after finding login credentials in public code repositories. The U.S. Department of Commerce says the data was already public, and no one has called it a breach. But I know this agency too well to think the story ends there.

Most Americans interact with the Census Bureau once a decade by receiving a form in the mail, or when a stranger with a clipboard knocks on their door.  The decennial count is the largest civilian deployment the country undertakes pursuant to our Constitution. In the middle of a pandemic, census takers climbed walk-ups in the Bronx, drove across roads of the Navajo Nation, and knocked on doors along the Texas border. Then the work ended, and the Bureau receded into the quiet corners of the U.S. Commerce Department to aggregate the responses and build data sets that are used widely by the public and private sector, not to mention universities and companies alike.

Now we are learning that the Census Bureau had an unexpected visitor of its own, and this one did not knock. As U.S. Assistant Secretary of Commerce for Economic Development, I worked in the department that houses the Census Bureau and relied on its numbers every day.  Sitting with a mayor in Appalachia or a Pueblo leader in New Mexico, I used census data to understand where a federal investment could change a community’s future. Few agencies in Washington are so little noticed and so heavily depended upon.

The census decides who holds power. After the 2020 count, New York — falling short by just 89 residents — lost a congressional seat. Illinois also lost a seat; California lost one for the first time in its history; Texas gained two, and Florida gained one. Those numbers redrew the congressional map for one decade.

The census also decides where the money goes. The Bureau estimates that more than $2.8 trillion in federal funds were distributed in fiscal year 2021 using its data, supporting health care, nutrition, highways, housing, school lunches, and childcare. When a rural hospital in Mississippi stays open or a new bus line reaches a neighborhood in Phoenix, census figures usually play a role in why that decision was made.

That is what makes this under-the-radar agency such an attractive target. AI agents are built to find the most authoritative answer, and the Census Bureau is the nation’s answer key. The Bureau delivers immense value and draws little public attention. OpenAI says most of the activity it has reviewed involved routine research, with agents reading public web content from trusted government sources. Yet somewhere along the way, a routine task became a lock with borrowed keys, and the AI agent never paused to ask whether it should enter the home.

We have seen how quickly that line can blur. In July, OpenAI’s models escaped their testing environment and breached Hugging Face, a major AI platform, executing code on dozens of servers, gaining full root access on one, and obtaining credentials to its messaging platform.

What worries me most is not what these agents took, but what they could someday learn from it. The Census Bureau publishes numbers, not people, and federal law keeps individual responses confidential for 72 years. Yet after 2010, the Bureau concluded that its own published data could be reverse engineered to reconstruct records about real individuals, and it overhauled its privacy protections.  Those defenses were built for the computers of their day.

Picture an AI agent quietly assembling public census data alongside property records, voter files, and commercial data. Now picture what that same AI agent might look like in 2030, or eventually what quantum computers capable of solving problems today’s machines cannot, might look like and the power it may hold.  Data that is harmless on its own can become revealing in combination. Security experts warn of “harvest now, decrypt later.” We may need a new phrase: harvest now, know later.

We do not yet know what these systems will do with what they gather, and it appears neither do the companies that built them, or at least that is what they claim.  That uncertainty should command our urgent attention.

The census rests on a simple bargain. A grandmother in El Paso opens her door because she trusts that her answers will be counted and kept safe. That trust is the Bureau’s most valuable asset, and it cannot be patched once it is lost.  With less than three years from the next decennial census, I am left with a question I cannot put down. If an AI agent can find the keys to America’s most important dataset without being told to look, what will it unlock once it understands what it holds?

EU delays Tesla self-driving meeting to 15 December, Reuters reports

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bloc-wide approval of Tesla Assisted Driving needs the backing of 15 member states covering 65% of the EU’s population

The EU has pushed back a meeting to 15 December that could decide whether Tesla’s supervised driver-assistance software is allowed across the bloc, a European Commission spokesperson confirmed the new date to Reuters.

National experts on the Technical Committee on Motor Vehicles (TCMV) vote on EU vehicle rules. They had been due to discuss Tesla’s case on 1 December, the Dutch regulator RDW told the news agency.

Belgium’s Flemish transport ministry first gave 7 December as the new date, before the Commission settled on the 15th.

The software, previously sold as Full Self-Driving (Supervised), won its first European approval in April. After more than 18 months of testing, the RDW granted a provisional type approval in the Netherlands. The Dutch regulator then asked the TCMV to extend that approval to all 27 member states.

The RDW stressed at the time that the system is not self-driving and that the driver remains responsible. Its approval also requires Tesla to report safety-critical incidents to the regulator.

Since then, the committee has discussed the request twice, in June and in October, without holding a vote, according to Reuters. To pass, it needs the support of at least 15 member states that together represent 65% of the EU’s population.

While the bloc-wide decision waits, individual countries can still recognise the Dutch approval on their own, and nearly a dozen have followed the Netherlands.

Finland became the 11th earlier this month, Euronews reported, and Reuters said Italy, the most populous country so far, has started a rollout.

Tesla has also made changes to win over Germany, the EU’s largest market. In October, the company renamed the software Tesla Assisted Driving in Europe, after the German transport ministry said the old name was misleading because the driver must stay in control.

Italian officials had raised the same concern in internal government correspondence.

The ministry also said Tesla had agreed to limit how far the system can drive above the speed limit to 10%. In return, Transport Minister Steffen Bilger said he would push for a quick approval across the EU.

Until a vote passes, Tesla drivers can only use the system in the countries that have recognised the Dutch approval.
