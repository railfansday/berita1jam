Europe's Hera mission returns to the chaotic scene of a cosmic smashup

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

“DART made a really, really large mess. However, we didn’t really know what kind of mess.”

Four years ago, NASA dispatched a small spacecraft on a suicide mission to show how humanity might deflect an asteroid on a collision course with Earth. The first-of-its-kind experiment worked beautifully as the solar-powered space probe slammed into asteroid Dimorphos, permanently altering its trajectory.

Using telescopes, astronomers measured changes in the orbit of Dimorphos around a larger asteroid companion named Didymos in the aftermath of NASA’s Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) mission. The spacecraft released some 10 billion joules of energy as it impacted Dimorphos in September 2022 at more than 13,700 mph (6.14 kilometers per second), shortening the asteroid’s 12-hour orbit around Didymos by about 33 minutes, 25 times the most conservative expectation.

But scientists could only learn so much from DART. The collision destroyed the spacecraft, as expected. DART recorded images as it took aim on Dimorphos, but only faraway telescopes and a small CubeSat that briefly detected the impact flash could see what happened next.

“We knew right away that the impact was successful, that DART made a really, really large mess,” said Sabina Raducan, a planetary scientist at the International Space Science Institute in Bern, Switzerland. “However, we didn’t really know what kind of mess. We were still missing pieces of the puzzle.”

The European Space Agency’s Hera spacecraft recently arrived in the vicinity of Didymos and Dimorphos after a two-year journey from a launch pad in Florida. On Wednesday, ESA released Hera’s first views of the scar left by DART on Dimorphos. The images show the car-sized impactor carved out a crater the size of a stadium, nearly as big as Dimorphos itself, a rugged chunk of extraterrestrial rock measuring about 500 feet (151 meters) across.

Mission planners selected Didymos and Dimorphos for the deflection experiment because they are similar to many near-Earth asteroids, but there’s no risk of them striking our home planet. Scientists weren’t sure what Hera would find when it got to the double asteroid, currently orbiting the Sun about 120 million miles (193.5 million kilometers) from Earth.

“I would say we had two kinds of predictions: either we made a crater, or we reshaped the asteroid,” said Patrick Michel, Hera’s principal investigator from Côte d’Azur Observatory in Nice, France. “In fact, we did both because the crater is so large that the shape has changed. Therefore, we are in a sort of a transition between just cratering and global transformation, and we are still not sure how this can happen.”

Hera found Didymos, the larger of the two asteroids, spinning on its axis just as DART observed on final approach in 2022. Dimorphos is no longer so stable.

“As Hera returned more images, it became clear that the DART impact has left Dimorphos tumbling wildly in its orbit,” says Michael Kueppers, ESA’s Hera mission scientist.

The Hera spacecraft will fly closer to Didymos and Dimorphos over the next six months to obtain more detailed observations. Hera will release two small sub-satellites to attempt landings on Dimorphos and collect data on the asteroid’s composition, internal structure, and mass.

Scientists will use this information to reconstruct the dynamics of DART’s impact four years ago and better understand how a future deflection mission might move an asteroid off a trajectory that actually threatens Earth. Near-Earth asteroids come in many shapes and sizes, with different densities and makeups, so knowledge of Dimorphos’s physical properties will anchor predictive computer models teaching scientists how best to deflect all types of asteroids.

“Once we understand, we have a breakthrough, which really solves planetary defense,” Michel said in an interview with Ars.

In its initial reconnaissance this month, Hera spotted hundreds of boulders surrounding the double asteroid. This was not entirely unexpected. A picture taken by the Hubble Space Telescope shortly after DART’s impact showed a cloud of material streaming behind Didymos and Dimorphos, but scientists were astonished by the extent of the debris swarm in Hera’s first images.

“As we approached Didymos and Dimorphos, we quickly realized the asteroids were not alone,” said Naomi Murdoch, planetary scientist at the Institut Supérieur de l’Aéronautique et de l’Espace in Toulouse, France. “In fact, they had a lot of company.”

An image released by ESA on Wednesday combines 11 long exposure frames from Hera’s hyperspectral camera. Image processors on the ground digitally removed the relatively bright Didymos and Dimorphos to reveal a panoply of dimmer streaks and dots, each of which is a rock fragment ranging in size from a football to a washing machine, Murdoch said.

“Hera came to visit two asteroids, and we found hundreds of companions,” Murdoch said.

One obvious explanation for the boulders is they were thrown off Dimorphos when DART slammed into it. But scientists cautioned they need more information before they can say that with surety. Hera is the first space mission to undertake a sustained study of an asteroid with its own moon. The debris around the two asteroids might be relics from the creation of Dimorphos some time in the ancient past.

“Hera will allow us to study that in more detail,” Murdoch said. “We really need to know more about these boulders and characterize them to understand if they’re linked to DART, if they’re linked to another process, and what the consequences might be for other asteroid systems in the Solar System.”

The boulder field will create challenges for navigating around the asteroids. The boulders are an added risk for the two small CubeSats Hera is supposed to deploy in January.

“We saw a couple of images that didn’t reassure me,” said Margherita Cardi, project manager for the Milani CubeSat. “There is a mix of excitement and panic.”

Claudia Balossi, spacecraft systems engineer for Hera’s other CubeSat, named Juventas, said her mission’s planned trajectory would take it right through the boulders as it moves within a couple of miles of the asteroids, then finally toward a landing attempt on Dimorphos, where it will measure the miniature world’s tenuous gravity field.

“We are right in the middle of it,” Balossi said. “The smallest ones are probably just as big as we are and the biggest ones are very big. In the worst case, a boulder will land on us. We will activate our gravimeter right away [if that happens],” Balossi joked.

The good news is the asteroids’ gravity is so weak that Hera, Milani, Juventas, and the hazards around them all move at snail-like speeds. Hera’s current trajectory takes it around the asteroids at a relative velocity of just a quarter-mile per hour, or about 12 centimeters per second. At that speed, there’s no danger of a sudden catastrophic hypervelocity collision between the spacecraft and the boulders lurking nearby.

Scientists have seen this at an asteroid before. NASA’s OSIRIS-REx spacecraft kicked up an estimated six tons of rock and dust when it gently moved in to collect a sample from an asteroid named Bennu in 2020. Low gravity and weak surface cohesion are a recipe for outsized effects.

“These types of bodies, because of their low gravity, have a very counterintuitive behavior,” Michel said. “Each time we interact with them, whether to take a sample or to make an impact, the response of the surface was not the one we predicted, simply because our intuition relies on what we experience every day on Earth. Even if the rules of physics are the same in the Universe, they express very differently depending on local attraction.”

When you're exploring a world as realistic and well-observed as GTA 6's Leonida, it's rare for a piece of benign infrastructure to stick out. But on night one of my playthrough, I clocked some weird streetlights.

A stylistic choice by Rockstar? Maybe, but it's awfully specific, and I had a feeling there was a deeper intention behind it. So I DMed my buddy Henry, an honest-to-god Floridian who was also playing GTA 6, and asked what's up. At first he thought strange streetlights in the Key Lento area of the map were special lights meant to not disorient turtles, but those are typically red, so probably not. And that doesn't explain the purple ones.

So Henry reached out to friends in south Florida, the region that Leonida's biggest cities are based on, and they cracked the case wide open.

"The purple [lights] are definitely real. We have a ton of them down here because whoever is in charge of that in the government bought a bunch of defective lights," the anonymous Floridian responded.

Yes, actual Florida cities are lit by these dubiously dim, yet undeniably cool purple lamps. According to ClickOrlando, residents first started reporting the purple streetlights in 2023. They put up hundreds of these things across the state before they started malfunctioning—it was such a kerfuffle that the government eventually released a statement saying their vendor really screwed the pooch.

Apparently the cause was a "spectral shift caused by phosphor displacement," which essentially means the LED lights were always blue, but the coating that was supposed to make appear yellow or white or whatever didn't work. So that's fun. If you already heard about this somewhere (other than your Floridian friend), it's probably because YouTuber AnyAustin also clocked these lights in his video published yesterday. Glad I'm not the only one who couldn't resist this rabbit hole.

Who won the Aaron Rodgers-Mike McCarthy battle? Mike Tomlin

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

After Aaron Rodgers' benching Tuesday night, the Rodgers-Mike McCarthy relationship reached critical mass Wednesday when the quarterback unleashed a torrent of accusations against his former head coach.

Among them: Rodgers said McCarthy didn't watch tape, missed team meetings, slept through a few he attended and even said "1-2-3, Packers!" while breaking a team huddle. Shortly after Rodgers' revelatory appearance on "The Pat McAfee Show," the Steelers released Rodgers.

Rodgers is now left to pick up the pieces and decide his suddenly cloudy future in what we all believed would be his final NFL season. McCarthy might have the bigger immediate mess, left to coach his struggling team in a game against the 8-2 Broncos and face some incendiary accusations that tend to undercut a coach's authority.

But you know who isn't losing sleep right now? The guy who's set to unleash his latest Minecraft creation. Wednesday night's subject: "Ballparks, stadiums and varsity sports venues." How relatively quaint!

Mike Tomlin lives the dream right now. He revealed his 12-year secret about his Minecraft, and the world has accepted it with bemusement and wonder. Tomlin also has found strong footing on NBC as one of its Sunday night analysts, offering listeners a natural delivery, a willingness to give unvarnished opinions, solid coaching-style breakdowns and his now-patented "Tomlinisms."

And he's doing it all with a big smile on his face.

"Man, I'm having a ball, man," Tomlin said on the "New Heights" podcast earlier this season. "It's part-time work, full-time pay, you know what I mean?"

Tomlin has always had humor in his back pocket. He has also displayed pretty good timing over the years. Tomlin knew when to shift the emphasis away from the "Killer B's" in their later years, and he knew it was time to move on from malcontent players such as Antonio Brown, Martavis Bryant and Diontae Johnson.

Tomlin also knew when — and how — to gracefully exit the Steelers. No scorched-earth policy. No bridge burning or axe grinding. He also seemed to understand his place in everything and that it was time to walk away.

"Football, man, is bigger than all of us, and I never took myself so seriously that I thought that I was immune from that," Tomlin said in a recent episode of the "Not Just Football" podcast with Steelers defensive tackle Cameron Heyward. "I never wanted to stay too long."

Tomlin mentioned carrying "19 years of baggage" and how that could weigh down the Steelers.

"I just thought we had the makings of a really good team, a team that is capable of competing for and winning a championship," Tomlin said, "but when I really started thinking about it, man, my bags might slow that down."

Aaron Rodgers showered praise on Mike Tomlin during an interview Wednesday on ESPN.

This is not to suggest that Tomlin never experienced controversy or tough times with the Steelers. There have been plenty of each. But Tomlin has come out looking rosy amid all the current ugliness over at the Rooney Sports Complex.

Rodgers even made a point of praising Tomlin — as he did throughout their time together — in trashing McCarthy on Wednesday.

"We're all coming from Mike T, the polar opposite," Rodgers said. "Mike T, leader of men. He knew everybody's name, knew where they came from. That's what I told [McCarthy]: 'You don't have to be Mike T, but you have to connect with the guys.'"

Heck, even McCarthy has thrown roses in Tomlin's direction, saying recently: "I'm really happy for Mike. I'm trying not to laugh because I've never seen him smile so much. I'm told he's very happy. He's doing a great job."⁠

Sit back, unveil your Minecraft world, earn praise from both sides — two parties who despise each other — and collect some NBC paychecks. Not the worst gap year imaginable.

Tomlin can do whatever he wants next year: go back to coaching, keep doing the TV/Minecraft thing or try something new. Heck, would it shock anyone if some Steelers were pining for Tomlin and letting the powers that be know how they feel?

Interestingly, the Steelers still technically control Tomlin's rights, as he stepped down with two years left on his contract. The thinking in NFL circles is that the Steelers could receive compensation from another team interested in Tomlin's services, should he decide to take another job elsewhere. But now?

A reunion would still feel like a bridge too far. But the odds of Tomlin coming back to Pittsburgh, however minuscule they might be, are certainly higher today than they were a week ago. This is the power and position he's earned in light of all the darkness descending on the city.

There isn't a single person entangled in this Rodgers-McCarthy mess who comes out looking cleaner than Tomlin. Say what you will about Rodgers, but he's not exactly known for seeing eye-to-eye with every coach he's ever had. And perhaps there's some ulterior motive at play here, but it's hard to find Rodgers saying a bad word about Tomlin.

And that might not be viewed as a positive to everyone, Rodgers being who he is. Some McCarthy backers or Rodgersphiles might even take it as an indictment of Tomlin allowing Rodgers to manipulate him, or that Rodgers' undying praise is evidence for Tomlin being too much of a players' coach.

Even still, by doing nothing coaching-related this season, Tomlin has given us a refresher of why he was roundly respected as Steelers head coach — beyond even his unquestioned résumé topper of never having a losing season.

Tomlin is viewed as having established credibility and culture. He knew his players personally. He's also been known for his consistency and fairness, even with the toughest of personalities and controversies. It was easy to see how well respected Tomlin is by how many players greeted him on the field when NBC broadcast the Steelers game two weeks ago.

Many ex-Steelers praised Tomlin as a leader of men on his way out of the job, too. Some, like James Harrison, have criticized Tomlin's coaching, but it's not as easy to find prominent Steelers questioning Tomlin's character on the record.

And now he gets to avoid this mess that's unfolding in front of our eyes. Tomlin walked away, wiped his hands of the situation and, by all accounts, appears to be living his best life. It might be tempting to tune into Tomlin's second episode Wednesday night, not only to witness his career respawn but also to see if he offers any new Tomlinisms, subtle breadcrumbs on his smoldering former franchise, or perhaps just a knowing look.

As Tomlin once famously said: "Don't blink. If you're a blinker, cut your eyelids off."

Liverpool: Andoni Iraola delivers Victor Munoz injury update after Club Brugge win

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Andoni Iraola is optimistic Victor Munoz’s injury might not be as serious as it first appeared after the Liverpool youngster hobbled off in the first half against Club Brugge.

The young Spaniard threw himself into an aggressive challenge on Jorne Spileers shortly before half-time - but came off far worse after appearing to knock knees with the Belgian.

Munoz looked to be in considerable pain as he stayed down and Liverpool’s medical staff were waved onto the pitch. There was a lengthy delay as he received treatment.

He was surrounded by concerned-looking team-mates as a stretcher was brought on to carry him off, but it was waved away and Munoz was able to leave the pitch under his own steam.

The fact he was able to do so suggests his injury may not be too severe, though Munoz still appeared to be in serious discomfort as he made his way to the bench with a pronounced limp.

Speaking at full-time, though, Iraola delivered a positive update on the player’s condition, acknowledging the incident looked concerning but expressing optimism that his injury is not a severe one.

“He was in a lot of pain when he left the pitch,” the Basque explained. “But he was a lot more optimistic after the game. We need to assess him.”

It remains to be seen whether he will be available for this weekend’s Merseyside derby, away at the Hill Dickinson Stadium.

His injury comes at a bad time for the Reds, who were already without Cody Gakpo and Ryan Gravenberch in Belgium.

Liverpool were one to the good when Munoz left the pitch, after Dominik Szoboszlai teed Alexander Isak up to score from the edge of the six-yard box with 13 minutes on the clock.

They went on to win 3-0, with Isak notching a brace after Florian Wirtz doubled their advantage.
