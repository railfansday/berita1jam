How Nvidia is reacting to Jensen Huang's return as CEO

US Attorney General Todd Blanche has backed Nvidia as a "good corporate citizen" while refusing to comment on whether it was being investigated by the administration.

Blanche said he had no information about Jensen Huang's security clearance status, as lawmakers demand answers about President Donald Trump's involvement in Huang's reinstatement as the AI microchip maker's chief executive.

Huang was given his job back less than two weeks after being forced to resign over revelations of extramarital affairs, including one with an alleged Chinese spy.

Asked several times on Tuesday if the justice department or FBI had investigated Huang, Blanche responded: "I don't know what you're talking about."

Huang is still set to appear before Congress next week, where he will be questioned over his relationship with a former executive for Megaspeed - the alleged China spy - due to national security concerns.

The US commerce department has also announced it was preparing to blacklist tech company Megaspeed, the Singapore-based Nvidia customer that has been suspected of helping divert advanced chips to China.

In a joint statement last week, the commerce and justice departments said they began investigating Megaspeed in 2024 "for potential export control violations".

But speaking to reporters on Tuesday, Blanche said the latter department has "hundreds, if not more" export investigations occurring at any one time.

"So when you say the expert investigation is still ongoing, I don't know what you're talking about," he said.

Asked if he was satisfied Nvidia had been co-operative with any efforts to get to the bottom of whether the company or Huang potentially knew about chips being diverted to China, Blanche said: "I have no reason to stand up here and say Nvidia is anything but a good corporate citizen."

"Obviously, if Nvidia was the target or was subject to some criminal investigation or they were obstructing or thwarting our investigation, then that wouldn't be the message that that the president is saying to the American people ... It shouldn't be what my message would be either," Blanche said.

While Nvidia's stock market value surged on the announcement of Huang's reappointment, some of the gains were reversed in volatile Tuesday trading.

The company's share price was down more than 7% in early afternoon trading, but remained well above the level it closed at on Friday, before Huang's return was announced.

Retail turnarounds are taking hold just in time for the holiday season

📅 2026-11-24  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Once-struggling retailers are entering the holiday quarter with improved performance and sales momentum as turnaround efforts begin to resonate with shoppers.

A handful of earnings reports on Tuesday pointed to a mix of factors behind the strong results, including a resilient consumer, stronger execution and demand for the right products at the right price.

Kohl's reported comparable sales growth of 1.2% for the period ended Oct. 31 and raised its full-year outlook as CEO Michael Bender said customers "have remained resilient" but are "migrating to value with their purchases."

He tied improvement to the company's work on product assortment, store experience and value.

Best Buy also on Tuesday posted its highest comparable sales growth in five years, at 6.1%, which prompted the electronics retailer to raise its full-year outlook.

"We have a tremendous amount of momentum," CEO Jason Bonfig told CNBC in an interview. "The fact that we saw pretty much broad-based growth across almost all of our categories is a lot of momentum that we're very excited about."

The company also credited execution, market share gains and a compelling product cycle alongside growing customer demand.

At Dick's Sporting Goods, quarterly comparable sales rose 2.6% at the core business and 0.5% at Foot Locker. CEO Lauren Hobart said "all income demographics grew" and the company "didn't see trade-down from best to better or better to good."

She similarly pointed to new products and market share gains within the category as contributing to the strong quarter. She also highlighted continued improvement at Foot Locker.

All three companies have been undergoing efforts to boost sales and get the right products on shelves to meet customer demand.

Target, likewise undergoing revitalization efforts, last week reported comparable sales growth of 5.6% in the third quarter, fueled by a 5% increase in comparable traffic. The retailer raised its full-year outlook.

CEO Michael Fiddelke at the time called the traffic gain "a healthy sign that guests are responding to our changes and choosing Target more often."

Even Gap CEO Richard Dickson told CNBC the company has moved from "rebuild mode" to "turnaround mode" at its Old Navy banner, a sign of progress, albeit not a broad-based portfolio recovery.

Michael Baker, a retail analyst at D.A. Davidson, told CNBC he sees both a resilient consumer and company-specific improvements as driving sector strength.

"Retail sales have been more resilient than most think," Baker said. His analysis showed retail sales, excluding autos and gas, rose 5.1% in the third quarter on a nonseasonally adjusted basis — the strongest quarter since the pandemic.

But he cautioned that growth appears to be coming more from transaction size than traffic, with inflation, promotions and higher units per transaction driving up spend.

Dollar Tree, which reported better-than-expected earnings on Tuesday morning, posted a 3.8% increase to traffic, accelerating from 0.4% growth in the prior quarter, suggesting more shoppers may be bargain hunting. Comparable sales at the retailer increased 4.6% during the period.

"Consumers are under pressure, and they are increasingly selective about where and how they spend," said Dollar Tree CEO Michael Creedon on the company's earnings call. "When under pressure, shoppers prioritize value and buy closer to need."

Comparable sales of everyday essentials rose 8.9% at Dollar Tree stores in the third quarter, while sales of nonessential items were essentially flat, the company said.

— CNBC's Laya Neelakandan contributed to this report.

The U.S. is preparing to raise tariffs on China, the E.U., and 14 other countries

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The U.S. Trade Representative is expected to unveil results of its industrial subsidies investigation as soon as Tuesday

Allen J. Schaben / Los Angeles Times via Getty Images

The U.S. is getting ready to hike duties on imports from China, the European Union and 14 other economies in response to their industrial subsidies, according to The Wall Street Journal.

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer could announce the results of his office's investigation as soon as Tuesday, according to Bloomberg. The timing could change, according to The Wall Street Journal. Tariff rates and an effective date are unclear.

The probe falls under Section 301 of the 1974 Trade Act, a provision that lets a president act alone in levying tariffs in response to unfair trade conduct, according to Bloomberg. The Office of the U.S. Trade Representative opened the investigation in March, following the high court's invalidation of President Donald Trump's earlier worldwide tariffs.

Greer has said that state backing, subsidies among it, has left the targeted economies with more industrial production capacity than they need. He has said that this lets companies in those economies undercut competitors abroad on price, which hurts American manufacturers. The U.S. Trade Representative's office declined to comment, according to The Wall Street Journal and Bloomberg. The White House did not respond to requests for comment from The Wall Street Journal or Bloomberg.

The new tariffs are expected to apply to the bulk of goods coming from the targeted nations, among them China, Japan, South Korea and the E.U.'s 27 member states, according to The Wall Street Journal. Steel, aluminum and automobiles are expected to be left out because they are already subject to duties under another law tied to national security.

Administration officials have said they plan to set rates close to the ones Trump put in place during 2025, ahead of the Supreme Court's invalidation of his initial round of tariffs in February 2026, according to The Wall Street Journal. Countries with trade agreements, including Japan, the E.U. and South Korea, are expected to face rates of 15% or lower. Vietnam and India could be hit with steeper rates that reflect the agreements they struck with Trump.

China is expected to be charged a steeper tariff than many of its peers because Trump officials have described its subsidies as especially far-reaching, according to The Wall Street Journal. The administration could add wording that would let it increase tariffs if a country's overcapacity in industry grows worse or if the country fails to change its policies during later talks.

The new duties are expected to stack on top of tariffs announced in July over forced labor, according to The Wall Street Journal. Those tariffs, which run from 10% to 12.5% on about 60 economies, are being contested in a lawsuit before the Court of International Trade, according to Bloomberg.

The Savage Model 99 Is Still One of the Greatest Lever Action Rifles of All Time

The latest for gun hunters and competitive shooters.

In the early years of the twentieth century whitetail populations were making a comeback and deer hunting was a growing sport. Lever action rifles ruled the woods for at least the first half of the century and beyond. Even in my early years of hunting in the late sixties, lever actions seemed to fill the gun rack in every deer camp. The most popular was the Winchester Model 94, usually in .30-30, and this rifle dominated the deer woods.

While the 94 was the gun for the masses, the gun guys, the hunters who “knew stuff” had a different pick. The Savage Model 99 rifle was often their choice. While the tube magazine on the Winchester and Marlin lever actions limited the rifles to use with blunt nose bullets, with its rotary box magazine the Savage 1899 could use pointed bullets for a ballistic advantage. It was also strong enough to handle powerful cartridges using that newfangled smokeless powder.

The lever action Model 99 featured an internal hammer and many advanced features like a cocking indicator and even a counter to show the number of cartridges in the magazine. It was simply a more sophisticated design than the other lever actions. The introduction of the .250 Savage cartridge for the Model 1899 in 1915 ushered a new world of deer cartridges. At the time most cartridges barely broke the 2,000 ft/s barrier, but this one hit 3,000 ft/s with an 87-grain bullet. The savviest hunters recognized the advantage over the big, slow, hard kicking, straight walled black powder cartridges that were still in vogue at that time.

Five years later in 1920, Savage changed the name to the Model 99 and introduced the .300 Savage, a cartridge that at the time matched the ballistics of the .30-06. With these advanced cartridges the Savage Model 99 rifle became the thinking man’s deer rifle.

Prior to WWI bolt action rifles were almost unheard of for deer hunting and the Savage Model 1899 lever action rifle led the industry in innovation and performance. Even after the war when bolt action sporting rifles began to emerge into the market, the Savage remained one of the most popular hunting rifles on earth.

So how did this rifle come to be? As with many of our great firearms in history it was because of one visionary, Arthur William Savage.

Savage was born in Kingston, Jamaica on May 13, 1857. He received an education in the United States and in England, which was probably designed to have him follow in his father’s footsteps and become a government bureaucrat.

But Savage wanted adventure, so as soon as they handed him his diploma he hightailed it for Australia to look for black opals and interesting experiences, as Teddy Roosevelt called them.

At some point he was captured by aborigines and held captive for a year. One version of the story says that his captors demanded a ransom, but his family refused to pay. The historian was unable to provide details as to why, but I suspect that Christmas dinners with his family were a bit tense after his escape.

Savage explored the wild Australian interior in a covered wagon. Later he became the owner of the largest cattle ranch down under. He married Annie Bryant while there and eventually had four sons and four daughters. After eleven years in Australia, Savage returned to Jamaica at the age of 34 to grow coffee.

Savage loved firearms and machinery. He was always tinkering on new ideas. He improved the British service rifle. Among his inventions was a torpedo that he sold to the government of Brazil. He also developed the recoilless rifle and the detachable rifle magazine. Savage soon recognized that the future was in America, not Jamaica.

He traveled to New York where he took a job managing the Utica Belt Line railroad in Utica, NY. In his spare time he developed a hammerless, lever-action rifle. He patented the design in 1893 with an unwavering belief that this gun would lead the way into a new world of firearms. With a few investors, he founded Savage Arms in 1894.

It took courage to open a gun company in a field that was dominated by Winchester, Remington, and Colt, but Savage recognized that the newly invented smokeless powder was about to change the rifle world and that the low-pressure, big-bore, rifle cartridges were going to become obsolete. Europe was already using smokeless powder in some cartridges and here in America in 1892, the Army had adopted the .30-40 Krag as our first smokeless powder cartridge. Some sporting rifles were chambered for this new cartridge and Savage could see the future was with small bore, high velocity smokeless powder cartridges.

The first Savage rifle was the Model 1892, although it never got into production. Savage built it to compete for a military contract, which ultimately went to the .30-40 Krag bolt action. Savage continued to work on the design and it evolved into the Model 1895. That rifle won a contract to supply rifles for the New York National Guard. But politics prevailed, as it always has and probably always will in NY, so the contract was cancelled. The poor soldiers were sent off to fight in the Spanish-American war with obsolete single-shot trapdoor Springfield rifles.

With the Model 1895 rifle Savage also introduced a new cartridge, the .303 Savage, a bottle-necked, .30-caliber (.311 diameter bullet) cartridge designed for smokeless powder. Savage created the cartridge with a military contract in mind, but that never developed so he turned to the sporting rifle market.

The Model 1895 rifle, which was reported to have been built by Marlin Firearms Company for Savage, was revolutionary as it was designed to use modern, bottle-necked, smokeless powder cartridges and could handle pointed bullets. The new, smaller .303 Savage cartridge had to compete with the physically larger and more popular cartridges of the day, like the .45-70 Government. At the time the public still equated killing power with physical size and convincing them that technology would allow this diminutive cartridge to perform better than those claiming brute size as their biggest asset would define the struggle for success.

Proving he understood marketing as well as design, Savage started running testimonials in his catalog about the effectiveness of the new cartridge. In those days the catalog not only displayed the products, but it was a source of information for hunters and gun owners in the era before internet, television, or radio. Savage recognized that and ran first person accounts of one-shot kills with the .303 Savage on moose, deer, caribou, sheep and even grizzly bears. But the clincher was from an Alaska resident who killed a whale with a single shot from the .303 Savage.

The Model 1895 evolved into the Model 1899 rifle. That went on to become of the most successful hunting rifles in history. On March 22, 1960 Savage presented the NRA with the one millionth rifle made. No one is quite sure about the total sales number, but it would continue to be made for 38 more years.

A little known fact is that Savage did much of the development work on his early rifles using the machinery at the Remington factory. It’s said that he offered his lever action design to Remington.

Remington and Winchester were owned by the same company at the time and it’s thought that Remington was being locked out of the lever action market by upper management. Winchester didn’t want the competition. It’s what led to Remington developing their semi-auto and pump action line of hunting rifles.

But if you think about it, if those in charge had a little more foresight and had allowed Remington to dip a toe into the lever action market the Model 99 would have had the Remington name on it and Savage Arms might never have existed.

Savage was a restless soul and in 1904 he sold his interest in the company to a group of businessmen and headed west to California. There he started the Savage Tire Company, which grew to employ as many as 2,000 people at one time. It’s said that he invented the radial tire. Later he would have citrus groves, drill for oil and mine for gold. He died in 1938 at the age of 81. His was a life well lived and his lasting legacy would be the Model 99 rifle.

Savage Arms of course continued on without Arthur Savage and by the 1920s it became the largest firearms company in the free world.

In what was probably the start of a Savage tradition, the Model 1899 and 99 rifles were made in a dizzying number of models and configurations. At one time there were more than 700 SKUs of the Savage Model 110 rifle. I am not sure the Model 99 matched that, but it sure tried. Entire books have been written about the various models of this rifle.

There were octagon and half octagon barrels. Carbines and rifles with barrel lengths from 20 inches to 26 inches. The military musket had a 28-inch barrel. The 1895 had a 30-inch tube. There was even a model with a .410 shotgun barrel. Some had a saddle ring and others had deluxe wood. Still others had deluxe wood and fancy engraving. Some rifles had cut checkering and many had no checkering at all. In later years as they tried to cut production costs and ultimately ruined the rifle, some of those stocks had pressed in rifling. Some 99s had straight levers and grips, but most had curved levers with a pistol grip. The forends ranged from Schnabel to a plane Jane straight forend. Some rifles were take-down and others were not. Some came with tang mounted peep sights and others with a very innovative scope mount. It wasn’t until 1950 that Savage started drilling and tapping the rifles at the factory for scope mounting. Some carbine models had a barrel band, but most models did not. The rotary magazine was at some point modified to remove the counting window and the traditional brass was replaced with steel. In 1965 they introduced the “C” model which featured a detachable “clip” magazine. (Yes, they called it a clip. No matter what they tell you on the internet, “clip” was a commonly used term to describe removable magazines in sporting rifles for decades.)

The Savage Model 99 features a hammerless design with a much faster lock time than a traditional lever action. The lever is connected to the breech bolt by a long, wide, curving piece of metal. This gives the rifle a distinctive profile when the action is open. The breech block locks into the action as the lever is closed creating a strong action design that is suitable to modern high-pressure cartridges. While it’s not often noted, the Model 99 action is a controlled round feed design. That means that the extractor captures the cartridge as it exits the magazine and holds it against the bolt face during its travel into the rifle’s chamber. There is a cocking indicator in the form of a pin that sticks out of the top of the action when the gun is cocked and recedes inside when it is not.

The stock is two pieces with separate forend and buttstock. The bottom of the metal action is rounded which makes this rifle a delight to carry in your hand.

The safety is a small catch on the right side of the lever. Pushed forward it locks the lever shut and prevents the rifle from firing. Pulled back it is in the fire position. This is opposite of the more common safeties seen on modern rifles where back is safe and forward is fire. Shooters were smart enough to know the difference back then. Sometime in the mid-sixties a top-tang shotgun style safety was added on some models, but the traditional style lever locking safety remained more popular.

The .303 Savage was the first cartridge introduced in 1895. It was more or less a ballistic twin to the .30-30 introduced the same year. These were the first sporting cartridges designed for smokeless powder and it’s debatable which one was first. The .303 Savage is a handloading-only deal now, but I have always wanted one just because. My friend Lane Pearce just bought one and called to gloat. I hate him.

The .22 Savage High Power was next. It was designed by Charles Newton and introduced in the Model 99 in 1912. The report calls the difference the Family Reliability Gap: 7.6, 13.1 and 14.2 percentage points, respectively. That difference matters for automated work in which an occasional correct answer is insufficient.

The 544 prompts form 136 families, each containing a seed and three controlled variants that preserve the underlying task while changing entities, clause order or visual attributes. The 20 subcategories include object counting, color identification, spatial reasoning, arithmetic, constraint satisfaction and false-premise verification. Of the total, 316 prompts combine text and images, while 228 are text-only.

One public example asks for the colors, in order, of the third letter of each word in a neon sign. The reference answer is green, yellow, white, blue, blue. Such tasks are deliberately easy to explain, while their variants test whether a correct answer depends on a particular phrasing or image.

Researchers evaluated the models zero-shot in a single turn without external tools, drawing six independent answers per prompt and grading the visible final answer with a deterministic Gemini 3.1 Pro judge. The analysis found that among the top 12 models, 61% to 85% of families with any failed rollout had just one problematic prompt while the other three were solved on all six runs.

The report’s failure analysis attributes 73% of wrong-answer rollouts to visual perception, including 31% involving position, direction or paths in images and 23% involving object counting. Among the top nine models, family pass^4 ranged from 83% to 97% on text-only families but fell to 64% to 89% on multimodal families.

The report’s reasoning-effort tests show that moving Gemini 4 Carbon from low to high effort raised family pass^4 by 17.4 percentage points overall, with a 42.4-point gain on Math and Algorithmic tasks versus 3.1 points on Vision tasks. The pattern indicates that additional test-time computation can catch symbolic and procedural slips while leaving visual-perception and variant-robustness failures largely unresolved.

The report’s screening process began with 315 complete five-prompt families. The researchers retained only families whose seed was solved by at least three of four independent human raters, audited ambiguity and variant fidelity, and standardized each surviving family to four prompts. Only 112 prompts in 28 families are public under CC-BY-4.0; another 432 prompts in 108 families remain private to reduce contamination and overfitting. The authors caution that tasks were adversarially selected to expose model blind spots rather than sampled uniformly from everyday queries, and list synthetic imagery, reliance on one Gemini autorater family and seed-only human testing as further limitations. HFE therefore functions as a stress test of elementary reliability; its scores do not estimate how often models will fail across ordinary use.

The Kilonova Catcher network has more than 150 registered members on several continents. Volunteers work side by side with professional astronomers and observatories. They have already contributed to more than 64 official reports shared through NASA’s General Coordinates Network, the system scientists use to share discoveries in real time. Volunteers’ observations have been credited for their contributions by being named authors in peer-reviewed scientific research papers. Kilonova Catcher volunteers also joined NASA’s lunar Impact Flash! project campaign during the Artemis II mission, recording the tiny flashes of light made when meteoroids strike the Moon. The network is getting ready to help follow up thousands of new alerts from NASA’s new Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope.

"Here at Kilonova Catcher, by chasing the light from gravitational-wave events, we’re seeing how a single collision reaches across the universe,” said Cristina Andrade, Kilonova Catcher head of operations at the Minnesota Institute for Astrophysics. “The observatories that make this possible will produce an unprecedented amount of data. We need more human minds, more people with their eyes on the sky and on the data, both literally and figuratively. And more importantly, we need more people to realize they are capable of great feats of science, regardless of their background."

The Kilonova Catcher project is co-led by Andrade and Principal Investigator Dr. Damien Turpin, a researcher at the French Alternative Energies and Atomic Energy Commission (CEA). Damien said, “I am inspired by the expertise and drive of our amateur astronomers. They are always digging for more information and pushing the limits of their understanding. This science could not be done without their contributions.”

Her friend died after giving birth. Now she’s writing legislation to save other moms

By Cassie Shortsleeve Updated Nov. 24, 2026

Mothering America is a regular column from Motherly and Chamber of Mothers sharing moms’ stories from across the country, showing the power of coming together to make change. Each piece is part of a larger effort to ensure every mother and family in the U.S. has the support and care to be well.

In 2008, Lauren Underwood was in graduate school at Johns Hopkins, earning a master’s in public health, when she met a woman with a big, bright smile named Shalon Irving.

She never would have imagined the impact that friendship would have on her life’s work. Underwood would eventually become a U.S. representative for the state of Illinois, fighting to protect moms like Irving, who died before she could see her friend take office.

At the time, Irving, who already had her PhD, was coming back to school for additional training in public health. “We were just like regular girlfriends, passing notes in class and everything,” Rep. Underwood says.

The two—Rep. Underwood, a registered nurse, and Irving, an epidemiologist—stayed in touch through the years. In January 2017, Rep. Underwood served as a senior advisor at the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, and Irving worked for the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). She had also given birth to a beautiful baby girl named Soleil.

Three weeks later, though, Irving wasn’t feeling well. “She went to the doctor several times, explaining that something wasn’t right,” Rep. Underwood remembers. On January 28, Irving died of complications related to high blood pressure. She was 36.

“Shalon had a career focused on ending health disparities, and she was so excited to become a mom,” Rep. Underwood says.

Today, Rep. Underwood is the architect of a series of 14 bills called the Momnibus, which was reintroduced to Congress this spring. The Momnibus addresses “every clinical and non-clinical factor” leading to preventable maternal deaths and disparities in this country; it’s evidence-based, data-driven, and offers states, communities, providers, and nonprofits the resources to help save moms like Irving’s lives. For Rep. Underwood, the Momnibus is personal.

While a member of Congress, Rep. Underwood is also a woman and a friend who, like too many in this country, lost a dear friend and felt a fire to make change.

Rep. Underwood recalls Irving’s funeral as devastating—particularly because many of those in attendance held positions of the highest authority in public health in this country. She remembers the head of the CDC speaking at Irving’s funeral and expressing bewilderment at her death.

For Rep. Underwood, it was no mystery why mothers like Irving die in childbirth and shortly thereafter. As a clinician, she had seen firsthand health disparities in action; as a Black woman, maternal mortality was an unfortunate “phenomenon where it’s just so common when you talk about the maternal crisis.”

Today, we know about 84 percent of pregnancy-related deaths like Irving’s are preventable—meaning that most of the moms who aren’t here today should be here, and could be here, with the proper supports in place. Black moms are more than three times as likely to die in pregnancy or the postpartum months than white women—a disparity that persists across income and education levels.

“People think higher education or higher income is protective. They think that if you are a healthcare worker, you’re going to get the best healthcare. If you know the risks, are self-aware, confident, and have agency, and you can speak up for yourself, then these kinds of things won’t happen to you. Shalon and her death fly in the face of all of those assumptions, and it breaks your heart.”

When she made the decision to run for Congress, Rep. Underwood knew that if she won, maternal mortality would be an issue she’d work on.

In 2018, Rep. Underwood became the youngest Black woman ever elected to Congress. She teamed up with Rep. Alma Adams (D-NC), who also had personal experience with maternal health: Adams’ daughter had experienced a very challenging labor and delivery, known in medical terms as severe maternal morbidity, or an experience that has lasting impacts on a mother’s health.

Together, in April 2019, the two formed the Black Maternal Health Caucus. At a crowded press conference, Rep. Underwood fought back tears. “To be in this moment with the dome of the Capitol behind us and these cameras and people asking questions and it’s this diverse, multi-generational, multi-ethnic, male and female moment of speaking about an issue that was so personal to me—I was just overcome,” she says. “It was a moment of power and resolve. It was an invitation. We were planting a flag, saying, ‘We’re here. We’re going to do this work. We know that there’s an opportunity to bring change.’”

Rep. Underwood didn’t expect the Black Maternal Health Caucus to grow into one of the largest bipartisan caucuses on the Hill. “We left that press conference and our phones started ringing, and it never stopped.”

Rep. Underwood has dedicated her career to ensuring everyone—moms included—has equitable healthcare so we can all feel our best. She’s also dedicated her career to ending the maternal mortality crisis. “We know exactly what to do. We just need to invest, have the resolve, and to say, ‘This is unacceptable. Let’s fix this issue.’”

She also reminds us that Congress works on the issues that members raise. “If a Member comes in and says, ‘This is important to my community, and this is an essential issue that we tackle,’ that’s how solutions advance.” Many of her ideas for bills, she says, come from your stories.

“Often, people think that their story doesn’t matter, and when they come in to advocate, they think they need to have statistics or be a walking encyclopedia,” Rep. Underwood says. “But we are in a relationship business, and connection is currency. The fastest way to build a connection is to share your humanity, tell your story, and invite space for that vulnerability.”

What that means for you: If you have a story to share, Members of Congress are home in their districts through the end of the month, with offices open Monday through Friday and available for meetings. (Not sure how to set up a meeting? Check out Chamber of Mothers’ ‘Moms at Recess’ guide, which includes step-by-step plans and tips for hosting a meeting with Members of Congress.)

Rep. Underwood also underscores the importance of being loud about solutions and progress in maternal health. She highlights the power of doulas, lactation consultants, and community support in maternal health—and the fact that, to date, one of the Momnibus’ 14 bills has been signed into law. Others have passed the House. The Momnibus has also unlocked more than $250 million for states, community organizations, researchers, and providers to save lives. (You can find out which projects are being funded with Momnibus dollars in your community through the Caucus’s Momnibus Money Tracker.)

“We want the news of a pregnancy to be a time of joy and hope and eager anticipation and wonder,” Rep. Underwood says. “This can’t just be a conversation around problems. We want people to know that we’re working on this, that we have their backs, and about the opportunities, help, and the resources that are available.”

NASA’s Northrop Grumman CRS-25 Mission Overview

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

NASA’s Northrop Grumman Commercial Resupply Services 25 mission, or Northrop Grumman CRS-25, will deliver about 11,000 pounds of science and supplies to the International Space Station. The mission will be the third flight of the company’s Cygnus XL, the larger, more cargo-capable version of the company’s solar-powered spacecraft.

The Cygnus XL will launch aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket from the Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. Following arrival to the station, astronauts aboard the space station will use the Canadarm2 to grapple the spacecraft before ground controllers robotically install it to the Unity module’s Earth-facing port for cargo unloading.

In addition to food, supplies, and equipment for the crew, Cygnus will deliver several new experiments aimed at improving drug delivery for cancer and osteoarthritis treatment, predicting cancer spread, advancing treatments for muscle loss, improving radiation sensing, and supporting food production for future long-duration missions. This research includes:

Cardinal Muscle 2.0 uses the microgravity environment to explore potential treatments for muscle loss. Muscle atrophy and strength loss experienced by astronauts after spaceflight share many features with age-related muscle decline on Earth and occur more rapidly in microgravity without exercise. Researchers will use a muscle tissue model to test different Food and Drug Administration-approved drugs used to treat other conditions can be repurposed to prevent or reduce muscle loss.

The DNA Nano B investigation uses microgravity to manufacture tiny, DNA-inspired particles designed to deliver medicine into dense tissues, such as cartilage and solid tumors. Microgravity may enable more uniform particle assembly, helping researchers refine production methods. The results could advance targeted treatments for cancer and osteoarthritis on Earth.

The Active Radiation Environment Sensor (ARES) will test a small radiation monitoring device to support future Artemis program and lunar missions. The system is designed to integrate with spacecraft systems and provide real-time radiation monitoring inside crew living areas. The results could inform radiation monitoring strategies and crew safety measures for future exploration missions.

Encapsulate-1 uses miniature replicas of patients’ tumors to study how cancer cells move, invade surrounding tissues, and potentially spread. Researchers aim to develop a space-based diagnostic tool that could predict cancer progression months or even years in advance, improving early detection and treatment decisions for patients on Earth.

Ohalo III is a new plant growth facility designed to help food crop production on future long-duration missions. The mostly automated system uses innovative water recovery and air exchange technologies to grow a variety of crops. This research could help lead to better food production methods to keep crew healthy and fed on future exploration missions, including those to Mars.

JOining demonstrations IN-Space (JOINS) will mature technologies that allow robotic systems to join materials directly in space. In-space joining could enable large structures to be assembled and integrated after launch rather than being constrained by the size and volume of a launch vehicle. These capabilities may be needed to build and repair large infrastructure, such as structures that will to support future exploration under Moon Base.

Testing technologies in space helps validate their performance in unique environmental conditions, matures their readiness for operational use, reduces technical risk, and builds confidence for future mission applications.
