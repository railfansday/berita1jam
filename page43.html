Skip the Glacier Crowds for Waterton Lakes National Park Right Across the Border

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Crypt Lake Hike - stock photo Crypt Lake hike in Waterton Lakes National Park (Photo: Getty)

A weak summer sun strains to break through a darkening wall of clouds, its rays catching the shale. My boots methodically kick the rock loose as I work my way up a steep slope of scree. I’m slick with sweat, my helmet is chafing, and I’m completely alone in my misery. But the painstaking plod up the ridgeline of Bertha Peak in Waterton Lakes National Park does come with its rewards. When I stop to catch my breath, my gaze sweeps south toward the rugged peaks of neighboring Glacier National Park before flicking north toward my home in Calgary, Canada, and the wild swathes of prairie that define southern Alberta.

Named for Bertha Ekelund, a bold, “wayward” Waterton pioneer whose colorful past included attempts to pass counterfeit money, Bertha Peak reflects something of the untamed wilderness the local legend called home. As I take in the view, it strikes me that this is a landscape that still rewards those willing to venture a little farther—something I’ve striven to do since my first visit to this park as a child.

While hikers flock to Glacier’s trails just across the border, I’m happy to enjoy the same wild landscapes without the crowds. And, at a time when Canada-U.S. relations are increasingly defined by political tension and division, this shared wilderness offers a different perspective. Established in 1932 as the world’s first International Peace Park, Waterton-Glacier reminds us that borders can connect as much as they divide. Peace parks have now been established in 30 places around the world, serving as symbols of cooperation, conservation, and understanding between neighboring nations. For Canadians like me and in-the-know Americans, Waterton offers a quieter way to experience the Rockies, with room to roam and the chance to find solitude in the mountains.

Despite sharing a border with Glacier, the experience of the two parks couldn’t be more different. Waterton Lakes receives a fraction of Glacier’s visitors; in 2025, Glacier saw more than 3 million visitors compared with just half a million in Waterton. You can drive the scenic Red Rock Parkway to the ruddy landscape of Red Rock Canyon without waiting in line, worrying about closures due to congestion, or having to book a shuttle. And while backcountry campsites still require permits, you don’t have to fight the crowds when reservations open to secure a night in the wilderness. A variety of trails from the family-friendly hike to Bertha Falls to the challenging Carthew-Alderson Trail, along with non-hiking pursuits such as kayaking, cycling, and dark-sky viewing, make Waterton an easy addition to a trip across the border.

In 2027, Waterton will also mark the tenth anniversary of the Kenow Wildfire, which burned nearly 20,000 hectares of Waterton in 2017, and its effects are still visible. Where thick stands of trees once stood, charred trunks now rise from lush regrowth, surrounded by the vibrant violet blooms of fireweed. The anniversary provides an opportunity to explore a larger story of shared landscapes, resilience, and cross-border conservation. As governments debate borders, the mountains offer a clear reminder: here, the international boundary is little more than a line on a map, and hiking across it feels like an uncomplicated expression of peace. Even amid the devastation, the fire revealed the resilience of the ecosystem and the importance of giving it space to recover.

A decade later, visitors can see forests regenerating, wildflowers returning and wildlife—including the broad-shouldered grizzlies, shaggy plains bison, and the elusive wolverine—moving through a landscape reshaped by fire. It’s a reminder that conservation doesn’t stop at the border: Waterton and Glacier remain a rare model of international cooperation, where hikers cross between countries through the mountains, wildlife moves freely, and conservation efforts extend across two nations.

The Prince of Wales Hotel, one of Canada’s most famous heritage sites, was built in part to lure booze-starved Americans across the border and away from the restrictions of Prohibition. You know you’ve arrived in Waterton Lakes when the hotel, perched on a grassy knoll above Upper Waterton Lake, comes into view against the mountainous backdrop. With the park’s striking peaks and indigo lakes, the hotel faces stiff competition for the spotlight. Yet the building has become an icon in its own right, symbolizing the enduring character of this under-the-radar corner of the Canadian Rockies. Turning 100 in 2027, the hotel was the brainchild of Louis Hill, a driving force behind the development of Glacier National Park. Hill saw Americans’ frequent trips to Europe as a failure of domestic tourism.

World War I and Prohibition delayed construction, and the hotel finally opened in 1927. When Alberta repealed its prohibition laws in 1924, Waterton gained a new appeal for Americans seeking a legal drink. Nowadays, visitors can take in panoramic views of Upper Waterton Lake and the surrounding mountains while enjoying a meal or drink in the historic lounge before retiring to a room that retains its original charm. The lakeview rooms are well worth the splurge (a night at the height of summer will set you back about $600-$700 CAD / $425-$495 USD). The Royal High Tea is a guest-favorite; a chance to get dressed up and savor the hotel’s old-world charm alongside delicate treats and a steaming pot of tea.

Today the hotel also draws visitors for the wilderness on its doorstep, and trust me, Waterton’s trails will make you earn the view. If you have time for only one adventure, I recommend Crypt Lake, the park’s best-known hike, which begins with a boat ride—expect to pay $35 CAD/$25 USD for a ticket—across Upper Waterton Lake before climbing toward a turquoise lake hemmed in by towering peaks. Near the top, a narrow ledge, a 60-foot ladder, and a short tunnel through the mountain turn a demanding hike into a full-on expedition.

Elsewhere, the landscape changes character. Red Rock Canyon glows crimson along the Red Rock Parkway, its rust-colored stone an unexpected contrast to the surrounding mountains. At Bertha Falls, water spills down a smooth rock face before the trail continues toward Bertha Lake, a high alpine basin that feels a world away from the townsite below. And from Bear’s Hump, the Prince of Wales Hotel looks almost miniature against the vast sweep of mountains and water. These are my go-to family-friendly hikes in Waterton, offering big scenery and memorable journeys without the commitment of a full day on the trail.

For those seeking a longer hike, I also highly recommend Akamina Ridge, where a challenging scramble along an exposed ridgeline leads to sweeping views of Wall Lake, Forum Lake and the rugged peaks straddling the Alberta-British Columbia border. Another favorite of mine is the long Carthew-Alderson traverse, which reveals a wilder side of the park, where the crowds thin and the scale of the landscape takes over.

From the shore of Upper Waterton Upper Lake, the international border is invisible, but out on the water, you realize how arbitrary it really is—mountains, forests, and wildlife pay no mind to the line dividing Alberta from Montana. Boarding the Waterton Shoreline Cruise Co., the world’s first International Peace Park feels like a living reality as the same continuous wilderness stretches across two countries.

Opt for the longer interpretive cruise to Goat Haunt, Montana, which includes a 30-minute stop at the ranger station. You’ll take in sweeping peak views, spot wildlife, and enjoy the novelty of being in two countries in one afternoon without passing a border checkpoint.

Hikers can cross the international border between Waterton and Glacier National Park, but must carry a valid passport and follow customs reporting requirements on both sides. Those entering the U.S. must report through the CBP ROAM app on the day of crossing, while anyone returning to Canada must report their re-entry to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) by phone. A popular option is to hike one-way to Goat Haunt and return by boat.

Waterton’s history isn’t confined to its mountain trails or the old buildings of the townsite either. Believe it or not, you can scuba dive in Waterton’s lakes. The water may be cold, but visibility can be excellent, making the sheltered and scenic waters of Emerald Bay of Upper Waterton Lake particularly appealing to divers. Beneath the surface, at a depth of about 65 feet, lies the wreck of the Gertrude, an early-20th-century paddle wheeler.

If diving in chilly mountain water isn’t your idea of fun, there are plenty of warmer-weather ways to get on the lakes. I recommend renting a kayak, canoe or stand-up paddleboard and getting out on the surface of the water to soak in those mountain views from a different perspective. Parks Canada does require non-motorized watercraft entering Waterton’s lakes to be rented within the park, with options including Blakiston & Company in town and Cameron Lake Boat Rentals on the shore of Cameron Lake.

For a glimpse of Waterton after dark, join a stargazing tour with Dark Sky Guides, co-founded by Keith Robinson, who has been guiding professionally for 25 years and was born and raised in the park. Waterton became an official International Dark Sky Park in 2021, and Parks Canada replaced the community’s streetlights with custom amber fixtures designed to reduce light pollution, using roughly one-third the power of lighting in major cities. The result is an unusually dark sky, making Waterton one of the most southerly places where I have consistently seen the dancing northern lights. The tour offers an introduction to the night sky while also exploring Waterton’s nocturnal wildlife—an activity I’ve found that’s great to do with kids.

If you’ve seen the hit show The Last of Us, you may remember a winter scene that gave you chills just watching it. That’s because winter in Waterton, where part of the show was filmed, is not for the faint of heart. However, I’ve found that if I’m willing to brave wild winds, freezing temperatures, and heavy snow I have the frost-dusted landscape almost entirely to myself.

Winter is also prime time for stargazing and aurora spotting, and with the earlier sunsets, I appreciate that I don’t have to stay up until the wee hours to catch them. By day, the park becomes a snowy playground, with cross-country skiing, snowshoeing, fat biking, wildlife watching, and winter walks. The best thing to do in winter here is to ski or snowshoe approximately two miles toward Cameron Lake. I book a couple of spots in a nearby winter-only six-person backcountry cabin (via the Alpine Club of Canada) and enjoy a family-friendly backcountry experience without avalanche risk. I also love to explore the Red Rock Parkway, which closes to vehicles in winter and becomes a nine-mile route for winter recreation.

While most businesses close for the winter, the charming Kilmory Lodge remains open. In the evenings, I cuddle up beside the roaring fire with a piping hot drink, then settle in for a meal at the Lamp Post Dining Room. The Parks Canada visitor center is also open daily during the snowy season, providing maps, trail and weather information, and winter safety advice.

One important note: there are no grocery stores or gas stations operating in Waterton during winter, so arrive with a full tank and provisions.

American Parents Coalition’s Alleigh Marré warns DSA-backed efforts are embedding socialist ideology in childcare, classrooms and school boards to shape children’s worldviews as early as preschool.

The Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) is taking its message into classrooms, child-care programs and school boards, urging organizers to approach child care "from a socialist perspective" through initiatives such as Socialist Sprouts, and its been an initiative for years, a parental-rights watchdog warns.

In New York City, the DSA's "Red Sprouts" program held a training session in October 2026 to equip organizers to provide "resistance-driven childcare" at demonstrations, meetings and community events.

The training encouraged participants to incorporate political themes into childcare through "radical storytelling", "cooperative games" and age-appropriate discussions.

"There's been a real influx in enthusiasm and their ability and desire to recruit and grow their movement," American Parents Coalition leader Alleigh Marré told Fox News Digital. "What's particularly concerning and insidious, is their direct efforts to apply that to not just adults, but to youth."

Marré said the American Parents Coalition is working to equip parents with resources to identify and question political activism in schools, including through its "Lookout" initiative.

The New York City DSA chapter also established Comrades With Kids, a family-focused organizing group that seeks to be a "political and social home base for working-class families." The group posted photos of DSA-themed coloring pages for children provided at a Zohran for Parents event in September 2025.

The New York initiatives are part of a larger effort by the DSA to incorporate childcare and political education into its organizing activities.

For years, the DSA's National Political Education Committee has offered training on approaching childcare for activists "from a socialist perspective", including activities for children at organizing events. In 2025, the committee outlined plans to develop a "Socialist Sprouts" curriculum geared for children, parents and caregivers involved in the movement.

The organization's efforts to introduce socialist ideas to children date back at least a decade.

In 2016, New York City DSA members launched a Socialist Sunday School for children ages 2 to 6. The program used political songs such as "Union Maid" and "Solidarity Forever," alongside children's books, including "The Lorax," to introduce discussions about corporate greed, environmentalism and organizing against unfairness.

In Ohio, the Cleveland DSA chapter created a children's coloring book to "help kids learn what we work on at DSA." Its illustrations include a version of "The Three Little Pigs" tied to the chapter's housing agenda.

The DSA's online store has also sold children's clothing promoting a "Green New Deal for Public Schools."

The DSA describes much of its childcare programming as a way to make political organizing more accessible to working-class families. Marré, however, argued that introducing socialist ideas to young children raises concerns about political indoctrination.

"The DSA really understands and values that if you can indoctrinate and teach these kids really young, their perceived value of, of socialism, that that's going to stick with kids for a lifetime," Marre said. "It's much easier to teach a child an idea or ideal in their infancy, and when they're very young and they're forming their worldview, and we have seen many efforts to try and capitalize on that."

"There's this really concerted effort to instill an ideology in really, really young children, rather than teaching kids how to think and introducing a breadth of ideologies of religious interests, etc.," Marré said. "It's really focused on socialist and communist aligned ideas."

Beyond its childcare programs, the DSA has also pursued a strategy to expand its influence over public education through local school board elections.

At its 2023 national convention, the DSA adopted an amendment prioritizing the recruitment and support of socialist school board candidates. Rose DuBois, one of the amendment's authors, argued that school board elections could help the organization reach "new kinds of voters, such as parents," while building the infrastructure needed to win higher-profile races.

Between Vision's mental mansion, Jocasta's white void, London, and Madripoor, VisionQuest sometimes feels like watching multiple shows simultaneously, playing into well-worn superhero tropes. VisionQuest's version of the Hex and the Witches' Road is the digital mansion, but the story too often pivots in a new direction.

That said, VisionQuest is still a genuinely compelling story, a worthy end to the Marvel Cinematic Universe's unofficial Scarlet Witch trilogy. Like WandaVision and Agatha All Along, it explores universal themes like love, grief, identity, and parenthood with emotional finesse, all while experimenting with new kinds of storytelling. All three series showcase how inventive superhero stories can be. Marvel doesn't need to be boxed in. Like one of the MCU's other best trilogies, James Gunn's Guardians of the Galaxy, this is the story of the Maximoff family: a family made real and held together by love and heartache. There's nothing more human than that. The best superhero stories make these otherworldly, overpowered beings feel relatable and flawed. That's exactly what VisionQuest and the rest of Marvel's Scarlet Witch trilogy do too.

Matt Mercer's Best Critical Role Quote Is Still the Most Inspiring Line in D&D History

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If you’re still on the fence about being a DM, listen to Mercer

Matthew Mercer is one of the world's most popular Dungeon Masters. Alongside other incredible DMs like Deborah Ann Woll, Brennan Lee Mulligan, and Aabria Iyengar, Mercer has undeniably shaped how players worldwide approach tabletop role-playing games. As Critical Role, the multimedia entertainment company Mercer co-founded, has grown and evolved over the years, one thing has remained constant: Mercer’s willingness to help other tabletop players. He is especially eager to support aspiring Dungeon Masters who hope to develop their skills and become as accomplished as he is, even appearing on the official Dungeons & Dragons YouTube page to provide assistance.

Mercer’s wisdom is well documented across multiple interviews, but only recently, as a fellow DM, did I find the most inspiring quote that everyone (regardless of whether you're a Dungeon Master or a player) should internalize as soon as humanly possible.

In an interview with Variety published in January 2026, Mercer and Mulligan sat down for a sincere conversation about everything and anything D&D. They reflected on their respective work as Dungeon Masters on Critical Role and Dimension 20, discussing the experiences and paths that shaped their careers.

One topic that came up was preparing a tabletop session. Mulligan asked Mercer what his philosophy is for preparing a tabletop session, and Mercer’s answer perfectly defines one of the core beliefs that we think should inspire players on either side of the table in their future games.

“Like many things, it comes with experience, and sometimes you have to fail to learn better.”

After decades of playing tabletop games and building a successful career from them, Mercer’s words capture the spirit of what makes role-playing tabletop games, particularly D&D, so engaging — our desire to perfect how we tell stories through interactive play.

We’ve all heard similar sayings about the benefits of failure, such as Henry Ford’s “Failure is only the opportunity to begin again, this time more intelligently,” or Chris Bradford’s “There is no failure except in no longer trying.”

Giving yourself permission to fail in D&D, however, is one of the most essential lessons a player needs to learn. Without it, the striving for perfection puts tremendous pressure on not just you, but everyone at the table with you.

For example: if you’re the DM, you’ll constantly be assessing whether your players are having fun, or feeling like you have to nudge them in a specific direction, and getting more and more alarmed when that doesn’t turn out to be the case. If you’re a player, you’ll be so busy overthinking whether your decisions will work out that you'll bring the game to a halt.

When I first started DMing, I scripted scenarios that I thought would completely derail the campaign and the story I wanted to tell if the players didn’t follow them to the letter. But that mindset was selfish: this wasn’t just my story, and I wasn't the only player at the table. It led me to make terrible, sometimes unfair calls as I tried to get everything back on track. Half the time, it didn’t work and only made things harder. Why didn’t I stop and pause to tell my players they’d thrown me a curveball once again — and that I, a Brit with only a passing knowledge of American sports, had fumbled it so badly?

The truth was that my anxiety about not doing things exactly as I’d planned in my head was making games feel far more terrifying than they needed to. I didn’t want to be wrong, but being wrong, and having people accept that about you and still want to sit at your table because they want to be part of the story you’re telling together, is one of life’s greatest pleasures.

Regardless of which side of the table you’re on, the fear of failure and the pursuit of perfection can gradually take over and distract from what matters most. When you try so hard to make every decision, encounter, or moment just right, it’s easy to lose sight of the reason everyone gathered to play in the first place: to have fun.

That’s why it’s worth keeping Mercer’s advice in mind. After all, failure is a gift that keeps on giving.

Early Wednesday (Nov. 4), NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory fell out of space, burning up in the sky over Sumatra. It was a fitting, fiery end for a telescope that spent 22 years studying the most powerful explosions in the universe — and, even with Swift gone, its observations will continue to feed scientific curiosity for years to come.

Swift's most important contributions to astronomy are not exactly eye candy. Designed to swivel toward deep-space explosions at a moment's notice, Swift excelled at capturing bright, fleeting and faraway specks of light. The multiwavelength observatory was equipped with optical, ultraviolet (UV) and X-ray instruments, allowing it to catch cosmic bursts of energy across the electromagnetic spectrum, zeroing in on flares of light that other telescopes might miss.

One of its most significant detections was a gamma-ray burst — an incredibly bright blast, likely emitted by a giant star as it exploded in a supernova before collapsing into a black hole. Swift's famous image of GRB 221009A, or the BOAT ‪—‬ short for "brightest of all time" ‪—‬ looks like a speckled orange bull's-eye, caused by waves from the explosion rippling across space.

How the Swift Observatory saw the gamma-ray burst GRB 221009A, which may be the most energetic emission of this kind ever seen.

But Swift also took millions of observations of the nearby universe, including galaxies in our backyard. One of its most jaw-dropping images is this 24-hour exposure of the Andromeda galaxy (M31), located about 2.5 million light-years from Earth.

Andromeda is the closest major galaxy to ours, and it may be destined for a shared fate. Scientists suspect that Andromeda and the Milky Way are drawing closer together and might eventually collide. The merger would add Andromeda's roughly 1 trillion stars to our sky, though there probably wouldn't be any life left on Earth to appreciate it.

In Swift's view of our encroaching neighbor, captured with the observatory's ultraviolet instrument, tens of thousands of Andromeda's stars shine like sapphires set in a sparkling ring.

According to NASA, the observatory detected some 20,000 ultraviolet light sources in this image, captured over two months from May to July 2008. These sources include thousands of dense, hot star clusters scattered across Andromeda's 150,000-light-year-wide ring, as well as the distant galaxy M32 (seen as a bright orb of light to the bottom right of Andromeda's center).

During his 22-year playing career, Javier Zanetti turned out more than 1,000 times for club and country.

The Argentina and Inter Milan great shared a dressing room with some of the greatest players of his generation and played under some of the game’s most influential coaches.

Few, however, left quite the same impression as Marcelo Bielsa, who handed Zanetti 52 international caps during his six-year spell in charge of Argentina.

Bielsa’s reputation for demanding the highest level of commitment from his players is well-documented, but for Zanetti, the former Leeds United manager’s eccentric methods went way beyond mere tactics and training sessions.

"He is a manager who knows how to get the absolute best out of his players,” Zanetti tells FourFourTwo. That was true for me, and it has been true for so many others.”

For Zanetti, what sets Bielsa apart from other elite coaches is his ability to connect on a much deeper, more meaningful level.

“He has a way of reaching something deep inside you, of reigniting that pure love of the game you had as a kid,” he continues.

“It was a wonderful six years working with him with the national team."

During that time, Zanetti’s international career began to flourish, with the defender able to benefit from playing under Bielsa for a sustained period.

A year after Bielsa left the Argentina job, a new era was ushered in when Lionel Messi made his debut in a 2005 friendly against Hungary.

Over the next seven years, Zanetti and Messi would share a pitch for Argentina 37 times, giving the Inter Milan star the opportunity to get a front-row seat for the accession of one of the game’s all-time greats.

“I was lucky enough to play alongside him for six or seven years with Argentina, right at his peak, and when he was just breaking into the national team,” Zanetti adds.

“He could, and still can, produce something that nobody sees coming. Quite simply, the greatest player who ever lived.”
