The Problem American Airlines Ran Into When It Inherited The World's 1st Airbus A321neo

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Robin Guess | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

American Airlines operates one of the largest Airbus A321neo fleets in the world, using the narrowbody aircraft to serve a variety of domestic and international routes. However, not every A321neo in its fleet is quite the same. Among its aircraft are 10 examples inherited from Alaska Airlines, which originally acquired them following its takeover of Virgin America. Although these aircraft offer similar capabilities to American's other A321neos, their original design has created an unusual fleet-management challenge.

The story goes back to 2017, when Virgin became the first airline to receive an A321neo. Several years and two airline ownership changes later, some of the aircraft eventually found their way into American's fleet. The problem is that they were built before Airbus introduced a significant cabin-design change, meaning American's cannot configure them in exactly the same way as its newer A321neos. This difference has also created an unexpected complication for passengers selecting their seats.

The A321neo represented an important development for the European manufacturer, bringing improved fuel efficiency, reduced emissions, and lower noise levels to the largest member of its A320neo family. Virgin became the launch operator of the aircraft, receiving the first example at a delivery ceremony in Hamburg, Germany, in April 2017. The aircraft was powered by CFM International LEAP-1A engines. At the time, Airbus highlighted the aircraft's ability to deliver fuel savings of at least 15% compared with previous-generation models. It also offered greater passenger capacity and longer range, making it an attractive option for airlines looking to improve the economics of their short- and medium-haul operations. Virgin planned to use the aircraft to expand its existing Airbus fleet while maintaining its distinctive onboard experience. The first aircraft was configured with 185 seats and was expected to enter commercial service on May 31, 2017, operating its inaugural flight between San Francisco International Airport (SFO) and Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport (DCA).

Its cabin included Virgin's familiar premium-oriented features, including multiple service classes and personal entertainment systems. The delivery was particularly significant because it marked the arrival of the first A321neo anywhere in the world. However, Virgin's relationship with the aircraft would not last independently for very long. Alaska Air Group had announced its acquisition of Virgin in April 2016, completing the transaction in December that year. The subsequent integration brought the two airlines together under the Alaska brand, creating a new chapter for Virgin's aircraft and outstanding orders.

Following the acquisition, Alaska inherited Virgin's aircraft and commitments, including its A321neo order. The aircraft were therefore no longer being introduced into an independent Virgin fleet, as originally intended. Instead, they became part of Alaska's broader operations, although the transition did not mean that every aircraft immediately entered service with the acquiring airline. Virgin had originally ordered 10 A321neos, and the aircraft subsequently became part of the complicated fleet transition following the acquisition. Only four of the 10 aircraft eventually transferred to American had actually operated for Virgin. This is an important distinction because the aircraft's original configuration was determined before the later ownership changes took place.

American ultimately took these 10 aircraft from Alaska, adding them to its growing Airbus narrowbody fleet. The transfer provided American with additional capacity without requiring it to introduce an entirely new aircraft family. Nevertheless, the inherited aircraft came with a design characteristic that distinguished them from the A321neos that the oneworld carrier had ordered directly from Airbus.

The aircraft were among the earliest A321neos produced, meaning they predated Airbus' Cabin Flex arrangement. Consequently, although they shared the A321neo name and many of the same underlying characteristics, their cabin layouts were fundamentally different. This would become particularly important when American began integrating them into its own passenger operations.

The A321neo has a larger capacity than the smaller A320, allowing American to transport more people without relying on wide bodies.

The central issue concerns Airbus Cabin Flex, a redesigned exit-door arrangement introduced to provide airlines with greater flexibility when configuring the A321's cabin. Rather than relying entirely on the older arrangement of four pairs of exits, the updated design repositioned certain emergency exits, including the overwing exits, to create additional opportunities for seat installation. The earlier aircraft inherited from Alaska retained the original arrangement, featuring eight exits in total, with four on each side of the fuselage. This was broadly comparable to the configuration used on the older A321ceo. While perfectly suitable from an operational and safety perspective, it limited how the airline could arrange seats compared with the newer Cabin Flex-equipped aircraft. The distinction matters because the position of emergency exits influences the number of seats that can legally and practically be installed in an aircraft cabin. Cabin Flex allowed airlines to make better use of available cabin space, supporting higher-density configurations without changing the aircraft's fundamental dimensions.

American's newer A321neos therefore benefit from a different layout that accommodates additional economy seating. The difference is also reflected in aircraft classification. American's newer aircraft are identified as A321-200NX models, while the former Alaska aircraft are listed as A321-200Ns. Although the distinction might appear relatively minor, it represents a meaningful physical difference that American has had to accommodate.

American began introducing the former Alaska A321neos into passenger service from Miami International Airport (MIA) in spring 2024. Rather than immediately undertaking a complete interior refurbishment, the airline initially retained the aircraft's existing Alaska cabin configuration. This allowed the aircraft to enter service while preserving much of the arrangement they had operated with previously. The original layout provided 190 seats, divided between 16 seats in first class and 174 seats in the main cabin. Compared with American's more conventional A321neo configuration, this meant fewer first-class seats but generally more space throughout the passenger cabin. For travelers, the difference was not necessarily negative, as the original arrangement offered a relatively spacious experience. However, retaining the inherited interior was not a permanent solution for an airline operating a large fleet with established cabin standards. American has spent considerable effort standardizing its narrowbody interiors through its Oasis refurbishment program, which introduced consistent seating arrangements and onboard features across numerous aircraft. The former Alaska A321neos would eventually need to undergo a similar transformation.

The first aircraft to receive the Oasis interior was N950XV, which underwent its refurbishment in San Antonio. The project demonstrated that American could bring the aircraft closer to its existing onboard standards, but it could not completely eliminate the differences created by their original construction. The aircraft's exit-door arrangement would continue to determine how many seats could be installed.

The A320 family has gone from strength to strength.

Following their refurbishment, the 10 former Alaska aircraft adopted a 190-seat configuration consisting of 20 first-class seats and 170 economy seats. This brought their premium-cabin capacity into line with American's standard arrangement, while retaining a smaller total number of seats than the airline's newer A321neos. The result is a cabin that looks more familiar but remains structurally different. American's regular A321neo configuration accommodates 196 passengers, including 20 first-class seats and 176 main-cabin seats. The additional six economy seats are possible because these aircraft have the newer Cabin Flex exit arrangement. The 10 inherited aircraft cannot simply receive the same number of seats because their original door positioning restricts the available cabin space.

This creates an interesting situation for American, which effectively operates two different versions of the same aircraft under one fleet umbrella. Both provide the benefits associated with the A321neo, including modern engines and improved fuel efficiency, but their interiors have different seating capacities and exit-row locations. From an operational perspective, this is an additional configuration for the airline to manage. The difference is particularly relevant because the 10 aircraft are not isolated examples that can be easily distinguished by passengers. They operate within the same broader A321neo fleet, and travelers may encounter either version when booking a flight. Although the cabin refurbishment has made the aircraft look more consistent, the underlying structural differences remain impossible to overlook when examining their seat maps.

Perhaps the most noticeable consequence of the unusual fleet arrangement concerns seat selection. American uses a single fleet code for both A321neo configurations, meaning passengers may not immediately realize which version they will be flying. However, the location of the emergency exits is different, creating two distinct seat maps despite the shared aircraft designation. The standard 196-seat A321neo has exit rows at rows 17 and 27, while on the 10 former Alaska aircraft, the corresponding exit rows are located at rows 11 and 24. Consequently, a passenger who selects an exit-row seat based on familiarity with one configuration could discover that the same row number has an entirely different purpose on the aircraft actually assigned to their flight. This can be particularly frustrating for travelers who deliberately pay extra for additional legroom.

A passenger expecting an exit-row seat could instead end up in a standard economy seat, potentially close to the lavatories. The differences extend beyond exit locations, with certain rows also having narrower seats or missing seat positions because of the aircraft's different cabin arrangement. The aircraft registrations provide another way to distinguish the two groups. The 10 former Alaska A321neos carry registrations ranging from N950XV through N959XV, while the remaining aircraft belong to the newer configuration. Ultimately, American inherited aircraft with an important piece of aviation history, but their early-production design has left the airline with a significant configuration difference.

10 Greatest American Martial Arts Stars Of All Time, Ranked

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The martial arts movie genre has changed a lot over the years, and many classics still hold up today. Those older films can feel like time capsules, and fans can find something to appreciate in them even if they lack the stylish, snappy choreography of their more modern counterparts.

Martial arts go hand in hand with the action genre, and audiences of all ages can appreciate stylish brawls that are far more intense and personal than any gunfight. Naturally, the people throwing those punches and kicks are the stars of the show, as making those fights believable is a physical and technical feat.

Few actors in the genre are as famous as Jackie Chan. He's a pioneer in modern martial arts cinema, adding humor and intricate stunt choreography to his films. It's a unique combination, especially compared to other classic fighting movies that are notably more grounded and violent.

Donnie Yen is another legendary actor in the space. He's a phenomenal martial artist and also choreographed many of his own fight scenes. Yen doesn't just know how to throw punches and kicks; he makes them look impactful. He's also starred in numerous classic martial arts movies, including the Ip Man movies.

Several notable American martial artists have also left their mark on martial arts cinema. The actors on this list are all beloved for their roles in a range of movies. They also have genuine martial arts training, and some still practice today.

Mark Dacascos has an expansive filmography with a career spanning decades. Many action movie fans will recognize him as Zero in John Wick: Chapter 3 - Parabellum, but he was also particularly prominent during the 90s and early 2000s.

Dacascos starred as Louis Stevens in Only the Strong in 1993. He's a former special forces officer and attempts to teach troubled students' capoeira. It's a remarkable movie because it was so unusual to see the fighting style in the spotlight at the time. He also starred in Drive and Double Dragon.

Martial arts are especially important to Dacascos because his father, Al Dacascos, founded Wun Hop Kuen Do. He's a lifelong martial artist who has reportedly trained in Muay Thai, Capoeira, and Wushu alongside his father's style.

Jeff Speakman boasts an extensive martial arts background and teaches Kenpo 5.0, which he founded. Speakman also reportedly has a black belt in Goju-Ryu Karate and a 10th-degree Kenpo black belt.

His filmography is less expansive than some other martial artists on this list, though that doesn't take away from his accomplishments in the genre. The Perfect Weapon is arguably his best movie and his breakout role. Speakman stars as Jeff Sanders in a one-man-army revenge story, serving as the ideal platform to showcase his Kenpo skills.

Fans of classic martial arts movies will enjoy The Perfect Weapon because Speakman's style is unique and full of stylish combinations. He also starred in several other 90s movies, including Street Knight and Deadly Outbreak.

Fans of martial arts movies will likely know Jim Kelly best as Williams in Enter the Dragon. His fights in the tournament and with Han are highlights of the movie, and he was a prominent actor in the genre in the 70s.

Away from the silver screen, Kelly won the middleweight title at the Long Beach International Karate Championships in 1971. He's stylish and agile, and his fights look authentic because he really does know how to throw punches and kicks.

Kelly also starred in several movies after Enter the Dragon, including Black Belt Jones in 1974. He also reprised the role in Hot Potato.

Philip Rhee is best known as Tommy Lee in the martial arts drama, Best of the Best. The US Taekwondo team is set to compete against the Korean team. They are the underdogs, but Tommy Lee emerges as their star fighter despite his history with Dae Han (Simon Rhee), a Korean fighter. The original Best of the Best is arguably one of the best American martial arts movies of the 20th century, and James Earl Jones even plays the US coach.

Rhee co-wrote Best of the Best, produced Best of the Best II, and made his directorial debut with Best of the Best 3: No Turning Back. He also starred as Tommy Lee in each movie.

Interestingly, Rhee is also a proven martial artist outside the movies. He began studying at an early age and holds black belts in multiple disciplines, including Taekwondo, Hapkido, and Kendo.

Cynthia Rothrock has an extraordinary martial arts record. She was a five-time world champion in forms and weapons between 1981 and 1985. She also holds multiple black belts in styles including Taekwondo and Tang Soo Do.

That extensive training translates beautifully onto the silver screen. Yes, Madam! is arguably one of her most important movies, starring Rothrock alongside Michelle Yeoh. Rothrock also led China O'Brien, a martial arts action movie where she plays a police officer forced to rely on her skills when she moves back to her quiet town in Utah.

Cynthia Rothrock is so influential in the genre that some fans call her the Queen of Martial Arts Movies. She has appeared in dozens of films, including more recent projects such as 2024's The Last Kumite.

Wesley Snipes is a beloved action star, best known for his portrayal of Blade, who's part human, part vampire. In the original Blade movie, he attempts to stop a vampire called Deacon Frost (Stephen Dorff) from completing an ancient ritual.

Blade is a flashy, stylish fighter, and quite convincing, as Snipes has martial arts training. In fact, he reportedly started at 12 years old and studied Goju-Ryu and Shotokan Karate. Snipes has also allegedly studied other styles, including Capoeira.

While Wesley Snipes certainly has real-world martial arts experience, his films are more action-focused than strictly fighting movies. One exception is Undisputed, which is more of a prison drama, but Snipes looks so authentic in his fight scenes thanks to his training.

Don 'The Dragon' Wilson is widely considered a kickboxing legend. He's an 11-time world kickboxing champion with multiple Hall of Fame entries for his achievements in the ring. His nickname isn't unfounded either, as he studied Pai Lum White Dragon Kung Fu.

Wilson is a kickboxing icon, and while he's less known among casual moviegoers, many martial arts fans will know him from the long-running Bloodfist franchise. He also starred in Ring of Fire as Johnny Woo, which started a trilogy.

Wilson's competition fighting experience translates well into his movie action scenes. He remains a respected name in kickboxing, even if his films are less well-known among general audiences.

Michael Jai White is arguably an underrated modern action star. He's starred in multiple martial arts movies, though Blood and Bone is a fan favorite. He stars as Isaiah Bone in the 2009 movie, an ex-convict who enters an underground fight circuit and quickly makes a name for himself.

The fights in Blood and Bone are stylish and brutal, and White has also earned impressive accolades outside the movies. He started training in martial arts at a young age and holds black belts in several Karate styles. White even demonstrates some of his techniques on his official YouTube channel.

Michael Jai White's moves look incredible in his films. The choreography is exceptional, and brawls feel intense and grounded.

Chuck Norris became an action movie star and a household name throughout his career. He has well-documented martial arts roots and a proven track record in contact sports.

Norris was also an important part of pop culture, with memes and even books telling grandiose tales about his strength. His real-world performances were just as impressive, as he held the World Professional Middleweight Karate Championship title for six consecutive years. Norris also trained alongside Bruce Lee and played Colt in The Way of the Dragon.

Chuck Norris has an expansive filmography and was one of the biggest action stars of the 80s. He also founded a martial arts system called Chun Kuk Do.

Bruce Lee was born in California in 1940 and is widely regarded as one of the most influential martial arts movie stars of all time. His contribution to the genre cannot be overstated, and his influence on fight choreography and action cinema can still be felt decades later. He also founded his own combat system called Jeet Kune Do.

Lee starred in several movies, though Enter the Dragon is his most popular. He enters a tournament on Mr. Han's (Shih Kien) island as a competitor. However, he's also there to gather evidence, as British authorities suspect Han is running a drug operation there.

Bruce Lee's fight scenes are incredible. His speed is utterly jaw-dropping, particularly during his encounter with Han's bodyguard, Oharra (Robert Wall). His bout with Chuck Norris in The Way of the Dragon is another classic scene and is among the most famous martial arts movie showdowns of all time.

Petition to Save Mass Effect Dev BioWare to Be Considered by Canada's Government

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A petition to save BioWare is set to be presented before the Canadian government.

The country's law states that any public petition with at least 500 signatures must be added to the House's agenda — and this fan-driven effort has amassed over 2,300 at the time of writing.

It argues that the Mass Effect and Dragon Age developer is "one of Canada's most important cultural exports to the world in the 21st century, selling tens of millions of units of their video games globally". It's a fair point.

"BioWare has been shuttered by its American parent company Electronic Arts, now owned by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund and Jared Kushner's Affinity Partners, and BioWare's closure represents an immense loss to and possibly a targeted attack on Canada's cultural legacy," it rather pointedly continues.

Suggested solutions include providing the company with the "necessary" funding to secure its independence, while also, ideally, recovering IPs like Mass Effect and Dragon Age from EA.

The petition concludes that the government should move to "protect Canadian businesses and cultural institutions from being dictated by American and foreign corporate interests."

The closing date for signatures is in March next year.

Godzilla Minus Zero Ending Explained: Where The Franchise Goes From Here

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Full spoilers for "Godzilla Minus Zero" follow.

For the longest time in the character's 72-year history, the "Godzilla" films were surprisingly light on continuity. During the Showa era films produced by Toho Studios during the 1950s through the 1970s, the most common recurring characters in the movies were the kaiju themselves. When the Heisei era occurred between 1984 and 1996, they established a continuity that was a bit more contiguous, but not much. The Millennium era became even more piecemeal continuity-wise, with most entries using the original 1954 "Godzilla" as a starting point but erasing everything else until the latest film (similar to how the "Halloween" franchise continuity has operated before). Even the MonsterVerse films produced by Legendary Pictures function most similarly to the Heisei era in terms of continuity, while Toho's 2016 "Shin Godzilla" is basically a one-shot.

All of this is to say that the "Godzilla" franchise is wildly unpredictable where canon is concerned, with each film or groups of films essentially treating it piecemeal. It's exceedingly rare that a true sequel has been made to a "Godzilla" film in the past, where a prior movie's cast and filmmakers have returned for a second round. That milestone has finally been achieved with "Godzilla Minus Zero."

The movie is an unabashed sequel to 2023's "Godzilla Minus One," as it's once again written and directed by Takashi Yamazaki. As such, it's a beautifully rich experience previously unseen in most "Godzilla" movies, one which builds from its predecessor in a big way. "Minus Zero" isn't just another "Godzilla" adventure, but a movie which continues the "Minus" series' character arcs and themes, and its emotional, somewhat cliffhanger-style conclusion indicates that the story may not be over yet.

"Godzilla Minus One" takes place between 1945 and 1947, during the immediate aftermath of World War II. Japan's loss of the war has left it hurting in more ways than one, with the country having to muddle through dire straits both physical and emotional. This is represented by the ensemble of characters we meet, chief among them Kōichi Shikishima (Ryunosuke Kamiki), a kamikaze pilot who deserts his suicidal duty, Noriko Ōishi (Minami Hamabe), a woman who takes it upon herself to rescue and raise an orphan girl, Akiko (Sae Nagatani), and Kenji Noda (Hidetaka Yoshioka), a Naval weapons engineer.

All three adults come to view the superpowered creature Godzilla as emblematic of their personal struggles. For Noda, Godzilla must be stopped for Japan to have a future. For Noriko, Godzilla is something to be survived. For Shikishima, Godzilla is the personification of his guilt, as well as a force of death and destruction who must be vanquished.

After Noriko's apparent demise during a Godzilla attack on Ginza, Noda spearheads a civilian-run mission in which a couple of Navy destroyers will alternately sink and raise Godzilla at dangerous speeds. As a failsafe, Shikishima has volunteered to fly one of the last available Shinden fighters to lure Godzilla out to sea. However, his real plan is to fly the plane, loaded with explosives, directly into Godzilla's mouth. He does so, but uses an ejection seat to escape at the last minute, proving he's overcome his guilt and has decided to live. Following the Godzilla victory, Shikishima learns that Noriko has in fact survived, and he and Akiko go to visit her in the hospital. As Noriko tearfully reunites with her new family, a mysterious black mark on her neck shimmers.

"Godzilla Minus Zero" picks up in 1949, when Noda is informed of another giant monster attacking Japan: Ghidorah. As Noda and his new advisor, meterologist Makoto Aoyama (Masami Nagasawa), plan to kill Ghidorah with lightning, Noda asks Shikishima for help. Shikishima tries to stay out of the battle for the sake of his now-wife Noriko and adopted daughter Akiko, especially as he and Noriko are the only ones who know that she survived Ginza due to being infected with Godzilla's cells, which might lead to her death soon.

Yet when Ghidorah survives the initial attack and a now-regenerated Godzilla reappears, Noda and biologist Kanji Murakami (Min Tanaka), a survivor of the Hiroshima bombing, decide the best plan is to lure Godzilla and Ghidorah into a deathmatch fight, with the humans killing the victor. To do this, Murakami has a device which means only Noriko and her G cells can "call" Godzilla.

Shikishima and Noriko agree to the plan, unaware that Murakami intends to later use Noriko as a way to get revenge on the USA. Godzilla and Ghidorah fight in Ginza, and the former eventually triumphs, though at the expense of Noda's life. When the Japanese army gives orders that Godzilla is not to be killed, Shikishima and Noriko mutiny against Murakami. However, Murakami reveals that Noda recently convinced him of the error of his ways, and the couple are allowed to escape while the scientist destroys his machine and his work.

Unfortunately, Murakami's good deed is punished when the US army arrests him. Meanwhile, Shikishima and Noriko try to abandon Akiko, knowing they're doomed to a life on the run from the authorities. Yet they ultimately can't leave her behind, and ride off as a family to an uncertain future.

"Godzilla Minus Zero" is a true second chapter in a continuing story. This is a fairly unprecedented occurrence in the Godzilla franchise, not just in the way the returning characters are built upon and expanded, but also in the way Takashi Yamazaki continues the themes from "Minus One." Rather than jumping ahead in time too far, "Minus Zero" is still a story of post-war Japan, and nowhere is this more evident than in Kanji Murakami's character.

Although Godzilla is shifted toward a more heroic light, as has happened many times over the franchise, he's still presented as morally dubious. If, in "Minus One," Godzilla stood for the sins of the Japanese (if not all humanity) come to punish them, "Minus Zero" looks at Godzilla as the country's answer to the atomic bomb: a destructive and ultimately uncontrollable weapon which should only be used as a last resort, if ever.

In addition to the war allegory, "Godzilla Minus Zero" takes the characters of "Minus One" beyond their absolution in that film. Much of "Minus Zero" concerns whether the happy (or relatively happy) future the survivors of that movie fought so hard for can even happen, as everything from the Ghidorah attacks to the potentially deadly G Cells in Noriko's body appears like an insurmountable threat. Yet while unfortunate consequences are unavoidable, Yamazaki's optimism ultimately shines through, especially in the way the bonds between the characters mean that all hope is never lost. Even the supporting cast from "Minus One," though given smaller roles here, contribute to this theme. The implication is that Japan, if not humanity, can have a bright future if everyone supports each other and serves their better natures rather than being selfish or hateful.

If "Godzilla Minus Zero" were to conclude the "Minus" continuity, then both "Godzilla Minus One" and "Minus Zero" would serve as a fully satisfying tale. Still, Takashi Yamazaki does leave "Minus Zero" on several cliffhangers, or at least unresolved questions. The ending leaves the ultimate fate of many characters uncertain, chief among them Murakami and the Shikishima family. In the case of the former, it seems likely that the US army intend to make Murakami work for them to get Godzilla under American control.

In the case of the latter, the Shikishimas are now going to be on the run, and it's unclear what their plan is to evade the government(s). It's neat how these films have set up so much good material with the human characters that there's enough interest in what happens to them before one even considers the kaiju.

But oh, the giant monsters! We cannot forget about them. Now that Yamazaki has introduced Ghidorah, the door is open for any other Toho-verse kaiju to turn up. In terms of the themes that the "Minus" films are dealing with, it would feel appropriate for other heavily allegorical beasts like Rodan or Hedorah to make their appearance. Although the MonsterVerse's "Godzilla vs. Kong" recently used Mechagodzilla as humanity's warmongering response to Godzilla, such a characterization would fit snugly into the "Minus" films, too. And, while "Minus Zero" only contains a fantasy sequence of Godzilla attacking NYC, there's the chance that the action could expand beyond Japan for real. While it's not clear whether there will be another "Minus" film at all yet, there's certainly no dearth of possibilities for one to explore.

Janus Electric secures battery supply deal for its battery-swap semi truck

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Australian conversion specialists Janus Electric have been celebrating a growing slate of orders for their Class 8 electric truck conversions, which ditch diesel in favor of advanced battery-swap electric power – but exactly how they’d deliver all those trucks was still a question. Now, we have an answer: Electrovaya will supply the batteries.

The concept of dropping an electric power unit into a Class 8 semi makes perfect sense, as most of these trucks are built as gliders. That is, rolling body and chassis without an engine and transmission that’s ready to receive power from Cummins, Detroit, International, or Volvo Penta. That’s the role that Janus Electric sees itself occupying, and it seems to be working.

Janus’ reservation book crept past 70 units this past summer, helped along in part by California’s HVIP Program and the US EPA’s Diesel Emission Reduction Act (DERA) Grants. When the HVIP voucher is combined with the Port of Los Angeles’ “Plus” support, customers can received up to $166,000 per truck conversion. Factor in the massive fuel savings of electric and the classic good looks of square-nose Western Star truck body, and it’s easy to see why they’d be a popular choice.

As Jerry Seinfeld taught us however, it’s one thing to take a reservation – it’s another thing to deliver. To help make it happen, Janus has inked a battery supply deal with Electrovaya that will see a $26 million initial purchase orders for Electrovaya battery packs to demand for Janus’ products in the US and Canada, along with the battery company purchasing approximately $1.4 million in Janus stock with (what seems to be) actual money.

“Electrovaya is an important partner to Janus Electric, and we’re particularly pleased it has decided to invest directly in our business,” explains Ben Hutt, Managing Director and CEO of Janus Electric. “The investment is a strong vote of confidence in our technology and approach to electrifying heavy transport.”

The Janus Electric-repowered trucks carry a stout, 480 hp electric motor drawing power from a standard 310 kWh battery pack, with up to two battery packs (one on each side) of the truck. In practice, that gives the Janus trucks up to 370 miles of loaded range. After that, the batteries can be DC fast charged in under 90 minutes, or swapped for fresh ones in about four minutes without too much in the way of specialized equipment. That’s not just Janus’ killer app, it means that a battery supply deal like this one with a strategic partner who’s invested in the fledgling company’s success is such a huge deal.

“Reliable battery supply is critical to our growth,” adds Hutt, “and Electrovaya’s expanding North American manufacturing capabilities will be important as we scale. Both companies see considerable opportunity in this market, and we look forward to building on our partnership.”

For their part, Electrovoya executives seem to know their role here, and seem excited to be a part of what Janus is doing. “Class 8 trucking is one of the most exciting and demanding frontiers in electrification, and Janus has demonstrated real commercial traction with fleets in the US, Canada and Australia,” said Dr. Raj Das Gupta, CEO of Electrovaya. “Janus has developed a practical solution to one of the major challenges facing heavy transport, enabling diesel-powered trucks to be converted to electric without compromising their commercial operations.”

Electrovaya will be expanding its American manufacturing capacity with lithium-ion cell, module, and battery pack production at its new, potentially BABA-compliant facility in Jamestown, New York, which is expected to come online in 2027.
