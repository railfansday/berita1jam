7 Near-Perfect Showtime Series With 10 Episodes Or Fewer

📅 Nov 28, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Since the age of streaming, Showtime has found itself outpaced by HBO, and its streaming service efforts were short-lived and have, at this point, been completely subsumed by its parent Paramount+. There are still original shows under the Showtime banner, however, and these seven are worth subscribing for.

The Showtime miniseries Picture Windows premiered in 1995 and ran for six episodes. An anthology, each episode of Picture Windows is a short film based on a different iconic painting. There's one based on Primavera by Sandro Botticelli, another based on Ballet Rehearsal by Edgar Degas, and so on.

Each episode also features a notable person behind the camera. Peter Bogdanovich, Joe Dante, and John Boorman are credited in the series. It's a beautiful, thoughtful miniseries, and though 30 minutes may sound like not enough time these days, it allows for sharp, quick-moving stories.

It's a series filled with some impressive stars as well, with Alan Arkin, Sally Kirkland, Robert Loggia, Steve Zahn, and John Hurt all making appearances. It only takes about three hours to watch, but it's also the kind of show that makes you pause after each episode and consider everything you've seen.

Out of Order is a six-episode miniseries from Donna and Wayne Powers about a man, Mark Colm (Eric Stoltz), who is in an unsatisfying marriage with Lorna (Felicity Huffman). Kim Dickens, William H. Macy, and Justine Bateman also star. The series was edited into a feature film for the DVD.

Mark decides that his midlife crisis will involve a woman named Danni (Dickens) and imagining his life as a sort of reality show. Lorna ends up with Steven (Macy) and begins drinking a lot. There's more to the series than that, though, and it ends up being a moving story of love lost and gained.

There's a meta quality to the series that may divide viewers, but the story is told with such brutal honesty that even if Out of Order's quirks are not your cup of tea, you'll be swept up in the tale. It takes a nuanced approach to relationships and has empathy for everyone involved.

Escape at Dannemora is a seven-episode miniseries based on the real-life 2015 Clinton Correctional Facility escape. Patricia Arquette stars as Joyce "Tilly" Mitchell, a prison worker at Clinton who is in a loveless marriage and is having an affair with David Sweat (Paul Dano), a convicted murderer.

Her relationship with Sweat eventually results in her willingness to help Sweat and another convicted murderer, Richard Matt (Benicio del Toro), escape Clinton, with disastrous consequences for all involved. The series begins with Tilly's interrogation after the manhunt is over, and backtracks.

The series takes its time to set up all the pieces that lead to the main event, and for a show with a promised prison break, there is a lot more character work than you may initially expect. It's all critical, though, and when the escape does happen, the groundwork that goes into the beginning of the show pays off.

Patrick Melrose is a five-episode series starring Benedict Cumberbatch as the wealthy, titular Englishman, Patrick Melrose. The series tracks five decades of Patrick's life, starting in the 1960s and ending in the 2000s. Over this time, Patrick attempts to overcome the demons imparted to him by a childhood of parental abuse.

The series uses flashbacks to help us understand Patrick's difficult upbringing and also tear down the tapestry of upper-class British society. Based on Edward St Aubyn's semi-autobiographical novels, Patrick Melrose is unflinching in its look at childhood trauma and its reverberations felt decades later.

It's a powerful series, with a moving ending that may not exactly satisfy, but is the right choice for such a hard show. It's a scathing look at aristocracy, and is unafraid to point the finger at societal problems as much as it is at personal problems. Cumberbatch is doing some of his best work as the complicated, haunted Melrose.

The Good Lord Bird is a seven-episode Western series based on the 2013 novel of the same name by James McBride. The series is based on the real-life exploits of John Brown, an abolitionist during Bleeding Kansas, whose anti-slavery efforts eventually resulted in the famous raid on Harper's Ferry.

Joshua Caleb Johnson stars as Henry "Onion" Shackleford, a fictional enslaved boy who escapes his captors and joins Brown's (Ethan Hawke) retinue. Onion comes to trust and believe in the eccentric John Brown, even as he appears to become more erratic over the years, and his fight against slavery more desperate.

It's a masterclass performance from Hawke, who imbues Brown with a supernatural fury and religious fervor that might make him seem crazy to a modern audience, but is a symbol of his disgust and revulsion at the practice of slavery. It's a thrilling, even fun series, with sobering moments that hit you hard in the chest.

Fellow Travelers is a seven-episode series based on Thomas Mallon's 2007 novel of the same name. Matt Bomer and Jonathan Bailey star as Hawk Fuller and Tim Laughlin, respectively, two men who begin a romance at the height of McCarthyism in America, tracing their relationship over the decades.

From Adolescence to Mare of Easttown to the classic Roots, these incredible TV miniseries didn't need a lot of episodes to make their point.

Hawk is a World War II veteran and State Department official who is terrified of being outed, while Tim is a devout Catholic and hard-line supporter of McCarthy whose worldview is suddenly flipped upside down after his encounter with Hawk. Their relationship winds through the tumultuous '60s and beyond.

It's an epic and full depiction of gay romance, including everything from Fire Island to the AIDs epidemic, and each moment in history is brought to life, often sadly, with the incredible performances of Bomer and Bailey, who share a moving chemistry. It's an expansive series that uses Hawk and Tim's beautiful relationship to personalize a story we don't see often enough.

A Gentleman in Moscow is a Paramount+ co-production that runs eight episodes and is based on Amor Towles' 2016 novel. Ewan McGregor stars as Count Alexander Ilyich Rostov, an aristocrat who returns to Russia from Paris after being exiled following the events of the October Revolution.

As an aristocrat, Alexander is under constant surveillance from the Russian state, but over the decades, he manages to make a life for himself despite the rules and restrictions placed on him. He meets and falls in love with actress Anna Urbanova (Mary Elizabeth Winstead), and the two attempt to survive the upheaval in the USSR.

McGregor and Winstead provide a winning combo, one that we saw once before in Fargo, but is much sweeter this time around. It's a beautiful show, where the history is secondary to the relationship between the pair, and it makes for one of Showtime's most moving, short shows.

Marvel Officially Confirms Another A-List Hero Struggles With Alcohol Addiction

📅 Nov 28, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

This article discusses alcoholism and problematic drinking.

Marvel has given one of its most recognizable Guardians of the Galaxy a surprisingly revealing character moment. In the most recent issue of The Amazing Venom #3, Rocket Raccoon openly acknowledges being drunk during a dangerous situation. His explanation for why he is still managing to function, however, makes the exchange considerably more concerning than an ordinary joke.

Rocket describes himself as “high-functioning” when Venom questions his drinking, suggesting that alcohol consumption is something he has learned to accommodate rather than confront. Although Marvel does not explicitly diagnose Rocket with alcohol use disorder, the admission places him alongside Tony Stark and Carol Danvers, two prominent heroes whose comic book histories have explored alcoholism.

The moment appears in The Amazing Venom #3, written by Jordan Morris, with art by Luke Ross and colors by David Curiel. The issue follows Fred Myers, formerly Boomerang, and the symbiote Passenger as the unlikely duo Comeback travel toward Klyntar. Their journey brings them to Knowhere, where they stop to refuel before continuing through space.

While there, Fred and Passenger cross paths with Spider-Man, who is bonded with the Venom symbiote. The group visits a local bar, where Rocket is among the patrons. The establishment draws a strange crowd whose members worship a deity and embrace an unruly lifestyle. Fred and Passenger encounter the gathering while looking for fuel, giving the stop an oddball atmosphere.

That changes when violence breaks out and the bar begins to collapse. Venom and Rocket move to get people out, and Venom points out that Rocket is drunk too. Rocket does not deny it or claim he has only had a drink; he answers that he is “high-functioning.” The exchange is brief, but its meaning is hard to overlook.

Rocket’s wording frames his drinking as something he believes he can manage while continuing to act, fight, and make decisions. That is the implication behind the label, because a person can keep up appearances or meet some responsibilities while alcohol use is still causing harm. The phrase is conversational, however, not a clinical diagnosis or a reliable measure of safety.

The comic does not explain how often Rocket drinks, whether he has tried to stop, or what consequences alcohol has had for him outside this scene. It therefore leaves important questions unanswered. What it does establish is that Rocket is intoxicated during an emergency and appears comfortable treating that condition as compatible with doing his job.

That contradiction gives the gag a sharper edge. Rocket’s confidence does not make him sober, and being able to function in one dangerous moment does not mean alcohol is harmless or under control. The scene also avoids turning the admission into a lengthy speech, allowing a short exchange to reveal a potentially serious pattern without resolving it.

Marvel has explored alcohol addiction through Tony Stark, whose struggle became important to the celebrated “Demon in a Bottle” storyline in Iron Man #120–128, published from 1978 to 1979. The arc showed drinking undermining Stark’s judgment and responsibilities, and the character’s later stories revisited recovery and relapse. His wealth, intelligence, and armor could not simply erase the problem.

Carol Danvers also confronted alcoholism during her time as Warbird with the Avengers. Her drinking escalated as she struggled with personal trauma and diminished powers, damaging her relationships with teammates. In Iron Man vol. 3 #25, published in 2000, Carol acknowledges her addiction at an Alcoholics Anonymous meeting with Tony’s support, placing recovery at the center of her story.

Rocket’s moment is much less developed than either of those arcs, and readers should not mistake one line for confirmation of a full addiction storyline. Even so, the comparison is meaningful, because Marvel is showing another powerful hero treating drunkenness as ordinary and manageable. Whether future issues explore the consequences or leave it as dark humor remains to be seen.

Stop Passing Paragraphs Between Your Agents

📅 Nov 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you've built a multi-step agent workflow, you've probably seen this. Step one does its job and returns a nice paragraph. Step two reads that paragraph and does its job. It all works in testing. Then someone tweaks the first prompt, the paragraph comes out a little differently, and step two starts doing something slightly wrong. No error, no crash. Just quietly worse results.

The problem isn't the model. It's that the contract between your steps is prose, and prose has no shape you can check.

The fix is boring and it works: make each step return structured data, and validate it before the next step runs.

Here's what that looks like with Pydantic:

Most model APIs and agent frameworks now let you ask for output that matches a schema directly. CrewAI has output_pydantic on tasks, and the major providers have structured or JSON output modes. The validation step is still worth keeping, because "supports a schema" and "always returns valid data" aren't quite the same thing.

A few things that make this work well in practice.

Only include fields the next step actually uses. It's tempting to ask for everything, but every extra field is one more thing the model can get wrong and one more thing to keep in sync.

Use Literal types wherever you can. "urgent" vs "Urgent" vs "high priority" is exactly the kind of drift that breaks routing logic downstream.

Let it fail loudly. If a required field is missing, stop right there with a clear error. A failure at step one with a good message is far easier to debug than a weird result at step four.

Retry once, not forever. One retry with the validation error included in the prompt fixes a lot of cases. If it fails twice, send it to a person or a dead-letter queue instead of looping.

Keep a summary field for humans. The structured fields are for your code. A short free-text summary is still useful in logs and for whoever reviews flagged items.

The side benefit is testing. Once each step returns a typed object, you can write normal assertions against it. A small set of fixed inputs with expected fields turns "does the agent still work after my prompt change?" from a vibe check into a test run.

None of this is new. It's the same reason we use typed function signatures instead of passing strings around. Agents just make it easy to forget.

Building a modern website requires more than choosing an attractive design or installing a theme. A successful website should provide a good user experience, load quickly, work across different devices, and make its content easy for search engines to understand.

Developers often focus on visual design first and consider performance and SEO later. However, these elements should be part of the development process from the beginning.

In this tutorial, we'll explore how to build a better website using HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and practical SEO techniques. We'll also look at code examples that can be adapted to WordPress and other website development projects.

HTML provides the foundation of every website. Using semantic HTML elements makes your code easier to maintain and helps browsers and assistive technologies understand your content.

Instead of using generic containers for everything, use elements such as header, nav, main, section, and footer.

Notice that the page has one primary heading and a logical structure. This makes the content easier to navigate and maintain.

A website should work properly on desktop computers, tablets, and smartphones. Responsive design allows the layout to adjust automatically based on the available screen size.

SQLRPGLE: embedded SQL done properly

📅 Nov 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 28 • Originally published at rainierio.dev

Embedded SQL in RPG is the default way to access Db2 for i in new code. It is also where I most often see sloppy code: no status checks, SELECT *, and cursors left open. This article gives a pattern you can paste into a real program.

SQL statements start with exec sql and end with a semicolon, and can span lines. The source member type is SQLRPGLE.

Same program in fixed format (what you will meet in most shops):

Fixed format has no exec sql ...; one-liner: the statement sits between C/EXEC SQL and C/END-EXEC on C+ lines (/ in column 7, EXEC SQL in columns 8-16). SET OPTION goes there like any other statement.

SET OPTION is not executable; the precompiler reads it. IBM requires it to precede every other SQL statement in the source, and you can code only one, so put all options in that one statement. It lets the source carry its own compile options.

Prefix RPG variables with a colon inside SQL.

Same program in fixed format (what you will meet in most shops):

A host structure maps columns by position, not name, so the select list order must match the structure. Always list columns explicitly.

Same program in fixed format (what you will meet in most shops):

If no row matches you get SQLSTATE 02000 (SQLCODE 100). If more than one matches, you get an error (SQLSTATE 21000, SQLCODE -811) unless you add fetch first row only. Decide which you mean. A null in a column needs a null-indicator variable (:cust.balance :balInd) or you get SQLCODE -305.

The precompiler gives you sqlstate and sqlcode automatically. Check after every statement. SQLSTATE is the portable value; SQLCODE is Db2-specific but handy.

Same program in fixed format (what you will meet in most shops):

DSPLY needs a variable in fixed format, hence MSG. DSPLY also cuts the message at 52 characters, so keep it short. If CHECKSQL is called earlier in the same source, add a D CHECKSQL PR N prototype first.

Warnings start with 01, not found is 02, and 00 is success. Anything else (class 22, 23, 42 and so on) is an error you must handle. For details use GET DIAGNOSTICS CONDITION 1 :msg = MESSAGE_TEXT (the CONDITION 1 is required for condition items like MESSAGE_TEXT, RETURNED_SQLSTATE and DB2_MESSAGE_ID). Capture sqlstate into a variable before you run it, or any other statement, because every later SQL statement can overwrite what you were about to test.

Use a cursor when you expect many rows: declare, open, fetch in a loop, close.

Same program in fixed format (what you will meet in most shops):

DECLARE is not executable, but it must appear in the source before the OPEN that uses it, and the host variables it references must be declared before it. closqlcsr(*endmod) closes cursors when the module ends. The ILE default is *ENDACTGRP, so a cursor you forget to close stays open until the activation group ends, and the next call's OPEN fails with SQLSTATE 24502 (already open). Close cursors yourself anyway.

Fetching a block of rows in one call reduces overhead. Use an array data structure.

Same program in fixed format (what you will meet in most shops):

SQLERRD(3) (short name SQLER3) holds the number of rows fetched. The last block is usually short: Db2 then returns SQLSTATE 02000 (SQLCODE 100) and the rows it did find, so process SQLERRD(3) rows first and stop afterwards. Reading it straight after the FETCH needs no extra statement; GET DIAGNOSTICS :fetched = ROW_COUNT gives the same count but is one more statement to get wrong.

When the statement text is built at run time, use PREPARE and EXECUTE. Use parameter markers (?), never concatenated user input, to avoid SQL injection.

Same program in fixed format (what you will meet in most shops):

The statement literal is split with + because a single literal would run past column 80.

For a one-off statement without parameters, EXECUTE IMMEDIATE :stmt is enough. A dynamic SELECT is prepared, then used with DECLARE CURSOR FOR s1 and OPEN c USING :value.

With COMMIT(*NONE) every statement is permanent immediately. With COMMIT(*CHG) (or *CS, *ALL) changes are held until you issue COMMIT or ROLLBACK.

Tables must be journaled to be used under commitment control; otherwise the statement fails at runtime (SQLCODE -7008, SQLSTATE 55019). The CRTSQLRPGI default is COMMIT(*CHG), so a program you compile without thinking about it hits this on any unjournaled file. On PUB400 a new SQL schema is journaled automatically, but tables created with CRTPF (see DDS to SQL DDL migration) in a plain library are not.

The precompiler produces a temporary source and then calls the RPG compiler. COMMIT(*NONE) is right for batch utilities (wrap the call in CL and use SBMJOB) and read-only programs; choose *CHG for transactional programs. The SET OPTION line in the source carries the same options with the member.

How to Self-Host Plex and Jellyfin: A Developer's Field Guide to Your Own Streaming Stack

📅 Nov 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 28 • Originally published at mangodeveloper.com

The first time I cancelled Netflix and tried to watch my own ripped movies on a hotel TV, nothing played. The file was H.265 10-bit in MKV. The TV didn't support it. The browser transcoder choked. My "save $20 a month" project turned into a weekend of reading about hardware transcoding, and that's the moment you realize media streaming is genuinely hard. This article is the guide I wish I'd had then.

Plex and Jellyfin both solve the same problem-your media library, served to your devices, with metadata, posters, and a Netflix-like UI. Plex is the polished commercial product with a paywall on the good features. Jellyfin is the AGPL-licensed fork of Emby that doesn't phone home and doesn't ask for a credit card. Most developers I know end up running both for a few weeks and picking a side. I'll show you how to run either (or both) on real hardware, with real transcoding, behind a real reverse proxy, reachable from anywhere.

Both projects are mature, both play every common format, both support hardware transcoding. The differences are about philosophy and friction.

I run Jellyfin in production. I run Plex on a second box for family members whose Smart TVs don't have a Jellyfin app. If your household is mostly Apple TV, Plex is still the safer bet. If everyone's on Roku/Fire Stick/web, Jellyfin is fine and you'll save the Plex Pass fee.

A typical self-hosted media stack looks like this:

┌──────────────────────────┐ │ Internet / Tailscale │ └────────────┬─────────────┘ │ HTTPS ▼ ┌──────────────────────────┐ │ Caddy reverse proxy │ │ (auto TLS via ACME) │ └────────────┬─────────────┘ │ ┌──────────────────┼───────────────────┐ │ │ │ ▼ ▼ ▼ ┌────────────┐ ┌──────────────┐ ┌──────────────┐ │ Jellyfin │ │ Plex │ │ *arr stack │ │ (Docker) │ │ (Docker) │ │ (Radarr / │ │ │ │ │ │ Sonarr / │ │ /dev/dri │ │ /dev/dri │ │ Prowlarr) │ │ render │ │ render │ │ │ └─────┬──────┘ └──────┬───────┘ └──────┬───────┘ │ │ │ ▼ ▼ ▼ ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ /mnt/media ← mergerfs( /mnt/disk{1..N} ) │ │ │ │ SnapRAID parity: /mnt/parity (1 disk) │ └──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

The key design decisions are: services in Docker, all media mounted read-only inside containers, hardware acceleration passed through /dev/dri, and one reverse proxy handling TLS for everything.

Keep the host layout boring and predictable. Every service gets a config dir under /opt, every persistent dataset under /mnt.

plaintext /opt/jellyfin/ # config, metadata cache, transcode dir /opt/plex/ /opt/caddy/Caddyfile /mnt/disk1/ # individual data disks, mounted at boot /mnt/disk2/ /mnt/parity/ # SnapRAID parity /mnt/media # mergerfs view of disk1..N ├── movies/ ├── tv/ ├── music/ └── photos/

/mnt/media is the only path the containers see. If you ever migrate disks or rebuild the pool, the container mounts don't change.

A single big disk is the easy path until it dies. RAID 5 rebuilds are stressful on modern high-capacity drives and the array is slow while it runs. The homelab consensus is mergerfs (file-level concatenation, no striping) plus SnapRAID (snapshot parity, run nightly).

conf /dev/disk/by-id/ata-WDC_WD40EFRX-1 /mnt/disk1 ext4 defaults,noatime 0 2 /dev/disk/by-id/ata-WDC_WD40EFRX-2 /mnt/disk2 ext4 defaults,noatime 0 2 /dev/disk/by-id/ata-WDC_WD40EFRX-3 /mnt/parity ext4 defaults,noatime 0 2

/mnt/disk1:/mnt/disk2 /mnt/media fuse.mergerfs \ defaults,allow_other,category.create=mfs,moveonenospc=true,dropcacheonrename=true 0 0

conf parity /mnt/parity/snapraid.parity content /mnt/disk1/snapraid.content content /mnt/disk2/snapraid.content content /opt/snapraid/snapraid.content data d1 /mnt/disk1/ data d2 /mnt/disk2/ exclude *.unrecoverable exclude /tmp/ exclude /lost+found/ exclude *.!sync exclude *.bak

set -euo pipefail LOG=/var/log/snapraid.log { echo "=== $(date) ===" snapraid sync snapraid scrub -p 8 -o 1 } >> "$LOG" 2>&1

Crucially: mergerfs is not RAID. If a disk dies before SnapRAID has run, the files written since the last sync are gone. SnapRAID is snapshot parity, not real-time. This is the trade-off. For movies you can re-rip, that's fine. For irreplaceable photos, back them up off-site too.

Direct play is when the client can decode the file as-is. Transcoding is when the server has to convert it on the fly. 4K HEVC to 1080p H.264 in software will pin 8 cores and deliver 4 FPS. The fix is hardware: pass /dev/dri into the container and let the iGPU do it.

vainfo should list VAProfileHEVCMain10 and VAProfileVP9Profile0. If you don't have it, install intel-media-va-driver-non-free on Debian/Ubuntu.

Add your media user to the render group so the container can access the device:

shell sudo usermod -aG render jellyfin sudo usermod -aG render plex

The classical symptom of "transcoding doesn't work" is the server going to 100% CPU and the video pausing every few seconds. That's software transcoding. The container is missing /dev/dri or the render group permission.

Here's a production-ready compose file. Note the /dev/dri device, the render group ID mapped via group_add, and the explicit no-new-privileges security option.

services: jellyfin: image: jellyfin/jellyfin:10.9.11 container_name: jellyfin restart: unless-stopped user: "1000:1000" group_add:

Inside Jellyfin, enable VAAPI under Dashboard → Playback → Transcoding. Pick /dev/dri/renderD128 as the device. Test with a 4K HEVC file forced to 1080p max and a 20 Mbps bitrate cap-CPU should stay under 10%.

Plex is similar but the image expects a claim token from plex.tv on first boot.

services: plex: image: plexinc/pms-docker:latest container_name: plex restart: unless-stopped hostname: plex devices:

Plex Pass is required for hardware transcoding. Without it, you'll see "Transcoding (cpu)" in the dashboard and CPU will spike. Don't waste a weekend debugging the /dev/dri perms if you haven't bought Plex Pass yet-it's gated behind the feature flag.

Plex uses network_mode: host because it broadcasts on multiple ports (32400 main, 32469 DLNA, 1900/5353 UDP for discovery). You can run bridge mode with explicit port mappings, but DLNA auto-discovery will break for clients that rely on it.

You want HTTPS for remote access and you want a single port forwarded. Caddy is the lowest-friction option because ACME is built in.

jellyfin.example.com { reverse_proxy 127.0.0.1:8096 { flush_interval -1 } encode zstd gzip

# Jellyfin's recommended headers header { Strict-Transport-Security "max-age=31536000; includeSubDomains; preload" X-Frame-Options SAMEORIGIN X-Content-Type-Options nosniff Referrer-Policy no-referrer # don't apply Caddy's default CSP, Jellyfin sets its own ?Content-Security-Policy "" }

@websockets { header Connection Upgrade header Upgrade websocket } reverse_proxy @websockets 127.0.0.1:8096 }

plex.example.com { reverse_proxy 127.0.0.1:32400 encode zstd gzip }

The flush_interval -1 is critical for Jellyfin-it disables buffering so streaming chunks reach the client immediately. Without it, you'll see 30-second startup stalls on every video.

services: caddy: image: caddy:2.8-alpine container_name: caddy restart: unless-stopped ports:

If you'd rather not open ports on your router, skip this whole section and use Tailscale.

Cloudflare Tunnel's free ToS technically excludes "large file streaming." People do it anyway and most accounts never get flagged, but if you care about the letter of the agreement, don't. Use Tailscale for family-if they can install a phone app, they can use Tailscale. Use port forwarding + Caddy for yourself.

shell curl -fsSL https://tailscale.com/install.sh | sh sudo tailscale up --advertise-tags=tag:media

On each client device, install Tailscale, log into the same tailnet, and you're done. The Jellyfin URL becomes http://jellyfin-server:8096 from anywhere on the tailnet. No TLS, no port forwarding, no DNS.

Both Plex and Jellyfin have hard opinions about file layout. Get this wrong and metadata matching turns into a part-time job.

plaintext /mnt/media/movies/ The Matrix (1999)/ The Matrix (1999) [1080p] [Bluray].mkv The Matrix Reloaded (2003)/ The Matrix Reloaded (2003) [1080p] [Bluray].mkv

/mnt/media/tv/ Breaking Bad (2008)/ Season 01/ Breaking Bad - S01E01 - Pilot.mkv Breaking Bad - S01E02 - Cat's in the Bag.mkv

/mnt/media/music/ Artist/ Album (Year)/ 01 - Track.flac

The patterns the metadata agents look for:

Avoid subfolders with extras, posters, behind-the-scenes mixed in. Put those in a separate Extras/ folder inside the movie folder-Jellyfin and Plex both special-case it.

Docker containers run as whatever UID/GID you tell them. If the host files are owned by 1000:1000 and you let the container run as root, you'll end up with files owned by root that you can't delete from the host later. Worse, if Jellyfin can't write to its config dir, the wizard silently fails.

Pick a UID/GID and stick with it across every service. Mine is 1000:1000.

shell sudo chown -R 1000:1000 /opt/jellyfin /opt/plex /mnt/media sudo chmod -R 775 /opt/jellyfin /opt/plex sudo find /mnt/media -type d -exec chmod 775 {} \; sudo find /mnt/media -type f -exec chmod 664 {} \;

The render group needs to be the same GID inside and outside the container. Check on the host:

Then in compose, group_add: ["109"] makes the container's process a member of that group. If the GID on the host is different (some distros use 44), change the number.

Prometheus node_exporter for the host, plus Jellyfin's built-in webhook for play events. The minimum useful dashboard tracks:

yaml services: node_exporter: image: prom/node-exporter:v1.8.2 container_name: node_exporter restart: unless-stopped pid: host volumes:

Pair with Grafana and a SMART exporter (smartctl-exporter) and you'll know a drive is failing before it dies. Don't skip this-nothing is worse than explaining to your partner why all the movies vanished on movie night.

Running as root in the container. Symptom: files created on the host array are owned by root and you can't rm them without sudo. Fix: set user: in compose, chown the host directories to match.

Forgetting the render group on the device. Symptom: Jellyfin says "VAAPI init failed" or silently falls back to software. Fix: group_add: [""] and ls -l /dev/dri shows the group can read renderD128.

Exposing Jellyfin directly to the internet without HTTPS. Symptom: login page on HTTP, your password goes in cleartext. Fix: Caddy + ACME, or Tailscale.

Mounting media read-write. Symptom: Jellyfin happily renames your carefully named files based on a bad metadata match. Fix: :ro on the mount. Use a separate /incoming volume if Jellyfin needs to write.

Putting the transcode cache on a spinning disk. Symptom: seeking is slow, transcoding stalls. Fix: transcode to /config/transcode on an SSD, or mount tmpfs.

Trusting one disk with no parity. Symptom: drive dies, library is gone. Fix: SnapRAID parity, plus off-site backup of anything irreplaceable.

Forgetting to update TLS SAN when changing hostname. Symptom: mobile app refuses to connect after a hostname change. Fix: regenerate cert, or use a wildcard.

"Videos buffer every 10 seconds." Open the Jellyfin dashboard while playing. If you see "Direct playing," the network is the bottleneck-your home upload or the client's download. If you see "Transcoding (cpu)," hardware accel isn't working. Check vainfo inside the container:

If vainfo doesn't list HEVC, the wrong driver is loaded. On Intel 12th gen+, you need intel-media-va-driver-non-free (Debian) or intel-media-va-driver (newer versions support all features).

"Plex remote access says 'not available outside your network.'" Plex's relay service uses a stun-style NAT check. If it can't reach plex.tv:32400 from inside, it won't publish. Check docker logs plex | grep -i relay and confirm outbound 32400/TCP works. Sometimes you need to set PLEX_ADVERTISE_URL to your public hostname explicitly.

"Music won't scan, every album shows as unknown." The metadata agent is probably set to TheAudioDB but your folder layout is wrong. Music agents are extremely picky: / ()/NN - .ext. Check Media Info on a single track-if Album and Artist tags are missing from the file itself, fix the tags with beet or MusicBrainz Picard before rescanning.

"Library scan is at 1% after an hour." Either the disk is slow (check iostat -x 1) or you've pointed Jellyfin at the wrong directory and it's walking the entire filesystem. docker exec jellyfin find /media -type f | wc -l should match what you expect. If it's millions of files, you've mounted something you didn't mean to.

"Subtitles don't show." Most clients only support SRT for direct play. PGS or ASS subs force a transcode. Convert with ffmpeg -i input.mkv -map 0:s:0 -c:s srt output.en.srt and put the SRT next to the video.

update: docker compose -f /opt/jellyfin/docker-compose.yml pull docker compose -f /opt/jellyfin/docker-compose.yml up -d docker image prune -f

backup: tar czf /mnt/parity/backups/jellyfin-config-$(date +%F).tgz /opt/jellyfin/config rsync -a /mnt/parity/backups/ user@offsite:/backups/jellyfin/ find /mnt/parity/backups -name 'jellyfin-config-*.tgz' -mtime +30 -delete

shell 0 3 * * 0 root make -C /opt update && make -C /opt backup

For major version upgrades (Plex, Jellyfin), snapshot the config volume first. With ZFS:

shell zfs snapshot tank/jellyfin-config@pre-$(date +%F)

shell lvcreate -s -L 5G -n jellyfin-snap /dev/vg0/jellyfin

If the upgrade breaks something, revert the snapshot. Without a snapshot, you're restoring from tarball and praying the database schema is compatible.

A real breakdown for a 4-disk NUC-based server:

Power draw is the recurring cost. A NUC + 4 spinning disks idle around 35W. At $0.15/kWh, that's about $46/year. You're not saving money in year one. You're saving money in year three, and you own the library.

Family with mixed devices. I run Jellyfin as the primary, Plex for the one Smart TV that doesn't have a Jellyfin app. Same media mount, two services, no duplication.

University dorm media share. A friend runs Jellyfin for his floor with Tailscale ACLs. Each student gets an account, watch history is per-user, and the admin can see who's hammering the transcoder at 2 AM.

Photo backup with auto-tagging. Jellyfin has an image library mode that auto-tags faces using a local ML model. Mount /mnt/media/photos, enable the plugin, let it run overnight. It's not Google Photos, but the photos never leave your network.

Personal music streaming. Jellyfin has a competent music player and integrates with Finamp on mobile. The audio quality beats Spotify's free tier because you control the source files.

Self-hosting your media is one of the few projects that delivers exactly what it promises: your library, on your hardware, on your terms. The setup is genuinely fiddly the first time-permissions, hardware transcoding, reverse proxy, and remote access all want to bite you. Once it works, it stays working for years. The trade-off is your time and the discipline to keep backups. If you're the kind of developer who reads this site, you'll find the fiddliness rewarding rather than annoying.

Generate a Maze With a Guaranteed Solution in Python (API Tutorial)

A maze generator is easy to write badly. Carve some passages, knock down some walls, ship it — and sooner or later it hands you a maze where the goal is walled off completely. In a game that's an unwinnable level. In a printed activity book, that's a kid discovering the answer key doesn't work, and a refund instead of a review.

So the maze generator I built for my books doesn't just carve — it proves. It walks the finished maze from start to goal before anything ships, and the proof travels with the puzzle. This is that generator, exposed as an API called ActivityForge. In this tutorial we'll call it from Python: send a size and a seed, get back a maze, its wall data, and the verified solution path.

The API lives on RapidAPI. The free tier gives you 100 calls a day, and every plan — free included — reaches every endpoint and every format. The paid plans only change how many calls you get, starting at $19/month. Nothing in this tutorial needs a paid plan.

The whole request is one POST. Two headers carry your key and the host; the JSON body carries the maze settings.

A few things worth knowing about the parameters:

The response describes the maze as data, not a picture. Coordinates are [x, y] — x is the column, y is the row — with start at the top-left cell [0, 0] and goal at the bottom-right. The walls field is a list of rows; each cell carries four booleans, top, right, bottom, and left, saying which of its walls stand. That's everything a renderer needs:

That's a real response, not a mockup — seed 42 will hand you this exact maze every time.

Here's the part that makes the maze safe to ship. Alongside the walls, the response carries a solution object: path (the route as [x, y] cells, start to goal), directions (the same route as words — right, down, left, up), and steps (the move count).

The generator builds a perfect maze — a spanning tree over the grid, so exactly one route joins any two cells — then breadth-first walks that route before returning anything. The solution is the route it walked, so it can't disagree with the walls. Feed the path back into the renderer as marked cells and the proof draws itself:

Trace the stars yourself: every step moves through a gap, never through a wall, from the S in the top-left to the G in the bottom-right. Generating levels? That directions list doubles as a walkthrough your game can replay. Printing? It's the answer key.

Because we asked for format: "all", the same response also carries svg (a print-ready page) and pdf_base64 (a vector PDF, base64-encoded). Saving the PDF is three lines:

That's the page you hand to a kid — titled, with "Find your way from START to GOAL!" on it, no drawing required.

Look back at the request: "seed": 42. Run it tomorrow, next month, on a different machine — same maze, wall for wall, same 38-step solution. Leave the seed out and every call returns a fresh random maze. For print: generate a catalog once, record the seeds, re-render identical puzzles forever. For a game: a daily maze that's identical for every player, from one integer.
