New Mexico is the reward

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The library is a bit chaotic, all the books are in the process of being boxed up for a move. I ask when they are moving and where to. The librarian doesn’t know herself. There are two computers there which Brokeback and i jump on to fill out our NDA permit forms. There isn’t any clear guidance on how to fill them out, so we call Fort Huachua and ask some questions. The person who answers say i need to have an active service member, veteran or DOD contractor as a sponsor. This doesn’t seem to add up to what the CDTC says about international hikers, we just need to be with a US citizen who also had the permit. So i skip this on the form. We go into a back room with whitish walls to take pictures  for the permit. Mine is pretty bad! I compile all the documents and send everything off to the Fort Huachuca email. Fingers crossed!

I wanted to do some blogging but turns out the library closes for lunch. Brokeback and i walk back into town. Chama is a pretty long town that stretches along the road, so its a while. I drop into the grocery store to get more of my resupply. There i meet Tim & Cassie again. They are staying with JJ and invite me to come over for a dinner they are cooking. I say yes, and after finishing we walk back into town. We spend the afternoon hanging out with the rest of the Oysters outside the Iron Rail Inn. Later we walk over to JJ’s with Tim & Cassie. There we make dinner together and chat for a good while. I also get to peek around the Exoticpax  and Aardwolf production rooms. Its pretty cool getting to see their setups as someone who also makes gear.

In true CDT fashion its covered in barbed wire and bones!

Groves of golden Aspen were very common on this part of the trail

Its pretty cold overnight, and at about 5am I’m awake. There’s frost on the ground and so after heading off to dig my morning cathole, i decide to pack up and start hiking in the dark.  I reach the edge of the mesa we are on shortly after dawn and am treated to views of the pine forests below.

Looking down towards Abiquiu in the morning

After this the trail descends down towards Ghost Ranch and pretty quickly the landscape changes from pine forest to something more arid. Its when we reach the Arroyo del Yeso that we get our first true sense of being in the desert!

Our first true view of the New Mexico desert near Ghost Ranch

Every September Albuquerque hosts the hot air balloon fiesta. Back in Chama we decided to go if we could get tickets. Its a couple of hours drive, through a desert landscape. I look around as we drive and wonder if this is what the rest of New Mexico is going to be like.

We get tickets for the morning session, so wake up at about 4am the next day. I feel pretty awful on the short car ride over. By the time we are walking into the Arena i start to feel pretty excited, and after getting coffee i feel pretty good.  I imagined we would be in a viewing area away from the balloon. However you get to walk in among them as they set up which was so cool! The first balloons lifted off just before sunrise to the strains of the US National Anthem. After this its was a constant stream of balloons being inflated and lifting off! There must have been around 100 balloons in the air at one point.  2027 SOBO’s, this is one amazing sidequest!

The first balloons lifting off before dawn

After leaving the festival we got breakfast and then napped back at the hotel. Did some laundry and just hung out for the rest of the day. Later we drove to an all you can eat sushi restaurant and demolished many rolls!

While visiting Albuqerque had been fun, it was definitely a little overwhelming to be in such a big city. So i was glad to be heading back to trail. Tengram dropped us off at Ghost Ranch around midday. He was going to camp with us that evening so we put camping bits in his car and get slacked again.

The crew hiking out towards the Rio Chama

The route out of Ghost Ranch is pretty nice, and goes on a piece of the land that you usually don’t get to access. Now there were big sandstone cliffs in almost all directions and it felt like we were truly in the desert. We crossed the highway and followed the dirt road up alongside the Rio Chama towards where the Ghost Ranch Alt rejoins the redline. Halfway along there is a campsite where Tengram is setup. We gather for dinner and then the projector comes out for the Second Twilight Movie! I enjoy it in a its so bad its good kind of way.

The others in the Oysters were going to do a backyard ultra to Cuba the next day, about 50 miles. I decided that this wasn’t really for me, i always prefer a more even split of days over doing an epic one and zeroing to recover. So after the film had finished i set off. It started to rain pretty soon after i set off. At first a little bit but gets pretty heavy. Walking down the dirt road in the dark and rain makes me wonder if i should have stayed and camped with the others. In the end i hike for an hour or so to rejoin the CDT redline and make a little progress. By the time i find somewhere to camp the rain has stopped. There is a little tree with some pine duff which isn’t too damp.

I’m woken up before by the Oysters passing as they start their ultra. While i’m not planning on hiking 50 miles, i still need to do a pretty big day if i want to catch up with them in Cuba. So i pack up and set off in the dark. The sunrise over the cliffs is pretty nice and the trail is kind of chill for a bit.

It follows a small stream in a shallow canyon before climbing up to a mesa. Here the trail has really been washed out by the heavy rain. The water has scoured out the streambed a good 4 or 5 feet in places, and there are areas where flood debris has been jammed up against trees. The result is a constant climbing in and out of the streambed trying to follow the trail. Its pretty tiring and I’m almost glad when i leave and get to climb up onto the Mesa.

From there the trail continues up high before dropping down towards a highway. Looking out over the landscape i realize that before i know it this journey will be over. I have a little bittersweet cry on my way down.

One thing this trail does is constantly surprise me with the landscapes. In my head New Mexico is an entire state like The Great Divide Basin.  Climbing up into The San Luis Parks Wilderness area it feels a little bit like I’m back in Colorado. There are loads of aspens and wide open meadowy spaces. I stop around sunset for dinner, then keep hiking into the dark. Its a little spooky, especially when i see hunters our with their headtorches on. I also go the wrong way for about a mile and have to turn back.

Eventually i  pass the San Gregoria Reservoir and come to the southern boundary of the Wilderness area. Here there is a trailhead with pit toilet, where i find a flattish spot to camp.  Im pretty tired and Its only another 20km into Cuba, which is all downhill and i should be able to easily do in the morning.

In the morning i set off from the trailhead and start descending. There are some cow paths and i end up paralleling the CDT for a while, and have to do a little bushwhack back to the path. As the trail descends the trees open up, the groundcover gets sparser and it feels more arid.  Soon the trail leaves the trees and comes to a little overlook. From here i can see out to Cuba. Now it feels like i am truly in the desert!

The rest of the way into Cuba is along dirt roads. Cuba is pretty notorious for loose dogs that sometimes come out and bite hikers. There are plenty of tales of hikers having to get stitches from bites. I’ll admit that dogs scare me a little bit, and so i pretty alert on my way in. Close to town there was a house with a couple of dogs outside, who barked at me, but otherwise stayed put.

In Cuba i reunite with the Oysters at one of the motels there around lunchtime. I do my town chores while we watch Tombstone and drink Coors Banquets. Later we head down to a cafe to get dinner. While we are eating a man comes over. His name is Jim and he does trail maintenance on the CDT around the area, and talks to us about our hike. Then says that he’s settled up the bill for us! A nice and much welcomed surprise.

This first week in New Mexico has been such a nice change in the nature of the hike. Without the need to push big days hanging over me, I feel like I can relax and really enjoy this last part of the hike.

Personal Finance (Not Investing) • Re: Employer Provided IUL [Indexed Universal Life]

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

It is not all bad. Think of it as a fixed income investment. But be careful about the LIRP pitches. The problem with using an IUL for retirement is that the Cost of Insurance (COI) gets really expensive after age 60. However, you are not stuck. If you own the policy, you can do a 1035 exchange into an annuity. Make it a direct carrier-to-carrier transfer with no policy loan outstanding, so you avoid the boot rule (cash or loan relief in an exchange is taxable up to the amount of gain).

Once the money is in the annuity, withdrawals come out gain first. If you retire early, you can withdraw the gains and pay the taxes during your bridge years, then annuitize once you only have basis left. The basis comes back tax free, so this can be more tax efficient than pulling money out of the policy itself.

You might want to check out the IUL Experiment website for more details on how IULs work.

NVIDIA SkillEvaluator shows which of six agents actually load your skill on aitmpl.com

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 22 • Originally published at Medium

I wired NVIDIA SkillEvaluator into aitmpl.com this week. Every skill page now has a Compatibility tab that says, agent by agent, whether Claude Code, Codex, Copilot CLI, OpenCode, Hermes and OpenClaw would load that skill, load part of it, or ignore it.

That is a different question from the one a security scan or a quality score answers. Those assume the skill reached the model. This one asks whether it ever got there.

A skill can follow the Agent Skills specification exactly and still never reach the model. Every harness has its own loader: its own YAML parser, its own folders, its own size limits. The same SKILL.md can work in Claude Code and be dropped by Codex with no error anywhere.

The rules come from versioned profiles that ship with SkillEvaluator, built by running each harness and capturing the request it actually sent to the model. So a rule describes what the harness did, not what its documentation says, and each profile records the release and date it was verified against.

The skill in that screenshot is devil, and it is a good example. Copilot CLI drops it because the description is 1,178 UTF-16 units, 154 over the 1,024 limit, and an emoji counts as two. Codex keeps the skill but cuts the description to 1,021 characters in the skill list, which is exactly the text the model uses to decide whether to call it.

The check is a CLI, so you do not need the catalog to use it:

It takes a skill folder, a folder of skills, or a plugin folder, and it exits 0 whatever it finds. It is also part of Tier 1 of skillevaluator validate, so skillevaluator validate ./my-skill --tiers 1 runs it alongside the quality, schema, PII, license and security checks.

Four flags describe how you actually run the agent, and they change the limits:

Without them it assumes an unknown context window, a native provider, a model that opens the skill on its own, and an interactive session.

The catalog run covers 945 skills against 6 agents, so 5,670 skill and agent pairs. Of those, 5,466 load as written, 203 load degraded and exactly 1 is dropped. By skill, 807 are clean in all six and 138 are not.

The non loading results are not spread evenly:

Two families cover almost everything. The first is size: OpenClaw counts each CJK character as four or more, so a long SKILL.md crosses its read cap long before you expect it to. The second is placeholders. $ARGUMENTS and ${CLAUDE_SKILL_DIR} are real Claude Code substitutions, and the other five agents hand them to the model as literal text. Claude Code's own four findings are the mirror image: a price like $4.00 in the body is read as an argument placeholder.

None of that shows up as a broken skill. It shows up as a skill that quietly does less than you wrote.

It does not gate anything. In the Claude Code Templates repo the job lives in skill-security-scan.yml, runs on skills changed in a pull request, and is marked continue-on-error, so it posts a report and never fails the PR. The comment goes on same repository pull requests; fork PRs get the result in the job summary instead.

On the site it is display only. The script that attaches the data says so plainly: the signal never changes ordering, search or ranking. Results are cached by file hash and SkillEvaluator version, so a catalog build only rechecks skills that actually changed.

And it does not replace running the agent. It predicts loader behaviour from profiles, which means it can tell you a skill will be dropped, but not whether it helps once it is loaded. That is a live evaluation, which is Tier 3 and needs a provider, an agent and Docker.

The pairing is the useful part. SkillSpector answers whether a skill is safe to install. The Harness Check answers whether the agent will read it. A skill needs both to be worth publishing.

What Is Multi-Level IVR? How to Design a Phone Menu That Works

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A multi-level IVR is an automated phone menu where a caller's choice can open another menu instead of ending at one destination. Each layer narrows the reason for calling, so one business number can reach many teams, languages, and self-service answers without a receptionist.

This guide helps you decide whether you need one, and how deep it should go. It covers what the research says about menu depth, how to build a menu from your own call log, and what one telecom regulator's newest rule can teach any business.

IVR stands for interactive voice response, the system behind "press 1 for sales". A single-level IVR asks one question and acts on the answer. A multi-level IVR can ask a follow-up, the way a good receptionist would.

You may also hear it called a nested IVR, a multi-tier IVR, or a phone tree. They all describe the same idea. The diagram shows one for an imaginary online store.

A public example from India's national railway shows the idea at scale. Business Today's 2021 report on the Rail Madad 139 helpline listed these details at launch:

That description is more than five years old, so the live menu may differ. The design choice still holds: an emergency never waits behind a tree.

A path can finish in one of three ways. The system can read out an answer, such as an order status. It can connect the caller to a team, using rules like those in our guide to call routing. It can also take a message for later.

Keypad menus don't need an app or a smartphone, because a push-button phone sends a standard tone for each key. The ITU's recommendation on push-button telephone sets covers these technical features. That reach matters wherever your callers may be on any kind of phone.

You need another level when callers must make a choice before the main question makes sense. These are the usual reasons:

A growing organisation chart isn't a reason on its own. Callers don't know how your teams are divided, and they shouldn't have to learn.

The usual advice is two or three levels and about five options. That advice is a reasonable place to start, but it deserves a closer look.

The five-option limit is often justified by a famous 1956 memory paper. A 2008 study in the journal Human Factors argued that the paper doesn't support the limit. The researchers gave people functionally equivalent IVRs, one broad and one deep, and asked them to complete email tasks.

The broad design won on both performance and satisfaction. The gap was largest for people with lower working memory capacity. The lead author's dissertation reached the same conclusion: shorter menus stacked more deeply were harder to use.

This doesn't prove that longer menus are always better. It is one experiment, on a speech-enabled system, and its tasks were about email rather than customer support. Treat it as a caution, not a rule for your callers. It does mean you shouldn't add a level just to shorten a list.

We built a small model to compare listening time. It assumes each option takes 3 seconds to say, each move to a new menu takes 2 seconds, and every destination is equally likely.

The model flatters deep menus, because it ignores wrong turns. A caller who picks the wrong branch has to back out and listen again. The chance of that rises with every extra decision.

So start with two levels, and use a third only on the one branch that needs it. If a menu seems to need a third level everywhere, try speech input or fewer destinations first.

GetVoIP's advice to map a month of inbound calls by reason is the right first step. The next step is to group those reasons in words a caller would use.

Why phone scans of text come out broken, and what a scanner filter should do about it

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You hold the phone over a letter, tap the shutter, and the app hands you a crisp white page. Then you zoom in and the small print looks chewed: letters with holes in them, thin lines that fade out halfway, a grey footer that is simply gone.

We hit this while building the document scanner in our own app, and the cause was not where we first looked. This post walks through what actually breaks the letters, how to measure it, and the fix that worked for us.

The first instinct is to blame the camera or the resolution. So we saved the raw photo before any filtering and compared.

Two shots of the same printed page, same light:

Same result at full size and at a reduced size, so it was not resolution, and not the save path either. The filter was turning readable soft text into broken text.

Most scanner filters do roughly three things:

On a sharp photo, a letter stroke is a dark core with a very thin grey edge. The curve makes the core black, the edge falls either way, and the letter looks crisp.

On a soft photo, the same stroke is a dark core with a wide grey fringe. Now the steep curve does something nasty: everything lighter than the cut-off goes to white. The fringe vanishes, and because the core of a thin stroke is itself not very dark when it is blurred, parts of the core vanish too. You get letters with holes along their edges.

It gets worse with a page-level strength setting. If the filter decides how hard to darken ink by looking at the boldest things on the page (a heading, a table border), then on a page with a strong title it concludes "ink is already dark enough" and gives the faint small print no lift at all.
