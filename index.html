Art Collector Barbara Jakobson’s Famous Upper East Side Townhouse On the Market for $9 Million

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Barbara Jakobson was a monumental force in the New York art world for over 60 years, serving as an influential collector, a fierce patron, and a longtime trustee of New York’s Museum of Modern Art (MoMA). She also had a sharp, curatorial eye and a truly singular style. That is perhaps best evident in her home on Manhattan’s Upper East Side, which doubled as her own ever-evolving gallery from the mid-1960s until her death in 2025.“I see the house as a vessel for an ongoing autobiographical exercise,” Jakobson told Curbed in a 2021 interview. “I keep the transformation as proof of life.” Related Articles R.M. Schindler's Modernist Beach House, Built for a California Health Nut, Hits the Market for a Breezy $18.8 Million Art Collector and Publisher Benedikt Taschen Lists Mid-Century Modern Beverly Hills Home for $25 M.

The five-story townhouse along East 74th Street in the Lenox Hill neighborhood was built in 1910 and later expanded by architect John Kingsland in 1956. Prior to Jakobson, filmmaker Elia Kazan lived in the 20-foot-wide abode while directing East of Eden (1955) and Splendor in the Grass (1961). Hollywood stars including James Dean, Natalie Wood, and Marlon Brando reportedly visited then.

Jakobson, a native New Yorker, bought the pad in 1965 and lived there for nearly six decades. During that time, she welcomed New York’s creative set, such as artists Jasper Johns, Andy Warhol, Cy Twombly, and Robert Rauschenberg. After her death last summer, the property was brought to market in April 2026 for $9.8 million. It received a price cut of over $800,000 before reaching its current asking price of $8.99 million. Leslie Hirsch, Edward Joseph, Howard Morrel, and Benjamin Anderson of Christie’s International Real Estate New York hold the listing.Nicknamed “the Squirrel House” on account of the bronze squirrel sculptures at the entrance, the home is whimsical yet refined. Red lacquered doors open to 6,000 square feet of stylish interiors, with eight bedrooms and six full bathrooms spread throughout.A grand entry hall with triple arches and custom wallpaper by artist Peter Halley leads to a parlor floor featuring a formal living room, a gallery, and a salon with a wood-burning fireplace. A staircase—on which Jakobson was photographed for Vogue—spirals down to a spectacular double-height great room, where an outline of Frank Stella’s 1971 Felsztyn III remains. It once hung there but was sold in 2005 at a Christie’s auction featuring works from Jakobson’s collection. Glass doors lead out to a private 350-square-foot garden.The guest bedrooms, including two junior suites with dressing rooms and en suites, sprawl across the next two levels. The primary suite, meanwhile, covers the entire top floor, offering a light-filled private lounge for reading, relaxing, or even pondering art.

Carson Daly, Laura Jarrett Ready to Launch ‘New Era’ at NBC’s ‘Saturday Today’

Carson Daly was looking for a new challenge in a long career in the media business. On Saturday morning, he’ll get it.Daly will team up with Laura Jarrett, Joe Fryer, and meteorologist Angie Lassman on NBC’s “Saturday Today” telecast, the latest attempt by one of the big broadcast networks to create bespoke versions of their morning franchises that appeal to weekend audiences with a little more time on their hands in the A.M.

“If people are waking up with us, we want to respect that and we want to give them something that’s worth tuning into,” says Jarrett, who joined “Today” in 2023, a year after moving to NBC News from CNN. NBC, ABC and CBS have worked to create weekend-news programs with a little more distinction from their weekday flagships. The Saturday telecast of CBS’ “Saturday Morning” puts a spotlight on topics such as food and music. ABC recently recalibrated the anchor teams on its weekend broadcasts of “Good Morning America,” adding Rachel Scott and Rhiannon Ally.

NBC has been encouraged in this vision by the Sunday telecast of “Today,” led by Willie Geist. The anchor offers a slower-paced hour of “Today” that tackles headlines as warranted, but also offers up a deeper interview with a person of interest as well as other features. Geist typically writes much of the show by himself. NBC News has experimented with turning Geist’s longer-form interviews into ticketed events, a move that generates live-media revenue.

Adding Daly, however, brings a person with a different set of skills to the fore of the Saturday show. Jarrett’s previous co-host, Peter Alexander, had a long tenure as a Washington and White House correspondent. Daly has cultivated a broader set of experiences, famously hosting MTV’s “TRL” earlier in his career, then going to host the wee-hours NBC program “Last Call” and host and executive produce “The Voice.”

Over that time, he’s learned to lead in-depth conversations and hold forth with musicians, newsmakers and entertainers. “I’ve always enjoyed longer-form interviews,” Daly says, and he studied Bob Costas and Greg Kinnear, who hosted late-night shows at NBC before his tenure started. “I have a natural curiosity,’” he says, and often comes into a segment with “one thousand questions” he might want to ask a guest — if only “Today” had more time for extended interviews

Daly has been a staple on “Today” since 2013, when he joined as a correspondent of sorts, curating interesting developments taking place on social media from the show’s “Orange Room.” These days, he’s more of a regular presence in the weekday telecast’s second hour, and is often found talking alongside Savannah Guthrie, Craig Melvin, Al Roker and other “Today” anchors.

Several months ago, he considered how he might push himself, and asked “Today” producers if they would consider letting him co-anchor on days when others were off — even on holidays. “I’ve never been afraid to take on a challenge,” Daly says. “I’m glad I stuck my neck out.”

Jarrett has been enjoying her time on “Today” as well as her position as NBC News’ senior legal correspondent. Saturday viewers can still expect to get the headlines, she says. “We will still do the news. We will still do special reports” if warranted, she says.

Jarrett has “a style on TV that I happen to not only respect but also enjoy watching, and so I think I’m going to learn a lot,” says Daly.

Daly takes up his new duties just as the holiday season approaches, a cycle that often brings a broader audience to “Today.” On Saturday, says Jarrett, viewers might get a peek at the Rockefeller Center Christmas Tree arriving in New York — in addition to the official launch of “a new era” for the Saturday program.

iOS 27.2 adds Apple Photos invisible watermark feature, here’s how it works

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

iOS 27.2 arrived last week, and among its changes for Apple Photos, the update adds an invisible watermarking feature when you use certain editing tools. Here’s how it works.

In the age of AI, what looks like a genuine photo might actually be a fake—or partly fake. That’s why tools like SynthID have been used to apply invisible watermarks to AI-generated content.

Starting with iOS 27.2, Apple now uses SynthID watermarks when you make AI-generated edits to photos in Apple Photos, or create all-new images with Image Playground.

Starting with iOS 27.2, iPadOS 27.2, macOS 27.2, and visionOS 27.2, some Apple Intelligence tools will add an invisible SynthID watermark to images that you create or edit:

Image Playground generates entirely new images, so it’s more what the average person would consider an “AI image.”

However, as Apple notes, the SynthID invisible watermark is also applied to genuine photos in your Apple Photos library if you use the AI-powered Clean Up, Extend, or Spatial Reframing tools to edit them.

It’s worth emphasizing the word “invisible” here. You won’t see evidence of a watermark anywhere on your photos, nor will others unless they explicitly check the image with a SynthID detector.

If you do want to check for the watermark, Apple recommends going to synthid.com, where you can upload an image and see its result.

Escape From Jurassic Park Trailer Unveiled 33 Years After Premature Cancellation

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Jurassic Park returns through the trailer for the TV show that never was.

Jurassic Park is one of the most iconic movie franchises of all time. The 1993 movie directed by Steven Spielberg made many believe that dinosaurs really could walk around with humans again one day, leading to the start of an impressive trilogy that mixed practical effects with VFX to create blockbusters that have stood the test of time. More recently, through the Jurassic World movies, the franchise has continued with a modern chapter, which has grown to include TV shows, but several years before that, a Jurassic Park series had already been planned as a spin-off to the first movie.

On X, Chris Pugh shared the unearthed pitch trailer for the canceled 1993 spin-off titled Escape From Jurassic Park.

The trailer for the animated Jurassic Park series starts with the franchise's iconic John Williams theme, showing calm moments of wonder with dinosaurs in the original Jurassic Park, first with a helicopter flying above the island, and then with characters fans know and love, like Alan Grant, Ellie Sattler, and more. However, the footage quickly ups the tension with lightning, humans in peril, dinosaurs fighting, and more. The Escape From Jurassic Park trailer teases how the spin-off series planned to follow the same kind of tone as the Spielberg movie.

At Jurassic Outpost back in 2016, Pugh shared more details about what the canceled Jurassic Park series would have entailed. According to the report, the plan was for the animated spin-off to serve as a direct follow-up to the events of Spielberg's Jurassic Park. As per the story outline developed at the now-defunct Universal Cartoon Studios before the show was scrapped in 1994, the plan was to have the core cast from 1993's Jurassic Park be part of the show, with a 23-episode arc that would have ended with a massive press conference where John Hammond would declare that Jurassic Park was formally open.

While Escape From Jurassic Park was canceled before it ever made it to the small screen, through the franchise's modern Jurassic World era, it has finally made that dream a reality. After all, Netflix has released two successful animated Jurassic World spin-off series that focus on new characters and returning players from the Jurassic World movies and connect to events in the Chris Pratt and Bryce Dallas Howard trilogy. Rewiring a running process's output (fd) with gdb

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 6 • Originally published at wada-dev.com

Sometimes you want to re-point a running process's output somewhere else without stopping it. You can do it just by attaching to the process with gdb and calling the close and open system calls directly. Using gdb not as a debugger but as a "tool for rewriting a running process's file descriptors."

There's a long-running process you've left going, and now you wish you'd kept its logs in a file. Or you want to peek at its output from another terminal. Normally you'd restart it with > log.txt appended, but sometimes you don't want to — or can't — stop it.

File descriptors are managed per process, and when you close one, the number gets reused. Using that property, you can close stdout (fd 1) or stderr (fd 2) and reopen a different file, rewiring where the output goes.

First attach to the target process with gdb.

Then just close the fd and reopen it with open. Descriptors are assigned the lowest free number, so if you close 1 and open right away, the return value is 1.

Open a path like "/tmp/app.log" instead of /dev/pts/5 and you switch stdout to a log file mid-run. The second argument to open is the flags; here we pass write-only (O_WRONLY = 1).

When “Working as Designed” Is the Problem

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I submitted a security report to Anthropic after Claude Code exposed my .NET User Secrets in its model context. They classified it as “Informative – Working as Designed.”

“Working as designed” is a triage label, not a verdict. But taken at face value, it points to something architectural: security is enforced tool by tool, not around the agent as a whole, and certainly not at the OS-boundary level.

.NET developers commonly use dotnet user-secrets to keep development credentials in their user profile, outside the project tree.

• Other sensitive application configuration

You launch Claude Code in your project directory.

You ask Claude to inspect your application configuration.

And/or read the plaintext secrets.json file.

%APPDATA%\Microsoft\UserSecrets\\secrets.json

Because tool output is returned to Claude, those credentials become part of the conversation context. This is not a vulnerability in .NET. It is a consequence of giving an AI coding agent access to a developer’s local environment.

Anthropic explained to me that Claude Code executes commands with the privileges of the developer’s user account. Their position is that commands such as dotnet user-secrets list require approval in the default permission mode. They also pointed to four security mechanisms:

Fair enough: an agentic tool has to execute commands. Allowlisting dotnet* is on me. But there’s a second problem underneath. secrets.json is unencrypted, so to the agent it’s just another readable file. In any case, their response made me look closely at what those mechanisms actually protect.

{ "permissions": { "deny": ["Bash(dotnet user-secrets list*)"] } }

Now Claude cannot execute the obvious command directly. But what happens if I ask Claude:

“Write a Bash script that inspects my .NET User Secrets configuration and execute it.”

The rule matches the command string, not what the script does. The same applies to Python, PowerShell, compiled executables and any other indirect execution path. PreToolUse hooks can inspect tool calls, but they cannot reliably interpret every script or execution path.

Manual approval doesn’t solve this either. The developer approves running the code, but approval does not limit what the code can reach. Blocking a command is not the same as restricting access to the data that command retrieves.

I then looked at Anthropic’s official sandbox documentation:

https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sandbox-environments

Anthropic offers several sandboxing approaches, and they cover different things. Out of the box, sandboxing is off. You have to enable it first. Once enabled, Bash, PowerShell and Monitor tool commands and their child processes are restricted. Claude’s file tools, MCP servers and hooks run outside it. File tools are gated only by permission rules.

Sandbox-bash is not a comprehensive isolation boundary around Claude Code. Granted, the sandbox runtime srt claude wraps the whole Claude Code process, including file tools, MCP servers and hooks. It is a beta research preview and does not run on native Windows.

On Windows, you need a container or virtual machine to get a sandbox. But here it gets tricky. WSL2 gets dicey fast. By default, WSL2 mounts Windows drives under /mnt, for example:

/mnt/c/Users/yourname/ /mnt/c/Users/yourname/AppData/

Microsoft documents that Linux processes accessing Windows files run with the permissions of the Windows user who launched WSL. On its own, that leaves your Windows profile readable from inside WSL2. Claude will still happily read your secrets, directly or through a tool, and expose them in its context.

Docker is a substantially better option. But the core problem remains the same: a container is only as isolated as its configuration. The difference is that Docker adds a boundary between Claude Code and your Windows host. Unlike WSL2, where your Linux environment can access Windows files through /mnt/c, a properly configured Docker container only sees the files, directories and resources you explicitly expose to it… nah snap… it means that Claude Read still has access to your User Secrets as you need them to launch your app.

Windows has filesystem ACLs, restricted tokens, AppContainer isolation, Job Objects and other process isolation mechanisms. This is not an argument that Windows lacks the necessary security primitives. It is a product limitation: Claude Code’s built-in sandbox does not support native Windows.

So far this has been accidental exposure: Claude runs a command and the credentials land in its context. Now consider deliberate exfiltration.

What happens when an agent has access to both sensitive local data and external tools?

Claude Code supports MCP integrations that can connect the agent to external services and systems. Depending on the integrations configured, these might include:

Now imagine an agent that can read local credentials and also call an MCP server that can send data out. The attack chain is short:

An agent gains access to a local credential or sensitive configuration file.

The credential enters the agent’s context or becomes available to an integration.

The agent invokes an external tool or MCP server.

Sensitive information is transmitted outside the developer’s environment.

A prompt injection could trigger it: malicious instructions in a repository, a compromised dependency, tampered documentation or any other untrusted content the agent reads.

Malicious MCP servers are not far-fetched. A rogue Postmark MCP package has already been found secretly BCC’ing an attacker on outgoing email.1 This is not some exotic theoretical attack; it is a baked-in supply-chain problem.

This is not a claim that every MCP server can access every local file, or that every Claude Code integration automatically permits arbitrary exfiltration. The point is architectural: an agent’s security depends on the combined privileges of all its tools, not just the sandbox around its shell.

The upshot: don’t use User Secrets on a machine where an agent runs. Until agents operate inside a real OS-enforced boundary, stop keeping secrets where an agent can trivially read them.

This is a workaround, not a fix. The real fix is the one I’d still like to see: a consistent, OS-enforced boundary around the whole agent, with native Windows support, shared filesystem policy across all tools, process-tree and MCP isolation, and network egress controls. Until then, don’t leave plaintext secrets lying around next to an agent.
