From non-aligned to pro-Western: How Cyprus has loosened ties with Moscow, built partner-ship with the US

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Republic of Cyprus long balanced ties between Russia and the West. Now it is phasing out Russian weapons, expanding defense cooperation with Washington and redefining its place in the Eastern Mediterranean.

In an interview with the Cyprus News Agency on October 4, the new US ambassador to Cyprus, John Breslow, outlined plans to upgrade the Andreas Papandreou Air Base in Paphos to accommodate large US military transport aircraft.

He also announced plans for more frequent visits by US warships to Cypriot ports and an expansion of joint military activities.

But one statement carried particular political weight: his assurance that "the United States will protect Cyprus in the future."

Although the remark does not constitute a formal US security guarantee, it reflects the growing cooperation between the two countries.

For a country that for decades maintained close ties with Moscow, viewed US foreign policy with suspicion and is not a member of NATO, the prospect of an expanded American military presence marks a significant geopolitical shift.

It is perhaps the clearest indication yet of Cyprus's gradual loosening of ties with Russia and its increasingly close strategic alignment with Washington.

The shift is particularly striking given Cyprus's political history.

In the years after it gained independence from the UK in 1960, the Republic of Cyprus sought to balance its relations between East and West.

Under the leadership of Archbishop Makarios III, the first president of independent Cyprus, the country became a founding member in 1961 of the Non-Aligned Movement, a forum of states that did not align with either of the two superpowers of the day, the United States and the Soviet Union.

This approach allowed Nicosia to maintain close ties with the Soviet Union without severing its connections to the West.

Even after joining the European Union in 2004, Cyprus maintained strong political, economic and military ties with Moscow.

Relations with the US, by contrast, remained overshadowed by the Turkish invasion of the island in 1974 and Washington's failure to take decisive action against Ankara.

The US prioritized maintaining its strategic relationship with Turkey, a key NATO ally, over taking a tougher stance against the invasion.

The US position fueled widespread anti-American sentiment in Cyprus, culminating in a violent demonstration outside the US Embassy in Nicosia on August 19, 1974, during which US Ambassador Roger Davies was shot dead.

Mistrust deepened further in 1987, when Washington imposed an arms embargo on the Republic of Cyprus, officially intended to prevent an arms race on the island and avoid undermining efforts to resolve the Cyprus conflict.

The US arms embargo contributed to the reliance of the Cypriot National Guard (the armed forces of the Republic of Cyprus) on Russian-made weapons systems, strengthening Moscow's position as a major military partner.

Cyprus's response to Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014 illustrated the strength of these ties.

The Cypriot government expressed serious reservations about imposing tougher European sanctions on Moscow. Then-Foreign Minister Ioannis Kasoulides warned that such measures could have devastating consequences for the Cypriot economy.

Relations with Moscow deepened further in February 2015, when President Nicos Anastasiades visited the Russian capital and signed a series of agreements with Russian President Vladimir Putin.

These included a naval cooperation memorandum facilitating visits by Russian warships to Cypriot ports. Moscow also agreed to more favorable repayment terms for its €2.5 billion loan to Cyprus in 2011.

Cooperation remained close as recently as October 2021, when then-Foreign Minister and current President Nikos Christodoulides signed a Strategic Cooperation Framework for 2022–2024 in Moscow alongside his Russian counterpart, Sergey Lavrov.

The decisive turning point came just four months later, in February 2022, with Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

According to Konstantinos Adamides, associate professor of international relations at the University of Nicosia, the invasion accelerated a geopolitical shift toward the West that had already begun.

"For the first time, the Republic of Cyprus openly and unequivocally chose to side with the West, despite the economic and political costs of breaking with Moscow," Adamides told DW.

Nicosia aligned itself with EU sanctions against Russia, despite the Cypriot economy's strong links to Moscow, particularly in services, investment and tourism.

The decision marked a significant departure from Cyprus's traditional foreign policy approach of maintaining a balance between Russia and the West.

According to Adamides, Cyprus's position toward Russia paved the way for closer relations with the US, particularly in the field of security.

Nicosia, he explained, succeeded in convincing Washington that it could serve as a reliable partner in the Eastern Mediterranean.

The rapprochement had already gained an institutional foundation before the war, with a bilateral statement of intent on security cooperation signed in 2018.

Further initiatives followed, including the Cyprus Center for Land, Open-seas and Port Security (CYCLOPS), a US-funded training facility inaugurated in Larnaca in April 2022. The center provides training for security personnel from Cyprus and other countries in the region in areas such as port security, counterterrorism and preventing the trafficking of hazardous materials.

An even more significant development for the Cypriot National Guard came in September 2022, when Washington announced the full suspension of the arms embargo imposed in 1987.

The decision allowed for the export of lethal military equipment to Cyprus, subject to the necessary US approvals. The suspension, which was not permanent and remains subject to periodic renewal, has been renewed until September 30, 2027.

Cyprus's strategic security reorientation is also significant because the country is not a member of NATO.

The crash-landing of an Iranian done on the British military base at Akrotiri in March reignited a debate in Cyprus about whether the country should join NATO.

At the time, President Nikos Christodoulides said that Cyprus was preparing for potential membership at military, operational and administrative level and would apply to join NATO "even tomorrow if it were possible."

He stressed, however, that joining the alliance is not possible because "the political conditions are not there, taking into account the well-known position of Turkey."

Cyprus's changing strategic orientation is also reflected in the modernization of its armed forces.

After decades of relying heavily on Russian military equipment, the country is seeking to gradually reduce its dependence on Russian-made T-80 tanks and Tor-M1 and Buk air defense systems.

The process accelerated following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, as sanctions against Moscow made it increasingly difficult to secure spare parts and ammunition for Russian weapons systems.

Another important step came in January 2025, when then-US President Joe Biden authorized Cyprus to participate in American defense procurement programs, including Foreign Military Sales (FMS) and Excess Defense Articles (EDA).

The decision gave Nicosia access to additional mechanisms for acquiring US military equipment, further strengthening defense cooperation between Cyprus and the United States.

Morte Jesus de Miguel, la polizia spagnola apre un'indagine sull'accaduto

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

La morte di Jesus de Miguel, giocatore del Tenerife, a seguito dello scontro di gioco con il portiere dell'Andorra Jesus Owono, sta scuotendo in queste ore il calcio spagnolo e non solo con la Federcalcio che ha annunciato anche il rinvio delle gare delle coppe nazionali, sia al maschile sia al femminile, in programma in segno di lutto per la tragica scomparsa del trentenne.

In merito a quanto accaduto si è mossa anche la polizia iberica, come riporta l'agenzia di stampa EFE, che ha aperto un'indagine sulla morte di de Miguel sottolineando come questo avvenga sempre d'ufficio in caso di morti non dovute a causa naturali: "La Polizia Nazionale ha aperto un'indagine sulla morte del calciatore del Club Deportivo Tenerife Jesús de Miguel , che ora è sotto la supervisione giudiziaria, come confermato all'agenzia EFE da fonti della Stazione di Polizia Provinciale. Il decesso del giocatore trentenne è avvenuto presso l'ospedale Nuestra Señora de La Candelaria poche ore dopo aver subito un violento impatto con il portiere andorrano Jesús Owono durante un contrasto aereo nei minuti finali della partita disputata lunedì scorso allo stadio Heliodoro Rodríguez López. Fonti dell'Alta Corte di Giustizia delle Isole Canarie spiegano che qualsiasi decesso non dovuto a cause naturali, sia esso accidentale o di altra natura, comporta l'apertura di un'inchiesta giudiziaria d'ufficio, come in questo caso".

Il procuratore capo di Santa Cruz de Tenerife, José Luis Sánchez-Jáuregui, ritiene "molto difficile" che venga aperto un procedimento penale per la morte del calciatore, poiché il colpo ricevuto è avvenuto durante un'azione di gioco e sarà necessario attendere la decisione della Commissione Disciplinare Sportiva. In dichiarazioni rilasciate all'agenzia EFE, Sánchez-Jáuregui ha sottolineato che un antico principio della Corte Suprema stabilisce che gli infortuni sul campo sportivo non sono punibili, salvo, ovviamente, in caso di aggressione da parte di un giocatore nei confronti di un altro durante un'interruzione del gioco.

Updated November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Kaja Perina

I know I'm not alone to have experienced people say something like, "I love my dog" and then observe countless different sorts of human-dog relationships that can change in the blink of an eye. I'm not saying that the people who claim they love their dog don't, but in many cases when they tell me that and I see how they mistreat their canine companion I can only say, "I glad they don't love me."

I've always wanted to know more about the variegated nature of human-dog relationships and was pleased to learn of Melina Jacques' recent book What It Really Means To Love Dogs: A Cultural And Ethical Exploration in which she discusses "a set of contradictions that are widely experienced but rarely named. Certain forms of intrusion become routine, while those expected to accommodate them are often left without a clear way to articulate what is happening."

Marc Bekoff: What are some of the topics you consider and what are some of your major messages?

Melina Jacques: One of the book’s central arguments is that dogs have become powerful cultural symbols associated with goodness, compassion, love, loyalty, and virtue. A significant portion of the book explores how this symbolic status emerged and why it is so vigorously defended.

The book examines how this symbolic status influences public attitudes toward dogs and can discourage critical discussion of issues that would be readily and openly addressed in other contexts. It explores the consequences of dog ownership for human communities, including attacks, chronic noise, neighbourhood conflict, relationship breakdown, and the diversion of resources and attention away from other priorities. For example, dog attacks in the United States result in reconstructive surgery approximately every 19 minutes, with children disproportionately represented among the victims. Yet discussions of dog attacks are often redirected toward individual circumstances or responsible ownership rather than examining broader questions about the normalization of dog ownership and public safety.

Persistent barking can have consequences far beyond sleep deprivation. Repeated exposure to unwanted, unpredictable noise can activate the body’s physiological stress-response systems, involving stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline. Over time, chronic noise exposure can contribute to sustained psychological distress and adverse cardiovascular and metabolic health effects, effectively making people’s own homes sources of chronic environmental stress.

Conflicts over dogs' behaviour and the prioritization of dogs' needs can also contribute to tension and relationship breakdown. These are not simply isolated inconveniences but consequences of a cultural environment in which the presence and interests of dogs are often treated as beyond question. Municipalities also devote resources to animal shelters and responding to complaints and attacks.

I also examine the ecological impacts of dogs, including predation, disease transmission, disturbance of wildlife, nutrient pollution, and threats to vulnerable species. Research discussed in the book implicates dogs in 11 vertebrate extinctions and identifies nearly 200 species worldwide that are threatened by their impacts.

These impacts extend beyond direct predation. The scent of dog urine, for example, can alter wildlife behaviour, causing wild animals to avoid otherwise suitable and valuable habitat. Dog waste also introduces nutrients into the environment, contributing to nutrient pollution and affecting surrounding ecosystems. Dogs can also introduce infectious diseases and parasites into wildlife populations. Free-ranging dogs, in particular, can act as reservoirs or sources of transmission for pathogens, such as parvovirus or canine distemper, that affect wild carnivores and other susceptible species, sometimes placing additional pressure on already vulnerable populations.

Hybridization presents another ecological concern. Domestic dogs can interbreed with wild canids, including grey wolves and golden jackals, introducing domestic-dog genes into wild populations and potentially compromising their genetic integrity.

Another major theme concerns dogs themselves. Although dogs are widely regarded as among the most loved animals on Earth, I question whether many aspects of modern dog ownership truly serve their interests. For example, breeding dogs to satisfy human preferences for particular physical characteristics can produce animals with serious inherited health problems, raising questions about whether commercial interests and aesthetic preferences are being prioritized over dogs’ physical well-being and quality of life.

Similarly, behaviours that may reflect frustration or unmet needs arising from unsuitable living conditions are increasingly being managed with psychotropic medications. Rather than addressing the underlying circumstances contributing to dogs’ distress, we may simply be medicating behaviours that humans find inconvenient or undesirable.

The book also explores the economic and psychological forces that sustain dog culture, including the role of the pet industry, social incentives, identity, and moral self-image. The pet industry, for example, has developed an enormous commercial ecosystem around dog ownership, encompassing food, accessories, grooming, training, veterinary services, insurance, and other products and services. These commercial interests benefit from the normalization and expansion of dog ownership.

At the psychological level, dog ownership can also become intertwined with personal identity and moral self-image. People may regard owning or adopting a dog as evidence of their compassion and kindness, making criticism of dog-related practices feel like a personal attack rather than an opportunity to examine the consequences of those practices. The book explores how these economic interests and cultural beliefs reinforce one another, helping to sustain a culture that is rarely subjected to critical scrutiny.

At its broadest level, the book asks a simple question: What does it actually mean to love a dog? My conclusion is that caring responsibly for animals requires us to look beyond sentiment and think honestly about the consequences of the relationships we create with animals.

MB: How does your book differ from others that are concerned with some of the same general topics?

MJ: Many books explore dogs through the lenses of behaviour, cognition, welfare, history, or companionship. My book differs in that it places these topics within a larger examination of dog culture itself and the consequences that arise from it.

Rather than treating dogs solely as animals, I examine them as cultural symbols and explore how that symbolic status shapes public attitudes, social norms, and policy decisions. The book also investigates an institution that is rarely subjected to critical scrutiny: pet ownership itself.

Drawing on multiple disciplines, it investigates why dogs occupy such a unique place in human society and what consequences follow from that distinction.

MB: Are you hopeful that as people learn more about what it really means to love a dog they will give their companion canine a better and more fulfilled life, a win-win for all?

MJ: Yes, although perhaps not in exactly that way. If readers come away with a deeper understanding of dogs and a greater willingness to think critically about their needs, many dogs could benefit. One of the book’s central themes is that genuine concern for dogs requires us to ask whether the lives we create for them are truly compatible with their interests.

At the same time, I hope readers expand their circle of concern beyond individual dogs to include human communities, wildlife, ecosystems, and other animals affected by our relationship with dogs. If the book encourages people to reflect more deeply on those questions, I would consider that a positive outcome.

Dog researcher Dr. Clive Wynne calls Jacques' book "a provocative corrective" and dog expert and bioethicist Dr. Jessica Pierce captures the essence of Jacques' book as follows:

"Dog lovers who care deeply about the well-being of dogs, and about our place in a shared social and ecological world, should read this book. Melina Jacques raises challenging questions that are too often ignored, asking us to examine both the burdens that contemporary dog-keeping can place on other people and communities, the impacts our practices can have on broader ecosystems, and the costs they can impose on dogs themselves. Thoughtful, provocative, and deeply humane, this is a book that deserves to be read with an open mind."

Dr. Jonathan Balcombe, ethologist, and author of What a Fish Knows and Beyond Tooth and Claw writes: "Melina Jacques has written a thought-provoking and highly controversial examination of our relationship with dogs. Her thesis challenges deeply entrenched assumptions about what it means to live with and care for dogs, and she makes a compelling case for reconsidering many of those assumptions. Jacques knows she is swimming against a powerful current, yet she goes forward determined and undaunted. I admire that. This is a worthwhile and provocative contribution to the literature on our relationship with other animals, and one that deserves a wide readership."

What US midterm elections mean for Ukraine, Iran and elsewhere

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Donald Trump's Republicans control the US Congress, but that could change after the midterm elections. The outcome of the ballot will not only impact domestic policy but also conflicts in Europe and the Middle East.

The outcome of the midterm elections will shape US policy for years — and have repercussions around the globe. Decisions made in Washington affect both US adversaries such as Iran and countries such as Ukraine, which is seeking more support from the United States.

President Donald Trump does not have the final say when it comes to deciding where the United States goes to war or which countries get financial or military aid. While the president wields more power in the US than in parliamentary democracies such as Germany, he does not govern alone — even in the land of opportunity.

The House of Representatives and the Senate, the two chambers of Congress whose composition voters will decide this Tuesday, wield considerable influence. They decide on legislation and government spending and also serve as a check on the president and the intelligence agencies.

The president can veto legislation passed by Congress. Congress can override that veto with a two-thirds majority in both chambers. The legislature also handles impeachment proceedings. In addition, the president can only appoint Cabinet members, US ambassadors and federal judges from a list of nominees the Senate approves.

Trump’s Republicans currently hold majorities in both chambers of Congress, making it easier for the president to govern. But recent polls suggest the Democrats could take control of at least the House of Representatives and possibly even the Senate. This could shift the direction of major foreign policy issues.

Joe Biden, a Democrat, was in the White House when Russia launched its invasion of Ukraine. Alongside Europe, the US stood out as a reliable partner, supporting Ukraine with financial aid, military expertise and, above all, large-scale weapons deliveries.

The situation changed abruptly under Trump. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy  famously received a notably frosty welcome during his 2025 visit to the White House. Trump now openly admits that he gets along better with Russian President Vladimir Putin than with Zelenskyy.

The US has suspended sanctions against Russia and reduced financial and military aid to Ukraine. If Democrats win majorities in both chambers of Congress, they could put increased support for Ukraine on the agenda.

"Democrats criticize the Trump administration for not doing enough for Ukraine," Brandon Conradis, a former DW journalist and now an editor at Bloomberg Government, told DW. "They can push legislation that would provide more aid to Ukraine."

If Trump vetoed such legislation, the outcome would depend on the balance of power in Congress. But a two-thirds majority could override his veto with support from some Republicans.

Trump launched the war against Iran alongside Israel on February 28 without consulting Congress. Under the US Constitution, Congress must approve a formal declaration of war against another country. Democrats have repeatedly voted against the war and even passed a resolution, together with some Republicans, calling for an end to the hostilities. The Trump administration has ignored it.

"I don't think Congress will have huge powers here," said Michael O'Hanlon, director of research for foreign policy at the US think tank Brookings Institution, told DW, but added that Congress could perhaps prevent a major escalation by refusing to fund it.

US support for aid projects around the world that help fight Ebola, AIDS and hunger was sharply cut back by President Trump shortly after he began his second term. Among other measures, he shut down USAID, the US agency responsible for foreign development assistance.

Whether a Democratic-controlled Congress could "reactivate" the agency against the president's wishes is questionable. But Congress could push for increased aid spending and investigate the controversial closure of USAID.

"Democrats would have oversight power and use it to scrutinize the closing of USAID," Conradis said. "There will be hearings, and they will drag officials they view as responsible in front of Congress."

Ultimately, the US remains a country with a Republican president. There will be no complete reversal of course. "It's not like if Democrats win control of Congress, everything is going to change. But some things will change because the power dynamics will be different."

Binance Just Got Hacked for $6.3 Billion. Is Its Coin Still a Buy?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

BNB's strong performance in the recent past might not continue for much longer.

On the evening of Oct. 29, hackers stole an estimated $6.3 billion in funds from the Binance crypto exchange, marking the biggest hack in crypto history. The exchange has since promised to cover every customer loss, and it still has more than enough money on hand for that promise to be credible.

The exchange's blockchain didn't seem to be directly affected by the attack, yet its native token, BNB (BNB -1.03%), was down nearly 7% over the 24 hours since the breach. It's still up by 212% over the last three years. So is BNB worth buying, or is the Binance hack the beginning of its end?

As of Oct. 30, Binance's partners had frozen at least $3 billion of the stolen funds, and the exchange had set aside $6.3 billion to make its customers whole.

The drained wallets were among those listed in Binance's monthly proof of reserves, briefly leaving Binance's Ethereum reserves covering only about 94% of customers' ether balances. By Oct. 31, Binance said a new reserve snapshot showed ether and nine other assets fully backed again. So the damage to Binance's reserves looks small and temporary.

Furthermore, the hackers didn't do or steal anything that would touch Binance's supply of BNB.

The coin is used on the exchange's blockchain, and holding it can buy fee discounts on the exchange itself, so its value depends entirely on investors' trust in Binance, as it has no utility outside its ecosystem. And despite the theft, there is little indication that people have lost enough faith in Binance to stop using its exchange -- the same as in prior instances where it was affected by market crashes, breaches, or other problems.

In my view, there wasn't a very strong investment thesis for buying BNB before the hack, and there isn't one for buying it now, either. Here's why.

BNB Chain destroys BNB coins every quarter via its Auto-Burn mechanism, which removes coins from the supply based on its price and how many blocks are produced by the chain over the quarter. There's also another supply-reducing mechanism that destroys a portion of the BNB that's used to pay each transaction fee on the network.

The point of those two processes is to make the coin more appealing to hold by linking the chain's utilization to its supply.

Nonetheless, its base of transaction fees hasn't grown over the last three years; in the last week of October 2023, it had $3.1 million in fees, whereas the same week this year saw only $2.9 million. And while it's true that the coin's price went up dramatically over that period, the data suggest that demand for blockchain use isn't consistently growing, which significantly undermines the idea that BNB is going to be a good investment over the longer term.

So, even if the price of BNB could be much higher three years from now, I don't think it's worth buying.

Tested: 2027 Genesis GV60 Magma Is a Riot

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Genesis brand’s first performance variant is quick and playful, yet sophisticated.

The Genesis brand’s first performance variant isn’t subtle, particularly when painted in its signature Magma Orange hue. And the ends of its rear wing are turned up, which, to us, gives a devil’s-horns vibe. That’s appropriate, because when driving the Magma, there might as well be a persistent devilish presence on your shoulder whispering, “Push harder.”

The smirks on our faces when driving the Magma are mostly about its immensely swift acceleration, satisfying ride and handling, or the silly-but-impressive 9000-rpm fake engine noises. But the name of this new subbrand also makes us think of “liquid hot magma” (in a Dr. Evil voice, of course).

The GV60 Magma is based on the excellent Hyundai Ioniq 5 N, which introduced the fake shifting and faux engine noises to this electric platform. Peak output is the same 641 horsepower as the Ioniq 5 N, but the Magma gets the full luxury treatment. In fact, the number of Magma-specific touches is staggering for such a low-volume model, including front bucket seats and black suede upholstery with orange stitching—even the key fob dons a Magma Orange shell. Although the front seats are comfortable and supportive, we were surprised to find no lumbar adjustability.

See all results for new 2027 Genesis GV60 Magma for sale near 27705

There are also plenty of Magma-specific displays in the gauge-cluster screen that have a chronograph feel to them. They include information such as the temperatures of the two motors and battery pack, as well as the 9000-rpm tachometer integrated into the speedometer display. Then there are all the configuration settings for the chassis and powertrain modes in the center screen. It’s all thoughtfully and tastefully executed. Plus, there are elements from the regular GV60 that still wow first-timers, such as the circular shifter that flips over and looks like a crystal ball when the car is parked.

While the Ioniq 5 N mimics a 7750-rpm four-cylinder with its engine cosplay, the Magma aims higher, instead playing the tune of a 9000-rpm V-6. Is it gimmicky? Absolutely. But it’s also impressively executed. And loud: With the adjustable noise level turned up all the way, the Magma’s interior volume measures 102 decibels—about the same as a Corvette ZR1X—as it rips its way repeatedly to its pseudo-9000 rpm over and over on the way to a 166-mph top speed.

Despite weighing roughly 90 pounds more, the Magma’s performance is all but identical to the Ioniq 5 N’s, with 60 mph arriving in a scant 3.0 seconds flat and a quarter-mile blitz of 11.1 seconds at 126 mph. The two run neck and neck, at least until the Magma’s longer Boost mode (15 seconds versus 10 in the Ioniq 5 N) causes it to pull ahead above 120 mph. Although there’s a Boost button on the steering wheel, helpfully, the Magma’s extra hit automatically engages when you push the accelerator all the way to the floor.

See all results for new 2027 Genesis GV60 Magma for sale near 27705

Unlike in the Ioniq 5 N, where using the shift mode significantly slows acceleration, Genesis claims no acceleration difference when using the Magma’s fake-shifting mode. With large power cuts between fake shifts, we were highly skeptical. But we measured it both ways, and the Magma does in fact run all but identical times whether faux-shifting or not.

Wearing newer Pirelli P Zero PZ5 Elect summer rubber, versus PZ4 versions on the Ioniq 5 N, the Magma hung on for 0.97 g on the skidpad, slightly exceeding the Ioniq’s 0.96 g. Braking from both 70 mph and 100 mph was pretty much identical.

While you might think that the Magma is an always-on type of car that never settles down—some performance variants can be like that—we were even more impressed at the layers of sophistication on display here. The ride and handling balance in its middle GT mode is terrific even on choppy, undulating roads. Steering is sharp and satisfying. Driven back to back to back against its most obvious competitors—a Cadillac Optiq-V and a Tesla Model Y Performance—the Magma was hands down our favorite. The only part of its dynamic behavior that we didn’t love is a brake pedal that feels slightly squishy as it blends between regen and the brake pads and rotors.

There were some other negatives too. For one, the GV60 Magma entry point is more than 10 grand dearer than the Ioniq 5 N’s, although the Magma’s $72,145 base price is still a relative bargain compared to plenty of other luxury-brand EVs. At highway speeds, the Magma isn’t luxury-car hushed, though, with obvious wind noise, and it measured no quieter (70 decibels and 25 sones) than the Ioniq 5 N when cruising at 70 mph.

Also, the GV60 has a slightly shorter wheelbase than the Ioniq 5, and rear-seat space suffers slightly accordingly. There’s not a lot of foot room under the front seat, and the rear seating height is rather low as well, pushing occupants’ knees upward. Then there’s range. Compared with the least efficient variant of the GV60 line, the Magma is down a significant 45 miles of EPA range, landing at a 222-mile estimate. But that’s a mixed-driving figure, and in our 75-mph highway-range test, the Magma made it only 200 miles.

Is the Magma answering a question many buyers are asking? Clearly not, as Genesis is selling it only at a limited number of dealers in just three states: New York, New Jersey, and California.

But for those who find their way to a Magma, fun is in store.

My Parents Took Me Voting. Now I'm Doing The Same With My Daughter.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Your candidate might not always win. But, as my parents taught me, it’s important to show up anyway.

My earliest memories of voting take place at the elementary school up the street from my parents’ house. Every Election Day, my parents would bring me and my younger sister along with them to the polls. I remember standing in the long lines impatiently wondering when it’d be our turn. I remember at least one year where there was a mock ballot for kids. And, perhaps most importantly to elementary-school-aged me, I remember receiving the prized Future Voter sticker at the end.

At the time, I didn’t think of any of this as civic education. My parents weren’t necessarily sitting us down for lectures about democracy or giving us pop quizzes on the Voting Rights Act of 1965 (although that would come later during my AP U.S. Government class). My parents were simply voting and we were watching. But, as it turns out, we were learning by observing.

My parents made it abundantly clear to my sister and me that voting was both a right and a responsibility — and that this wasn’t always the case for people who looked like us. As Black girls growing up in America, that history mattered. We learned that Black people had marched, organized, been beaten and even killed for the right my parents exercised so routinely at the elementary school around the corner.

So, when Election Day came around, we went. And not just for the presidential elections every four years. My parents participated in every election — local, federal, didn’t matter. The responsibility was the same.

By the time I was finally old enough to vote myself, I couldn’t wait.

I missed being able to vote in the 2004 presidential election by a year, which meant the 2008 election would be my first chance to vote for president. And I felt elated that my first presidential vote would be for the country’s first Black president.

At the time, I was working as a reporter at my community newspaper in a conservative county in Maryland. On Election Day, a colleague and I spent hours driving around to various polling places, interviewing voters and reporting on turnout before returning to the Board of Elections to wait for results.

After work, I went home and watched the national returns with my dad. When Barack Obama walked onto the stage in Chicago’s Grant Park with his beautiful Black family, I remember staring at the television in disbelief.

Wow, I thought to myself, soaking in the moment. I just witnessed history.

But elections don’t always end with the history you hoped to witness.

In 2016, I proudly cast my ballot for Hillary Clinton and went to bed believing we were about the elect the country’s first woman president. We all know how that turned out.

Four years later, I voted for Joe Biden, but I was especially excited about his running mate, Kamala Harris, whom I’d supported in the Democratic primary. Watching a Black and Asian woman become vice president meant something different to me. Of course, representation isn’t the only reason to vote for someone and I think we’re all doing ourselves a disservice if we pretend as if identity is entirely separate from politics. As a Black woman in America, I’d spent most of my life looking at the highest echelons of power and rarely seeing anyone who looks like you.

In the spring of 2024, I picked up my then two-year-old daughter Violet from daycare, went to swim lessons and then swung by our neighborhood elementary school to vote in the Illinois presidential primary. With her little puffs and binky in tow, she naturally became the star of the polling place.

Our next-door neighbor, Mr. Steve, was working the polls that day and gave Violet a Future Voter sticker. She was entranced. To her, the sticker was probably the best part of the whole outing, which, when I thought about it, was exactly how I felt when I was her age (and, let’s be real, is also how I feel as an adult).

Afterward, we went outside to the school playground and, as I watched her run around, it hit me what a full-circle moment this had become. And then I had another thought: This could be a children’s book.

I wanted to write a book that could explain voting to children around Violet’s age: something that would talk about walking into a polling place; why grown-ups stood in line to fill out ballots; what even is a ballot; and why any of this matters anyway.

That fateful afternoon eventually became the premise of Violet Goes Voting, an early reader about a little girl accompanying her parents to the polls and learning that voting is one way we use our voices. But the book was just the beginning.

That fall, my husband and I took Violet with us when we voted in the presidential election. This time, I had a very specific picture in my head of what the moment might look like in both real life and the book version. My daughter would sit on my lap as I cast my ballot to elect the first Black woman president of the United States.

My parents had taken their Black daughters to the polls so we would understand that our voices mattered. Decades later, I would take mine with me as I helped elect a Black woman to the White House. Except, of course, that’s not what happened.

Like so many people, I woke up the next morning devastated. The history I had imagined sharing with my daughter would have to wait. Since then, I’ve thought a lot about what, exactly, I want Violet to inherit from me and learn from all of this.

Because maybe the lesson was never supposed to be that if you show up, your candidate will win. My parents certainly never promised that to me. What they taught me was the importance of showing up anyway.

Voting is a small part of civic engagement, but it was my introduction to it nonetheless. Long before I knew what a primary was, before I understood the harsh reality of voter suppression or could name any of my congressional representatives, before I was old enough to fill in a bubble next to a candidate’s name, I knew what Election Day looked like.

It looked like my parents making time to go to the polls. It looked like my sister and me eagerly tagging along. It looked like waiting our turn. And, yes, it looked like getting a sticker at the end.

Now, as a mom, I understand that those seemingly ordinary trips were teaching me something much bigger: This is what our family does. We show up. We pay attention. We responsibly exercise the rights people before us fought to secure. We don’t take them for granted.

Only time will tell who Violet will vote for when she’s old enough to cast her first ballot. And, frankly, we have a while before that happens. She’s much more interested in playgrounds than politics at the moment, which is exactly how it should be for an innocent five-year-old.

But I hope that when her first Election Day arrives, she’ll look back on our family selfie from outside the voting place in 2024. I hope she’ll remember me reading to her the book that she inspired. I hope she understands why walking into that booth and making her own choice carries a history older than the both of us combined.

But most of all and most importantly, I simply hope she shows up. Because that’s the inheritance my parents gave me and my sister. And now it’s mine to pass on.

Salesforce to buy Cresta and bring its AI voice agents into Service Cloud

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cresta’s AI voice agents answer customer calls for Marriott, Comcast and Alaska Airlines. The deal follows Salesforce’s $5.7bn agreement for the customer messaging firm Braze and its plan to buy Rune.

Salesforce has signed a definitive agreement to buy Cresta, whose voice agents handle complex customer calls from start to finish, Salesforce said on Monday. The deal should close in the first quarter of Salesforce’s fiscal year 2028, subject to regulatory approvals.

Salesforce did not disclose a price. It agreed to pay $2.7bn, according to a person familiar with the deal, as Valida Pau and Kevin McLaughlin reported for The Information. TNW has not independently verified the figure.

Cresta is nine years old and started out at Stanford University’s AI Lab, The Information reported. The deal builds up Salesforce’s customer service business, the outlet wrote.

Cresta calls itself a voice-first operating system for customer experience. Its voice agents hold natural, real-time conversations and handle interruptions, Salesforce said. Marriott, Comcast and Alaska Airlines use them at enterprise scale.

The same platform guides human representatives during live calls and analyses every interaction. It also coaches staff and tests the voice agents, holding both to the same quality standard.

“Voice is where service is hardest to get right, and where it matters most to customers,” said Kishan Chetan, EVP and GM of Agentforce and Service Cloud at Salesforce.

Salesforce plans to join Cresta with Service Cloud, its customer service software. The aim is to link each conversation to the customer’s history and to the outcome, which Salesforce lists as resolution, retention and revenue.

After the deal closes, Cresta will keep supporting the contact centre and telephony platforms its customers use today, Salesforce said.

“Now, by uniting our platform with Salesforce, we will realize our full vision at scale, embedding real-time conversation intelligence and agentic experiences directly into Salesforce’s first-class customer service Platform,” said Ping Wu, chief executive of Cresta.

Cresta follows other recent Salesforce deals. It agreed a $5.7bn Braze deal on 20 October. Days before Cresta, it announced plans to buy Rune, a national security software company.

In September, Salesforce closed its Fin purchase.

Sarina Wiegman's England will face France, Austria and Portugal in the UEFA Women's Nations League, a competition that doubles as qualifying for the Los Angeles 2028 Olympics.

The draw also placed the Republic of Ireland, Wales and Scotland in League A.

Only the team finishing top of each group secures a place at the Games, with group winners also advancing to the Nations League semi-finals.

The Republic of Ireland have the hardest-looking assignment on paper, having been drawn alongside world champions Spain, as well as Norway and Switzerland.

Wales must contend with Denmark, Italy and Iceland, while Scotland's group features Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden.

Northern Ireland, competing in Group B, will meet Poland, Finland and Belarus.

Matchdays one and two are set for February and March, with the third and fourth rounds of games following in April. The final league stage fixtures come in June, ahead of the Women's World Cup in Brazil.

The semi-finals are due to be played in October, while the third place play-off and the final are scheduled for late November and early December.

Teams finishing fourth in League A will drop into League B, while the winners of League B will move up.

England missed out on the Paris Olympics in 2024 and representing Team GB after failing to win their Nations League group the previous year.

The Lionesses were placed in a group alongside the Netherlands, Scotland and Belgium. Despite winning 6-0 in their final group game against Scotland, England were still pipped to top spot by the Netherlands on goal difference.

Wiegman’s team will not want to slip-up again, but will not have things all their own way in a group that also contains France.

England have also not secured their spot at next year’s World Cup yet, as they must first get past Ukraine in a two-legged play-off, with the two games taking place later this month and in early December.

England have achieved significant success under Wiegman in recent years, winning the Euros in 2022 and 2025, either side of making the World Cup final in 2023.

They will now set their sights on qualifying for next year’s World Cup ahead of a push for Olympic glory the following year.
