การเลือกเว็บแทงบอล ออนไลน์ เว็บไหนดี ที่รองรับระบบ ฝาก-ถอน Auto และ True Money Wallet ควรพิจารณาเว็บตรงที่มีความน่าเชื่อถือ ได้มาตรฐานสากล และปรับอัตราค่าน้ำคงที่

การคัดเลือกโปรโมชั่นคุ้มค่า ถือเป็นตัวช่วยเพิ่มทุนชั้นดี แต่หากเลือกโดยไม่เช็กเงื่อนไขให้ถี่ถ้วน เครดิตฟรีที่ได้มาอาจกลายเป็นภาระในการถอนเงิน การพิจารณา เว็บแทงบอลดีที่สุด จึงต้องดูควบคู่กับความยากง่ายของเงื่อนไขการทำยอดหมุนเวียน (Turnover) ด้วย

ยอดเดิมพันสะสมรวมทั้งหมด (ได้+เสีย) ที่ระบบกำหนดให้คุณต้องเล่นให้ครบตามเกณฑ์ก่อนถอนเงินออก

สูตรคำนวณ: (เงินฝาก + โบนัส) x จำนวนเท่าเทิร์น = ยอดเดิมพันสะสมที่ต้องทำ

การวิเคราะห์บอลกับการเลือกเว็บบอร์ดหรือ เว็บแทงบอลออนไลน์ เป็นสองปัจจัยที่ต้องทำงานควบคู่กัน หากวิเคราะห์แม่นยำแต่เลือกเว็บที่ไม่มีประสิทธิภาพหรืออัตราราคาค่าน้ำเสียเปรียบ โอกาสทำกำไรระยะยาวก็จะลดลงอย่างมาก

การคัดเลือกแทงบอลเว็บไหนดี ในปี 2026 เพื่อให้ได้เว็บแทงบอลดีที่สุด เชื่อถือได้ หัวใจสำคัญที่สุดคือการเดิมพันกับเว็บตรงไม่ผ่านเอเย่นต์ การันตีความปลอดภัย 100% การเลือกเว็บพนันบอล ดีที่สุด จะช่วยให้คุณสมัครเว็บบอล และวางเดิมพันได้อย่างมั่นใจ ถอนเงินได้จริงทุกยอดบิล

Matthew McConaughey's son Levi McConaughey shared sweet insight into their father-son bond in an exclusive interview with E! News at the Way of the Warrior Kid premiere.

Levi McConaughey is never dazed nor confused about Matthew McConaughey’s guidance.

In fact, as the 18-year-old—whose role in the Apple TV movie Way of the Warrior Kid marks his second onscreen appearance—explained, his dad shows up for him in ways that’s more than just alright.

“He's in my corner as my dad and as my friend,” Levi told E! News in an exclusive interview at the movie Way of the Warrior Kid premiere in Los Angeles Nov.19, “and I couldn't be more happy and blessed and thankful for him being a dad to me, which means a lot.”

“I’m just thankful to be in an environment with G [director McG] and Chris and Jude and Ava,” he noted, “and everyone to do with this movie that made it a really collaborative, creative environment. It was great.”

Matthew’s words of wisdom echoes what he’s previously shared about what he’s emphasized to Levi as he steps in front of the camera.

“You’ve got to be able to be able to detail with rejection, that’s part of it,” the Oscar winner told E! News in August. “You’ve got to hopefully love doing it and love the process so much that enough ‘nos’ don’t matter. As Matthew noted, you miss 100 percent of the shots you don’t take.

“I just try to push, to say, ‘It’s better to make a fool of yourself and find out’ then to leave an audition and go, ‘Oh I had that moment and I hesitated,’” he continued. “Make a fool of yourself. Fail more. Try and fail more. No one gives as much of a damn as you think they do.”

A certain character’s fate hangs in the balance.

Nothing is simple in MobLand. Two seasons in, the plot has racked up more lines than Eddie put up his nose the night he stabbed Tommy Stevenson in season 1. Actually, that’s not strictly true. One thing is simple in MobLand: Eddie. He is a simpleton of truly Homeric (as in Simpson) proportions: a smarmy, sneering, simpering Freudian mess, channelled with gimlet-eyed precision by Anson Boon. All he wants is his God-given right to be respected.

Trouble is, when you’re an anti-Midas who turns everything he touches to shit, respect is a dish that never leaves the kitchen. Eddie is, in Kevin’s words in season 1, “a knifey little twerp.” That stabbing set the whole bloody saga in motion, so it’s only right that two seasons later we end with Eddie the Incompetent. Full circle, then, like a roundabout made of corpses.

MobLand opened with Eddie turning a stabbing into a gang war, so it seems fitting that we end with Eddie turning an assassination into an own goal. But we’ll get to that.

It seems Conrad’s chickens are coming home to roost, and his marbles are jangling around his head like loose change. We open the episode with his card being declined at a petrol station. In frustration, he shoots the till boy dead. But he knows what it means.

“We’re getting lifted,” he tells Maeve in the car. Police are raiding the entire business: bank accounts are frozen, warrants are out, and Tattersall is telling reporters the Harrigans have links to terror groups across the globe. The family convenes at a dingy old safe house out of town.

“We’re terrorists,” says Seraphina. “We’re looking at life in Guantánamo. It’s now or never.”

Her plan has been bubbling for a while. She calls it Harrigan 2.0: going global.

Remember the diplomatic suitcase of diamonds Seraphina collected from her billionaire stepdad Charlton in the last episode? It was meant to fund her and Bella’s takeover of the Harrigan operation. Now, it seems, Harry has it. He takes her aside to let her know he knows. “How did you know?” she asks, incredulous.

Then he says the three words he says more than any others in MobLand: “It’s my job.”

Inside the safe house, the family are all there, and everyone has a plan to pitch. Kevin wants to crawl back to Kat and hand over Conrad, Maeve, and Eddie. “Clean the house,” he growls to Harry, out of earshot of the others.

Conrad, meanwhile, isn’t sure what to do. “What are our options?” he asks Harry.

“We don’t have any,” comes the terse reply. “Either we run seven ways and it’s every man and woman for themselves, or we go back to Kat and try to strike some kind of deal.”

Kevin proposes offering her 50 percent and first working out who’s a liability. “And right now I’m seeing a lot of liabilities. Right, Harry?”

Bella has had it up to ’ere with Kevin’s pusillanimity. “Oh, you nearly got to the end of a sentence without saying ‘Harry,’” she taunts. “I know he saved you from a bumming once but… Jesus.”

Kevin pulls a pistol and fires, missing her ear by millimeters. Conrad draws. “Put the gun down, boy,” he hisses. Bella steps forward. “Go on then, big boy.”

You could cut the tension with piano wire.

“Fuck this, I’m getting us out of this mess,” says Seraphina, defusing the standoff by fetching a fistful of diamonds from the car. “Fifteen Type IIa brilliants. North of ten million euros,” she tells Conrad, placing them in his hand.

The plan: offer Kat a cut of the empire. “This time tomorrow we’re back in business.”

Remember that glorious, long-winded speech Conrad gave in Spain about the good old days, how he came from nothing, and how Maeve has been his compass through the carnage of his life? “We were born outlaws and we’ll die outlaws,” he bellowed.

Well, now he knows the jig is up. Looking balefully at the floor, he grunts: “Do it.”

So how will Harry play this? He slips out to his 4x4 and rings Kat. “At 5 p.m. I will meet your diplomatic courier. I will pass to said courier €10 million in untraceable diamonds. Upon authentication, I will give you the location of the Harrigan family.”

A double cross? It must be a ruse. He simply MUST have a plan… Then, hanging up, he mutters: “Fuck this, I’m done.”

He returns to the family and lies: “Kat will take the ten million and 50 percent.” Too easy by half.

As Frankie put it two episodes ago: “Is that it? Unless you’ve got some brilliant plans, that is actually a bit shit. What are you up to, Harry?”

We’re more than halfway through and haven’t heard a peep from Frankie, our laconic, crocodilian villain. Ah, but here he is, getting the news of Harry’s volte-face from Tattersall. “How do we know it’s not a bluff?”

“He’s not stupid,” says Tattersall. “He knows when the game’s up.”

“Well, you know me,” leers Frankie. “I can’t resist a gamble.” Guns are grabbed from car boots, and off he pootles with a gaggle of tooled-up goons to send the Harrigans back to Hell.

It’s all unfolding very quickly now, as denouements always should. Back at the safe house, Conrad is still seeing the dead, including the petrol station lad he just murdered. Maeve says he’s been saying their names in his sleep.

The old loon’s marbles are now departing his head like passengers from a cancelled flight. But there’s one thing that’ll straighten any man out: a black pearl of Maeve wisdom. “We’ve been stuck up tighter holes than Kat McAllister’s,” she reassures him. “But when you’re stuck up someone’s hole, there’s at least two ways out.”

Quite so. And who better to lead the way than Harry? As ever, he picks the exit that leads straight back to where he came in. Unbeknownst to us, he has gone up Kat’s backside, rummaged about, and come out the other end holding her entire career. The “betrayal” was a ruse from the start.

So when Frankie’s goons arrive armed to the teeth, it’s a trap: flash grenades, smoke grenades, poison gas, and Zosia on the exit with a machine gun for anyone daft enough to run. “Oh, Harry,” concedes Frankie, before a mighty gunfight erupts and Harry tears through the house like Keyser Söze in a gas mask. “So this isn’t the beginning of a beautiful friendship,” smiles Frankie, choking in the corner. Harry shoots him in the knee.

Then Harry rings Kat. Those “untraceable” diamonds? Very traceable indeed, straight to a terrorist organization. He has bribed a low-ranking diplomatic courier to deliver them along with a “little present, just for you.”

She opens the box, ashen-faced. It’s Donnie’s blue and rotting head. “This one you might find harder to justify,” he tells her.

“My family are gonna hunt you down and feed you your balls,” she spits.

“Probably,” says Harry. “But not before they feed you yours.”

Checkmate, Kat. The black queen has no more squares to go.

But wait … Harry can offer her one square. “I can fix this,” he says.

Here’s what she’s going to do. She’ll resign. She’ll kill the police investigation. The Harrigans’ accounts will reopen and they’ll come off the terror list.

Cut to a London corridor of power, where a faceless civil servant informs DS Kavanagh that “continuation of your terrorism financing line will interfere with an active joint intelligence operation between British and American security services. You will stand down at the close of business.”

And so, full circle. Maeve is electrocuting Frankie’s balls with a car battery. Kevin is building bridges with Bella. Conrad is going madder by the day. And Eddie?

The Semtex he blackmailed Paul for was meant for Harry, at Maeve’s behest. In the hands of any other Harrigan, this would be a doddle. But not Eddie. Not the knifey little twerp, a man who could trip over his own foreskin in an empty car park.

And he does. But first, the hints. Something strange has been going on with Jan all episode: the soppy Damien Rice soundtrack, the wistful dream sequences of the good times she shared with Harry. Her Harry. The one she fell for in the first place. She seems to have forgiven him. They’re even taking a family holiday.

But there’s one last stop before they go: Harrigan HQ, where Eddie is lying in wait. While Harry negotiates the terms of the holiday with Conrad, Gina and Jan stroll over to the lake. It’s Eddie’s moment, his chance to shine. He plants the Semtex in their car, hides around the corner, arms the bomb, finger hovering over the detonator … then a phone call.

By Finn McCool’s cobblers, it’s Kelly “Mad Dog” O’Connor, the lunatic son of the Medusa, whom Maeve sent Eddie to kill. “I’m coming for you,” growls the voice. “The Mad Dog is coming.”

Gina, Jan and Harry return to the car. Only Jan gets in. In his quivering, fearful state, Eddie trips and drops the detonator.

That’s Jan up in smoke, along with Harry’s plans for a quiet life. It may spell the end of Jan and all her problems, but it’s only the beginning of Eddie’s. He now has Mad Dog O’Connor to answer to, and Harry.

Then the credits roll, and what a gloriously cruel place to leave it.

The only other loose end is Frankie. We’re left to assume he met a long, painful death at the business end of Maeve’s car battery, but we never see a body. I truly hope he does a Harry and wriggles out of one last tight hole—Maeve’s hole—with one good knee and a long memory.

Season 3 can’t come soon enough. When Harry works out whose finger was on the detonator, Eddie’ll wish a knifey little twerp is all he could be.

GameStop boss Ryan Cohen (last seen attempting to buy eBay) has called for the return of physical game discs, lamenting the lack of control players have over digital purchases in a chat with Bloomberg held on the eve of Grand Theft Auto 6's release.

In case you missed it, a media meteor impacted yesterday and blasted our landscape free of life. Grand Theft Auto 6 is here and—despite our Ted Litchfield's noble attempts to get you all thinking about Queen's Domain instead (you should play Queen's Domain)—it's all the world can talk about. It's also only available digitally.

"Unquestionably, the decision to move to all digital has hurt consumers, and it’s also hurt GameStop", said Cohen, remarking that players ought to be able to do with their purchased games as they will: "When you buy a game, you should be able to sell it, trade it or lend it to a friend". Cohen did not namecheck GTA 6 specifically, but did say that he had been speaking about this issue with all "the obvious" publishers.

GameStop, of course, makes a tidy chunk of change from resales of games traded in at its stores—a segment of its business that becomes impossible to maintain in a world without physical discs for players to bring in and sell. Publishers oppose trade-ins "because removing a used copy eliminates an alternative to buying new", says Cohen, accusing them of "taking away competition" by restricting sales to digital-only channels.

GTA 6 is a portent of the world to come. Earlier this year, Sony announced that PlayStation would drop physical discs starting in January 2028, and has held firm to that course in the face of a vocal player uproar.

Issues around game ownership have reached the point that politicians are weighing in: last month US lawmaker Richie Torres announced the Power To The Players Act, that would prompt the US Federal Trade Commission to protect players' access to purchased games, even in the case of online-only games that get unplugged.

"When you pay for a game, you should own it", said Torres. "Consumers must have the right to own, the right to play, and the right to transfer the games they buy. A game that can be taken away the moment a company pulls the plug is not a purchase. It is a rental disguised as a sale".

When the client is a good fit on paper, but still not a good fit

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I was so excited to work with a new client. 

Perfect for my expertise and the type of company I prefer to work with. Meeting the client for an exploratory call felt like a dream. A well-respected brand to add to my portfolio.

But once the contract was signed, it was a struggle from Day 1. We parted ways six months later. 

Most advice about struggling client relationships assumes there is an obvious problem, like that the client is overly demanding or hard to reach. But in this case, it was something else. The vibes were off. When that happens, it’s less obvious what to fix. 

Recognizing the difference between a difficult client and a poor fit can help you decide sooner whether to adjust the working relationship or simply walk away.

Why a client can look right on paper and still not work

The things I vet for during the sales process can be different from the things that make a working relationship successful. 

Before signing, I look at the potential client’s industry and the scope of work. The client’s budget has to be aligned with my rate, and I have to feel confident that I can deliver what the client is asking for. That’s the relationship “on paper.”

But what’s often unknown is how the client interacts with a consultant or contractor (like me). I’m also unaware of any internal politics at the company that might impact the working relationship. Those details usually become apparent once I start delivering actual work. 

Is it a difficult client or a poor fit?

A difficult client has problems like not paying on time or asking for work outside of the scope. These can be fixed (or at least managed) with a solid contract and clear boundaries. Most of the biggest client red flags solopreneurs face are in this category.

A poor fit is different. The client may pay on time and stay within scope, but other parts of the working relationship feel strained. Maybe stakeholder feedback gets under your skin because of how it’s presented. Or you find yourself dreading the work, even though, on paper, it’s what you had agreed to do.

One thing to ask yourself: Would a different consultant or contractor have the same trouble with this client? If the answer is yes, you’re likely dealing with a difficult client. But if you can picture someone else doing great work for them, the problem is probably fit.

A second question: If this client always pays on time and never asked for anything outside of scope, would you still want the work? A “no” usually means fit is the issue.

What to try before you walk away

A poor fit sometimes might improve if you’re able to directly address the thing that’s causing you angst. It’s worth trying before you decide.

If the problem is, for example, “too many cooks” providing you with feedback and contradictory opinions, you might be able to solve this. Something like:

“It’s easier for me to make sure I deliver what you need if you consolidate your feedback. Can you work on your end to send me a list of thoughts/changes? That way, I don’t start down one path only to find that you want me to go in a different direction.”

In some cases, your client might be willing to change — they may simply not have experience working with a contractor or consultant. 

You might also give yourself a checkpoint, like an additional month of work after the conversation. If nothing has changed by then, you have your answer.

Admitting the relationship isn’t working

When you work solo, a poor fit can drag you down. In a company, a colleague might take over the account, or a manager could reassign the work. As a solopreneur, there’s no one to hand it to. And since your income depends on the work, ending the relationship means replacing that revenue.

But staying has a cost. Stress. Frustration. All the things you were supposed to escape by choosing a solopreneur life instead of a corporate job.

If the conversation with your client doesn’t change anything, more effort probably won’t fix it. At that point, calling it a poor fit and moving on is a business decision. 

It helps to remember that a poor fit says nothing about your skills or the client’s character. It simply didn’t work out.

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Devices found to misinterpret medication instructions and other scenarios impacting human safety

The Dutch Data Protection Authority (AP) has officially advised consumers against buying AI-powered smart glasses after safety tests revealed concerning behaviors beyond their controversial recording capabilities.

The AP recently tested six different models of smart glasses and found glaring issues, including a willingness to display dating advice to an adult glasses-wearer who expressed an interest in a minor.

In another case involving a cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) simulation, testers noted that one pair of glasses informed the wearer that emergency services had been called and were scheduled to arrive at the scene, despite no one having made the call.

Some glasses did not properly process safety warnings on children’s toys, others made mistakes when reading medication leaflets, and some misinterpreted warnings for extreme weather events.

The AP did not specify the models it tested, saying only that they tested six models ranging from under €100 ($110) to over €500 ($550), including two that "were extensively tested in simulated real-life situations where human safety played a key role."

A spokesperson also declined to match any specific behavior to a model by price point (minor dating advice linked to a €354 pair of glasses, for example), adding that the research was intended to highlight the risks of AI glasses in a broader sense.

"Glasses that film you unnoticed are already concerning. Glasses that also give you incorrect advice when someone's life is at stake go a step too far," said Monique Verdier, vice chair at the AP.

"AI offers many opportunities, but we must be able to trust that technology. With the camera glasses examined, that trust cannot currently be justified."

The warning issued this week follows the regulator's previous work on smart glasses. The AP reminded the public in September that everyone has the right to privacy and encouraged organizations to ban them where necessary.

The same warning mentioned the potential risks regarding data processing under the GDPR, as well as those related to the training of AI.

The AP doubled down on these earlier criticisms, saying the latest tests showed that the AI functions rely on processing data using servers mostly located outside of the European Union. Relatedly, it found that users were not given sufficient information about how the AI was processing the data they captured.

On a cybersecurity note, the tests threw up a range of issues, including the ability for people nearby the glasses-wearer to connect to the device without permission, view recordings, and in some cases operate the camera.

Further, the regulator echoed the pre-existing concerns about smart specs - those that helped give them their colloquial name “pervert glasses.”

Its tests found that with "virtually all of the camera glasses examined," the recording lights that are supposed to clearly show when a user is recording were dim and easily coverable.

The AP said that models could also be wangled into recording even when that light indicator was covered, which goes against what manufacturers so often claim.

"As a result, bystanders can barely see whether they are being filmed," it said.

The technology has a number of beneficial use cases, such as assisting the visually impaired, those with lower literacy levels, or for any number of other scenarios where people need help understanding the world around them

However, the watchdog's official stance on the technology, as it is today, deems smart glasses "unsafe… due to the risks to users and bystanders."

Verdier said politicians in the Netherlands and across Europe should take note of the results when developing AI policy.

"It is important that we, as a society, learn to deal with these types of high-risk AI products," she said.

"Simply banning everything provides some leeway in the beginning, but we cannot sustain that indefinitely. It is important that we collectively work on awareness, resilience, and ethical conscience."

When DEF CON announced a no pervert glasses policy ahead of its event in August, the move was broadly welcomed by attendees.

"Love to see a ‘no pervert glasses’ policy at DEF CON," said Eva Galperin, EFF director of cybersecurity, at the time.

Several other organizations have implemented similar restrictions, including Monopoly Events, which manages UK Comic Cons, national treasure pub chain Wetherspoons, and even Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE).

Most members of the general public will have at least come to terms with the fact that the smartphone generation, and their willingness to snap shots anywhere and everywhere, means there is no enshrined human right to privacy in public spaces.

Public video recording once carried an air of expectation or consideration for bystanders’ personal privacy, although smartphone users have slowly eradicated that over time.

Like it or not, it's generally understood that a smartphone is usually recording something - or someone - at any given time, especially in urban areas.

On paper, the fact that smart glasses can record in public spaces isn’t wildly different from what’s already come to be expected from smartphones.

However, given how similar they appear to analog glasses and the ability to cover the recording light, it makes the act of public recording via glasses far more surreptitious, and thus concerning.

With smartphones, it's easy to see from the user's body language - holding the phone out in front of them - that they are recording. There are no overt signals displayed by wearers of smart glasses during image or video capture, which fuels a sense of unease about the technology in general.

A quick search online for "smart glasses privacy violation" yields a slew of complaints about the technology, often regarding behavior toward women and children.

There are also worries about how easily the devices can get past security checks, potentially providing opportunities to those who wish to cause greater harm than a mere blight on personal privacy.

A joint investigation by The Observer and BBC Radio 4 recently revealed how AI-enabled glasses went undetected through the airport-style security scanners used in the UK parliament.

The two news outlets found that a £60 ($78) pair of Chinese-made glasses bypassed the scanners and could be used to read upside-down documents on MPs' desks, recording data such as phone numbers and addresses to be accessed at a later point. ®

Conte on Chelsea success story, meeting Kante and Fabregas destiny

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Antonio Conte reveals what changed to win the Premier League at Chelsea, the Arsenal player that ‘killed’ them, his funny reaction to meeting N’Golo Kante, and why Cesc Fabregas was ‘destined’ to be a coach.

The coach is currently waiting for his next role, after walking away from Napoli with the Scudetto and Supercoppa Italiana in his two seasons at the Stadio Diego Armando Maradona.

He sat down with the Cronache di Spogliatoio podcast and discussed his long career as a coach, including the experience at Chelsea from 2016 to 2018.

In that spell at Stamford Bridge, he won the Premier League title immediately, then the FA Cup, but admits it required a radical rethink of his Juventus tactics.

“I had this sort of 3-3-4 formation at Juventus, so when I went to Chelsea, I started with the idea of a 4-2-4 formation, but we didn’t have the players at that moment to make it work,” said Conte.

“I had Diego Costa, Eden Hazard, Wilian and Pedro as the wide men, and Oscar as a second striker, so I used a 4-2-3-1 with all of them and kept some of the same movements.

“We had started well, but after every game I told the staff, we are winning, but I don’t feel this is my team. We were top of the table, but after the break for international duty we took on Francesco Guidolin’s Swansea and it was chaos.

“Anything could’ve happened, it was 2-2, but when there is a sense of instability, these are not my teams. I need sides who control the game. I felt that I didn’t have complete control.”

The turning point came from a painful defeat to local rivals Arsenal.

“It was my first season in the Premier League, but there were Pep Guardiola, Jurgen Klopp, Jose Mourinho, all the best managers. We lost 2-1 at home to Liverpool, then were 3-0 down at half-time to Arsenal.

“Hector Bellerin was killing us down the left, it was one of the few times I was ashamed,” said Conte on the Italian language podcast.

“I realised that was the time I had to change, because I needed to give more protection to Oscar.

“I put Ivanovic on the right with David Luiz and Cahill, Azpilicueta on the right, basically a 3-4-3. It was the turning point, as the next game against Hull City I put Azpilicueta in the back three, he is a soldier and works wherever you put him, and Moses who Chelsea had wanted to sell.”

Conte also revealed that some of the meetings with Chelsea players didn’t quite go as planned, but they worked out in the end.

“It was not a transfer session worthy of Chelsea. Kante had won the Premier League title with Leicester City, I asked for two players, Wanyama and Kante. I’d seen Kante play several times, with him it was as if we were playing with 12 or 13, he never felt tired.

“He walked in behind his agent, I couldn’t even see him. Kante is not a tall man, but he looked like a child, with this big smile. I thought, I expected him to be taller…

“I hoped I hadn’t made a mistake asking for this player, the Premier League they were all huge and physically strong, but the fear only lasted a minute. I spoke to him, Kante is one of the best players I ever coached, and an exceptional lad.”

Conte reveals the aim at Chelsea that season was for a top 10 finish, but they ended up winning the Premier League.

“We won 13 games in a row, that is when I felt this was my team, it was in control. Before that, playing three at the back was practically blasphemous in England, but now it has become a cult and many teams do it.”

Cesc Fabregas was in that Chelsea team too, and has now gone on to become a coach in his own right at Como, qualifying for the Champions League.

“His curiosity, his desire to learn, to modify, Cesc was destined for greatness,” insisted Conte.

“He was a thinker on the field too, so that helped. He could see the move before he got the ball, was in the right position to receive it. When you get these players who think, they are destined to be coaches.”
