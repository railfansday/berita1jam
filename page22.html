Tired of GTA 6? Here's 23 lesser-known gems that might beat it at its own game

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You can do a lot of things in GTA 6: fishing, golfing, running, jumping, dancing, racing, roaming, shopping. It goes on! There's romancing, birdwatching, DIY, and even more besides. It really is an everything game. But GTA 6 isn't for everyone.

So what if you were in the mood for some of this stuff but away from Leonida? Where else might you go? Consider this the slightly tenuous set-up, as we continue a week of Not-GTA, for us to highlight some of our favourite smaller games; brilliant lesser-known titles from the last 12 months or so that, in their own special way, beat GTA 6 at its own game.

It's very sweet that the central, driving force of GTA 6 is the strained romance between Jason and Lucia. You are, essentially, playing a virtual matchmaker, helping these star-crossed lovers reconnect and rediscover each other after fate conspires to drive a wedge between them. But you know what's better than that? Being an actual matchmaker, matching icons on a grid and enjoying the microdose dopamine rush when you clear a line on a board.

So here's Titanium Court, which goes even further: with every line cleared, you come closer to escaping a weird, 80s-core fairy castle in a game that's partly telling a story about introspective self-doubt, and partly about warring fairies. If you're getting tired of texting your virtual lover every 10 minutes, perhaps try lighting a virtual cigarette in Titanium Court, instead - I've heard it does wonders for your health.

How do you find out if you can really depend upon someone? That's a question at the heart of GTA 6. And the answer is: play Big Walk. There's nothing like gesturing through the window of a sound-proofed room hoping your friend or loved one will understand what two arms waggling around means. It means 'the high-pitched warbling sound plaque', obviously. Big Walk's wonderfully stripped-back world is also an antidote to GTA 6's maximal world. The silence and space in the game is absolutely the point. What will you fill it with?

It's not all roof repairs and late night paintjobs. Sometimes home improvement requires more drastic measures. And in Virtue and a Sledgehammer, that's taken to violent extremes. This is a game, in developers Selkie Harbour and Deconstructeam's words, about family, tradition, and being a misfit. Where an entire town - the protagonist's childhood home - faces a total spiritual teardown. Here, a brutal sledgehammer-swinging reckoning awaits as you unearth memories - some happy, some decidedly not - through violent destruction, cutting through mechanical human facsimiles with the faces of the ones you love. It's haunting, devastating stuff; a very different kind of do-it-yourself.

You work, you sleep, and then you die. That's how it's got to be. So if you're going to toil mercilessly for the man, you can at least hope for an interesting employee. And what's more interesting than the weirdo threesome at the centre of Servant of the Lake?

Developer Rusty Lake's eponymous series returned this year with another macabre blend of point-and-click puzzling and room-escape-style adventure. And it's a corker. Perhaps not as dazzlingly experimental as some other recent entries, but it's still a delightfully grim time as you work through your three days of servitude, butlering for the oddball Vanderboom family. Beware the blood moon.

You work, you sleep, and then you die. But! What if you dream of breaking free? Escaping the chains of drudgery? That's the starting point of Denshattack!, a Jet Set Radio-inspired adventure all about sticking it to the man. Or, more specifically, a shady megacorp trying to suppress human ingenuity, creativity, and spirit. It is, as Dom put it in their five-star review, a game of "anti-capitalist, pop-punk optimism", which sees you careening across a colourful Japan - as a speeding, trick-flipping train, of all things - to bring the Miraidō corporation to its knees. Stylish and satisfying.

Umph-umph-umph-umph umph-umph-umph-umph. Oh yeah, that's the good stuff. If you want to shoot things and see them explode, and do that with immense satisfaction and a hypnotic, pulsating electronic soundtrack, this is the one. Sektori takes the feedback of a satisfying trigger pull and boils that down to its purest essence, often in spiralling, kaleidoscopic form. Hitting a full set of letters - M-I-R-A-G-E, S-E-K-T-O-R-I, etc. - summons a rainbow of action that feels like genuine ecstasy. Pure blasting and survival.

Umph-umph-umph-umph umph-umph-umph-umph. But this time imagine it's a disco beat. Let's breathe in the night, bask in those pulsing lights, and pretend we'll live this moment forever. Or, you know, we'll just shuffle our way through the throng of the dancefloor like a massive game of sokoban. That latter's the premise of developer Miracle Tea's Club Soko; a game about traversing an underground nightclub - one shunt at a time - to discover the secrets it holds. And it's good 'un, bringing a thinkier rhythm to its pounding beat.

Most people think of fishing as a calm escape from the hustle and bustle of daily life - that's certainly the case of GTA 6. But relaxation, I say, is for posers, which brings us to How to Fish. In developer Dazed Games' silly, chaotic physics-based fishing sim, you're given an arsenal of guns to unload upon fish and foe alike. Beware the seagulls, who have learned to love the taste of human flesh over the bounty of humble chip shops. It's great fun, especially co-operatively, so grab some pals and have a blast.

Neasied, the island where Queen's Domain unfolds, might not be as dizzyingly expansive as some open-worlds, but the stuff it manages to pack into a relatively compact space is astonishing. This is a game crammed with secrets, rich with mystery; a world so dense with enigmas it's a joy to explore. There's more than a touch of FromSoftware to its broader retro-inspired combat rhythms, but there are hints of Zelda and Metroidvania too. It's one of the best games this year - full of surprise and wonder as its absurdly elaborate puzzle threads unfurl - and an extraordinary adventure.

Money too tight to mention? How about ditching the life of crime for a spot of restaurant entrepreneurialism? That's the idea behind Nivalis Night - and, actually, the life of crime is still an option. First seen in 2020 courier adventure Cloudpunk, Nivalis is a striking, sprawling cyberpunk dystopia of flying cars, neon lights, and suffocating smog that sets itself apart with its lo-fi style. And in this year's follow-up, you can finally get out of your car and live in the world. But it's not just a nifty first-person tycoon sim, it's a game of stories, and developer Ion Lands imbues its city with real warmth and humanity; a perfect accompaniment, it turns out, to all the Vangelis-like synths and sushi.

There's street racing and then there's developer Milestone's Screamer. It's a narrative-focused racing game which leans into a far arcadier sort of drive compared to the more realistic Grand Theft Auto. It's also got a great anime aesthetic, so you can live out any MF Ghost fantasies you have tucked away. Or maybe Initial D for the drifting aficionados among you.

Okay, fine, so it's more of a slap than a punch, but developer Double Fine's Thank You Bus Driver still packs in more violence than you'd expect from a driving sim. This game jam experiment turned full release feels a bit like a first-person Crazy Taxi as you try and get passengers to their destination before time runs out. Happy passengers means more 'thank yous', which means more time for more pick-ups, and it's a brilliantly chaotic loop.

It's got bags of personality - its scrappy black-and-white doodle town and oddball passengers are a treat - and a bunch of neat hooks. Most notably, HQ intermittently imposes new rules - no talking! No toenail clipping! - which is where slaps come in. Rulebreakers need discipline if you don't want to lose your 'thank yous', so you spin around and wallop them about the chops. Just try not to drive off the road.

Sure, Leonida's landscape holds its own kind of sunkissed beauty, but The Game Bakers has created something really special with Cairn. It might be a game focused on mountain climbing, but it's hard not to stop and admire the view whenever you reach the next plateau. As you make your way up Mount Kami, you're greeted with almost constant lovely views, some of the most gorgeous vistas released this year. Cairn is the sort of game that warrants putting the controller down and just taking the sights in for a moment or two.

As a doctor of dubious repute once said, where we're going, we don't need roads! So wave goodbye to metropolitan squalor, farewell to suburban suffocation, and hello the great outdoors. Over the Hill, the follow-up to Funselektor's brilliant Art of Rally, is all about off-road roaming through the vast, untamed wilderness. It's a game of purposeful meandering and patient delights and you explore the secrets of, among other places, its starlit deserts and rolling forest hills. You'll bounce your 4x4 over rocks, crawl sluggishly up hills, and winch through squelching mud, and it's all so slow. That's deliberate though. And as the result, amid the gentle strum of soothing guitar, is a wonderfully meditative game.

There are few other games released this year that nail the ruthlessness of vehicle-to-vehicle combat like Airframe Ultra. An absolutely gorgeous battle-racer in which you take control of a bulky airframe. You can smack the daylights out of other riders with chains, bats, and other makeshift melee weapons. Failing that, ramming into people at full speed works wonders too. A gritty game that pleases the eye and nourishes the violent soul.

Life, eh? One minute it's all Leonida sunshine and motorboating, the next you're on the run. But it's how you deal with each roll of the dice that matters. And that's very much the premise of Cosmo D's wonderfully weird Moves of the Diamond Hand. It's back to the jazz-drenched, oddball world of Off-Peak City we go, this time as election day looms. The legendary Circus X has returned to town and you want a place in its show. What follows is a first-person narrative adventure that takes the tabletop RPG-inspired dice-throwing of Cosmo D's acclaimed 2022 game Betrayal at Club Low and turns it into something richer, deeper, wilder, and weirder. And yes, it's got a killer soundtrack.

I see you smirking, pal - cut it out. Early on in GTA 6 you're tasked with hosing off a boat in the marina. And if that scratches an unknown itch within you, PowerWash Simulator 2 has all the nozzle blasting you could hope for. Yes, it's a slightly older one, but all 2026's DLC means it still feels new. Even more grimy buildings and filthy objects are waiting for a hero with a soap dispenser, just begging for a deep clean. Is that you? Then live your dream!

Browsing the tourist traps and fashion emporiums for the next big look is all well and good, but have you ever considered taking matters into your own hands? Dressmaking might not seem like the most appealing way to spend your time if you're not particularly into wearing dresses or seamstressing. (It's me, I'm that person.) However, Dressmaker and the sheer amount of clothing combinations it makes possible are a very pleasant surprise.

Weaved throughout it all is a story that sees you designing dresses for different characters and the events they're attending, then stitching your ideas to life piece by piece with tactile minigames, And you can design your own dresses too! I can only imagine GTA 6's Jason might even enjoy designing a dress or two for Lucia if he had Dressmaker on his JoyStation...

Creator, writer, director, composer, and 'perceptual architect' Johannes Zuch's Tenebrae Lux might be wowing the critics in GTA 6, but for my money this year's best game-within-a-game is Forbidden Solitaire. It's 2019 and you've stumbled upon an old copy of notorious CD-ROM adventure Forbidden Solitaire. It was pulled from the shelves amid some mysterious deaths, but that won't stop you from playing. What follows is a wonderfully creepy haunted media yarn - told across police records, press clippings, 90s local news reports, grisly photos, even a recreation of a cheesy true-crime-style mystery show - which brilliantly evokes the 90s multimedia boom with its lurid, awkward pre-rendered game-in-a-game visuals. It also, in case you're wondering, plays a damn good game of solitaire.

I like birds. I like them so much that it's at the top of my bucket list to find a Wonga Pigeon one day. So, you can imagine how I felt finding out that GTA 6 has over 60 birds to spot, alongside binoculars and a phone camera to fulfil my bird-watching dreams.

Before GTA 6 however, there came a lovely little multiplayer game called Flock Around developed by Friend Shaped Games that lets you explore multiple biomes while trying to snap photographs of birds from around the world and fill out your guide book. I was enamoured by its simple loop of wandering around and waiting for birds to strike a perfect pose, take selfies with them, and maybe even watching them empty their insides all over your friends. I don't think it's possible to be pooped on by a bird in GTA 6, so by default, Flock Around takes the crown as one of the best games for snapping pics of birds this year.

GTA 6 might use the gorgeous relationship between Lucia and Jason to demonstrate what basic human empathy looks like in a culture that promotes instant gratification and selfishness, but Penguin Colony uses a, erm, penguin colony to highlight the existential dangers of fascism and the pervasive threat of racism and colonialism. I know which piece of social commentary I prefer.

It's not hard to have a better cleaning regimen than GTA 6's Jason: the man apparently isn't aware his nethers need a wash too. But designer Robert Yang's Rinse and Repeat's has a solution for those struggling at showertime: get someone else to help you. Rinse and Repeat is a "steamy first-person showering game ... about consent and safety" and got a remaster earlier this year as part of Yang's Radiator Forever compilation. Yang's award-winning work frequently explores queer subculture, and the NSFW compilation also contains three other games - Hurt Me Plenty, Succulent, and Stick Shift - with more to follow. Better yet, it's completely free.

Forget cracking safes in GTA 6, what about cracking the codes in a machine that seems to be, well, more than it seems? This is the hook in Inkle's TR-49, a game about a machine that once lurked in the mysterious depths of Bletchley Park's legendary codebreaking vaults, alongside the Enigma Machine. This machine works differently. It's effectively an archive you search around using codes in the format of letter-letter, number-number, hence TR-49. But the codes you use and what you find, and how you find them: that's up to you. And sure, there's probably not gold bullion on the other side of this code, but perhaps there's something even more rewarding.

Morning Minute: Ethereum Focuses on Security As AI Threats Rise

Morning Minute is a daily newsletter written by Tyler Warner. The analysis and opinions expressed are his own and do not necessarily reflect those of Decrypt. And check out our daily news show 'FOMO HOUR' covering all of the top stories and market action.

The Ethereum Foundation has doubled its protocol security team and made cryptography its top priority. The reason isn’t quantum computers.

Protocol lead Kev Wedderburn wrote this week that “recently AI emerged as a more urgent risk”, and the distinction matters. The quantum threat has always been a hardware problem, and hardware problems announce themselves years in advance because somebody has to build the machine. The AI version is a math problem. A sufficiently capable model could find a structural shortcut in the elliptic curve cryptography that secures Ethereum and Bitcoin, and it would need no special hardware to do it.

Researcher Justin Drake raised the scenario in October. In a worst case, he said, existing signatures could become vulnerable within months.

An Anthropic mathematician named Levent Alpöge posted a claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis, produced with an unreleased version of Claude, along with a formalized proof archive. Nobody has verified it. It doesn’t break elliptic curve cryptography and nobody is claiming it does. What it does is demonstrate the capability class Drake was describing, roughly a month after he described it.

Axios reported Tuesday that more than two dozen people familiar with internal discussions at OpenAI and Anthropic say employees worry their most advanced unreleased models may never be safe enough to release. The concerns named include reward hacking, models that hide or misrepresent their reasoning, and systems capable enough to find weaknesses in the safety measures their own developers built. OpenAI’s chief scientist proposed Sunday that labs agree on shared limits on how far they train models.

The plan replaces elliptic curves with hash-based cryptography, which rests on fewer structural assumptions about mathematics. It touches how users authorize transactions, how validators secure the network, and how layer-2 data gets verified. Drake proposed pulling the deadline from December 2029 to December 2028. Ethlabs withdrew its faster-block-production proposal from the Hegotá upgrade to free up engineers. The Foundation has deployed AI agents to hunt bugs in its own infrastructure, which is a reasonable response and also an admission about where the advantage currently sits.

Wedderburn’s own framing: it’s harder than the Merge, and there’s less time. He also acknowledged that moving faster makes it easier to ship bugs.

Bitcoin is on the same road. Blockstream’s Jonas Nick published a security analysis of SHRINCS last week, the apparent frontrunner to replace Bitcoin’s signatures, with sizes running from 548 bytes to 5,777 against today’s 64 to 71.

So the AI threat is looming (and accelerating), but the two largest crypto assets have teams that are responding. Now we wait on action, and more assurances that crypto is safe from AI threats. Assurances that could ensure the bottom is in fact in, if and when they arrive…

All Blacks great Beauden Barrett close to Bristol move

New Zealand great Beauden Barrett is close to agreeing a deal to join Bristol Bears in what would be one of the highest-profile signings in Prem history.

The 35-year-old, who is widely considered one of the best All Blacks of all time, won his 150th international cap in Saturday's defeat by England in Twickenham.

It is understood he is in discussions about joining Bristol on a two-year deal after the 2027 Rugby World Cup.

Barrett is also the subject of interest from clubs in France as he plots the final stages of his illustrious career. An announcement on his future is expected in the coming weeks.

Bristol have yet to comment on the situation, but sources close to the deal feel the Bears are in a strong position to sign the 2015 World Cup winner.

New Zealand's international season finishes this weekend with a rematch against England in the Nations Championship third-place play-off (Saturday, 16:40 GMT).

As a new addition to the Prem, Barrett's salary would sit outside the cap as a designated marquee player.

While he will be 36 after next year's World Cup in Australia, Bristol are confident the back still has plenty to offer both on and off the field.

Barrett came through the ranks at Wellington-based Hurricanes, before joining the Blues in 2020, while he has also had stints in Japanese club rugby.

A move abroad after the World Cup would close the door on All Blacks selection, with eligibility rules preventing New Zealand from picking overseas-based players.

He would leave as one of the greatest All Blacks in history, and is closing in on Sam Whitelock's all-time caps record (153).

Bristol have made a handful of big-name signings in the past, such as New Zealand and Tonga winger Charles Piutau and Fiji talisman Semi Radradra.

If the signing is completed, Barrett would link up with Wales superstar Louis Rees-Zammit in the Bears backline.

Millennials living in rural areas have 42% more documented chronic conditions on average compared to those in other environments, a new report from athenaInstitute found.

And, millennials in rural communities are 47% more likely to have at least two chronic conditions. This heavier chronic disease burden extends to other generations as well, analysts note.

Half of Baby Boomers (50.4%) in rural areas have three or more documented conditions, which the report says is “on par” with non-rural individuals in the Silent and Greatest generations.

The 2026 Rural Ambulatory Care Report (PDF) analyzed athenahealth network data across 12.3 million rural patients between 2021 and 2025, as well as more than 30,000 clinicians and 1,861 rural practices across the entire U.S. Factors such as appointment activity, workforce composition, payments and more were examined.

Athenahealth CMO and athenaInstitute co-chair Nele Jessel, M.D., said in a statement the findings show while rural practices are not “standing still in the face of enormous challenges,” there are “limits to how much more they can absorb as patient needs continue to grow.”

“They are adapting how they deliver care and finding ways to keep patients connected to the care they need,” Jessel said. “With renewed focus on rural healthcare, there is a real opportunity to build on what is working by investing in the workforce, technology and models of care that can help these practices continue serving the communities that depend on them.”

Primary care physicians in rural areas have to wear multiple hats, as specialty care needs at practices are higher in rural communities.

The report found that primary care handles 68.7% of visits involving anxiety or depression diagnoses versus 55.1% in non-rural areas. Moreover, 25% of rural patients with documented anxiety or depression had a substance use disorder—up from 19.8% in non-rural communities.

Data shows rural and non-rural healthcare practices collected roughly the same share of what they were owed by insurers in 2025, but analysts note a difference in patient balances.

Rural practices collected about $70 of every $100 owed, while non-rural practices collected about $78—which analysts say is about a 10% gap.

Another potential source of financial vulnerability for rural practices is Medicaid. Medicaid covered 27.5% of rural visits, which analysts note leaves these practices more exposed to eligibility changes set to begin in 2027.

It noted that in 2025, the festival generated more than $300 million in economic impact for New Orleans and Louisiana, but that after several months seeking a multi-year commitment and a reported lack of engagement from city officials, talks appear to have broken down.

“The city’s RFQ (request for quote) this summer signaled it never intended to offer ESSENCE a multiyear agreement. To be clear, talks have not stalled over vendor payments. We need a partner that is committed to that growth and to the people it serves. ESSENCE has always evolved, and we are evolving in the best interest of our community. That is what Black women deserve.”

The station reported that New Orleans tourism officials are meeting with major festival producers as they look to launch a new festival after negotiations with Sundial Media over ESSENCE broke down over unresolved payment issues. In addition, the City of New Orleans issued a statement promising the upcoming announcement of a new festival.

“On December 9, the City of New Orleans and our tourism leaders will announce a new, world-class festival celebrating Black culture over the Fourth of July weekend,” read the city’s announcement. “We are partnering with some of the nation’s top Black entertainment producers who will bring major national talent to the Superdome while also showcasing our incredibly talented local artists and musicians.”

The letter from Essence noted that the 2027 event will take place elsewhere, but at press time organizers had not announced which city will host it next. “New Orleans, you helped make us who we are. And no matter where our journey takes us next, you’ll always be part of our story and in our hearts,” the farewell concluded.

Over the years, the festival has hosted everyone from LL Cool J to Usher, Lauryn Hill, D’Angelo, Destiny’s Child, Jill Scott, Al Green, Erykah Badu, Femi Kuti, New Edition, Jamie Foxx, Boyz II Men, Gary Clark Jr., Lizzo, Chance the Rapper, Bruno Mars and many more.

Luca Guadagnino apologized to costume designers at the Marrakech Film Festival on Wednesday, a year after drawing backlash for comments he made about his preference for working with fashion designers on his films. However, the Italian filmmaker doubled down on his admiration for his frequent collaborator Jonathan Anderson, who most recently designed the costumes for his upcoming Silicon Valley drama “Artificial.”

“I apologize to costume designers,” Guadagnino said during a conversation at the festival. “There are so many amazing costume designers that I admire so greatly through the history of cinema and contemporary history.” Related Stories Emma Stone Ambushes Druski’s ‘SNL’ Monologue in Adorable Fashion ‘SNL’: Druski Dons Whiteface as Elizabeth Holmes in ‘You Can See Everything’ Parody

The controversy dates back to last November, when Guadagnino, speaking with fashion journalist Tim Blanks at the Business of Fashion’s annual VOICES conference, suggested that traditional costume designers were more concerned with the garments themselves than the bodies inhabiting them.

His comments drew the ire of several prominent costume designers, including Oscar winners Jenny Beavan (“Mad Max: Fury Road,” “Cruella”) and Kate Hawley (“Frankenstein”), who took to the comments section of Business of Fashion’s Instagram post to dispute his characterization of their craft. Beavan wrote at the time, “I can only add I am thankful he will never want to work with me!”

In Marrakech, Guadagnino further explained his stance. “It’s just that I like to be in conversation with people who have an immediate touch with the pulse of how we live,” he said.

Guadagnino singled out Anderson, who, in addition to serving as creative director of Dior, has designed the costumes for three of the filmmaker’s movies: “Challengers,” “Queer” and “Artificial.” “I know he’s someone who sees life in front of him and cares about it,” Guadagnino said of Anderson. “It doesn’t deal with fashion, per se.”

The pair’s latest collaboration, “Artificial,” chronicles the rise of OpenAI and the dramatic ousting and subsequent reinstatement of its CEO Sam Altman in November 2023. The film stars Andrew Garfield as Altman, Yura Borisov as OpenAI co-founder Ilya Sutskever and Ike Barinholtz as Elon Musk.

“I cannot think of anything more far from an idea of fashion or sophistication or form than Silicon Valley and tech bros,” Guadagnino said. “They actually are gross the way they present themselves.”

The filmmaker went on to examine how the sartorial codes of the tech industry reflect a broader shift in how the world’s wealthiest and most powerful people communicate their status. “[Anderson] was so smart in understanding every one of these characters and truly never had the temptation of painting something in a better color,” Guadagnino said.

“I believe that’s also a language of power,” he continued. “The flip-flops of Zuckerberg are a comfortable item he wears, but also a way for him to indicate his position toward your position.”

“Artificial,” which had its world premiere at the New York Film Festival in October, will screen at Marrakech Film Festival on Nov. 26. The film will be released by Neon in select theaters on Christmas Day before expanding nationwide on Jan. 8.

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Walking on water represents one of the most striking modes of locomotion in the natural world and plays a crucial role in animals' colonization of ecological niches at the water surface. However, insects specialized for this locomotion (obligate surface dwellers) belong to only two groups: the living Gerromorpha (Hemiptera) and extinct Chresmodidae (stem group of Polyneoptera). Elucidating how extinct insects moved on the water surface has proven highly challenging, and the adaptive evolution of different insect lineages to ecological niches at the water surface remains poorly understood.
