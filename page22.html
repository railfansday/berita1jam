Zidane: ‘Italy played better in France, but they won tonight’

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Zinedine Zidane feels Italy ‘played better in Paris, but they won this evening’ when inflicting his first defeat as France coach, and defends Chelsea’s Malo Gusto from criticism.

Les Bleus had got off to the ideal start at San Siro when Michael Olise pounced on a Gianluigi Donnarumma howler playing out from the back.

Francesco Pio Esposito equalised, but they felt that when Sebastiano Esposito blazed a penalty wide, it would be a turning point in the match.

Instead, the Azzurri got back on their feet quickly, winning 3-1 with Davide Frattesi and another penalty, this time converted by Pio Esposito.

“We had a good initial approach and held the ball well, but after the break we lost our way, especially defensively,” confessed Zidane in his press conference.

This was an unusual situation, as before tonight France had not scored in the first half in any of their last nine matches.

“We stayed in the game, did good things in possession, but we fell away in the second half, especially after going 2-1 down. It is part of the process.”

France were decimated by injuries, missing Inter’s Marcus Thuram, Juventus defender Pierre Kalulu, Roma midfielder Manu Koné, Kylian Mbappé, Ousmane Dembélé, Aurelien Tchouameni, William Saliba, Jules Koundé, Maxence Lacroix, Maghnes Akliouche and Esteban Lepaul.

Malo Gusto had terrible problems at right-back dealing with Nicolò Cambiaghi first, then Federico Dimarco.

“Your job is to analyse what you need to analyse, but my focus is on recharging the batteries for the next game,” replied Zidane to questions about the Chelsea defender.

“We are all united, we didn’t defend well in general as a team and need to improve. I won’t change players over one game. Malo Gusto gave away a penalty, but it can happen.”

Olise scored the opener, but then made little impact as the match wore on at San Siro.

“You cannot always play at the top level for 90 minutes. Olise had some good performances, he wasn’t the only one who fell away in the second half, the whole team did.

“We wanted to win the game tonight to ensure we had first place on Sunday, now we must win the final match against Turkiye, but that was the objective anyway regardless of this evening’s result.”

Italy are the only team who Zidane has been unable to defeat as France coach, earning a 1-1 draw in Paris and this 3-1 victory.

“The most important thing is to create chances, and we did hit the woodwork twice. I am not disappointed, I would’ve been if we had not had such good possession play. What we need to analyse is that we weren’t at the right level off the ball.

“I think Italy had a similar performance to in Paris defensively, which is what they do best. I must admit, I thought they played better with the ball at their feet in the first game, but they won tonight and that’s all.”

Mbappé was not even on the bench this evening, but could he recover in time for the final group game with Turkiye on Sunday?

“We hope so, that is the plan and why he has not gone back home. We just decided not to risk it tonight over a slight problem.”

Harry Kane reflected on a “crazy few weeks” after scoring the winning goal against Croatia on his record-breaking 126th appearance for England.

The England captain struck a record-extending 92nd international goal to seal a 2-1 win over Croatia at Wembley on Thursday night, a result that confirmed the team's progress into the quarter-finals of the Nations League.

The Ballon d'Or winner became England's most capped men's player of all-time in that match, having equalled the record held by Peter Shilton last month. Shilton was on hand to make a pre-match presentation to the star striker, along with the Prince of Wales.

England had beaten Slaven Bilic's side 7-0 in Croatia 40 days earlier, but this was a much more keenly contested affair. Kane was happy England got the job done, setting up a top-of-the-table shootout against Spain on Sunday.

“It’s been a crazy few weeks,” Kane told BBC Radio 5 Live. “Of course it takes a lot out of you emotionally, physically and mentally and all these things.

“I'm very good at staying focused on the job and what needs to be done but it's a hard balance because you want to take it in. Breaking the record is a massive thing in the history of our game, seeing the tifo there, having my kids with me, and being presented by all the people was beyond dreams.

“Once the whistle goes and you're on the pitch, you just start focusing on what your job is, pressing and playing the ball, playing assists and goals and that just takes over. It's always been in me that I'm able to switch, and of course now I can enjoy it with my family on the pitch after this.

“I’ve always said all these big milestones that I’ve had, when you win the game it makes it 10 times better and I’ve been lucky enough to win all of them, especially here at Wembley so it’s nice to do it again.”

Spain defeated Czechia 2-0 in Prague on Thursday as the world champions extended their historic unbeaten streak to 43 matches. That run stretches back to March 2024.

Luis de la Fuente's side had to come from a goal behind to beat England 3-2 in the reverse fixture in September. Kane is looking forward to the return contest at Madrid's Metropolitano Stadium.

“After the first game when we lost here against them, we knew we left a bit out there, we knew we could have done a little bit better,” Kane added on ITV.

“We said to ourselves ‘let’s win every game from now until the next one and see what happens’. It’s the perfect opportunity now to try and prove ourselves.”

Procter & Gamble vs. Starbucks: Which Stock Is a Better Buy for 2027?

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Procter & Gamble's steady cash machine goes head-to-head with Starbucks' bid for a brand-led comeback, and the valuation gap may tip the scales.

Choosing between Procter & Gamble (PG +0.21%) and Starbucks (SBUX -2.39%) requires balancing the steady reliability of essential household goods against the growth potential of a global coffee recovery story.

Procter & Gamble serves billions with daily necessities, while Starbucks relies on brand loyalty in the premium beverage market. Both companies face distinct macroeconomic shifts as they aim to deliver value heading into 2027.

Procter & Gamble operates as a global leader in consumer packaged goods, selling trusted brands across categories like beauty, grooming, and home care. The company reaches customers through diverse channels, including grocery stores and digital commerce. A significant portion of its sales comes from mass merchandisers, and Walmart (WMT +0.41%) accounts for nearly 16% of total net sales. Customer concentration like this adds a layer of risk to the business, especially since the top ten customers represent roughly 43% of its sales.

Financial performance remained steady in FY 2026, with revenue reaching approximately $87.0 billion, a 3.3% increase over the previous year. The company reported net income of close to $16.0 billion, maintaining a strong net margin of 18.4%. This net margin represents the percentage of revenue that remains as profit after all expenses are paid. This consistent profitability underscores the company's ability to manage costs while navigating a competitive global marketplace for essential goods.

As of its June 2026 balance sheet, the company reported a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.6x. This ratio measures total debt relative to shareholder equity, indicating that the company uses a moderate amount of borrowing to finance its operations. The current ratio, which measures the ability to pay short-term obligations with short-term assets, was roughly 0.7x. Furthermore, the company generated free cash flow of nearly $15.1 billion. Free cash flow is the cash a company generates after accounting for the money spent to maintain or expand its physical assets.

Starbucks continues to expand its global footprint with more than 41,000 stores across 86 markets. The company is currently implementing its "Back to Starbucks" strategy to revitalize store experiences and improve operational efficiency. It relies heavily on its Global Coffee Alliance with Nestlé (NSRGF +0.03%) to distribute packaged products. In its latest annual report, the company described its focus on being one of the top retail stocks globally by leaning into its North American segment, which accounts for roughly 74% of revenue.

In FY 2026, the coffee giant reported revenue of approximately $37.9 billion, representing growth of 2% compared to the prior year. Net income for the period was nearly $2.5 billion, resulting in a net margin of close to 6.7%. While revenue has grown, the company is working to improve its bottom-line efficiency. This focus on profitability is central to its long-term plan to navigate changing consumer habits and rising costs in its global store network.

Regarding its financial health, the balance sheet dated September 2026 showed a debt-to-equity ratio of -2.9x. This negative value means that total liabilities exceed shareholder equity. The current ratio stands at approximately 0.8x, suggesting a tight but manageable position for meeting short-term debts. During the same period, Starbucks generated free cash flow of nearly $3.7 billion. This cash generation is critical for funding its store renovations and returning capital to shareholders through dividends and buybacks.

Procter & Gamble faces risks from geopolitical and macroeconomic volatility, which can disrupt global supply chains and inflate material costs. Fluctuations in foreign currencies and trade policy changes may also impact its international profitability. The company must navigate an intense retail landscape where competition for shelf space remains high. Additionally, its dependence on complex IT systems exposes it to potential cybersecurity threats and data breaches that could disrupt its global operations.

Starbucks is subject to risks regarding its brand relevance and the successful execution of its new strategic initiatives. The company relies on high-quality arabica coffee, making it vulnerable to commodity price volatility and climate-related disruptions in coffee-growing regions. Labor relations and potential unionization also present challenges, as they may lead to increased wage inflation and higher operational costs. Furthermore, the company faces legal and financial uncertainty from evolving global tax rules and potential litigation in various markets.

Procter & Gamble offers a lower earnings multiple, while Starbucks appears more attractively priced when comparing its market value to its sales over the past twelve months.

I'd go with Starbucks. It's committed to a major turnaround, and the results are already showing up in its profits. After sales slumped, new CEO Brian Niccol launched a plan called "Back to Starbucks" in 2024 to win customers back with faster service, simpler menus, and refreshed coffeehouses. Comparable sales have now grown for five straight quarters, and adjusted earnings per share jumped 54% last quarter. With more than 1,000 new coffeehouses planned worldwide, the recovery is turning into expansion.

In its recently reported fiscal Q4 2026, Starbucks beat earnings estimates, and its margins widened for the third straight quarter. Even with shoppers watching their budgets, more U.S. customers came through the door, and each one spent more.

To give P&G its due, it owns household names like Tide, Pampers, and Gillette, and shoppers buy its everyday essentials in good times and bad. But as a mature business, it grows slowly, and higher costs can squeeze its profits. Starbucks, by contrast, is growing again, with a successful turnaround in place and a fiscal 2028 plan ahead of schedule. That makes it my pick for the long haul.

I get why people would call celebrities by their characters’ names. Of course, you can’t have Grey’s Anatomy without the Dr. Meredith Grey, so it’s not like the show could wipe the slate completely clean.

As of now, it’s hard to tell how long Grey’s Anatomy will remain on the air, but there doesn’t seem to be any end in sight. Known for pushing himself to psychological and physical limits for his work, Leonardo DiCaprio is considered one of the greatest actors of his generation. After Titanic, he pivoted to taking on more mature roles instead of playing similar heartthrobs and forged a career-defining partnership with Martin Scorsese.

Similar to DiCaprio, Tom Hanks is considered one of the greatest actors of all time, but the Forrest Gump actor took a very different route to building his career. Hanks got his start in community theater and television comedies and transitioned to playing everyman heroes and historical figures.

Instead of pursuing a dramatic pivot, the actor built long-term working relationships with mainstream directors like Steven Spielberg, Robert Zemeckis, and Ron Howard. He also took on commercial projects, such as voicing Sheriff Woody in Disney and Pixar's Toy Story franchise.

Despite having very different careers, their paths once crossed in Spielberg's 2002 biographical crime comedy, Catch Me If You Can, inspired by a true story. Fans can now watch the movie for free in the comfort of their home.

Based on the semi-autobiography of real-life con artist Frank Abagnale Jr., Catch Me If You Can takes some creative liberties and sees DiCaprio as the titular con man trying to escape consequences in a cat-and-mouse chase with Hanks's FBI agent Carl Hanratty.

In addition to two of the biggest names in the movie scene, the crime comedy's star-studded cast also features Christopher Walken as Frank Abagnale, Jennifer Garner as Cheryl Ann, Ellen Pompeo as Marci, and Elizabeth Banks as Lucy.

The Jaws director was particularly invested in Frank Abagnale Jr.'s story, having lived a similar chapter in life following his parents' divorce. Star Trek’s Ultimate Prequel Officially Changed Canon Forever

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Star Trek: Strange New Worlds became the franchise's ultimate prequel in season 4, with Captain Christopher Pike (Anson Mount) and the USS Enterprise becoming the catalyst of human history. Directed by Chris Fisher and written by Akiva Goldsman and Henry Alonso Myers, Strange New Worlds season 4's premiere, "Valles Marineris," was the first and arguably the biggest of the season's 'big swings.'

For 25 years, prequels have largely dominated the Star Trek franchise. From 1966 to 2001, the saga of Star Trek was forward-moving. Star Trek: The Original Series started in the 23rd century, and Star Trek: The Next Generation began the canon's massive expansion in the 24th century. That ended with Star Trek: Enterprise, which launched in 2001 as Star Trek's first prequel series.

J.J. Abrams' Star Trek movies were a prequel trilogy set in the alternate Kelvin timeline. Star Trek's Paramount+ streaming era began with Star Trek: Discovery, which was also a prequel in its first two seasons. Star Trek: Picard, Star Trek: Lower Decks, and Star Trek: Prodigy finally moved the 24th century forward into the 25th, but Star Trek: Strange New Worlds went back to the 23rd century again.

Star Trek: Starfleet Academy is now the franchise's farthest point in the 32nd century, until Star Trek: Short Treks' "Calypso," which is set a thousand years later. Among Star Trek's four major prequel sagas, however, Strange New Worlds went back further and became more instrumental than any of them.

Star Trek: Strange New Worlds season 4's premiere turned the series into Star Trek's ultimate prequel. In "Valles Marineris," the Starship Enterprise time-traveled 65 million years into Earth's past. This led to two game-changing revelations: Mars had a space-faring society, and the Red Planet was at war with the fifth planet in the solar system occupied by an insect species called the Dol'drm.

Star Trek: Strange New Worlds' fifth and final season only has 6 episodes to answer a lot of questions left behind by season 4.

"Valles Marineris" kept the bombshells coming, as the Martian Commander Cassell (Saffron Burrows) came aboard the Enterprise and stole a warp cell to make a doomsday weapon against the Dol'Drms. Instead of stopping the Martians, Pike allowed their genocide when he realized there was no fifth planet in Earth's solar system. Thus, the destroyed Dol'Drm planet became the Main Asteroid Belt between Earth and Mars, and a remnant became the Chicxulub impact meteor that wiped out Earth's dinosaurs 65 million years ago.

In turn, the Martians abandoned their doomed homeworld, which became barren of life. Meanwhile, Commander Una Chin-Riley (Rebecca Romijn), an Illyrian, becomes the second known alien to visit Cretaceous-era Earth, following a crashed Dol'Drm ship that Una, Lieutenant La'an Noonien-Singh (Christina Chong), and Lieutenant Erica Ortegas (Melissa Navia) discovered.

Most importantly, Captain Pike's choice not to prevent the Dol'Drms' genocide directly led to humans replacing dinosaurs as Earth's dominant species. Star Trek: Strange New Worlds not only sent the USS Enterprise to Earth's prehistoric past but made Pike and his crew responsible for all of human history to come. What more can Star Trek ask of a prequel?

Star Trek: Voyager fans who watched Star Trek: Strange New Worlds season 4's premiere understandably wondered about the Voth. "Distant Origin," the 23rd episode of Star Trek: Voyager season 3, revealed that the Voth, a reptilian-humanoid species in the Delta Quadrant, were descended from Earth's hadrosaurs.

Over the last 65 million years, knowledge of the Voth's origins was lost and replaced by religious doctrine.

According to Voth paleontologist Professor Forra Gegen's (Henry Woronicz) 'Distant Origin Theory,' the Voth evolved on Earth and somehow survived the meteor that wiped out the dinosaurs. The Voth then developed space travel capabilities and left Earth, eventually arriving in the Delta Quadrant. Over the last 65 million years, knowledge of the Voth's origins was lost and replaced by religious doctrine.

Star Trek: Strange New Worlds' Starfleet Officers may have visited Cretaceous era Earth, but they only explored a tiny amount of ground. The Voth were likely on another land mass elsewhere on Earth, and may or may not have had the technology to detect the spaceflight-capable Martians, Dol'Drms, or the USS Enterprise. Even if they did, the Voth lost that knowledge in the succeeding 65 million years.

Why Boeing 787-9 Airlines Face Different Maintenance Costs With Rolls-Royce & GE Engines

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Craig Russell | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

Selecting a powerplant for a widebody fleet is one of the most consequential financial commitments an airline executive can make, locking the carrier into a multi-decade maintenance relationship that is quite tough to get out of. During the years where Boeing developed the Boeing 787 family, it made the decision to offer two distinct engine options, with the General Electric GEnx-1B and the Rolls-Royce Trent 1000. The dual-engine strategy successfully distributed supply chain risk and gave network planners purchasing leverage, but it also exposed airlines to vastly divergent operating economics and maintenance profiles.

Over the past decade, the operational histories of these two powerplants have gone on their own individual paths, driven by unexpected durability hurdles, time-on-wing variances, and costly fleet modifications. As a result, maintenance expenses can differ by millions of dollars per aircraft because of the engineering differences, repair liabilities, and long-term financial structures tied to each manufacturer.

Boeing's decision to engineer the 787 to accept two mutually exclusive engine types was rooted in lessons learned from earlier widebody programs. Offering both the General Electric GEnx-1B and the Rolls-Royce Trent 1000, Boeing wanted to show airlines that they no longer needed to be locked into a single supplier monopoly, but also to protect the airframer from production problems if one manufacturer faced factory delays. The flexibility allowed carriers to match engine performance characteristics to their specific route networks, balancing fuel burn against operating weights and thrust ratings.

The General Electric GEnx-1B is built on a twin-spool design featuring a carbon fiber composite fan and Twin Annular Pre-Swirl (TAPS) combustors, sharing operational lineage with engines powering larger Boeing aircraft. In contrast, the Rolls-Royce Trent 1000 is built on a signature three-shaft (triple-spool) configuration with intermediate-pressure stages and hollow titanium fan blades, designed exclusively for the Dreamliner family. These fundamental engineering differences meant that maintenance tooling, spare parts inventories, and overhaul procedures were entirely unique to each fleet type.

Over at the airlines, the choice initially came down to commercial inducements, leasing structures, and projected aircraft utilization. Airlines with heavy historical ties to Rolls-Royce naturally gravitated toward the Trent 1000 to maintain cross-fleet maintenance familiarity, while operators favoring General Electric chose the GEnx-1B for its established high-bypass efficiency. However, as aircraft accumulated operational flight hours, the underlying maintenance realities of each powerplant began to drift off in ways that reshaped global fleet orders.

The divergence in maintenance costs accelerated rapidly as early iterations of the Rolls-Royce Trent 1000 encountered intense, unforeseen durability hurdles. As noted by AirInsight, the powerplant's initial years in service were plagued by fatigue and corrosion issues within its intermediate-pressure turbine (IPT) blades, leading to accelerated wear and premature component failures. Although Rolls-Royce introduced the upgraded Trent 1000 TEN standard to resolve these issues, subsequent engineering evaluations showed that high-pressure turbine (HPT) blades were also wearing out significantly faster than projected design models anticipated.

What came was tough operational fallout for airlines flying Trent 1000-powered Dreamliners. It was immediate and severe, characterized by unexpected engine groundings, plummeting dispatch reliability, and soaring maintenance expenses. Major international operators such as Air New Zealand and British Airways were forced to ground aircraft for lengthy inspections and unscheduled overhauls, as reported by Aviation Week. To stick to already published flight schedules during these mandatory groundings, several affected carriers had to lease older, less fuel-efficient backup aircraft from the open market, incurring millions of dollars in supplementary operational expenses.

Airlines and their respective maintenance departments became reactionary, where engineers had to manage aggressive removal schedules and emergency component replacements well ahead of standard lifecycle intervals. While safety was rigorously maintained through constant borescope inspections and regulatory airworthiness directives, the financial toll of keeping these aircraft operational strained airline engineering budgets and disrupted long-term route planning.

While around 34% of the Dreamliners flying today have RR engines, only around 8% of the known engine option orders are for RR engines.

The mechanical difficulties encountered by the Trent 1000 triggered profound financial consequences for Rolls-Royce and caused a noticeable shift in global widebody purchasing patterns. Industry reports from Aviation Week indicate that the cumulative cost of retrofits, customer compensation, and engine modifications surpassed $3 billion for Rolls-Royce, with aviation analysts estimating that up to 500 engines across the worldwide fleet needed remediation. The massive financial liability prompted corporate restructuring and an intensified engineering focus on redesigning hot-section components to restore customer confidence.

Concurrently, the maintenance disparity heavily influenced subsequent airline procurement decisions. Fleet data compiled by Simple Flying highlights a pronounced market shift: roughly 35% of the active Dreamliner fleet operates on Trent 1000 powerplants, while 65% relies on General Electric GEnx engines. In recent sales campaigns, widebody customers overwhelmingly selected GE, ordering 677 GEnx-powered aircraft compared to just 71 for the Trent 1000. Rolls-Royce's leadership, including engineering chief Simon Burr, candidly acknowledged that the company had surrendered market share because earlier iterations of the engine were simply not sufficiently durable.

That market contraction demonstrated how crucial predictable maintenance performance is to an aircraft's commercial success. Even though airlines frequently put pen to paper on aggressive initial acquisition discounts when finalizing engine selections, the long-term cost of unreliability and frequent shop visits quickly outweighs upfront savings, making durability the primary metric for fleet evaluation.

At the heart of widebody maintenance economics is the concept of time-on-wing, or the operational duration an engine can complete commercial flights before needing a major workshop overhaul. A General Electric GEnx-1B engine stays in the wing three times longer and can frequently achieve more than 20,000 operating hours before its first major shop visit, maintaining consistent fuel efficiency and low thermal stress. This extended durability significantly reduces the frequency with which an airline must unhang heavy powerplants from the wing and transport them to certified overhaul facilities.

In contrast, the early generations of the Trent 1000 suffered from time-on-wing intervals that were a fraction of their competitor's benchmark, leading to repeated, premature shop visits. When an engine suffers an unplanned removal, the financial impact extends far beyond the repair bill. Transport logistics, spare engine leasing fees, and teardown labor mean that a single unscheduled shop visit can easily exceed $5 million in direct and indirect costs, severely impacting an airline's quarterly profitability.

Predictability is paramount for airline finance departments trying to stick to fixed operating budgets. While GEnx operators benefit from stable, linear maintenance amortization schedules, Trent 1000 operators historically faced fluctuating maintenance reserves and unexpected capital outlays to cover recurring shop visits. As a result, the reliability gap created a clear economic divide between the two subfleets, influencing how asset managers value leased 787-9 aircraft depending on which engine hangs beneath the wing.

Each engine can run into millions of dollars.

Determined to reverse its competitive disadvantage, Rolls-Royce instituted a comprehensive technical turnaround centered on its newest engine standard, the Trent 1000 XE. Designed to triple the time-on-wing performance of the older TEN variants, the XE incorporates advanced thermal barrier coatings and redesigned turbine geometry. Leadership at Rolls-Royce, including CEO Tufan Erginbilgic, noted that targeted operators could see up to six years between major shop visits, as per Reuters. Following initial certification milestones in June 2025, rollout programs are actively modernizing in-service units across global fleets through late 2027.

On the operator risk side, many Rolls-Royce airlines rely on TotalCare agreements, comprehensive power-by-the-hour maintenance contracts where the carrier pays a fixed rate per flight hour, effectively transferring the financial liability of shop visits back to the manufacturer. Rolls-Royce committed to upgrading existing Trent 1000 engines to the improved durability standard at no additional charge to airlines. However, industry analysts at Aircraft Monitor point out an enduring secondary cost: because the Trent 1000 fleet now spans multiple engineering build standards and retrofit configurations, airlines and maintenance hubs must manage complex parts inventories and tooling for varying sub-generations.

With the parts fragmentation comes an administrative and logistical overhead that can complicate line maintenance. TotalCare agreements protect airlines from direct overhaul bills, but the operational friction of supporting a mixed-standard engine fleet is still a distinct and important consideration for engineering planners comparing the streamlined configuration of the GEnx-1B ecosystem.

The divergence in maintenance costs between 787-9 aircraft powered by General Electric GEnx engines and those equipped with Rolls-Royce Trent 1000s is a case study in the unforgiving economics of modern propulsion engineering. In the beginning, the Trent 1000's early durability struggles and subsequent retrofit campaigns cost manufacturers billions and reshaped market share, but now, with ongoing technical enhancements like the XE standard, reliability is now firmly under control at Rolls-Royce.

Ultimately, choosing between these two powerplants is a choice between initial acquisition incentives and long-term lifecycle risk. The lessons learned from the Dreamliner's dual-engine experiment will continue to guide how airlines evaluate maintenance predictability across all future widebody acquisitions. Many airlines still have the 787 on order, and many others are likely to add new orders over the coming years, so it will be interesting to see if that split between both engines becomes far more even.

Prime Video’s Lord of the Rings Series Is Officially Correcting an Early Mistake

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Adapting J.R.R. Tolkien is never going to be an easy job, particularly when you're dealing with a story that spans thousands of years, introduces entire civilizations, and features enough characters to populate several television shows. That's the challenge The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power has faced since its debut, with the Prime Video fantasy epic juggling storylines involving Elves, Dwarves, Númenóreans, Harfoots, and the increasingly troublesome Sauron. While the series has certainly never lacked ambition, keeping all those moving parts together hasn't always been straightforward. And now, one of the people responsible for bringing Middle-earth to television has acknowledged that the show may have been asking a little too much of its audience.

Speaking exclusively with Collider's Maggie Lovitt for an episode of our Behind the Scenes series, The Rings of Power co-showrunner Patrick McKay admitted that the first two seasons occasionally tried to juggle too many storylines, revealing that Season 3 has been deliberately designed to bring the narrative into sharper focus. Rather than continuing to expand the show's enormous collection of characters and subplots, McKay and fellow showrunner J.D. Payne have made Sauron's creation of the One Ring the central event around which everything else revolves. He told Collider:

"You would think that would be one of the real challenges of this show, but actually, the challenge is trying to keep it small. J.D., my co-showrunner, and I have imaginations that we don't know how to stop. They are these waterfalls of ideas, and really we're constantly trying to focus them and make sure we're not asking too much of an audience. I think there were times in prior seasons that, just being our own harshest critics, we realized looking back we were juggling too many balls.

We were asking the audience to juggle too many balls. We're so close to it; it makes perfect sense to us. But one of the things this season we really tried to do is focus it. This is the season where Sauron makes the One Ring. Everything should tie into that. And if it doesn't, even if it delights us, maybe that's not the time for that story."

It's a wild admission to make, but it's one that makes a lot of sense, because the One Ring is, well, the One Ring. And it should be the focus of a show called The Rings of Power. The decision to focus the third season around the One Ring also reflects the show's broader approach to adapting Tolkien's Second Age, which contains major historical events separated by enormous stretches of time.

"We looked at the text, we looked at the history of this particular era of the mythology, and there were these series of events that to us felt like they could lead one right into the next, right into the next, and build momentum with an audience the way that a serialized show should," explained McKay. "And then really the work that we do is taking those fixed points, whether it's the forging of the One Ring — that's a big moment this season — or it's Pharazôn bringing a massive Númenórean army to Middle-earth — that's a big moment this season — and there's others. And then it's about, 'How do we make those feel like earned emotional turns in a story rather than fixed historical events, if that makes sense?'" He went on to say:

"We want to bring audiences to it who maybe aren't anticipating those moments or don't know that they're going to come. If you're a Tolkien superfan and a lover of the books and a scholar of the lore, in theory, there's all kinds of things in every episode that you're responding to and picking out that we're dangling there, whether it's an Easter egg or a huge historical moment, like you're saying. But also, for my mother, who's never really gotten Lord of the Rings, it's not her thing, it should play like a novel does, where it's an emotional experience even if it wasn't in Middle-earth."
