Tribeca Prizewinner Darko Lungulov Wraps Production on Serbian Coming-of-Age Drama ‘1970’: ‘It Is a Story About Today’ (EXCLUSIVE)

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tribeca prizewinner Darko Lungulov (“Here and There”) has wrapped production on “1970,” a coming-of-age drama set against the backdrop of President Nixon’s visit to the former Yugoslavia during the Vietnam War. The film will take part in the Agora Works in Progress strand at the Thessaloniki Film Festival before heading to Tallinn’s Baltic Event industry showcase later this month.

“1970” follows 13-year-old Draško, who — after being abandoned by his parents — runs away with the rebellious 16-year-old Eržika. Drifting from place to place, swept up amid a roiling climate of anti-war protests, rebellion, free love, wild rock concerts, drugs and decadent parties, his desperate journey home becomes an awakening of heartbreak, loss and freedom. The film is written and directed by Lungulov, who shares a producing credit with Snezana van Houwelingen. It’s produced by This and That Productions (Serbia), Papa Films (Serbia), Mirage Film (Hungary), Samsa Film (Luxembourg) and PomPom Film (Croatia), with financing and support from Film Center Serbia, the Hungarian National Film Institute, Film Fund Luxembourg, the Croatian Audiovisual Center, Film Fund Vojvodina and Creative Europe MEDIA.

Speaking to Variety in Thessaloniki, Lungulov explains that while his period drama is set more than 50 years in the past, “it is a story about today.”

“The film takes us to a time when young people believed they could change the world — something we seem to have forgotten,” the director says. Pointing to recent events in his native Serbia, where after two years of struggle, a student protest movement ousted longtime strongman Aleksandar Vučić last month, Lungulov says that “that possibility came incredibly to life again.”

The Belgrade-born director, who studied filmmaking in New York, is best known for “Here and There,” which won the Best NY Narrative Award at Tribeca in 2009 and was Serbia’s submission for the foreign-language Oscar that year. The film, which contrasted the adventures of an enterprising young Serbian in New York with the experiences of an aging New Yorker in Belgrade, was praised by Variety’s Ronnie Scheib as a “laid-back character study [that] accrues small epiphanies with patient care, its mordant Eastern European humor tempered by a gentler sense of postwar absurdity.” His 2014 follow-up, the tragicomedy “Monument to Michael Jackson,” premiered at Karlovy Vary.

Lungulov’s last film was the 2023 documentary “A Fleeting Dream,” which followed the rock band Yu Grupa — a long-running musical act that, coincidentally, was formed in 1970. His latest will also incorporate documentary elements, with “1970” utilizing a mix of “real and fake home movies, newly shot 16mm and digital footage,” he says. “It is a film based on my own memories, and I wanted to create the hero’s inner world, his memories and the atmosphere of 1970 through the film’s internal language.”

Mirroring the political climate of that time, with raucous anti-Vietnam War protests greeting Nixon’s visit to Yugoslvaia, Lungulov notes that his two young leads — Andrija Femić, who plays Draško, and Lilla Gregus, who plays Eržika — were both “very active in the student movement” that brought down Vučić. At the time of filming, Gregus was a 22-year-old student at the Academy of Arts in Novi Sad, the Serbian town whose railway station disaster in 2024 jumpstarted the protest movement.

On one occasion, Lungulov himself joined the young actor and her fellow students at a demonstration after a rehearsal for “1970.” It’s that echo of the past in the present day, he says, that makes his film so timely.

“I feel this story is about today — young people searching for their voice in a hopeless world and refusing to be broken by bullying authorities, which feels like our last hope in today’s scary times,” he says. “And the Serbian students brought that hope back to us in Serbia, and perhaps to the world.”

The Thessaloniki Film Festival runs Nov. 5 – 15.

House Republicans to elect a new leader as they look to rebuild from midterm defeat

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Rep. Jim Jordan, R-Ohio, left, and Rep. Steve Scalise, R-La., confer during a news conference at the Capitol on June 8, 2022. The two GOP leaders are competing to replace Mike Johnson as the top House Republican. J. Scott Applewhite/AP hide caption

Just one week after losing the majority in the midterm elections, House Republicans will meet behind closed doors on Tuesday to pick a minority leader for the next Congress.

The choice to succeed Mike Johnson, R-La., as the top House Republican will be between current House Majority Leader Steve Scalise, R-La., and Ohio Republican Jim Jordan, a co-founder of the ultra-conservative House Freedom Caucus.

The election — which will be conducted by secret ballot — represents a high-stakes decision for the party that will help determine which way it goes as it looks to regroup from a staggering defeat that saw Democrats flip more than 40 GOP seats en route to winning back the majority.

"I think what develops here will become a larger, external conversation about the direction of the Republican Party," said former House Speaker Pro Tempore Patrick McHenry, who held the gavel in an acting role during the tumultuous GOP speakership fight in 2023.

Republicans say they are ready to turn the page on their losses and start rebuilding to regain voters' trust. But first, they will have to decide between two candidates who would bring very different styles of leadership.

In Scalise, 61, House Republicans would be choosing a form of continuity — the No. 2 House Republican since 2023, and someone who has served in broader GOP leadership roles for more than 10 years. He's seen as more moderate than Jordan, and in a letter to colleagues announcing his candidacy, the close Trump ally said his approach to the job would be "conservative and consensus-driven."

"My number one goal as Republican Leader will be to win back the majority and I expect you to hold me accountable to that mission," Scalise wrote. "To succeed, we will need to be effective in both the oversight and legislative fights ahead, but we will also need to lay out a clear and optimistic vision for America."

"We have big tasks ahead, and tested leadership is needed now more than ever," he added.

In Jordan, 62, Republicans would be choosing a veteran of both the House Oversight and House Judiciary committees — two panels poised to play a central role in the new Congress as Democrats plan for a long list of investigations into President Trump and his administration.

A former college wrestling coach known for his pugilistic style, Jordan has pitched an aggressive posture to his colleagues, writing that "offense, not defense, wins elections."

"For too long, we have operated without a clear plan to survive the hour or the day in front of us. That will not cut it against an emboldened and energetic Democratic party," Jordan said in his letter to colleagues, taking a swipe at the House leadership team led by Johnson and Scalise.

"I am battle-tested on the oversight front and I know the Democrats' playbook," he added.

The contest is a rematch from 2023. Back then, Jordan and Scalise were vying to succeed Kevin McCarthy. After multiple candidates failed to secure enough votes, Republicans ultimately voted for Johnson.

Leslie Shedd, a Republican strategist, said the candidates will need to break through a process that can be like "family-on-family violence."

"It's really important … to lay out a very clear, substantive vision of how they plan to lead this conference out of what is now a desert," she said.

John Leganski, who was an aide to McCarthy, the former House Speaker, estimates more than 70% of the incoming House GOP conference was elected after Trump won his first term. As a result, they were largely made in his image or won because of it.

That means, Trump could wield a major influence over the direction of the House Republican conference as a result. So far, he's staying out of the race, though in 2023 he did endorse Jordan.

Leganski said that the size of Democrats' victory last week will limit leverage for Republicans under the eventual House Minority Leader when the new Congress is seated in January.

"I think whoever wins your job is to … number one, try to win back to the House," he said. "And number two, make life as difficult for the Democrats as possible, largely by voting no."

That's all we ever hear about NFL offenses. If you don't run the ball, you are dead.

Run games? The Bengals are 30th in rush yards per game, while the Packers are dead last. Neither is close to 100 yards per game, although their numbers are slightly up over the past three games. Neither team averages 4.0 per rush; the Packers are at 3.0, which ranks 31st in the league.

Both are now in my top-10 of the Power Rankings, with Green Bay moving up to No. 10 this week, with the Bengals a few spots ahead at No. 7.

Staying there for both of them will mean continuing to play the same style of football, which means not allowing the old, tired run-it folks to influence play-calling. Play to the strengths and don't get caught up in the adages about running the football.

Run the ball? Not these two. Nor should they —and both just might ride the right arms of their quarterbacks to a Super Bowl.

All noise-cancelling headphones, including earbuds, have some passive noise cancellation. While over-the-ear headphones naturally block out noise by wrapping around the ears, earbuds don't block sound as effectively. Instead of covering your ears, wireless earbuds deliver sound directly into the ear canal to drown out other noises. They rely more on active noise cancellation (ANC) to reduce environmental noise.

ANC is an electronic process within the headphones. Internal microphones listen to your surroundings and then invert the sound, effectively canceling it. Sometimes, with noise-cancelling headphones and earbuds, you'll hear a slight buzzing or humming in the background, and that's the ANC at work. The best ANC earbuds shouldn't have this buzzing, however.

Warhammer Survivors Review - Bringing A Tank To The Mad Forest

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I spent hours carving through termagants with a chainsword and watching their sprites explode into a viscera of gems before Warhammer Survivors finally clicked. It wasn’t until I unlocked the tank and started rolling through the Ork Stronghold, turning pixels into paste beneath the running gear, that I finally got it: this isn’t just a Vampire Survivors reskin. It’s a Warhammer power fantasy that lets you unleash an orbital bombardment on the poor denizens of the Imperial Bastion, doling out the Emperor’s duty while you rummage through chests to turn garlic—ahem, Shield of Faith—into the Emperor’s Wrath.

Warhammer Survivors almost reminds me of those old ‘00s crossover games, like Peggle Extreme, where child-friendly unicorns are coupled by Half-Life headcrabs as blood drips from their horns. It’s unashamedly Vampire Survivors underneath the prettier UI, boltguns, and fusil-cannons, but once you push through and begin to unlock the more experimental Warhammer weapons, it becomes its own beast.

If you’ve played Vampire Survivors or its myriad clones, you’ll know the core gameplay loop: kill enemies, collect gems, acquire weapons, kill more enemies. Warhammer Survivors is familiar but a little more sluggish, with its hulking brutes clad in Space Marine armor. But even as someone who isn’t embroiled in the tabletop side—with passing knowledge thanks to Dawn of War and Boltgun—it just felt cooler. The whip was always a bit flimsy. Serviceable, but you toss it aside the second you get something better. Turn that whip into a chainsword, and suddenly I can’t let go of the thing.

Warhammer Survivors is split into two campaigns with their own progression systems, so it’s not just 40K on offer. Age of Sigmar is more familiar, with its high-fantasy tropes that throw you straight into a stage brimming with rats and other vermin. But this was another concept I just wasn’t sold on, as passing through the realmgate from one universe to the other was a bit of a slog at first. The two campaigns share so much DNA in their starting weapons, and both have the exact same passives, that it took a while to appreciate what each had to offer as I was forced to retrace my steps. Again, once I got past the first few hours, the two began to diverge with truly unique weapons and stages that felt every bit as rewarding to experiment with—it’s just a shame you have to push through to truly appreciate it.

So much of Warhammer Survivors pays homage to Games Workshop’s miniature wargame, such as paint buckets that unlock new characters and a bit box menu to view all your weapons in. But one of the most inventive ways that it captures the feeling of placing tiny figurines onto a board is how unlocking new weapons works. Not every character can use every item, so you’ll gradually build a squad with their own unique arsenal. There are mages brandishing the shield of faith, grenadiers lobbing grenades, Space Marines carving with the chainsword, and a tank. Just a tank. Can you imagine having a tank in the Mad Forest? That’s what this game is.

It’s a crucial part of what makes Warhammer Survivors feel less like a reskin and more like its own distinct entry into the Survivors genre canon, but I’m not sure it lands. Using weapons like the shield of faith, which is Auroch Digital’s answer to garlic, is a lot less satisfying when it’s permanently off-center or lagging behind as your troops get separated by the horde. It turns the satisfying gameplay loop of becoming an unstoppable powerhouse into a fiddlier game of wrangle-the-squad. It doesn’t matter so much when evolutions are brought into the fold, and suddenly you have five unstoppable heretics deleting everything in sight, but it’s another hurdle that slows the pace and makes the early moments of a game less rewarding.

And while Warhammer Survivors does eventually diverge and become its own experience, the early game is so similar to Vampire Survivors, with blatant reskins of weapons and passives, that I was even able to use Vampire Survivors guides to figure out evolutions. So much of the initial crawl is carried by the sheer cool factor of Warhammer’s aesthetic. It’s a bit of a shame that the early game is tantamount to a texture pack, imagining what Vampire Survivors weapons would look like in Warhammer, rather than imagining what Warhammer weapons would look like in a survivor game. Especially given how much better the game gets when Auroch Digital finally starts taking more ambitious swings.

After years of waiting, this forgotten chapter is finally ready for an explosively stylish return.

With that being said, the pixel art, thematic UI, and retro synthwave fantasy music are all a step above Vampire Survivors. Hearing the war drums clang as I marched along the rivers of molten realmstone in the Bloodwind Spoil, hurling poison knives and tempest axes, had me completely enthralled by the Age of Sigmar universe. As much as it owes to Vampire Survivors, it feels rooted in Warhammer, right down to the Orks launching a Waaagh! that summons massive, enraged hordes, to the end-of-stage boss encounters like the enormous Norn Emissary tyranid in place of the Grim Reaper.

Warhammer Survivors takes a while to sound the horn, and spends so much of its early hours awkwardly stuck between itself and the Vampire Survivors foundation. Some of its ideas fall flat, and it doesn’t always reach the heights of its inspirations, but once it sheds its roots and embraces the outlandish theocratic tyranny of tanks rolling on Poxwalkers, it becomes a moreish sandbox where smashing together miniatures just to see what happens is endlessly entertaining—and thankfully, it won’t get you kicked out of the hobby shop.

GTA 6's Missing Data Suggests It Might Be Censored In Certain Regions

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Pre-loading for GTA 6 has begun. That means you can put the game on your console right now; you’re just not allowed to play it yet. It also means people who know how to do so can take a peek inside some of the GTA 6 files, and they’ve quickly discovered something interesting.

Actually, they’ve discovered something interesting is missing. Tex2 has listed GTA 6’s PlayGo chunk metadata (thanks, VGC). PlayGo is effectively the PlayStation equivalent of Xbox Smart Delivery, splitting the data into chunks so that those chunks can then be divvied up depending on what a particular system needs.

It’s largely used to divide up what’s needed for last-gen and current-gen versions of the same game. However, it can also be used to remove certain elements of a game in select regions. That appears to be what has happened here, and Tex2 points out that one of those chunks is missing from GTA 6’s metadata in Australia and New Zealand.

GTA 6’s metadata is split into three categories: story, minigame, and core. It’s a core section that has been omitted from the version of GTA 6 being sold Down Under, which suggests it's something out in the game’s massive open world that will be missing.

The size of the missing chunk is small, just 704 KB. Hard to believe there’s anything that small in a game that’s now confirmed to need more than 200 GB of free space, no matter where you plan to play it. That tiny file size suggests whatever it is that’s missing from GTA 6 in Australia and New Zealand, it’s not something anyone is likely to miss.

It’s so small, and GTA 6 is so big, that it might be something we take months to even find.

The file size suggests that this is probably a texture of some sort that has been removed, potentially for legal reasons. Australia somewhat infamously has stricter rules than other parts of the world when it comes to drug use in video games that give you a gameplay advantage. It can, and has, resulted in games being refused classification. Perhaps that missing metadata chunk is linked to that specific kind of drug use in GTA 6.

It’s now only nine days until we can play GTA 6 and try to solve the mystery ourselves. Those playing in Australia and New Zealand will get a head start too, since the game will launch there first due to the time difference. They will need to wait for the rest of us to solve this missing data mystery, though, as they’ll need people outside of that region to know what it is that’s missing.

The theory that codes in boxes are being used to avoid leaks at all costs just became a little bit more credible.
