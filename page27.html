Cosmos 3 Edge, Part 3: SO-101 Sim-to-Real and Robot Control on Jetson Thor

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at blog.cavedu.com • AI-assisted

In the first two articles, we introduced Cosmos 3 Edge on Jetson and explored the Physical AI workflow from visual understanding to action reasoning. The remaining step is to turn an AI decision into a machine's movement: the action in perceive, reason, and act must change something in the physical environment.

NVIDIA's SO-101 Sim-to-Real Learning Path offers a useful development route, from calibration and simulation through data collection, VLA training, autonomous tests on a real robot, and Cosmos-based dataset augmentation.

One distinction is essential: the SO-101 learning path uses GR00T VLA as its main robot policy. Cosmos is used for dataset augmentation, not as a direct replacement for GR00T in that course. NVIDIA's separate Cosmos 3 Edge route demonstrates a post-trained robot policy running on Jetson AGX Thor.

This final article connects these complementary pieces of the Physical AI ecosystem.

A conventional robot controller accepts low-level inputs such as joint positions, velocities, or other control signals and drives the motors accordingly. It does not, by itself, understand a request such as “Pick up the industrial object and put it in the yellow container.”

That apparently simple task involves several questions:

The NVIDIA GR00T architecture combines visual observations with language for task understanding, followed by action output for the robot.

A Physical AI system connects sensors, perception, world understanding, a policy, actions, and the robot. It also needs feedback. After every action, the environment has changed. New observations must influence the next decision. This is closed-loop operation.

The learning path also includes Cosmos augmentation and autonomous tests. Choosing a model is only one part of the challenge. Data, training, simulation, and physical evaluation must form a coherent process.

At a 333 Robotics Community meetup in September 2026, the original author made a related observation about LeRobot: the robot hardware is not the whole story. Data and policy are central to the ecosystem.

Physical AI draws attention to the AI model, but practical robot development often starts with calibration. Both the SO-101 follower and its teleoperation leader arm need calibration so the system knows their joint states and operating ranges.

If the model's representation of the robot does not match the physical mechanism, a more capable model will not simply erase that mismatch. The system's understanding must align with the actual robot body.

Testing directly on a real robot can be costly. Failures can damage equipment, disrupt a test setup, or stop a production line. Isaac Sim provides an environment for teleoperation, data collection, and policy evaluation before transferring a candidate policy to the real robot.

The development sequence is simulation, data collection, policy training, evaluation, and physical deployment. Simulation helps explore and test the policy; real-world evaluation remains necessary.

In the SO-101 learning path, Cosmos appears as a dataset-augmentation strategy. The idea is to increase the diversity of training data rather than rely only on a limited set of demonstrations.

Simulation demonstrations, real data, and generated variations can contribute to a broader training dataset. Text-to-video and image-to-video workflows can introduce visual variation, including backgrounds, lighting, appearances, and environments.

The goal is not attractive video for its own sake. The variations must help the trained policy generalize to different conditions, while preserving the task-relevant information needed for training.

The original article also points readers to Cosmos 3 Nano on NVIDIA's model platform. Cosmos-family capabilities extend beyond visually realistic generation to relationships among observations, motion, and actions; the exact interface depends on the selected model and workflow.

These are distinct roles within the ecosystem. In the SO-101 course, GR00T VLA provides the manipulation-policy route and Cosmos supplies augmentation. The separate Cosmos 3 Edge demonstration deploys a post-trained policy on Thor. Combining their names does not automatically create a supported plug-and-play integration.

In the cited on-device workflow, the policy server runs on Jetson AGX Thor. This brings policy inference close to the robot rather than requiring every observation and action decision to travel through a remote AI server.

Jetson is therefore more than a camera-processing box. It can serve as the robot's onboard AI computer, subject to the workload's memory, compute, software, and integration requirements.

As discussed in Part 2, a policy can predict a sequence of actions for the robot to execute. The system observes, predicts an action chunk, begins execution, observes again, and replans.

The key question is whether inference timing and action execution fit together. Repeatedly pausing the robot while waiting for a large model to decide on one small movement is different from generating motion segments and updating them through feedback.

Chunking does not remove the need for a controller, robot-specific action conversion, or validation. It provides a way to organize policy predictions across time.

Both aim to connect perception, decision-making, and physical action. A working robot still needs cameras, perception, a policy, actions, a controller, the physical mechanism, and feedback.

The SO-101 workflow tackles the broader development problem: how can a policy trained and evaluated in simulation remain useful on real hardware? Cosmos 3 Edge demonstrates a path for world/action modeling to become an edge robot policy.

The current learning path should not be read as “Download Cosmos 3 Edge, connect an SO-101, and press Run.” The policy, data, embodiment, camera arrangement, and controller must be matched and evaluated.

Even with a model, data, Jetson, and an arm, the practical question remains: What success rate do we obtain after the real robot starts moving?

This is why real evaluation matters. Simulation measures performance in the simulated environment. Deployment on the physical robot reveals how the system handles the actual world.

Part 1 introduced how Cosmos 3 Edge helps interpret observations. Part 2 connected world understanding to action decisions. This final article places those capabilities inside a full robot-development workflow.

The destination is a continuous loop: observe the world, understand the task, choose actions, execute them, and observe the changed world again. Answering a visual question or generating an impressive video is only one part of that process.

Alongside task success, the original article highlights intervention rate: how often must a human step in per 100 cycles? A system requiring frequent rescue is not yet a dependable production tool.

Completing a task once may be luck. Repeating it 10, 50, or 100 times under different lighting, object positions, and environments provides more useful evidence. Better data, more accurate simulation, stronger policies, faster edge inference, and reliable closed-loop control all matter.

Physical AI depends on a robust workflow connecting robot, simulation, data, VLA, Cosmos, and real-robot evaluation. The next topic is preparing OpenUSD assets for simulation—the bridge toward SimReady content.

The 7-Gate Production Launch Checklist for Solo Developers

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A solo launch rarely dies from a missing feature. In my experience watching one-person products ship (and in the ones I've broken myself), it dies from one of three things — and all three are testable in an afternoon:

Every gate below exists to catch one of those three before a stranger finds it for you.

A gate is a check with a written exit criterion and an artifact that proves it passed. Not a feeling. Not "it worked when I tried it." An artifact:

If you cannot point at the artifact, the gate is open and the launch is a bet.

Gates 1–5 and 7 are blocking. Gate 6 is soft only because a broken launch with no comms template still launches — it just hurts more.

"Freeze the scope" sounds like project management. It is actually a testing requirement: you can only test a fixed set of behaviour, and every feature you add after the smoke pass invalidates part of that pass.

The rule I use is mechanical: from the moment the second full smoke pass starts (T-1), the deployable artifact changes only for security fixes, data-loss fixes, and the launch checklist itself. Anything else is a commit on a branch, merged after launch.

Write the freeze as one page you can check a build against: what ships (3–7 user-visible capabilities, each with one sentence and one URL), what explicitly does not ship, who can use it, what it costs, and what "working" means — the single number you will check on day seven. A launch scope without a success number becomes a launch you cannot evaluate.

Every "it works locally" story is a story about a difference. The audit's job is to find the differences on purpose, before a stranger finds them by accident.

For each variable and key, capture five things: name, purpose, where it is set, whether staging has the same value, and when it was last rotated. Then follow the rules that prevent the usual damage:

The highest-value ten minutes of the whole audit is the test-key search: grep production config for sk_test, sandbox, localhost, 127.0.0.1, :3000, example.com, changeme. A payment provider left in test mode is invisible for days — the page looks fine, nobody can pay.

A deploy that crashes in ten seconds is infinitely better than one that serves 500s for an hour.

Also check the boring, cheap, catastrophic things: apex domain resolves as well as www, the certificate covers both, and there is exactly one HTTPS redirect hop. Lower the TTL on records you might change to 300 seconds 48 hours before launch. If your domain sends mail, confirm SPF, DKIM and DMARC before the first launch email — that email is often the first your domain has ever sent, and it lands in spam.

A backup file that has never been restored is a rumour. The only claim you can make with confidence at launch is: I restored last night's backup into a scratch database and counted the rows.

A restore that takes 90 minutes is not an emergency plan, it is an outage with a schedule — and knowing that in advance changes how you launch.

The other half of data safety is the expand–contract pattern, the single most useful discipline for one-person teams. Split every risky schema change into three releases: expand (add the new column, nullable, nothing reads it yet), backfill and switch (write both, read new, flip back by changing one line), contract (drop the old column a week later). At every point the previous release still works, which is what makes a rollback a redeploy instead of a restore.

And keep an irreversible list: dropping a column, rewriting values in place, deleting storage objects, rotating a key whose old value is still in use, sending an email you cannot recall. If the list is not empty, either split the release or write down explicitly why you accepted it.

Unit tests prove functions behave. Smoke tests prove a person can get from the front door to value without help — and on launch day the second one is what matters.

Three properties make a smoke suite useful: it is twelve paths, not two hundred (fast enough to run three times in the last two days); it runs in the same conditions as the stranger (fresh account, private window, phone viewport, no admin flag, real network); and it is recorded with a timestamp and the conditions — "pass, 14:20, private window, phone, cellular" is evidence.

The twelve paths, chosen because they are where solo products break for someone else while working for their author:

Path 7 is the one most often faked. A webhook that fires correctly once is not the same as an endpoint that is idempotent, signature-checked and tolerant of out-of-order delivery. Replay it twice and confirm the second delivery changes nothing.

Add a column to your checklist notes called conditions. "Pass — private window, fresh account, 5G" is worth ten times "pass".

Set the standard in one sentence: if the site breaks, you know within five minutes without looking at it.

If you install only two things before launch, install error tracking and one uptime check — then force both to fire, so you know the path from failure to your phone actually works. That round trip, from your deliberate error to your screen, is the artifact.

Two settings matter more than the rest: environment tagging (without it, staging errors page you at 2am) and noise filters (an error tracker that sends forty notifications a day gets muted in week two, and then it is not monitoring at all).

Check the URL a customer actually uses, not /health on the internal port. A health endpoint that returns 200 while the database is down is worse than no check, because it reports green through an outage:

Have the health check do a one-row read against the database and return non-200 when it fails. That is the difference between "the server is running" and "the product works".

And keep an alert budget: maximum three alerts before launch (site down, error rate above threshold, one business counter at zero). Every alert must be actionable at 2am, and every alert needs one line written next to it: if this fires, do X. An alert without a written response is a notification, and notifications get ignored.

Most launch pain is not technical. It is a vague message arriving at the worst possible moment, and you composing a reply while you are also debugging. Write the three messages before launch, so on the day you paste instead of think:

And when a bug report is vague, answer with structure: acknowledge it, ask the two questions that make it reproducible (what did you click right before it happened, and roughly what time it was), and commit to a time you can actually meet. Then keep that time, even if the answer is "not fixed yet, here is what I know."

Solo means the authority is never in question — only the threshold.

Before launch, answer this on paper: if I roll back this deploy, does the database still work with the old code? If the answer is no, your only path is forward, and you should know that before you press the button rather than discover it at T+40.

Ask these out loud, in this order, in the last hour before you press launch. Any "no" is a delay, not a discussion.

Question 5 is the one that gets skipped and the one that costs the most.

GBase Database Online Dbspace Expansion: Add a Chunk or Extend an Existing One?

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Summary: When a dbspace runs low on capacity, GBase Database(GBase 8s) provides two expansion approaches: adding a new chunk or extending an existing chunk. This tutorial explains both methods, their commands, prerequisites, differences, and post-expansion verification steps to help DBAs expand database storage safely.

When database storage approaches its capacity limit, adding more disk space is not the only consideration. DBAs also need to decide how that capacity should be incorporated into the existing storage structure.

In GBase Database(GBase 8s), there are two approaches:

Both approaches can expand an existing dbspace, but they use different commands and have different prerequisites.

Adding a new chunk is a straightforward way to increase dbspace capacity.

One important rule: the page size of a new chunk must match the page size of its target dbspace.

For example, a dbspace created with a 16KB page size requires compatible 16KB chunk storage. You cannot use a different page size when adding a chunk.

GBase Database also supports adding a chunk through the SQL Administration API:

Both methods add a chunk to the existing dbspace.

Do not stop at a successful command response. Confirm that the new chunk is actually registered and available.

The second approach is to extend an existing chunk rather than attach another one.

In GBase Database(GBase 8s), this operation uses the SQL Administration API.

First, mark the target chunk as extendable:

First: The argument is a chunk number, not a dbspace name.

Find the chunk number in the Chunks section of onstat -d. Use the actual chunk identifier shown in your instance rather than assuming that it matches the dbspace number.

Second: Check the underlying file system before extending.

The file system containing the chunk file must have enough available capacity. Otherwise, the extension cannot be completed successfully.

Unlike adding a new chunk, this approach does not create another chunk entry. It increases the capacity of an existing chunk.

Regardless of which method you choose, check three things before executing the expansion command.

Confirm that the target file system has enough available space for the requested expansion.

For an existing chunk extension, verify the chunk number through onstat -d. An incorrect chunk number means you may target the wrong storage resource.

When adding a new chunk, confirm that its page size matches the target dbspace.

Also check file ownership, permissions, and whether the target chunk path is already in use.

These checks are inexpensive compared with troubleshooting a failed storage change.

The two approaches solve the same capacity problem, but their operational characteristics differ.

My default preference is adding a new chunk, particularly in production environments.

It provides flexibility in storage allocation and can distribute capacity across different disks or file systems.

Extending an existing chunk is useful when the underlying file system has sufficient capacity and maintaining a stable chunk count is a priority.

However, neither approach should be treated as automatically risk-free or universally reversible. Check the supported rollback and recovery procedures for your GBase Database version before making production changes.

A successful expansion command is not the end of the operation.

There are three follow-up checks worth including in every GBase Database storage expansion runbook.

Verify the chunk count and available pages in the target dbspace.

Inspect the Chunks section and check the flags column.

Investigate any abnormal status, especially a chunk marked with PD.

This keeps the storage inventory consistent with the actual database configuration.

Without these checks, administrators may believe a dbspace has been expanded even though the expected capacity is not available.

The commands are not particularly complicated. The real work is checking prerequisites, selecting the right target, understanding the operational impact, and verifying the result.

A reliable storage expansion procedure should always follow this sequence:

Check capacity → Select expansion method → Execute → Verify chunk status → Update inventory

Online storage expansion is not complete when the command returns successfully. It is complete when the additional capacity is confirmed and the storage records reflect the actual state.

Disclosure: I'm the solo developer of music2prompt, the tool this post is about. This post is about what broke, not a sales pitch. Everything in it applies to any audio + LLM project. I drafted this post with help from an AI assistant; the numbers come from my own logs.

Suno v6 launched on September 9, 2026 and retired the older models. Overnight, a lot of people's prompts stopped working the way they used to. The most common request I see is simple: "I have a song I like. Write me the v6 prompt that sounds like it."

So I built a tool that listens to a reference track and writes two things: a Styles line and an Exclude Styles list. This is what I learned along the way.

My first version asked the audio model for everything: genre, mood, instruments, BPM. The genre and instruments were usually good. The BPM often wasn't. It was frequently off by double or half, or rounded to a "typical" tempo for the genre.

Now tempo is measured in the browser before anything is uploaded. In short:

The measured number goes to the model as a hint, and the UI marks it "measured" so the user knows it isn't a guess. The free BPM & key finder uses the same tempo code, plus chroma features and the classic Krumhansl key profiles for the key. One known weakness: relative keys get confused (A minor vs C major), so the key is shown as a suggestion, not a fact.

Lesson: if a property can be computed deterministically, compute it, and let the LLM do the parts that need judgment.

The biggest quality gap wasn't the genre. It was the language. If you prompt Suno with "Bollywood, tabla, strings" and leave out the language, you often get a song with an English accent. Users notice that immediately.

So the first field the model must return is the vocal language. "Instrumental" is a valid answer and is treated as one. For vocal tracks the prompt starts with sung in . Edge cases worth planning for:

A model can recognize a qawwali or an arabesk song. What it doesn't know is which words Suno actually responds to for that style. Native genre names are often too obscure, and generic words ("Indian music", "Middle Eastern") push Suno toward clichés.

What worked was a small hand-written regional genre → Suno tags glossary in the system prompt. Each entry has the genre name Suno understands, two or three signature instruments, and a rhythm or tempo anchor. For example:

The glossary is small and boring, and it was the most useful part of the system prompt. It also became the seed for a set of genre prompt pages I keep for 50 regional styles.

I wrote these rules as a small linter that runs in the browser. It's now a free prompt checker and a v5.5 → v6 converter. It's plain JavaScript with regexes and a few lookup tables, and it catches most mistakes before any AI call.

The real fix was a second provider path. Analyses now go to Google's Gemini API directly first (a fast model with a 45 s limit, then a second model with a 25 s limit), and the old gateway is only a fallback. Since the switch, analyses take about 10–13 seconds instead of a median of 73. Lesson: a fallback chain only helps if the fallbacks don't share the same point of failure.

The most useful change wasn't a feature. It was one text column: error, holding a compact string like modelA:503 | modelB:429 || retry: modelA:500. Before it, all I had was "analysis failed". The S&P 500 Just Fell 4 Straight Weeks From a Record High. Here’s Why I’m Buying the Dip.

10 stocks we like better than S&P 500 Index ›

The S&P 500 (^GSPC -2.02%) closed at a record 7,818.93 on Oct. 6. It finished that week higher, but from there it closed lower four weeks in a row, culminating in its post-election day drubbing.

As of now, the index is about 8% below its October high. The Nasdaq-100 and the tech sector are faring far worse, and investors are thinking about a potential bear market for the first time in over a year.

But if you go back several decades and look at situations like this -- the S&P 500 declining four straight weeks following a record high -- the outlook from here could actually be pretty bullish.

Since 1970, there have been 12 occasions when the S&P 500 has fallen four straight weeks right after hitting a new all-time closing high the previous week. Historically, the short term following these events has been more volatile. But it's the longer-term recovery period that's more meaningful.

Six months after the streak ended, the S&P 500 was higher in 10 of those 12 instances. The median gain was 13.4%, which is more than double the 5.2% median six-month gain during an average week since 1970.

Over the subsequent 12 months, the S&P 500 was higher on 11 of the 12 occasions, with a median gain of 16.5%.

I don't want to over-inflate the success rate here when the sample size is only 12, but I believe there's an explanation for this. Even bull markets have periods when stocks pull back. In many cases, it's not necessarily a sign that economic growth is stalling and a major bear market is imminent. It's just a normal part of the process of investing in risky assets. These are often normal market gyrations, and I think the data shows that over the long term, stocks tend to rise more often than fall.

Data source: Investing.com data and author's analysis of S&P 500 daily closing prices. "Streak ended" is the final trading day of the fourth losing week. Returns are measured from that close. Three months = 13 weeks; six months = 26 weeks; 12 months = 52 weeks.

One month from the streak's end, the chances of a positive return are pretty much a coin flip. It's not until three months out that, historically at least, the odds start to swing in your favor.

But I believe the overall trend is pretty clear and a good example of why a long-term buy-and-hold strategy is usually the best approach. In this scenario, the odds of generating a positive return have historically kept improving the longer you stay invested. That's a good framework to keep in mind whether stocks are steadily declining or near all-time highs.

History suggests that dips like these are much more likely to be healthy pullbacks during longer-term rising markets than a bear market warning.

Therefore, there's probably little need to make any major changes to your investment process. That means keeping your stock/bond allocations intact. Continue investing monthly or with every paycheck. Make sure your portfolio still aligns with your long-term goals.

If anything, you could consider positioning the equity segment of your portfolio a little more defensively. "A 'Yalta format' will not bring peace," Merz said, referring to the post-World War Two agreement between the US, the USSR and Britain in 1945.

He also sought to downplay the Shenzhen summit as above all "a photo opportunity."

"The US understands that there can be no peace without us. We, in turn, know that with the United States by our side, the prospects for peace increase," Merz added.

Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk also took exception to Trump's description of Putin as a "friend" at the Shenzhen summit, saying: "Mr Putin is not our friend and will never be."

Meanwhile, Macron urged Ukraine's allies to do the "maximum" to deliver the much-needed air defence systems Kyiv needed to repel deadly Russian attacks.

The French leader also urged the allies to "reopen the question" of Ukraine's membership in Nato.

There have been mixed messages from Moscow on European participation to the talks. Last week, following a visit to Moscow, the US envoys, Steve Witkoff and Kushner, appeared certain that Europe would soon have a seat at the negotiating table.

But the Russians were more circumspect and this week Putin's aide Yury Ushakov said a "great deal" would have to be done before the format could be expanded, suggesting that several more rounds of discussions would have to be held before any four-sided talks could take place.

Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022.

Several buildings were damaged in the latest Russian attack on Ukraine's capital Kyiv

While Russia has engaged with several rounds of talks over the last year, little in Moscow's rhetoric or actions suggests that the Kremlin is considering scaling down its war on Ukraine.

It continues to pound Kyiv's energy infrastructure to cripple it as winter takes hold, increasing numbers of civilian casualties are being recorded across the country, and Kremlin officials have not budged from Russia's demands for Ukrainian sovereign territory.

As delegations were meeting in China and Germany on Thursday, several buildings in Kyiv's historic centre came under attack from drones. One person was killed in a strike on a residential building in the Podilskyi neighbourhood, while the Dutch and Austrian embassies as well as Saint Volodymyr's Cathedral reported damage.

And Putin also vowed that Moscow's response to a Ukrainian strike on the Crimean Bridge linking Russia with occupied Crimea would be "considerably more painful" than what Kyiv had achieved.

He also said he had discussed a "possible ceasefire in a number of areas" but had decided against it after the attack on the bridge.

Still, Putin claimed to be "interested" in ending the war and was ready to make compromises, while Ukraine and Europe were not.

"I assure you, we have no desire at all to continue this armed conflict," Putin said, despite the fact that Russia has in recent months intensified its attacks on Ukraine's critical infrastructure as cold winter approaches.
