Lutra, a South Korean startup, has debuted a new brand called Humans of Authenticity (HOA) that’s launching another screen-free digital camera that incorporates a large open viewfinder into its design, as spotted by PetaPixel. The camera is reminiscent of the Godox C100 but the HOA A1 positions its lens and sensor close to the center of its large viewfinder window so that a photographer can maintain eye contact with their subject, or feel more in the moment while framing a shot without staring at a screen.

The HOA A1 First Edition is expected to launch on December 1st in a very limited run of just 100 units. Preorders haven’t started yet, but the camera will be priced at 360,000 South Korean won, or $289 for US customers. The A1 features a 16-megapixel, 1/2.53-inch Sony sensor paired with a 26mm f/1.75 lens. There is a tiny LCD display on the back but it’s used to display information like battery life and your remaining shots. Everything else about the camera is strictly point and shoot with a shutter button located on its top edge.

The HOA website doesn’t include extensive details about the camera’s capabilities, or even sample shots captured by the A1. According to the site the company is still “fine-tuning the camera’s color and tone in collaboration with photographers.” It does confirm that instead of copying photos to a device or PC using a USB cable or by removing a memory card, the A1 connects to Wi-Fi and wirelessly makes your shots available by tapping your smartphone to the back of the camera.

A wireless connection to the internet isn’t needed to take photos, but you’ll need access to Wi-Fi to view and download your shots. The A1 uses a system called ShootProof that authenticates every image captured with an anonymous certificate recorded to a public blockchain. That feature doesn’t appear optional, but there’s also a signature mode you can turn on or off that lets you sign your name in an accompanying app and then have it overlaid in the corner of every photo.

Shopping for a Child Car Seat? These Are the Ones Worth Considering

We break down the best car seats for safety, comfort, and everyday convenience to help you find the right fit for your family.

This article will be updated periodically with new information.

Choosing the right car seat is one of the most important decisions you’ll make as a parent, but sorting through dozens of models, safety features, and installation systems can quickly become overwhelming. From infant carriers to convertible seats, the best options balance safety, comfort, ease of use, and a secure fit in your vehicle.

While all car seats sold in the U.S. must meet federal safety standards, not every seat is equally suited to every family. We looked at top-rated models, practical features, and everyday usability to help narrow down the choices, whether you’re bringing home a newborn, upgrading for a growing toddler, or preparing for the next stage.

Buying one car seat that can handle nearly every stage of childhood is appealing, especially when it doesn’t sacrifice convenience. With four configurations, rear-facing, forward-facing, high-back booster, and backless booster, the Graco 4Ever DLX is designed to serve families for up to 10 years. That versatility, combined with its practical features, makes it our Top Pick.

Graco’s InRight Latch connectors click into place, while an integrated belt lock-off helps simplify seatbelt installation. The 10-position headrest adjusts alongside the no-rethread harness, and six recline positions accommodate growing passengers. The RapidRemove machine-washable cover is another welcome feature, allowing parents to tackle accident, spills, and other kid messes without uninstalling the seat.

The 4Ever DLX is relatively bulky, and its 40-pound rear-facing limit falls short of some competitors. Still, its four-stage versatility, easy adjustments, and thoughtful design make it an excellent all-around choice for families.

The Maxi-Cosi Pria All-in-One delivers impressive versatility for the price, making it a smart investment for parents who want one car seat to last through multiple stages of childhood. It accommodates children from 5 to 40 pounds rear-facing, 30 to 65 pounds forward-facing, and 40 to 100 pounds in booster mode, eliminating the need to purchase separate seats as your child grows.

Convenience is another selling point. The one-click Latch connectors simplify installation, while the QuikFit headrest and harness adjust together without rethreading. Three recline positions, plush infant inserts, and breathable fabrics help keep passengers comfortable on longer drives. Machine-washable upholstery and dishwasher-safe cupholders are especially welcome given accidents are inevitable with kids.

The Pria is relatively bulky, so smaller vehicles may present fit challenges. Still, its comfort, adjustability, and long-term usability make it an excellent value compared with pricier convertible models.

A compelling case for spending more on a car seat is Britax’s One4Life Slim, especially if you prioritize easy installation and long-term versatility. Its standout feature is Britax’s ClickTight installation system, which uses the vehicle’s seat belt to secure the seat without the usual wrestling match. At just 17.5 inches wide, its slimmer profile also makes it practical for families with limited back-seat space.

Designed to accommodate children from 5 to 120 pounds across rear-facing, forward-facing, and high-back booster configurations, the One4Life Slim offers years of use. The 50-pound rear-facing limit is especially appealing for parents who want to keep their children rear-facing longer. A no-rethread harness, adjustable headrest, and machine-washable cover add convenience.

It’s heavier and considerably more expensive than basic convertible seats, but the One4Life Slim is designed to handle nearly every stage without complicating life for new parents.

Extended rear-facing capability and strong independent crash-test results make the UPPAbaby Rove a compelling option for parents moving beyond an infant car seat. It supports children from 14 to 50 pounds rear-facing and 30 to 65 pounds forward-facing, offering years of use without an immediate upgrade as your child grows.

UPPAbaby’s QuickGuard installation system features spring-loaded belt lock-offs, while the SmartSecure indicator changes from red to green to help confirm proper installation. This is extremely helpful and honestly puts a parents mind at ease if their child is secure.

A multidirectional tether, side-impact protection pods, and energy-absorbing foam add to its safety-focused design. The no-rethread harness, five recline positions, and removable, machine-washable cushioning make adjustments and cleanup more manageable.

The Rove has earned excellent crash-protection marks, although testers found its bulky dimensions and installation challenging in some vehicles. It’s also unsuitable for newborns under 14 pounds. Still, its extended rear-facing limits and thoughtful features make it a strong contender for convertible seats.

For new parents, a car seat that’s easy to install correctly and convenient to carry can make a difference in daily life. Chicco’s KeyFit 35 delivers on both fronts, combining practical safety features with a detachable carrier that works with compatible Chicco strollers. That’s a win-win.

Designed for infants weighing 4 to 35 pounds and measuring up to 32 inches tall, the KeyFit 35 features Chicco’s SuperCinch Latch tightener, LockSure seat-belt lock-off, and built-in leveling indicators to simplify installation. An anti-rebound bar adds another layer of protection, while the no-rethread harness and adjustable headrest accommodate growing babies.

Chicco’s reputation is rooted in easy installation and reliable use for everyday. The carrier weighs about 10 pounds, and its infant-only design means you’ll eventually need a convertible seat. Still, its convenience, stroller compatibility, and details make this is an excellent choice for a baby’s first car seat.

Car seats aren’t one-size-fits-all. Infant seats are designed exclusively for rear-facing use, while convertible and all-in-one models accommodate children through multiple stages. Pay close attention to the manufacturer’s height and weight limits, not age alone. The NHTSA recommends keeping children rear-facing as long as possible, until they reach the seat’s maximum rear-facing height or weight limit.

Even highly rated car seats won’t work equally well in every vehicle. Check your owner’s manual for approved seating positions and installation instructions, and consider how much space the seat takes up, especially in smaller cars. If you have multiple children, width matters even more. Whenever possible, test the seat in your vehicle before you buy it.

A properly installed car seat is essential, and complicated installation systems can lead to mistakes and stress. Look for clear leveling indicators, accessible belt paths, and user-friendly LATCH connectors or seatbelt lock-offs. Remember, LATCH isn’t necessarily safer than a seat belt used correctly. A certified Child Passenger Safety Technician can also help verify your installation.

Think beyond the initial purchase. Machine-washable covers, adjustable harnesses, removable cupholders, and stroller compatibility can make daily use considerably easier. All-in-one seats offer long-term value, but infant carriers are often more convenient for newborns. Check expiration dates, manufacturer warranties, and how long your child can realistically use each configuration before deciding which features justify the price.

The safest car seat is one that fits your child’s height and weight, installs correctly in your vehicle, and is used properly every time. All new car seats sold in the U.S. must meet federal safety standards, but independent crash testing and ease-of-use ratings can help distinguish models. For newborns, choose a rear-facing infant or convertible seat approved for their size.

NHTSA recommends keeping children rear-facing until they reach the maximum height or weight limit specified by their car seat’s manufacturer. Many convertible seats accommodate rear-facing children up to 40 or 50 pounds, letting them stay in this safer position well beyond their second birthday.

Replace a car seat when your child exceeds its height or weight limits, the seat reaches its expiration date, or it has been involved in a moderate or severe crash. NHTSA advises that seats involved in minor crashes may not need replacement if they meet specific criteria. Always check the manufacturer’s instructions and inspect the seat for damage or missing components.

Infant car seats are designed for rear-facing use and typically feature detachable carriers that make transporting newborns easier. Convertible car seats stay installed in the vehicle and accommodate children through the rear-facing and forward-facing stages. Infant seats offer greater portability, while convertible models generally provide better long-term value. The best choice depends on your vehicle, budget, and daily routine.

Worcestershire trio aim to make more Cup magic

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

It's now 55 seasons since non-league Hereford United's 2-1 fourth-round replay win over top-flight Newcastle United entered English football folklore as arguably the greatest FA Cup shock of all time.

Elsewhere in the Midlands, though, there have been many other upsets and moments of cup magic too.

Telford United (in 1984) and Kidderminster Harriers (in 1994) both made it to the fifth round, and Worcestershire village side Alvechurch hold a record they will keep forever - unless the rules are changed again - after taking four replays to see off Oxford City in a qualifying round tie in 1971.

Worcester City were involved in a record-breaking penalty shootout against Scunthorpe in 2014 - the same year they famously beat Coventry City.

But, while the memory of that day at Hereford - Ronnie Radford et al - surpasses all other memories, across the Worcestershire border, it perhaps still irks a bit.

Airbnb’s technology chief holds a stake in Chesky’s AI lab, and independent directors approved the deal. It landed with Airbnb’s fastest revenue growth in three years and a 2027 date for its own AI agent.

Brian Chesky at an Airbnb event in San Francisco in 2018

Brian Chesky’s AI lab paid Airbnb $7.1M for some of its intellectual property and to cover certain expenses, Airbnb disclosed in its quarterly filing on Thursday. Chesky, Airbnb’s chief executive, founded the lab and controls it.

Under the deal, signed this month, Airbnb can choose to join the lab’s future funding rounds. It also gets a licence to use some of the lab’s technology inside its own business. Chief technology officer Ahmad Al-Dahle holds a stake in the lab and works for it too. The Information, which reported the disclosure, said Chesky has funded the lab himself so far, citing a source. TNW has not independently verified that.

“Their roles with the AI Lab do not change their duties and responsibilities to Airbnb,” the filing said of the two executives.

A special committee of independent board members approved the deal under Airbnb’s policy on related-party transactions. The filing does not name the lab or say what it is building.

Al-Dahle joined Airbnb in January from Meta, where he led generative AI, Bloomberg reported at the time. We reported in June that Chesky was building an AI lab of his own.

Instead of a chat window, the agent will build each answer from maps, photos, comparisons, calendars and itineraries. It will also handle changes, refunds and payment problems after a booking.

Chesky argued that general assistants such as Meta’s Muse and Instinct are not built for travel. They still send Airbnb little business, he said on the call, as Business Insider reported.

“We’re not seeing a lot of traffic, and even fewer bookings, from Muse and Instinct yet,” Chesky said.

Agent traffic peaked in early October and has not grown since, he added. Airbnb’s own support assistant now resolves about 40% of customer issues without a human. Support costs per booking fell more than 17% from a year earlier, the company said.

Godzilla Minus Zero Has A Post-Credits Surprise (But It's Not A Scene)

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Godzilla Minus Zero's credits don't finish with a bonus post-credits scene teasing Gojira's next adventure, but that doesn't mean you should leave as soon as they start rolling. Picking up two years after Godzilla Minus One's ending, Takashi Yamazaki's sequel turns the Oscar-winning Japanese monster movies into a proper franchise.

As a result, there is an entirely new set of expectations for Godzilla Minus Zero. The 2023 original was not guaranteed to get a sequel, leaving all of Yamazaki's ideas for where the story could go to happen before the credits began and audiences risked missing any additional setup. But it is much more common for sequels to include credits scenes to promise audiences more movies.

Like its predecessor, Godzilla Minus Zero does not include a post-credits scene of any kind. That is not a sign to leave the theater as soon as the movie wraps up. There is a special audio cue for those who stick around for the whole time.

Warning: The following contains spoilers for Godzilla Minus Zero!At the very end of Godzilla Minus Zero's credits, there is audio of Godzilla stomping through rubble several times, followed by one final roar. This is the same way that Godzilla Minus One's credits ended, using the audio as a final tease for audiences rather than using a proper after-credits scene.

The meaning of Godzilla Minus Zero's post-credits audio has multiple interpretations. It is not used on every Toho Godzilla movie, so this could have become Yamazaki's signature way to finish his entries. However, since Godzilla is still alive and free at the end of Godzilla Minus Zero, it could also be argued that this is a tease that more destructive encounters between humans and Gojira will come.

Even before the final Godzilla Minus Zero audio surprise, the credits are worth sticking around for. Naoki Satō's incredibly epic score plays throughout the entire thing, so the credits become a final chance to experience his great music. Furthermore, it is also an opportunity to appreciate everyone who worked so hard to make Godzilla Minus Zero a reality.

The audio-only tease may be a bit disappointing for anyone hoping for a proper post-credits scene setting up a third film, but it is par for the course with this franchise. Godzilla Minus One didn't have one either. More broadly speaking, Godzilla movies rarely implement using Hollywood's franchise-expanding tool.

Across the 40 Godzilla movies released between Toho and American studios, only a handful have post-credits scenes. The last was 2019's Godzilla: King of the Monsters. You have to go back to 2003's Godzilla: Tokyo S.O.S. to find a live-action Toho production that included a proper post-credits scene. Otherwise, Toho's animated trilogy of Godzilla: Planet of the Monsters, Godzilla: City on the Edge of Battle, and Godzilla: The Planet Eater all had one.

Godzilla fans have not been conditioned to expect credits scenes in the monster's movies. It is traditional for these films to let their endings be where the story ends. This forces the films to include any sequel setup before the credits roll and lessens the possibility of teasing fans with stories that may not happen.

Even without a traditional post-credits scene, Godzilla Minus Zero still leaves the door open for this franchise to continue if Yamazaki wants to make a third. The movie ends with Kōichi, Noriko, and Akiko going on the run, Kanji becoming a captive of the United States, and Godzilla heading back into the ocean after killing the powerful King Ghidorah.

All of those threads could be expanded upon in a Godzilla Minus Zero sequel. But none have made it so a third film is mandatory to ensure Yamazaki's take on the kaiju goes unfinished. After all, Godzilla Minus Zero was promoted as the franchise's end by Toho.

Producer Kazuaki Kishida previously discussed Godzilla Minus Zero's sequel chances with ScreenRant at New York Comic Con, saying, "So this was true from Minus One to Minus Zero as well, but while we're working on any one Godzilla film, we don't think about any other film but the one we're working on. Because we have no idea what's going to happen with it."

Only time will tell if the story continues beyond Godzilla Minus Zero, but the post-credits audio is a clever way of hinting at Godzilla being involved in more destruction, even if we never see it.

Feedback Needed: Project by former DOJ lawyers shows people how to participate in federal rulemaking process

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When Rebecca Bond left her position as chief of the Justice Department’s Disability Rights Section in May 2025, she knew what she wanted to do next.

“I wanted to help people understand the power that they have to impact policy by telling their stories when a rule is open for comment,” says Bond, who spent decades working with federal regulations. That taught her how important public input is in the rulemaking process and taught her that there’s a general lack of understanding as to how rulemaking affects people in simple ways.

Additionally, Bond says many people don’t know that they can participate in the process. In October, she and Kristin Stitcher, former deputy chief of the Disability Rights Section, launched Public Powered Policy, a project dedicated to informing the public about the rulemaking process and empowering them to participate in it.

They were joined initially by a former paralegal who has since started attending law school and now serves as an adviser.

“Our goal is to reach a large swath of people, not just lawyers,” says Bond, who serves as the project’s executive director. “We are trying to reach the teachers in Missouri and the potato farmers in Idaho.”

A podcast, Regularly Speaking, marked the group’s launch. It focused on the Federal Trade Commission and what responsibility online platforms should have when scammers use their advertising tools to impersonate banks or government agencies. The show is available on Apple, Spotify, and YouTube.

Stitcher says that while rulemaking can sound dull, it’s astonishing how much it impacts people’s day-to-day living.

Rules “can affect the quality of your water and air, what’s in your kids’ school lunches, and what transportation is available to you,” says Stitcher, the group’s director of policy and programs.

The group also communicates timely information about upcoming rules via Instagram reels, using the handle @publicpoweredpolicy. And Bond and Stitcher plan to educate the public through writing opinion pieces and training grassroots organizations.

Public Powered Policy is sponsored by the Social Good Fund, a California nonprofit. Bond also received a Leadership in Government Fellowship administered by Assets Under Movement, and the project welcomes individual donations.

The Public Powered Policy website explains the basics of how the administrative rules are written, the process for draft versions, and how to participate in public comment and make an impact.

Someone giving specific feedback about “lived experience” can influence what a rule ultimately says, according to Stitcher and Bond.

“Our goal is not to tell people what to think or comment, but to give them the information on how to comment and let them decide how they want to do that or if they want to do that,” Stitcher says.

Stitcher advises people commenting on rules to simply tell their stories with as much personal detail as possible.

“You are the expert in your own life,” Stitcher says. “They won’t know unless you tell them.”
