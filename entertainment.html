Stephen King's 1 Main Request For Hit Sci-Fi Adaptation's Return This Month Confirmed

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

EXCLUSIVE: When The Institute returns for its second season this month, it will feature a scene that was personally requested by author Stephen King, according to the show's lead star.

The Institute is based on King's 2019 novel, which tells the story of kids with telekinetic and telepathic powers (including Luke Ellis) who get abducted by a mysterious organization known as the Institute. Six years later, MGM+ and creator/showrunner Benjamin Cavell debuted the TV adaptation, which stars Ben Barnes, Joe Freeman, Simone Miller, Fionn Laird and Mary-Louise Parker.

King serves as one of the executive producers on The Institute alongside Cavell, Jack Bender and others. In an interview with ScreenRant's Grant Hermanns, Barnes, who plays Tim Jamieson, revealed that the bestselling horror author had one request for The Institute season 2. He "very deliberately" wanted to see the children use their powers to "get cash out of an ATM."

King had that visual in his head, which Barnes can relate to because "when you're dreaming and conjuring stories, or writing songs, or anything, you have something very specific in your head."

Cavell already had a connection to King before working on The Institute, having co-created and executive-produced Paramount+'s 2020 adaptation of The Stand, so the author was familiar with Cavell and knew how he worked, especially when it came to bringing his stories to life on the screen.

King was "quite thrilled" with how The Institute turned out, according to Barnes, who explained that Cavell made sure to incorporate certain themes that King typically includes in his books, like "fighting back against the man" and the "powers that be," as well as how innocence can be corrupted and "really ask[ing] difficult questions of people that are in charge."

Ben Barnes: "We didn't particularly have individual connections with Stephen King, but obviously, Ben Cavell, our showrunner, did, and I think he was quite thrilled with how the first season turned out thematically. Certainly, in terms of the thing that you associate with Stephen King so deliberately, his concerns about fighting back against the man, against the powers that be, the corruption of innocence, the power of youth — in this case, the supernatural power of youth — to stand up for itself, and kind of really ask difficult questions of people that are in charge. I think so long as we carried through those themes into the second season, then we were going to be on the right path. I did hear that he very deliberately wanted the kids to use their superpowers to get cash out of an ATM. [Laughs] That was his one visual, in his head, which I connect to because when you're dreaming and conjuring stories, or writing songs, or anything, you have something very specific in your head that you're like, 'Oh, it'd be cool to see that.' I love that he deliberately said, 'I want to see that.'"

Barnes and everyone who works on The Institute knew that if they continued to carry those messages forward into season 2, "then we were going to be on the right path."

The first season earned a 64% critic score and a 69% audience score on Rotten Tomatoes. Now, a little over a year later, the cast and crew are back for the second season, with Game of Thrones Alfie Allen joining the cast as billionaire Nolan Reeves.

After The Institute season 1 finale saw the characters destroy the facility, Luke, Tim and the other survivors are now working towards their next goal: to uncover the crimes committed by the Institute.

King's stories have been adapted numerous times throughout his career, a trend that's not slowing down anytime soon. Recently, there have been movies based on The Monkey, The Long Walk and The Running Man as well as Mike Flanagan's hit new TV show Carrie, which has already been renewed for a second season.

The Institute is also coming back for season 2, as the drama, secrets and conspiracies continue to grow with each episode.

The Institute season 2 premieres Sunday, November 8 at 9 p.m. EST on MGM+.

The Institute, released in 2025, follows 14-year-old genius Luke Ellis, who is kidnapped and wakes up at a mysterious facility filled with children possessing unusual abilities. Meanwhile, Tim Jamieson, a former police officer, seeks a fresh start in a nearby town, inevitably intertwining his fate with Luke's.

7 Upcoming Fantasy Shows, Ranked by Hype

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

2026 has been a pretty solid year for fantasy television. So far, the year has seen new seasons of shows like House of the Dragon and Interview with the Vampire, as well as brand-new series like Avatar: Seven Havens and Witch Hat Atelier. However, as fun as those shows are, there are several exciting fantasy shows in the pipeline right now that could potentially blow those hits out of the water.

With release dates across the rest of 2026, 2027, and beyond, several upcoming shows have garnered sky-high hype among fantasy fans, drumming up lots of conversations ahead of their premieres. Not all of the conversations are positive, of course, and there’s at least one upcoming project that’s been talked about for all the wrong reasons, but on the whole, the future of fantasy television does look pretty bright. Without further ado, here are some of the most fascinating upcoming fantasy shows ranked by hype.

Based on J. K. Rowling’s bestselling fantasy books, Harry Potter is an upcoming fantasy series that’s the second adaptation of the novels after the film series. Starring Dominic McLaughlin as Harry, Alastair Stout as Ron Weasley, and Arabella Stanton as Hermione Granger, the show’s first season adapts the first book, Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone, and details Harry’s discovery of the wizarding world and his first year at Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry. The ensemble cast also includes John Lithgow as Albus Dumbledore, Janet McTeer as Minerva McGonagall, Paapa Essiedu as Severus Snape, and Nick Frost as Rubeus Hagrid.

HBO has big plans for Harry Potter, with early reports estimating that the new show could span a decade of production. The series has already been renewed for a second season as well. However, the whole project has been largely met with the opposite of hype. Comparisons to the movies (the last of which came out in 2011), negative public opinion of J.K. Rowling, and the idea that the whole project is a naked cash grab have all led to a generally dismal reception from the core fanbase ahead of the show’s release.

Created by Richard Garfield, Magic: The Gathering is one of the most successful game franchises of all time. A trading card game that’s expanded to various other media, the franchise is set across multiple realities and centered on Planeswalkers, dueling wizards with the ability to cross between worlds. While the game has been adapted as comics and novels before, it’s yet to get a movie or TV adaptation.

The Magic: The Gathering series adaptation has been in development since 2019 and has undergone many behind-the-scenes changes, but it finally looks like the show might actually make it to screens. The new show will be a Netflix animated series with Terry Matalas (12 Monkeys) serving as showrunner. The series was previewed at the 2025 Annecy International Animation Film Festival, but it doesn’t yet have a release date. Nonetheless, fans of the franchise are certainly excited for the show’s release and for the upcoming movie adaptation directed by Matt Johnson (Tony).

One of the bestselling romantasy books of the 2020s, Rebecca Yarros’s Fourth Wing is the first book of the Empyrean new adult fantasy romance series. A series adaptation of the books has been in the works at Prime Video since 2023, with Yarros serving as an executive producer on the project alongside Michael B. Jordan, Lisa Joy, Jonathan Nolan, Liz Pelletier, Athena Wickham, and showrunner Meredith Averill. The show, like the book, follows the story of Violet Sorrengail, who joins the Basgiath War College and trains to become a dragon rider, encountering deadly quests, gruelling competition, and unexpected romance. Esther McGregor will star as Violet, with Emilio Sakraya as her rival/love interest, Xaden Riorson.

Fourth Wing is one of the biggest BookTok hits of the romantasy genre, so its series adaptation has a lot of people very excited, and with good reason. Featuring fast-paced action, high-tension romance, and, of course, dragons, Fourth Wing has all the ingredients to become a fantasy TV landmark to rival Game of Thrones. Whether it will actually achieve that potential remains to be seen.

Mike Flanagan’s The Dark Tower is an upcoming multi-season series adaptation of the fantasy book series by Stephen King. Published between 1982 and 2012, the books revolve around Roland Deschain, a gunslinger on an unending quest across a surreal landscape in search of the titular tower, which sits at the center of a vast multiverse. The books were previously adapted as a 2017 film, which was widely panned by critics, but the series is a complete reboot of the story, and it’s been reported that it will star Sam Strike and Jasper Pääkkönen.

Spanning eight connected novels, one novella, a children’s book, and connections to almost all of King’s celebrated novels, The Dark Tower is the holy grail of Stephen King adaptations. It’s easy to see why the upcoming series has so many people excited, even though it doesn’t have a release date or any concrete details as yet. What little we know about The Dark Tower so far suggests an in-depth adaptation that could prove to be Flanagan’s most ambitious project yet and one of the most fascinating fantasy shows ever made. Or, like the movie, it could just be an expensive failure; only time will tell.

It’s no exaggeration to say that Brandon Sanderson is one of the biggest names in fantasy literature today, and The Stormlight Archive novel series, part of Sanderson’s Cosmere universe, is one of the author’s most popular hits. Currently comprising five novels and two novellas (with plans for a total of 10 novels), it’s an expansive saga set on the battered world of Roshar, which is threatened by magical storms, political tensions, and a looming cataclysm. The books have already inspired a VR experience and a tabletop game, and will soon become an Apple TV series.

As part of Sanderson’s deal with the streaming platform, the author will be personally leading adaptations of his Mistborn novels and The Stormlight Archive, the first as a movie and the second as a TV show, the first season of which will consist of 10 episodes. It’s still early days — the project doesn’t even have a showrunner yet — but it’s easily one of the most hotly anticipated fantasy shows currently in development. With complex character dynamics, intricate worldbuilding, and a fascinating magic system, The Stormlight Archive series has all the potential to become one of the biggest fantasy shows of all time, though, of course, right now, that’s all just speculation.

Developed by Sony Pictures Television, Amazon MGM Studios, and PlayStation Productions, Prime Video’s God of War is the first series adaptation of the video game franchise. Based on the franchise’s Norse mythology era, the show follows Kratos and his son Atreus on a journey to the highest peak of the nine realms, where they hope to spread the ashes of their wife and mother, Faye. Ronald D. Moore serves as the showrunner, and the series stars Dave Bautista and Callum Vinson as Kratos and Atreus, respectively.

God of War fans have been waiting for a live-action adaptation for a long time, and the series is quite highly anticipated as a result. The production has had some significant delays, with original Kratos actor Ryan Hurst suffering an injury during filming, which has prompted a recasting and reshoots. We don’t know when the show will finally arrive, but Prime Video has given it a two-season order, and production is currently expected to end sometime in October 2027.

The second anime adaptation of Eiichiro Oda’s One Piece, The One Piece is a new original net animation show produced by Studio Wit and set to release on Netflix in February 2027. The series is a fresh reboot of the franchise adapted directly from the manga (not the iconic 1999 anime), and it will follow the adventures of Monkey D. Luffy (Mayumi Tanaka), an aspiring pirate who sets out in search of the titular treasure alongside his motley crew, the Straw Hats. Directed by Masashi Koizuka and written by Taku Kishimoto, with character designs by Kyoji Asano and Takatoshi Honda, the show’s seven-episode first season will adapt the first 50 chapters of the manga.

One Piece is one of those anime/manga franchises that’s so big it’s recognizable even to people who’ve never seen an anime before, so it’s pretty understandable why Wit’s The One Piece is so highly anticipated. The new adaptation is expected to fix the classic anime’s pacing issues and elevate the animation style, giving the manga the fully modernized adaptation it deserves and bringing a new generation of viewers to the franchise.

Best Animated Feature Predictions Commentary (Updated: Nov. 3, 2026): This year, animated features aren’t just for kids. And that’s a good thing.

Laika’s “Wildwood” is the company’s first PG-13 film in its 17 years as a feature studio. At 130 minutes, it ties the record for the longest English-language animated film ever made. Director and producer Travis Knight is already an Oscar nominee for “Kubo and the Two Strings” and has earned considerable respect from his peers and audiences alike. “Wildwood” is among the year’s most acclaimed films and, despite a less-than-stellar box office performance, currently looks like the one to beat.

Knight was among the filmmakers featured in this year’s Pixels and Pencils panel, Variety’s conversation with animation directors at the SCAD Savannah Film Festival. He was joined by Brad Bird (“Ray Gunn”), Daniel Chong (“Hoppers”), Joel Crawford and Januel Mercado (“Forgotten Island”) and Leah Nelson (“Tangles”), all of whom are also in the animated feature race.

And that’s just the group represented on the panel. Louis Clichy’s “Iron Boy” and Andrew Stanton’s “Toy Story 5” are among the other contenders helping make this one of the category’s most competitive lineups in recent years.

Since “Guillermo del Toro’s Pinocchio” won the animated feature Oscar at the 95th Academy Awards, neither Disney nor Pixar has won the trophy. Instead, “The Boy and the Heron,” “Flow” and “KPop Demon Hunters” have taken home the prize, marking a significant shift for a category largely dominated by those two studios since “Shrek” won the inaugural award in 2002.

Then there’s “Tangles,” a film that makes no attempt to disguise its ambitions as an adult story. First-time feature director Leah Nelson co-wrote the film with Sarah Leavitt, adapting Leavitt’s graphic memoir about her mother’s battle with Alzheimer’s disease in the late 1990s. Abbi Jacobson voices Sarah, while Julia Louis-Dreyfus plays her mother. Louis-Dreyfus is also a producer, alongside Seth Rogen and Lauren Miller Rogen.

Both “Tangles” and “Wildwood” are also being submitted in the drama categories at the Golden Globes, where animated films have traditionally defaulted to comedy. It’s technically a matter of category placement, but it also signals something about how these creative teams want their films to be perceived.Of course, none of this is entirely new. “Persepolis” and “Anomalisa” earned Oscar nominations years ago, and Japanese filmmakers have been making animation for adults for as long as the medium has existed.

Only three animated films — “Beauty and the Beast,” “Up” and “Toy Story 3″ — have ever earned best picture nominations, and the last of those came 15 years ago. In a year where we could find our first documentary feature nominated for best picture (“You Can See Everything”), could an animated film join the lineup, too?

Beyond screens: Mercedes-Benz's new holographic heads-up display

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

After pioneering the passenger screen, Mercedes has designs on the windshield.

Do screens in cars represent luxury? Judging by the latest car interiors, the answer would seem to be an emphatic “yes.” Screens aren’t just getting larger; they are multiplying across the dashboard and morphing into different forms and concepts. Indeed, an interior’s screens are a pretty good indicator of what brand made the car—or at least how much the owner paid for it.

But is this actually what customers want? Are more screens really better? And even if they are, luxury car features inevitably trickle down to cheaper, more mainstream offerings, and screens can only get so big. That leaves luxury automakers with another question to answer: “What’s next?”

Mercedes-Benz is working on a new display alternative that could provide an answer. For now, it’s known as the holographic display. Forget the images from Star Trek and Star Wars that just popped into your head, though. It’s effectively a more advanced form of heads-up display, or HUD.

Whereas a traditional HUD projects images onto the windshield glass, the holographic display has two main components. It still uses a projector, but its key element is a special transparent film laminated into the glass. The film uses holographic optical elements, or “super-small mirrors,” as Mercedes development engineer Lena Hees described them, that selectively reflect the light emitted by the projector, keeping the image visible even in direct sunlight while hiding it from the driver’s view.

Today, the passenger-side touchscreens in Mercedes and other luxury vehicles rely on driver-monitoring cameras or screen polarization to keep the driver from seeing what is playing on the passenger side.

Speaking of passenger screens, the holographic display is clearly being considered as a future alternative or replacement for this technology. Although screens in cars are increasingly ubiquitous, a recent JD Power study indicated that car owners classify passenger screens as “not necessary” and have generally negative opinions about them. Mercedes has clearly observed similar opinions but for slightly different reasons.

“We have customers who like the Hyperscreen,” said development engineer Maximilian Hilger of the pillar-to-pillar screen found in an increasing number of Mercedes models. “But there are others who want to maintain luxury materials.”

In other words, the massive screen design doesn’t seem as luxurious as the pillar-to-pillar bands of wood or other materials found in previous-generation models. And that’s not an opinion isolated to Mercedes’ customers.

New Mercedes chief design officer Bastian Baudy recently told German magazine Auto Motor Sport that “Materiality and haptics must remain tangible—with switches, buttons, and intuitive controls. Real materials are at the heart of Mercedes: metal, wood.”

Mercedes CEO Ola Källenius went further, grouping interior quality and materials among the brand’s “forever values,” along with safety and maintaining what a Mercedes is like to drive.

That presents conflicting priorities, however.

“Yes, you need to have all the [technology] that a customer could ever dream of and wants,” Källenius told Ars. “But you need to package it in an aesthetically pleasing way. That’s also part of it.”

That brings us back to the holographic display. Keep in mind that the photos in this article show early prototypes I saw at Mercedes’ research facilities in Sindelfingen, Germany. The prototype projector sits inside a rather crude, trapezoidal hole dug into the dash of a right-hand-drive, pre-2027 S-Class. It’s also loud, with a cooling fan whirring like a laptop visiting too many junk-filled websites.

The production version would have a much smaller projector opening than those of the prototype and existing heads-up displays. Manual Holstein, a manager working in Mercedes research for future displays, indicated that the projector could be small enough to be hidden behind wood or metal trim pieces. Holstein also said you won’t hear a fan in the production model and that the resolution would improve.

The resolution is certainly considerably better than any current head-up display, including Mercedes’ own, which features augmented reality elements. Full-color, commercial-like videos of the C-Class Electric played during the demonstration, and the display was sufficiently bright, even though Holstein said the prototype was operating at just 20 percent of its maximum brightness.

Issues remain, however. The resolution may be good compared to the arrows, script, and graphics of a current HUD, but it’s poor when compared to a passenger touchscreen. The video’s contrast was also too high, and as you can see in the photos, the picture was distorted into a narrow height due to the angle of the windshield. Frankly, no one would choose to watch anything on it over a phone, tablet, or conventional passenger screen. It needs to improve by orders of magnitude before becoming a viable alternative.

Again, though, this is just a prototype. Holstein said the holographic film size, or “eyebox,” can be made much bigger and be raised higher than the HUD-like height of the prototype. He also dismissed the distortion as a problem unique to the prototype.

Imagine nearly half the windshield transformed into a virtual screen that’s visible only to the passenger. Beyond videos or other entertainment, Mercedes envisions the holographic display highlighting points of interest in the view through the windshield. A similar display could be placed on the driver side, showing situational information, enabling video calls, or providing entertainment when parked. In the future, it could even let drivers watch things other than the road when Mercedes’ Level 3 autonomous driving system allows them to take their eyes off it. At the very least, the technology could replace today’s HUDs and their larger projector boxes.

Having experienced the prototype while the car was moving, I also think the holographic display could help prevent the motion sickness that can come from staring at a passenger screen while the car is moving. The higher screen angle and ability to see more of the outside world beyond created a notably different visual experience.

Of course, the question remains: “Is this what customers want?”

If the JD Power survey found that customers don’t care about passenger touchscreens, will they be any more enthusiastic about holographic video displays? That seems doubtful. But that’s just as much of an open question as whether the production version will actually live up to its promises.

There’s also another matter: What, exactly, defines luxury? Before Bastian Baudy took over as Mercedes’ chief design officer this year, his predecessor and former boss Gordon Wagener echoed Källenius’ thoughts about the direction of the brand’s interiors. He was considerably blunter, though.

“Screens are not luxury,” Wagener told ABC News. “You have a better—and bigger—TV at home, right? And every car has a big screen. So we have to create luxury beyond the screen. That’s why I talk about craftsmanship and sophistication.”

If the holographic display lets automakers emphasize traditional design and materials while still offering unique technology, it could be a promising way forward.

Parties That Changed the World: Barbie’s Premiere and Gone With the Wind’s Debut

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The world’s most famous doll was inspired by a spunky, high-end call girl named Lilli. Described as a “gold digger, exhibitionist, and floozy,” the character of Bild Lilli was created in 1952 by German cartoonist Reinhard Beuthien after he was asked to draw a filler cartoon for the inaugural issue of the tabloid Bild-Zeitung. Lilli, a buxom blonde with a perky ponytail and sky-high stilettos, sat beside a fortune teller. “Can’t you give me the name and address of this tall, handsome, rich man?” she cooed. The cartoon was an instant hit, and in 1955, Lilli was immortalized in plastic.

A year later, Ruth Handler discovered the Lilli doll while on a vacation in Europe. She bought several. One was for her teenage daughter, Barbara, to keep; the rest were brought back to Mattel, the toy company she had cofounded with her husband, Elliot, and businessman Harold Matson. (Mattel is a portmanteau of both men’s names.) Lilli—with comically large breasts, chic outfits, and heavy makeup—was precisely what Handler had been looking for.

Robin Gerber, author of Barbie and Ruth, wrote: “The doll looked too much like a prostitute for the company’s taste,” so Mattel’s design team (slightly) softened Lilli’s exaggerated proportions, renamed her Barbie, after Barbara, and before long, she would become the physical ideal for generations of young girls and women everywhere.

Sixty-four years after Barbie hit shelves, she finally got her close-up in Barbie, a feminist manifesto in a pair of pink stilettos. The film’s premiere and after-party at the Shrine Auditorium marked Warner Bros.’ largest premiere to date—and the cherry on top of a bubble-gum-flavored, years-long marketing blitz. According to Event Marketer, “the affair was like Barbie herself: high-energy, glamorous and very, very, colorful.”

It was a beautiful day in Hollywood. Even by Los Angeles standards, the weather on Sunday, July 9, 2023, was almost impossibly perfect—sunshine, low 70s, and a cool breeze—as if Hollywood itself has been dropped into the fantasy world of Barbie Land.

The event kicked off with a splash. The pink carpet was tricked out with replicas of pieces from the film’s beach scenes: a lifeguard station, a towering lighthouse, faux palm trees, and a 3D ocean wave, with carpeting that shifted colors to mimic sand. A wide range of influencers were invited to get first dibs on photo ops before the stars arrived. But the real showstopper was the two-story replica of Barbie’s Dreamhouse, complete with a 2D faux pool, a DJ spinning tracks, and the trademark spiral slide. “We had to have that slide,” Wendy Robino, SVP of Warner Bros. marketing events, said at the time.

On Sunday, they wore pink: Writer and director Greta Gerwig, along with cast members Issa Rae, America Ferrera, Kate McKinnon, Ryan Gosling, and Michael Cera, turned out for the premiere in every shade of Barbie’s signature color, turning something long dismissed as girly into a celebration of individuality, joy, and girl power.

But the belle of the ball wasn’t one to blend in. After weeks of thinking pink, Margot Robbie—the film’s star and producer—arrived in black. Her custom Schiaparelli look re-created 1960s’ Solo in the Spotlight Barbie: a black sequined dress with a single red rose stitched onto her tulle skirt, matching opera gloves, Manolo Blahnik mules, and a diamond necklace. Movie Star Barbie fully committed to the bit, even clutching a pink handkerchief as she posed in front of a pink Corvette. Robbie’s blond hair was swept into a high ponytail—reminiscent of the one first worn by Lilli.

Following the official screening—marking the first time Barbie was revealed to the public—it was time to party.

The theme was “Barbie’s Blowout Disco Party.” Fittingly, one attraction invited guests inside a glittering, 12-foot pink disco ball. Justin Lefkovitch, whose company Mirrored Media, partnered with Mattel to create it, tells VF the entrance created “a shroud of mystery” for partygoers. Inside, a six-foot 1:1 Dreamhouse showcasing the new Barbie the Movie doll collection was surrounded by gold and silver streamers and over a dozen custom pink disco balls. The goal, Lefkovitch recalls, was to “transport guests into the world of Barbie.”

Nicki Minaj was the first guest to arrive at the party, event producer Austin Johnston tells VF—“five-foot-long hair all the way down to the floor.” A theme park designer by trade, Johnston and his company, AKJohnston, transformed the after-party into what he calls a “pop-up museum.” Barbie Land’s pastel-colored cul-de-sac was re-created as “a 65-foot-tall, 70-foot-wide” inflatable, its landmarks crammed together like a kitschy postcard. At the end of Barbie’s driveway, Mark Ronson presided over a hot pink DJ booth, playing hits from the soundtrack by Ice Spice, Dua Lipa (a.k.a. Mermaid Barbie), and Nicki Minaj—whom Johnston had to “very kindly” ask to get off Barbie’s Corvette.

Tucked beneath the second-floor mezzanine, a series of alcoves became larger-than-life photo sets, with photographers on hand to capture guests in action. Partygoers could retrace Barbie’s journey to the Real World—by bike, boat, and rocket ship—or step inside the Kendom man cave, Weird Barbie’s house, and life-size Barbie and Ken toy boxes. Not everyone hired to re-create Barbie Land had actually seen the movie by then; only a handful were invited to be part of that “really restricted small group,” Johnston says.

At the center of the room, guests sipped pink cocktails under a giant disco ball while an 11-foot, glitter-coated pink B twirled above. Another bar doubled as a fashion show, with four life-size mannequins dolled up for disco in original costumes from the film’s dance number, including Barbie’s gold sequined jumpsuit and the Kens’ matching white-and-gold tracksuits.

Obviously, the food was on theme too. Wolfgang Puck catered the evening’s Barbie-Q, serving grilled salmon, summer melon salad, roasted Pink Fir apple potatoes, char siu glazed grilled chicken, and charred tricolor cauliflower.

Even the night’s corporate sponsors knew better than to steal Barbie’s spotlight. OPI set up a nail salon, and Xbox provided a gaming station where players had their pick of two General Motors rides: Barbie’s Corvette or Ken’s Hummer. A highlight for Johnston was spotting Robbie racing a virtual Hummer while her Australian parents cheered her on. “No one paid her to do that,” he says. “She’s just like, I want to have a drink and play a video game at my premiere.”

For one night in Hollywood, women could be anything. And 84 years earlier, another city had hosted another rager in honor of another larger-than-life heroine. While Los Angeles turned pink for Barbie, Atlanta resurrected the Old South for Scarlett O’Hara.

All hell broke loose when British actor Vivien Leigh was cast as beloved heroine Scarlett O’Hara in the big-screen adaptation of Margaret Mitchell’s epic, Gone With the Wind. Fans and journalists were outraged that such an iconic Southern Belle would be played by a foreigner. At the time, Leigh was yet to star in a Hollywood film and was best known stateside for her affair with Laurence Olivier. Gossip columnist Hedda Hopper sniped: “Out of millions of American women, [producer David O. Selznick] couldn’t find one to suit him.”

Hopper was exaggerating, but not by a lot: During a grueling two-year nationwide search for his leading lady, Selznick reportedly met with over 1,400 actresses. After angry fan letters poured in, Selznick’s publicity machine kicked into overdrive—and eventually, the public came around to the idea that a refined Brit was somehow easier to swallow than a Yankee.

But perhaps the most convincing endorsement came from Leigh’s own leading man. Even Clark Gable, who initially considered Leigh “too demure” for the Southern firecracker, was won over by her dedication and declared she “was Scarlett every minute.” His own casting, meanwhile, had been a dream come true from the thousands who flooded MGM and Selznick’s offices with letters demanding he play Rhett Butler

The shoot was famously plagued by drama, and by the time filming wrapped, the cast and crew had earned themselves a party.

But Selznick had bigger fish to fry—namely, finishing the movie in time for its premiere. He left the party planning to local organizers and his trusted story editor Kay Brown, who had convinced her boss to buy the film rights in the first place. But since Atlanta was still tightly in the grip of Jim Crow, not everyone could join in. Leigh’s costars Hattie McDaniel and Butterfly McQueen were excluded from the festivities.

Selznick argued, unsuccessfully, with local organizers to get McDaniel on the guest list. Gable threatened to not attend the premiere in solidarity with his friend, The Hollywood Reporter stated, but McDaniel ultimately talked him out of it. “While it was unfortunate to exclude Mammy, it was the wisest policy,” Brown wrote, per Matthew Bernstein, former professor of film and media studies at Emory University.

Meanwhile, white Atlanta was gearing up for the party of the century. Former president Jimmy Carter later called it the South’s “biggest event” in his lifetime. According to Time, then governor Eurith D. Rivers was prepared to call in the National Guard, while Mayor Hartsfield urged every woman of Atlanta to dust off her hoop skirts and each man to “sprout a goatee, sideburns, and Kentucky colonel whiskers.”

Hollywood came in hot. Leigh and Gable made their grand entrance by limousine, waving to the 300,000 spectators who lined seven miles of streets in downtown Atlanta—including a number of nonagenarian Confederate veterans who were invited as special guests. Leigh arrived with Olivier while Gable brought his new wife, Carole Lombard. Fun fact: Gable reportedly hadn’t even wanted to play Rhett Butler until Louis B. Mayer offered to help bankroll his divorce so he could marry Lombard.

Of the motorcade, Time wrote: “Crowds larger than the combined armies that fought at Atlanta in July 1864 waved Confederate flags, tossed confetti till it seemed to be snowing, gave three different versions of the Rebel yell, whistled, cheered, goggled.” Leigh wept as the crowd welcomed the British actress “back home,” as if she’d just returned from battle.

The multiday event included a costume ball thrown by the Junior League that would have Abraham Lincoln rolling over in his grave. Decked out in hoop skirts, bustles, and Confederate-era uniforms, 6,000 guests descended on the Municipal Auditorium, which had been transformed into a recreation of the film’s Monster Bazaar. Black participants were relegated to serving or entertaining. Among them was the Ebenezer Baptist Church choir, whose singers reportedly performed dressed as enslaved people in front of a façade of Tara, a fictional plantation from the film. One of the singers was 10-year-old future civil rights hero Martin Luther King Jr.

Vivien Leigh arrived in a velvet gown designed by Walter Plunkett. Though Olivier escorted her costar Olivia de Havilland instead—to keep up appearances—Leigh was the belle of the ball, especially in hometown hero Margaret Mitchell’s absence. Eighteen years earlier, the young novelist had performed a risqué “apache dance” at a charity gala, which cost her her Junior League membership. Now, with Hollywood celebrating her masterpiece, some suspected Mitchell was getting the last laugh by snubbing the biggest party in town.

Young women fangirled all over Gable. According to Time, one woman fainted at the sight of him. Another was given a choice: a Christmas gift or a chance to meet the heartthrob. She chose Gable. After he kissed the 11-year-old, she gasped, “Now am I a woman?”

Governor Rivers declared December 15, 1939, a statewide holiday in honor of Gone With the Wind’s world premiere, the grand finale of the three-day extravaganza. Searchlights swept the Southern sky, and flashbulbs popped as 18,000 spectators crowded Peachtree Street. Even Loew’s Grand Theater got a glow-up. Its Romanesque façade was disguised as a grand Southern plantation—four towering white columns were erected to support a temporary façade with floral garlands, potted trees, and an enormous portrait of Leigh and Gable hung above the entrance.

Inside, all 2,000-plus seats sold out at $10 a pop—the equivalent of about $240 today. That’s not exactly chump change, considering Atlanta was still licking its wounds after the Great Depression. Young women in antebellum costumes greeted guests before Mayor Hartsfield took the stage to kick things off. Selznick and the film’s stars received standing ovations that lasted a full minute. But the loudest cheers were for Mitchell, now in attendance, who joined Leigh and Gable onstage.

The lights dimmed for nearly four hours of melodrama. When Rhett Butler delivered his immortal parting shot—“Frankly, my dear, I don’t give a damn”—the audience reportedly gasped, then erupted in applause as he left Scarlett in his dust.

Before Gone With the Wind—still the highest-grossing film of all time when adjusted for inflation—premieres were usually exclusive screenings in Hollywood or New York. 1939’s antebellum extravaganza turned an entire city into a movie set, setting the stage for modern spectacles like Barbie.

Both parties also offer a revealing snapshot of the societies that threw them. For Gone With the Wind, the industry glorified an imagined past; for Barbie, Hollywood toasted an imagined future. The Barbie premiere wasn’t just a love letter to a feminist film: It was also a giant corporate juggernaut designed to sell a product whose history was tangled up with the very stereotypes the film was questioning. It paid off: Following the film’s theatrical release, Mattel reported a third-quarter increase in worldwide gross billings of 27%, and Barbie toy sales in the United States increased 25% for the July-August combined months compared to the same two-month period a year prior. The immersive premiere, brand collaborations, worldwide activations, and fan participation blurred the line between marketing a movie and creating a cultural event.

The film also became Warner Bros.’ most successful release of all time, grossing $1.4 billion worldwide and making Greta Gerwig the first solo female director admitted into the hallowed halls of the billion-dollar boys club. The premiere celebrated a movie that changed the cultural conversation while handily outgrossing the testosterone-fueled Oppenheimer—a spectacular rebuke to the assumption that chick flicks have a glass ceiling.

Its timing made the celebration particularly potent. In that moment, Hollywood was still finding its footing after the pandemic; the premiere offered something audiences were missing. “It eventized going back to the movies. It eventized the premiere,” says Lefkovitch.

For one night, partygoers got to revel in a fairy tale where women ruled the world. But outside Barbie Land, the irony had all the subtlety of Ken’s Mojo Dojo Casa House. Hollywood was throwing an enormous party celebrating a fantasy of female empowerment while women were still fighting for equality in the real world. There is “definitely some hypocrisy,” agrees Lefkovitch, who made a point of thanking his all-female team.

Decades earlier, Atlanta had thrown its own enormous party, celebrating a fantasy of Southern womanhood while reinforcing the racial and social hierarchies of the real South—and conveniently leaving the horrors of slavery out of the picture. (The film itself did just that, presenting a fictional plantation where the most loyal individuals enjoyed being enslaved.) Black performers were welcome to entertain white audiences, but not to celebrate alongside them; not a single seat inside Loew’s Grand Theater was available to Black moviegoers.

Together, these indignities galvanized the local Black press. In a column for Atlanta Daily World, Reverend John Clarence Wright wrote, “As Atlanta goes mad with the Wind one hundred thousand of her citizens should become more thoughtful than ever of the course that must be pursued to gain their rights as free men and citizens in a land where the spirit of secession and slavery still lives.” With Black moviegoers barred from Southern theaters, the Pittsburgh Courier dryly observed, “Negro reaction… will have to wait until the film comes North.”

The premiere and the film’s success helped propel the NAACP to fight not just individual films, but toward a broader campaign to change Hollywood itself. In 1942, NAACP leader Walter White spoke directly to studio heads in an attempt to persuade them to steer away from demeaning Black stereotypes—like the ones onscreen in Gone With the Wind—and to increase the roles available to Black actors in Hollywood.

White’s approach infuriated the very woman whose absence from the Atlanta premiere had helped expose Hollywood’s hypocrisy: Hattie McDaniel. As a Black woman, McDaniel’s choices were limited—“I’d rather play a maid than be one,” she once said. Writing for Vanity Fair, Hadley Hall Meares noted that McDaniel was “incensed” that White had negotiated with the studios on her behalf, and believed she and her fellow Black actors deserved their own seat at the negotiating table.

McDaniel did get a seat at the 1940 Oscars—but it was at a small table at the edge of the room, separated from her Gone With the Wind costars. The ceremony, after all, was held at the Cocoanut Grove nightclub, which had a strict color line. When her name was called, McDaniel gushed: “I sincerely hope I shall always be a credit to my race and to the motion picture industry.”

Meares described McDaniel’s life as “a tightrope walk.” She was expected to satisfy the white executives who controlled her career, the Black audiences who wanted better representation, and her own ambitions as an actress.

Vivien Leigh also made history that night, becoming the first Brit to win the Oscar for best actress. She dedicated her award to Selznick for his “energy, courage, and very great kindness.” After all, he had been partly responsible for convincing an initially outraged public that Leigh was worthy to play their beloved Scarlett O’Hara. Years later, the Academy lavished Barbie with eight nominations, though it snubbed Gerwig for best director and Robbie for best actress. (Frankly, my dear, they didn’t give a damn about the two women who created Barbie Land.) If Hollywood could build an entire universe in which women ruled the world, why was it still so difficult to imagine one outside the movie theater?

Billie Eilish and Finneas O’Connell took home Barbie’s only Oscar, for best original song. Their winning composition poses Barbie’s existential question while echoing the expectations heaped upon Scarlett, Leigh, McDaniel, Robbie, and Gerwig: “What Was I Made For?”

It turns out the inspiration for Barbie—the quintessential American toy—wasn’t even made for little girls. Lilli dolls were sold to grown men in European tobacco shops and adult toy stores, becoming popular gag gifts. “Men… put them on their car dashboard, dangled them from the rearview mirror, or gave them to girlfriends as a suggestive keepsake,” Gerber wrote. Long before Barbie could be anything, she was something for men.

Eighty-four years apart, Hollywood threw two spectacular parties for women who refused to play by the rules. But the rules were still very much in place when the lights came back up.

It’s essential to determine where the LED light strip will be installed before deciding which product to purchase. Some designs are for specific purposes, such as stair lighting or television backlighting. This specialization differentiates those products from standard LED light strips.

Light strips are available in a variety of lengths, typically from 1 to 32 feet, though some strips run up to 50 feet long or more. The brightness and intended purpose usually correlate to their length. For example, LED light strips intended for television backlighting or use on stairs tend to be shorter for easier installation.

Low-brightness LED light strips can be up to 32 feet long with about 300 LED lights. High-brightness LED light strips may have the same number of LED modules, but they’ll usually only be about 16 feet long. Most light strips feature a flexible circuit board that can bend around corners or run over curved surfaces. Some LED light strips are so flexible that they can be bent up to 90 degrees without damaging the circuit board.

However, as the power of an LED light strip increases, the flexibility decreases, with some light strips being completely rigid in design. These inflexible products tend to be much shorter, sometimes only 1 foot long, and significantly brighter than the average light strip. They can also feature motion-sensing technology, making them ideal for lighting stairs.

As with LED light bulbs, the brightness of an LED light strip is measured in lumens and is dictated by the density of the LED lights within 1 meter.

Ideal brightness can be challenging to figure out, and some manufacturers don’t include this information. By taking note of the LED density, you can determine the average brightness of the light strip. Most light strips come in two densities: standard density (SD) of 30 LEDs per meter (9.1 LEDs/foot) or high density (HD) of 60 LEDs per meter (18.3 LEDs/foot).

SD light strips produce an average of 540 lumens and need 27 watts of power. They can be up to 32.8 feet long. HD light strips provide an average of 1,080 lumens, need 40 watts of power, and come in a maximum length of 16.4 feet.

Some light strips come in only one color, while others can have color selections that range in the millions. The color option chosen is entirely dependent on personal preference.

Color temperature in various types of light bulbs is measured in degrees Kelvin (K) and describes how warm or cool a light’s color appears; lower numbers indicate warmer color temperatures. The soft glow of an average incandescent light bulb is a warm temperature of 2,700K, while natural daylight is much cooler, averaging 6,500K.

We interviewed interior decorator Vivianne Chow, the owner of Viv and Tim Home in Chicago, on this subject. She shared some expert advice. “Make sure that the color temperature of the LED light strips matches what you currently have at home. I recommend sticking to 2700K or 3000K for a warm white look instead of 5000K, which is a little bit too cool-toned.”

Wattage measures how much energy the light strip requires to function at its optimal brightness. If a light strip requires more energy than the power source can provide, it won’t work correctly, causing the light produced to be weak. LED light strips generally operate using batteries or power adapters plugged into wall outlets, but some options can be hardwired directly into the home’s electrical system.

LED light strips with lower brightness requirements, such as a 540-lumen strip, will also have reduced wattage requirements. As brightness demands increase, power demands also rise, with a 16.4-foot SD light strip requiring 27 watts of power and a 16.4-foot HD LED light strip requiring 40 watts.

LED light strips that connect with smart-home devices like Amazon Alexa or Google Home can even be controlled using voice commands, while more straightforward options with just one color may only have a remote to turn the light on or off.
