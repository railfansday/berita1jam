Prime Video’s adaptation of Lee Child’s Reacher delighted and awed everyone as soon as Alan Ritchson was cast. Finally, there would be a book-accurate take on the drifting military man fighting on the side of good. Tom Cruise’s divisive interpretation of Jack Reacher was entertaining but far from what book fans had expected. Ritchson proved that he had the stature and the skills to pull off this beloved character. Hints about his rise to the top cropped up earlier in his career in one of the best sci-fi franchises of all time, The Hunger Games.

Ritchson was lethal in the second movie, Catching Fire, as a Victor from a Career district named Gloss. Due to the Quarter Quell that rocks all of Panem, he and his sister, Cashmere, are reaped from the existing pool of tributes, along with Katniss (Jennifer Lawrence) and Peeta (Josh Hutcherson). Though his dialogue is minimal, Ritchson commands the screen in his role as one of the many victims of The Hunger Games. Gloss and his sister are sent back to the Games even though they seemed to beat the system. They are admittedly among some of the tributes who volunteer, but it doesn’t make their stories any less tragic.

The Hunger Games has always been more than a YA romance with a love triangle. The franchise was unfairly lumped in with lesser dystopian series when it was the crowning jewel of the genre. Suzanne Collins’ work set itself apart in a story where the government uses children killing each other for entertainment. The message is even more subtle and harrowing when it comes to the Careers.

When Gloss is reaped, the Games lose all the luster for the Careers. In an interview with Caesar Flickerman (Stanley Tucci), he emphasizes that he isn’t going by choice. This is the sentiment shared by all the tributes, but in some ways, it is even more tragic when it comes to the Careers. The same can be said for Gloss and Cashmere, the brother-sister duo who won their games back-to-back. The Careers are used to create the illusion that there is nothing wrong with Panem. These are people who volunteer to enter a gallant pageant, which is a reminder of what the country has lost. Stacy Keach Played A Villain In One Of The Best Batman Movies Of All Time

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Actor Stacy Keach passed away on November 10, 2026, leaving behind a huge list of film, television, and theatre credits across his lifetime. Typecasting never really caught up with Keach. He could play diametrically different characters, like how he acted as both the morally upright Warden Henry Pope in "Prison Break" and Neo-Nazi leader Cameron Alexander in "American History X."

Even so, Keach's deep voice often coded him as a tough guy you don't want to mess with. DC Comics and Batman fans will recognize that voice from "Batman: Mask of the Phantasm," one of the best Batman movies, animated or otherwise. The titular villain is a skull-masked, scythe-wielding vigilante targeting gangsters in Gotham City. Unlike Batman (Kevin Conroy), the Phantasm employs lethal force.

Keach voices the Phantasm (at least when they're wearing their mask). The Phantasm doesn't say that much, admittedly, because silence serves their menacing presence more. But whenever the Phantasm first appears in a plume of smoke, they announce themselves with this line: "[Name], your angel of death awaits!" Keach makes the Phantasm's voice live up to those words. It's deep and ghostly, with an echo that sounds like he is genuinely an apparition from beyond the mortal plane.

"Mask of the Phantasm" features a dual timeline structure; half of the movie takes place in Bruce Wayne's youth before he becomes Batman. He romances Andrea Beaumont (Dana Delany), the woman he almost chose over becoming Batman. Keach has a second role in this storyline as Carl Beaumont, Andrea's financier father. Naturally, you then make the leap (subconsciously or not) in thinking that Carl is the Phantasm, especially once we learn he was once indebted to the gangsters the Phantasm is killing in the present. That's just what the movie wants you to think.

Okay, enough hiding behind spoilers: The Phantasm is actually Andrea herself. She and her father had to flee the country to dodge his loan shark debtors, which is why she canceled her engagement to Bruce all those years ago. But the gangsters caught up to the Beaumonts, killed Carl, and now Andrea is back for revenge. (Amusingly, the twist of Andrea being the Phantasm was spoiled by some tie-in toys.)

Keach's part as Carl gets back to his acting range. He plays, essentially, the Grim Reaper and a terrified father, and he's great in both parts. The red herring of Carl being the Phantasm becomes text late in the movie, after Andrea and Bruce rekindle their relationship and she explains why she left him — except for the part that her dad is dead. Diegetically, Andrea has given her alter ego her father's voice to throw suspicion off herself. While framing her father might dishonor his memory, she's also playing the part of Carl Beaumont's vengeful spirit to kill his killers.

Batman having a romantic connection with a foe is nothing new — just ask Catwoman or Talia al Ghul. The Phantasm's backstory has the foundation for a redemption, but happy endings are hard to come by in Gotham City. "Mask of the Phantasm" commits to being a romantic tragedy; Batman doesn't reach or redeem Andrea, who chooses her vengeance. The final two scenes of the film show Andrea and then Bruce, both alone again. But at least Batman still has Gotham.

"Mask of the Phantasm" remains widely beloved and is often considered the late Kevin Conroy's greatest performance as Batman. Stacy Keach as the Phantasm was an essential ingredient, too, and may he also rest in peace.

Prime Video’s Lord of the Rings Series Is Officially Correcting an Early Mistake

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Adapting J.R.R. Tolkien is never going to be an easy job, particularly when you're dealing with a story that spans thousands of years, introduces entire civilizations, and features enough characters to populate several television shows. That's the challenge The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power has faced since its debut, with the Prime Video fantasy epic juggling storylines involving Elves, Dwarves, Númenóreans, Harfoots, and the increasingly troublesome Sauron. While the series has certainly never lacked ambition, keeping all those moving parts together hasn't always been straightforward. And now, one of the people responsible for bringing Middle-earth to television has acknowledged that the show may have been asking a little too much of its audience.

Speaking exclusively with Collider's Maggie Lovitt for an episode of our Behind the Scenes series, The Rings of Power co-showrunner Patrick McKay admitted that the first two seasons occasionally tried to juggle too many storylines, revealing that Season 3 has been deliberately designed to bring the narrative into sharper focus. Rather than continuing to expand the show's enormous collection of characters and subplots, McKay and fellow showrunner J.D. Payne have made Sauron's creation of the One Ring the central event around which everything else revolves. He told Collider:

"You would think that would be one of the real challenges of this show, but actually, the challenge is trying to keep it small. J.D., my co-showrunner, and I have imaginations that we don't know how to stop. They are these waterfalls of ideas, and really we're constantly trying to focus them and make sure we're not asking too much of an audience. I think there were times in prior seasons that, just being our own harshest critics, we realized looking back we were juggling too many balls.

We were asking the audience to juggle too many balls. We're so close to it; it makes perfect sense to us. But one of the things this season we really tried to do is focus it. This is the season where Sauron makes the One Ring. Everything should tie into that. And if it doesn't, even if it delights us, maybe that's not the time for that story."

It's a wild admission to make, but it's one that makes a lot of sense, because the One Ring is, well, the One Ring. And it should be the focus of a show called The Rings of Power. The decision to focus the third season around the One Ring also reflects the show's broader approach to adapting Tolkien's Second Age, which contains major historical events separated by enormous stretches of time.

"We looked at the text, we looked at the history of this particular era of the mythology, and there were these series of events that to us felt like they could lead one right into the next, right into the next, and build momentum with an audience the way that a serialized show should," explained McKay. "And then really the work that we do is taking those fixed points, whether it's the forging of the One Ring — that's a big moment this season — or it's Pharazôn bringing a massive Númenórean army to Middle-earth — that's a big moment this season — and there's others. And then it's about, 'How do we make those feel like earned emotional turns in a story rather than fixed historical events, if that makes sense?'" He went on to say:

"We want to bring audiences to it who maybe aren't anticipating those moments or don't know that they're going to come. If you're a Tolkien superfan and a lover of the books and a scholar of the lore, in theory, there's all kinds of things in every episode that you're responding to and picking out that we're dangling there, whether it's an Easter egg or a huge historical moment, like you're saying. But also, for my mother, who's never really gotten Lord of the Rings, it's not her thing, it should play like a novel does, where it's an emotional experience even if it wasn't in Middle-earth."

Under international law, hospitals have a protected status, but they have been repeatedly targeted during the Gaza war.

Israel accuses Hamas, which retains security control of parts of Gaza not occupied by Israeli troops, of cynically exploiting civilian infrastructure. It says the group stores weapons and runs command and control rooms in hospitals, which Hamas denies.

Israel does not allow international news organisations, including the BBC, into Gaza, making it difficult to verify what is happening in the territory.

Chabname Zariab’s Tallinn-debuting debut feature includes images shot in Afghanistan and shines a light on women and a "taboo" topic in a story that is "a personal responsibility and an act of quiet resistance."

Golshifteh Farahani‘s (Alpha, Pirates of the Caribbean: Dead Men Tell No Tales, The Pear Tree) new film, writer-director Chabname Zariab’s debut feature When She Hears the Bells (Les Clochettes de Kaboul), is both a thriller and a call to stand up for human rights.

France-based Iranian actress Farahani stars as Leila, a young widow seeking a fresh start in Kabul, the capital of Afghanistan. But she soon finds out that the kitchen, in which she finds work, is a so-called bacha bazi house. These are establishments in which boys and young men are forced to dress in women’s clothes and dance for entertainment – and for sexual and other exploitation. At the same time – it is 2021 – the Taliban is moving closer and closer to Kabul. Leila tries to flee and also help a group of boys escape before the city falls. Sayed Hashimi (13 Days, 13 Nights) co-stars in the film, which includes images actually shot in Afghanistan. When She Hears the Bells premieres in the First Feature Competition of the 30th edition of the Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF) in Estonia.

Pierre Cottereau handled cinematography, while the editors are Xavier Sirven, Héloïse Pelloquet and Julie Duclaux. Zariab directed When She Hears the Bells, which she co-wrote with her sister Parande Zariab. Writer-director Zariab, an Afghan creative based in Paris, wanted to take on repression across society. And she adds: “The story also highlights the reality for women both before the Taliban’s return and since August 2021, when millions saw their rights erased and the world largely looked away. For me, telling this story is both a personal responsibility and an act of quiet resistance.”

And here is what the writer-director says about When She Hears the Bells.

Where did you shoot most of the film? And I heard you also shot parts in Afghanistan. What can you share about that?We had first considered shooting in Iraqi Kurdistan, whose landscapes and faces strongly resemble Afghanistan’s. We went on location [with] scouts there, then changed course at the last minute because of the bombings between Iran and Israel in 2024. The film was ultimately shot almost entirely in Casablanca, Morocco.Some of the images were indeed shot in Kabul, because I wanted authentic footage of that city. It was essential. But it was out of the question for us to go there. So I called a friend who is a cinematographer in Afghanistan — I’m not giving his name, for his safety — and we worked remotely. I would send him screenshots of my film so that the wide shots would blend well with the images I had shot in Morocco, or I would ask him to go to neighborhoods and roads that I know. These images are mainly wide shots, meant to capture landscapes and neighborhoods, so that the film rings true without exposing the people who live there.

Why did you want to tell a feature film story about bacha bazi in Afghanistan and what does the practice of bacha bazi say about life and society there?I am very sensitive to the cause of children and animals, whom I consider to be the most vulnerable beings in the world. It must be said that bacha bazi is not the subject of the film but the arena in which we follow the journey of an Afghan woman, Leila, played by Golshifteh Farahani, who tries to save children before everything collapses in the Kabul of 2021.Coming back to bacha bazi, it is a practice that has existed in the region for centuries. It has disappeared completely in some countries. It was also on its way to disappearing in Afghanistan. When I was a child, in the 1980s, the practice existed in remote areas but no longer in the big cities. Then came the Taliban’s arrival in 1996 — the moment when my family and I left the country where I was born to settle in France — and it was completely banned, according to many reports, such as one by the NGO The New Humanitarian.

But it surged again with the arrival of American troops, who had been ordered to turn a blind eye to bacha bazi in order to win over Afghan warlords. This scandal was confirmed years later by journalistic investigations, as well as by official Pentagon reports in 2017. Little by little, having “a bacha” — literally, having “a boy” — became a social marker, a sign of wealth and power. Today, according to the Afghan press, such as Amu TV, “the new Taliban” have “adopted” the practice. According to its journalists, the perpetrators are rarely prosecuted.Above all, it reveals the impunity that persists thanks to the influence of those who practice it. Bacha bazi is only one symptom among others of a violent society that separates women from the social landscape, and therefore from desires. Imagine a society in which men and women live separately and are not allowed to cross paths. To me, that is quite frightening.

You made a short film about bacha bazi that was focused on the boys. I love that your feature film also shows us women’s lives and difficulties. How important was it for you to show us this women’s perspective, both before and after the Taliban’s return, and what do you hope audiences will take away from the film?My short film focused on the boys because my concern at the time was what children endure, in my country of origin as elsewhere. A child who grows up in fear or violence carries the marks for life, and it is through childhood that a nation’s destiny is shaped.When the Taliban took Kabul in 2021, I was developing another project, set in Paris but still tied to Afghanistan. I decided to abandon it in order to make a film set in the country. I could not ignore the cause of women, who already held a fragile place in that society and whom the Taliban are trying to erase more and more. Today, women are no longer allowed to work and girls are no longer admitted to school beyond the age of 12. What I hope audiences take away is the distress of an entire society. I believe my country is going through a very deep collective suffering. It was a deliberate choice to show the period before the fall of Kabul from a woman’s point of view, because the period after, the whole world has seen on the news, and still sees today.I also wanted the film to end on that collapse, and on the question of those who leave, because fleeing horror when everything is falling apart is both a positive escape for Leila, the character, and no guarantee of a peaceful life afterward. I’m thinking of Dheepan by Jacques Audiard, which begins on the other side of the fence.

How did you get Golshifteh Farahani to star in the film? She brings so many layers and dimensions to her role.The hardest part was making contact with Golshifteh. It took several months. The first time I saw her was in About Elly by Asghar Farhadi. I held on to her image for a long time. Then I followed her work in Chicken With Plums by Marjane Satrapi and later in The Patience Stone (Syngué Sabour) by Atiq Rahimi, where she played an Afghan woman and spoke Afghan Persian almost perfectly. That is also one of the films that made me feel this character could only be for her.

Her commitment to women’s causes mattered just as much, because it echoed what I wanted to tell. So I waited until she agreed to meet me. Once she learned about the subject, she accepted very quickly. She is an extraordinary actress and an extraordinary woman. She was incredibly supportive on set. Many of my actors were non-professionals or came from the theater. Golshifteh not only mastered her own performance but also guided the other actors, and almost choreographed her movements with the children to help them act and to keep takes from being interrupted.

She is a woman who breaks down barriers, and we were on the same page about that. In the evenings, we sometimes gathered in one another’s apartments to play music and sing. Golshifteh can play many instruments. One of the actresses was also a singer, and we had other musicians in the group. Those evenings were incredible, full of emotion. We were Afghans and Iranians together, united by what we had in common: our culture and our exile. And Golshifteh was also key in fostering that cohesion. It was beautiful!

How did you decide on the title for the film ‘When She Hears the Bells,’ or ‘The Bells of Kabul’ in France?My short film was called Au bruit des clochettes (At the Sound of the Little Bells), and its English version was When You Hear the Bells. The feature film also tells the story of the country. It is a story within history. For the French version, I wanted to put Kabul in the title and keep “the little bells,” which are somewhat enigmatic — for a Westerner, you don’t know what the little bells are. People from the East — Iranians, Afghans, and so on — know exactly what it refers to.

It was interesting, in fact, because during casting, some of the people we contacted refused to audition simply upon learning the title. They knew exactly what it was about: the little bells are bacha bazi. And they refused because the subject is too taboo, too sensitive. For the English version, since Leila ends up in a bacha bazi house, When She Hears the Bells seemed right to me because it puts the female character back at the center. It is her own story, the story of an ordinary woman who becomes a heroine and whose journey becomes extraordinary.

Is there anything you would like to mention?Thank you. This film was born out of a concern. If audiences come away with a better understanding of what Afghan women, and Afghans more broadly, are living through today, and with the feeling that this suffering deserves to be faced head-on, then it will have done its part. Because indeed, today, the world has once again abandoned this country to its fate. There are so many misfortunes and new conflicts that have emerged that people no longer talk about this population. It was important to me to make this film in order to shine a light on them.

For the financing, we received the trust of many partners, who were moved by the subject and convinced of the necessity of this story. Now I hope the film will find an echo and a distribution commensurate with the importance I attach to it.

Right now, winter is beginning, and the country’s economic situation is catastrophic. People struggle to feed themselves and to heat their homes. Children are dying of malnutrition in some regions. There is no work, no real future. There is also a cultural genocide: attempts are being made to erase the Persian language and other languages in favor of Pashto, the language spoken by most of the Taliban.

Very recently I read that in a Hazara neighborhood — the ethnic group to which the character Marie in my film belongs, as one of the two friends at the hair salon — the Taliban gave families 72 hours to leave their homes. Homes that they had bought, and that are now being confiscated from them. This shows that the persecution of the Hazara also continues with complete impunity. Not a day goes by that I don’t follow what is happening there.

I have a deep love for this wounded country and its people. Afghanistan is a country of many ethnic groups, some of which try to crush the others while defending themselves against outside aggression, all in the most brutal poverty. Yolande Zauberman ended her documentary M with a quotation from Franz Kafka: “I am among my own with a knife to protect them, I am among my own with a knife to attack them.” I think I could have borrowed it too.

Rocky’s Best Line Is Still the Greatest Quote in Franchise History

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Sylvester Stallone’s 1976 Oscar-winner Rocky carved itself into Hollywood history thanks to its timeless tale of underdog spirit, and a catalog of memorable moments. Stallone’s script was filled with lines that are quoted to this day – the triumphant cry of “Adrian!," Mickey (Burgess Meredith) declaring, “You’re gonna eat lightnin’ and you’re gonna crap thunder!” or Apollo Creed (Carl Weathers) joking “Apollo Creed versus the Italian Stallion. Sounds like a damn monster movie.” There’s one quote, however, that stands out among the rest, one that sums up everything about Rocky Balboa, and the franchise that tells his story, is all about. It’s not said in the ring, or on top of the famous steps, but in one of the boxer’s most private moments.

The scene in question comes the night before the climactic fight against Creed. Rocky (Stallone) walks around the arena, then back home to his girlfriend Adrian (Talia Shire). Sitting on the edge of the bed, he quietly confesses that he can’t beat Apollo, before adding: “It really don't matter if I lose this fight. It really don't matter if this guy opens my head, either. 'Cause all I wanna do is go the distance. Nobody's ever gone the distance with Creed, and if I can go that distance, you see, and that bell rings, and I'm still standin', I'm gonna know for the first time in my life, see, that I weren't just another bum from the neighborhood.”

At that one moment, The Italian Stallion’s journey comes into perspective. He is a fighter from the street, going up against the heavyweight champion of the world. The odds are against him, and even he can’t see himself knocking out a man who is at the peak of a sport when he had only ever been a manager. However, there’s a way to win even if he loses. That speech becomes the key question of the movie, and every sequel that came after it: can you go the distance?

Anthony Ippolito's uncanny performance and a heartwarming script make this an unmissable film.

Rocky isn’t the story of the preparation for a boxing match. It is the story of personal triumph, of one man who seemed like he didn’t have a future, whose own trainer called his choices “a waste of life,” betting on himself to go toe-to-toe with the best in the world. It’s about becoming more than you thought you could be, pushing the limits of what people expect of you, and shocking the world not by winning or losing, but by going the distance.

It’s a mindset that has prevailed in the six Rocky movies. Rocky II, arriving in 1979, saw the boxer strive to prove the first fight wasn’t a fluke. The third film saw him made complacent by money and fame, having to recapture his underdog spirit when he comes up against a fighter as hungry as he used to be. The fourth film deals with personal grief and revenge, while the fifth sees Rocky turning away from his life’s work in order to be a father and husband. Even his final fight, in 2006’s Rocky Balboa, is less about stepping into the ring than it is proving he still has something to give. Whether on the top of the mountain or haunted by the past, going the distance was always the goal.

It’s easy to assume every Rocky movie ends in glorious victory, but some of the key moments in the franchise are moral victories where someone else’s hand is raised. The first film sees Apollo win on a points victory, with the press and fans surrounding the champion, while Rocky and Adrian embrace each other as if they had won, because the underdog went the distance. Rocky III ends with a victory, but it is defined by a loss, as Rocky underestimates Clubber Lang (Mr T.) and the resulting defeat reignites the spark. Rocky IV sees Apollo die in the ring, a devastating end to a defining relationship, while Rocky Balboa comes full circle as, now in his 60s, he still finds victory in defeat, going all 12 rounds with a much younger champion.

While he stood across formidable men in the ring, Rocky’s most formidable opponent was always himself. Going the distance meant overcoming flaws and demons and becoming who he knew he could be. In the first two Creed movies, where Rocky becomes the trainer of Adonis Creed (Michael B Jordan), the son of his old rival-turned-friend, he imparts that same wisdom onto his protégé. Making Adonis stand in front of a mirror, Rocky points to the reflection and says: “That's the toughest opponent you're ever going to have to face.”

It all comes back to that one moment in Rocky’s small apartment, and a speech that would encapsulate everything that matters about the world’s most famous boxer. Winning and losing are secondary to the personal mountains we have to climb, and the knowledge that we have gone the distance against the odds can be better than any championship.

The 10 Most Hopeless Movies of the 21st Century, Ranked

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There are some movies that leave you feeling uplifted, inspired, or even ready to take on the world. Sadly, these aren't those movies. These are the most hopeless ones of the 21st century that take a much bleaker view of the human experience, whether that be exploring grief, addiction, cruelty, loneliness, or the terrifying possibility that some situations simply can't be fixed.

From the backdrop of a dying planet to stories that focus on ordinary people watching society completely collapse, these movies aren't interested in reassuring happy endings. They're often difficult to sit through, all while attaining their unforgettable emotional impact. So buckle up, because these are some real heavy ones.

Set in a dystopian future where Earth has been rendered uninhabitable by environmental destruction, a group of passengers boards a luxury spaceship for a routine voyage to Mars. Unfortunately, the ship soon collides with space debris, leaving it knocked permanently off course, without a way to correct its trajectory. Now, drifting further into the vastness of space, the passengers are forced to face the terrifying possibility that they may never reach their final destination.

Aniara is a chilling mediation on mortality, denial, and the human need to believe there's something waiting for us at the end of the journey. For one, throughout this forgotten sci-fi flick, the survivors initially cling to distractions, relationships, and artificial comforts—only for those to eventually become increasingly meaningless as the reality of their circumstances set in. And by the time its final moments arrive, the film has made the sheer indifference of the universe feel almost unbearable.

After receiving a prestigious grant, theater director Caden Cotard (Philip Seymour Hoffman) begins an ambitious stage production that recreates an entire city inside a warehouse. But as the project grows increasingly elaborate, the boundary between production and reality begins to disappear, all while Caden becomes consumed by his own existential anxieties and relationships.

Few films capture the exhausting weight of simply being alive quite like Synecdoche, New York. Its surreal structure reflects Caden's inability to make sense of his existence, as time slips away and his grand artistic undertaking becomes an increasingly futile attempt to understand himself, those around him, and mortality itself. Funny in places, with an overwhelming sadness beneath the absurdity, this unsettling portrait of a struggling man is a stellar directorial debut by the visionary Charlie Kaufman.

When hunter Llewelyn Moss (Josh Brolin) stumbles upon the aftermath of a drug deal gone wrong, he makes the fateful decision to take a case filled with millions of dollars. Unfortunately, that puts him in the crosshairs of Anton Chigurh (Javier Bardem), a ruthless hitman who will stop at almost nothing to retrieve the money. But as Sheriff Ed Tom Bell (Tommy Lee Jones) tries to track them both down, he finds himself confronting a level of violence and cruelty that seems increasingly beyond his understanding.

The Coen brothers' adaptation of No Country for Old Men is a masterclass in tension, but its bleakness comes from something more unsettling than its brutal violence. Chigurh feels less like a conventional villain than an unstoppable force of chaos, while Bell's growing disillusionment reflects a world that no longer seems to operate by any moral code he recognizes. There are no easy victories or comforting explanations here, just people trying to make sense of a cruel and indifferent world.

Four films, four completely different ways of handling money, trouble and other people. This is the one you would survive, why it suits you, and what it would cost you to be there.

You have quietly arranged your life so that very little can get to you, and a surprising number of people find that infuriating. You would be completely at home here.

You talk your way through life and it works far more often than it should. Give you a bad situation and an audience and you will find a way out of both at once.

You would survive Fargo because you are the only kind of person who does: patient, decent, and completely unimpressed by anybody trying to be clever.

You do not expect the world to be fair and you stopped being surprised about it a long time ago. That clear-eyed quality is exactly what this film asks for.

With civilization collapsed and most of the world's survivors reduced to desperate scavengers, an unnamed father (Viggo Mortensen) and his young son (Kodi Smit-McPhee) travel in search of warmer weather and a semblance of safety. But their journey is a constant struggle as they scramble for food, avoid hostile strangers, and hold on to the small sense of humanity that separates them from the surrounding horrors.

What makes The Road so crushing is that its bleak world is never just a backdrop for survival thrills. Yes, the father's determination to protect his son is deeply moving, but it also comes with the terrible knowledge that he may not be able to keep him safe forever. Their bond offers the film its only real source of warmth, making every encounter with the cruelty of the outside world feel that much more devastating. It's a story about love enduring in the worst possible circumstances, but one that never pretends love is enough to fix everything.

Béla Tarr's The Turin Horse begins with a mysterious incident involving a famous philosopher, who is said to have broken down after witnessing a horse being beaten in the street. From there, the film follows the horse's owner, Ohlsdorfer (János Derzsi) and his daughter (Erika Bók), as they go about their daily lives on an isolated farm. But when the horse suddenly refuses to work, increasingly strange events suggest that even their small, familiar world is beginning to come apart.

Shot in long, hypnotic takes and told through a deliberately repetitive rhythm, The Turin Horse makes the slow collapse of existence feel almost physical. Meals become a mechanical ritual, the weather grows more hostile, and the characters' options seem to shrink with every passing day. There's no dramatic catastrophe or last-minute revelation to offer relief. Instead, Tarr builds an atmosphere of total exhaustion, as though the world is simply running out of reasons to continue.

After the death of his brother, Lee Chandler (Casey Affleck) is forced to return to his hometown, where he discovers that he has been named guardian of his teenage nephew, Patrick (Lucas Hedges). Unfortunately, the arrangement means putting his solitary life in Boston on hold and confronting a community filled with painful memories.

Manchester by the Sea is heartbreaking because it understands that grief doesn't always lead to healing, closure, or some grand emotional breakthrough. Lee's past has left him carrying a guilt so profound that even the possibility of a fresh start feels impossible, and Affleck's performance makes that pain all the more affecting. Not every wound can be fixed, and the film is simply brave enough to leave that truth unresolved.

Lars von Trier's psychological drama follows two sisters, Justine (Kirsten Dunst) and Claire (Charlotte Gainsbourg), as a mysterious planet approaches Earth. The film is split into two parts: first Justine's lavish wedding reception, which descends into utter chaos, then, the days leading up to the planet's predicted collision, when the sisters react to the impending apocalypse in very different ways.

Rather than building towards a frantic race for survival, von Trier uses the looming catastrophe to explore depression, anxiety, and the strange comfort that can come from knowing there's nothing left to fight against. Dunst gives a remarkable performance, capturing Justine's despair without reducing her to it, while the film's haunting imagery makes its cosmic destruction feel undoubtedly terrifying. Its hopelessness is total, and yet there's an eerie serenity in the way it embraces the inevitable.

Selma Ježková (Björk) is a Czech immigrant working in a rural American factory, who is gradually losing her eyesight due to a hereditary condition. Determined to prevent her young son from suffering the same fate, she works tirelessly to save enough money for an operation—occasionally escaping the hardships of her daily life by retreating into elaborate musical fantasies. Sadly, a devastating betrayal threatens everything she's worked towards.

The contrast between the film's whimsical sequences and its increasingly brutal reality makes Dancer in the Dark almost unbearably sad. Selma's optimism and selflessness are admirable, but they're also exploited by the people around her, so much so that her maternal determination becomes the very thing that destroys her. Björk delivers an extraordinary performance, bringing a fragile, almost childlike sincerity to a character whose kindness is met with astonishing cruelty. And by the time the film reaches its final act, its refusal to offer an easy comfort feels downright merciless.

Oh Dae-su (Choi Min-sik) is a man who's mysteriously imprisoned in a private cell for 15 years without being told why. And when he's suddenly released, he sets out to discover who abducted him and what motivated his captivity—only to find himself drawn into an increasingly elaborate game orchestrated by a strange man.

Oldboy may be famous for its ferocious action, but its true horror lies in the devastating revelations waiting at the end of Dae-su's journey. Park Chan-wook turns a seemingly straightforward revenge story into a vicious examination of guilt, obsession, and the destructive power of secrets—with Choi anchoring this film with a masterful performance. Better still, the final twist doesn't simply shock. It leaves the audience questioning whether survival can even be considered a victory.

Darren Aranofsky's psychological drama follows four people whose lives are gradually consumed by addiction. Harry Goldfarb (Jared Leto) and his girlfriend Marion (Jennifer Connelly) dream of building a better future together, while his friend Tyrone (Marlon Wayans) hopes to escape the poverty and instability of his upbringing. Meanwhile, Harry's mother, Sara (Ellen Burstyn), becomes obsessed with losing weight after receiving the prospect of appearing on television, even as that turns her to prescription pills.

Few films are as relentlessly bleak as Requiem for a Dream, which charts the slow destruction of its characters with an intensity that's difficult to shake. The frantic editing, distorted visuals, and haunting score make their spirals feel almost suffocating, with stellar performances (like Burstyn's) amplifying the devastation. There's no romanticizing addiction here, nor any suggestion that good intentions can protect people from its consequences. By the end, all four are left with utter wreckage, making this one of the most harrowing films of the century.

73 Years Later, John Wayne's Greatest Final Quote From 'Hondo' Still Officially Defines the Western Genre

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

What defines a genre? Is it a set of pre-established or agreed upon conventions? Is it a specific aesthetic or sort of character followed? When it comes to the Western, the genre is recognizable by those who know it best—and sometimes, that includes the characters within it. If there’s a single concluding line spoken by John Wayne in his big-screen horse operas that most perfectly defines the genre, it’s undoubtedly Hondo Lane's final remarks about the Apache in Hondo. In just a few short words, the hero makes it clear what this genre is truly all about.

As the titular hero watches the Apache take off in retreat, hoping to regroup before their next conflict with the U.S. Cavalry, Buffalo Baker (underrated Hollywood talent Ward Bond) notes that this will be the end of the tribe. “Yeah. End of a way of life. Too bad, it's a good way,” Hondo replies before urging the wagons forward. It’s a small moment of pause and reflection that, on the surface, appears to be nothing more than Hondo acknowledging the fading dominion of the Apache. However, upon further examination, we can see that it’s a line that speaks rivers of truth into the genre itself. The Western is largely about a period in American history that bridges the gap between the untamed West and the civilization that followed. Countless Western protagonists, Hondo included, are caught between the gun-toting life they’ve always known and the coming progress that threatens to strip down that identity for the sake of a more "tamed" society.

Many a Western teases the “end of the Wild West.” It's a common theme in the genre that reaches from some of the Duke's earliest pictures to his last. In this regard, perhaps Hondo is no different. The Western hero, unless domesticated, has no place in this new, "civilized" world. This line uttered by the Duke reflects the constant struggle that genre heroes—including folks like Alan Ladd’s Shane, Paul Newman and Robert Redford’s Butch and Sundance, or even Clint Eastwood’s “Man With No Name”—endure with each new interpretation. Hondo may be one of the best examples of the basic horse opera plot—one based on a short story by famed Western author Louis L'Amour—but it concludes with a sober-minded reflection that speaks to the Western genre as a whole, no matter what form it takes. (Consider reading the novelization, by the way, which L'Amour penned himself and can easily be finished in a weekend!)

The Western "way" of living was always best embodied on the big screen by John Wayne. Hondo is just one of many examples of Duke-led horse operas that could easily define the genre. The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance, Stagecoach, The Cowboys, Rio Bravo, True Grit, and, of course, The Searchers are the most obvious films that come to mind. But none of them conclude with these simple words that remind the viewer just what the genre is about — and just like the Wild West era of history came to an end, so too does the Western film continue to remind us that this brief window into American history is a limited period, and the Western heroes are no more. Perhaps this side of Wayne's decades-long career, we can best understand that idea. After all, the Western genre is not at the same height that it was when it dominated film, television, and radio in postwar America.

Perhaps there's something to the fact that the genre's golden age concluded in the mid-1970s, around the same time John Wayne himself rounded out his career. In 1975, Gunsmoke, the long-running television series that Wayne helped launch in 1955, came to an abrupt end the same way the Wild West era did itself. A year later, the Duke's final on-screen role as J.B. Books in The Shootist premiered in theaters. This was a far cry from Hondo, but there's no doubt that, by looking at the past, the 1953 picture was able to predict the Western's future. Of course, the Western itself has never completely died. Its popularity may have waned over the years, but the horse opera continues to captivate viewers today for the same reason it did back when Hondo hit theaters — folks are looking back at a time long past, hoping it will reveal something more about our future.

Huupe Mini 2 Review: More Game Than It Can Handle

If your mom was one of those who always said, “Don’t play ball in the house,” well, she’s going to absolutely hate the Huupe Mini 2.

Huupe manufactures smart basketball hoops and add-on kits for existing setups that track shots, light up the board when a point is registered, and include social features like global challenges and leaderboards. The Huupe Mini 2 is the pint-sized, indoor version of that idea, clipping over the top of a door so you can shoot hoops without ever having to leave the room.

I’ve been testing one out for weeks, and while it’s a fun diversion, some of its features overreach beyond its core functionality, resulting in an experience that bricks as much as it dunks.

The Huupe Mini 2 is, effectively, a smart television with a basketball hoop mounted to it. Or rather, a smart TV that comes with a basketball hoop that you have to mount yourself, courtesy of four screws and a little elbow grease. The LCD screen measures 19 inches diagonally, plus a hefty border that includes stereo speakers and a camera used to track you and your b-ball prowess. (Total size of the backboard is 25 inches diagonally, which fits comfortably within a door frame.)

A 5-inch inflatable rubber basketball is included, along with a small hand pump and needle. Also in the box are a small but otherwise traditional remote control—required to do anything with the Huupe—and a USB-C charger and lengthy cable so you can top up the battery without removing it from the door.

After a quick charge, the unit is ready to set up. I was surprised to discover there’s no app for the device; while larger Huupe hardware works with one, the Mini 2 is completely self-contained and web-based. Just connect the backboard to your Wi-Fi network through a process that you step through with the remote, just like you would on any modern television.

What’s new vs. the original Huupe Mini? According to the company, most of the upgrades are behind the scenes: a faster CPU, a larger battery (rated lifespan is now four hours), better display quality, and “significantly” better shot tracking. New games are also included, which I’ll cover in the sections to come.

Once connected to the web, the Huupe Mini 2 opens up a world of entertainment to you, and, to my great surprise, not all of it is basketball-related. At first blush, the options available are actually quite daunting. It’s all arranged into traditional tiles on the home screen, which you scroll through with the remote.

Just Huupe is the board’s most basic scoring system, tracking shots and recording your shooting percentage in an untimed session. If you want to get competitive, Huupe Live and the Daily Prize Challenge are social games where you challenge players worldwide to score the most points in a given time frame. The winner gets a nominal prize—10 bucks for the daily challenge, a “cozy sweatsuit” for a monthlong challenge. Other games are fairly self-explanatory, like Most 3 Pointers in 90 Seconds and Family Mode (basically Horse).

Many of the games require online friends to play against; if you don’t have pals with their own Huupe Minis, you’ll probably be locked out of these modes—not that they add much beyond their multiplayer features.

Things get a little kookier—and buggier—from there. For example, Huupe Flyers is designed to be played without a ball. It’s a gesture-based version of Flappy Bird, wherein you literally flap your arms around to keep a basketball in the air, tracking your movements via the camera much like an Xbox Kinect. Unfortunately, unlike the ball-based games, I was never able to play it, as it failed the calibration process every time I tried to fire it up.

The system also works as a fairly straightforward streaming platform. Included are apps for YouTube, HBO Max, ESPN, Disney+, Prime Video, Hulu, Spotify, and more. There’s one glaring omission in that list, of course, and I’m not talking about Paramount+ but rather Netflix. Without Netflix support, can you even call yourself a streaming device? It’s also worth noting that the built-in speakers are surprisingly weak. On some apps the audio is OK; on others it’s nearly inaudible.

The Huupe Mini 2 includes not just a sensor that can detect when a basket is successfully made; it also includes a camera to track how far the shooter is from the backboard. This is used to assign point values to various shots (depending on the specific game you’re playing), with more points earned the farther away you are from the hoop.

The good news is that in weeks of playing on the Huupe Mini 2, I never noticed a time when a successful basket was recorded as a miss or vice versa. The distance calculation, however, is another thing. I’ve shot from across the room and had it count as 1 point, while some shots from just a few feet away ring up as 3 points. I could never figure out much rhyme or reason for this variability, except that shots straight-on to the backboard generally registered as lower point values, even if they were distant, while shots from the wings earned more. Of course, navigating all of this is highly dependent on the geometry of your room. Unless you have the Huupe Mini 2 set up in an empty garage, you are probably, like me, going to have plenty of obstacles that get in the way of a lot of your shots—and plenty of crawling under furniture to retrieve wayward balls.

I can say with authority that it is harder to make a basket on the Huupe Mini 2 than on a regulation basketball goal, even at a very close distance. The hoop is small, and the ball tends to rebound wildly off the springy backboard. It’s quite unforgiving, and it didn’t really make me want to spend a lot of time playing, chasing balls that bounced into the corner or rolled under the bed.

The other big problem: When the included inflatable ball hits the Huupe Mini 2, the impact is extremely loud, to the point where I was promptly informed by my wife that it was not appropriate for use when anyone else was in the house. While Huupe says the device is intended solely for use with the included ball, I found a workaround by purchasing a 5-inch foam basketball from Amazon, which makes a bit less noise on impact and also softens the rebound a bit. It has had zero trouble registering baskets.

Battery life is another issue. While four hours is plenty of playing time, the battery continues to drain when the Huupe is in standby mode, which meant I would play a few games in the morning, ostensibly shut the Huupe off, then come back in the afternoon to a completely dead device. The Huupe can run with the power cord attached, but only once the battery has charged to at least about 5 percent.

Unfortunately, even turning the power off is puzzlingly difficult. The power button on my remote control did nothing; I ultimately had to turn the system on and off with the physical button on the bottom of the backboard.

At $429 (on deep discount from a hefty list price of $799), the Huupe Mini 2 sits perhaps just over the edge of what might pass for a quirky holiday gift—and I think it’s safe to say no one is buying this device for themselves. You’d have to be a huge basketball nut to get enough mileage out of the device to make it worthwhile. I expect you would also have to loathe leaving your room. With today’s kids, who knows.

How the American French Film Festival Is Bringing French Cinema to 3,000 Los Angeles High School Students With Packed Screenings of ‘In Waves’

The American French Film Festival, which celebrated its 30th anniversary this year, has been on a journey to cultivate a new generation of French movie lovers for two decades with its educational program for Los Angeles high school students.

This milestone edition brought around 3,000 students to the DGA theater for five packed screenings of “In Waves,” Phuong Mai Nguyen’s heart-wrenching animated feature which was bought Netflix at Cannes. Adapted from AJ Dungo’s graphic novel at set in Southern California, “In Waves” is a love story between a shy teenage boy and the girl who teaches him how to surf and how to live, before she succumbs to cancer.

The screenings, which drew applause and tears throughout, also gave students the opportunity to engage in the post-screening Q&A with the film’s producer, Priscilla Bertin, and screenwriter Fanny Burdino.

Spearheaded by Anouchka Van Riel, the festival deputy director, the program brings together more than 60 schools over five days, with roughly half coming from the public school system and half from private institutions. It also reaches beyond French-language classes to students studying performing arts and other creative disciplines.

In an interview with Variety, Van Riel discussed the importance of the programme to allow younger audiences to discover international cinema at a time when arthouse moviegoers are aging and foreign-language films face growing challenges in U.S. theaters. She also talked about the program’s rapid growth, why subtitles are no longer the barrier they once were, and the powerful response to “In Waves” which Netflix is positioning in the Awards race.

How many students and schools participate in the program?

We have more than 60 schools participating over five days, so that’s more than 12 schools a day. We organize five screenings of the same film, each with around 600 students. That’s approximately 3,000 students every year. I’ve built a database of around 300 schools, and we start reaching out in May. Within a week of sending the first email, half the seats — around 1,500 — are already booked. Then I spend the summer filling the remaining places. There’s enormous demand. We also make a point of having a mix of public and private schools, roughly 50/50. That’s very important to us. For public schools, transportation can be a challenge. Some have to apply for grants just to pay for the buses. There’s an entire economy around organizing these trips, and a lot depends on the teachers.

How does the festival work with teachers to prepare students for the screenings?

We work with the American Association of Teachers of French, which prepares two educational guides, one in French and one in English. The French-language guide is around 100 pages long. It’s incredibly detailed and allows teachers to explore the film from multiple perspectives, including its themes and cinematic language. The guides are designed to help students study the film before or after the screening. And we don’t just work with French-language classes. Around half of the participating classes are English-speaking. Many Los Angeles high schools have performing arts programs, and those students come because they’re interested in cinema and want to meet the filmmakers. The majority of these kids have never attended a film screening followed by a Q&A. That’s something we want to change.

That’s surprising in Los Angeles, where the film industry is everywhere.

It’s extraordinary. I’m a member of the Producers Guild, and I receive invitations to screenings and conversations with filmmakers almost every day. There are incredible events happening constantly, but these kids don’t have access to them. Of course, if their parents work in the industry, that’s different. But otherwise, most students aren’t exposed to that world. I grew up in a small town in Alsace, and I remember my French teacher taking us to the movies all the time when I was in middle school. Some of those screenings even had Q&As. There’s a tradition of that in France that doesn’t really exist here in the same way. We used to organize masterclasses for university students, including at USC, but we realized it was difficult to attract them. They’re already surrounded by so many opportunities on campus. With high school students, we can work directly through the teachers. That’s how we’ve developed this program.

Is the goal ultimately to cultivate a younger audience for French cinema?

Absolutely. It’s about renewing the audience. A couple of years ago, we screened “The Marching Band,” and when the film was eventually released theatrically in the U.S., I was invited to moderate a Q&A. I loved the film, so I was very happy to do it. But the theater was only half full, and I don’t think there was a single person under 65. That’s the problem. Foreign-language films have traditionally been considered niche in the U.S. Streaming platforms have helped change that perception somewhat, but we still need to familiarize younger audiences with these films. That’s why this program is so important.

Are subtitles still an obstacle for younger American audiences?

Not nearly as much as people think. We’ve conducted surveys with students after the screenings, and more than 80% say subtitles aren’t a barrier to watching foreign-language films. It’s not a completely scientific study, but it gives us a good indication of how attitudes are changing. Between streaming platforms, social media and series like “Squid Game,” young people have become accustomed to watching subtitled content. Sometimes there’s still a little hesitation before the screening. But once they’re sitting in the theater, they realize it’s easy. I really don’t think subtitles are the problem for this generation anymore. Distribution is a major part of the problem. That’s why streaming platforms have become so important for the circulation of foreign-language films. But there’s also the question of exposure. If young people aren’t given opportunities to discover these films, they’re not necessarily going to seek them out themselves.

This year’s selection was “In Waves.” What made it particularly suitable for the program?

There’s a wonderful connection between this film and the festival. The producer, Priscilla Bertin, had already come to the festival two years ago with “Wild Diamond,” and at the time she was in post-production on “In Waves.” We became familiar with the project very early, and it made complete sense for us to bring it back this year. What’s interesting is that the film is based on an American graphic novel that was discovered by a French producer in a secondhand bookstore in France and developed into a movie. We explain that background to the students before the screenings, including why it’s considered a French film and the response has been extraordinary.

The screenings were packed, and the students seemed particularly moved by the film. What was the atmosphere like?

It was very striking. At the first screening, I went straight into introducing the filmmakers for the Q&A, and I realized something wasn’t right because the students were still processing what they’d just watched. Usually, when a film ends, the students immediately line up to ask questions. But with “In Waves,” it took time. Every day, there was this moment of silence. The film had affected them so deeply that they needed a little time before they could speak. There’s something cathartic about sitting in a dark theater with other people, crying together and sharing the same emotions. You feel safe because everyone is experiencing that grief together. I think young people have fewer opportunities to experience cinema that way now. That’s what made these screenings so special.

‘Avedon’ Review: Ron Howard’s Thrilling Documentary About Richard Avedon, the Great Photographer Who Turned Head-On Portraits Into an Art Form

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Avedon was a paragon of high fashion and also the ultimate democratic humanist. The film explains that paradox and makes his images sing.

“Avedon,” Ron Howard’s documentary about the legendary photographer, is a fantastic movie. It’s the best documentary Ron Howard has made (my favorite before this: “The Beatles: Eight Days a Week”), and it’s been edited in a way that’s so supple and artful that its technique should be studied in filmmaking classes. (The film’s editor is Andrew Morreale.) Woven through the movie are hundreds of photographs that were shot by Richard Avedon, and there’s no question that the images are stunning, but the way the movie layers them into your consciousness is nearly musical in its finesse. And that helps to tell the very large story “Avedon” is telling: how Richard Avedon changed the metaphysics of photography, shifting the way we see the world. There’s a paradox running down the center of Avedon’s work that feels as epic and tingly as the 20th century, and that is this: He was a paragon of high fashion — the world of glamour and impossible beauty — but he was also, as a maker of images, the ultimate democratic humanist. The beauty of “Avedon” is that it explains that paradox and makes those images sing. Howard interpolates old clips and interviews, placing Avedon, with his sleek command, at the center of the movie. He also talks to models and critics and media and art-world luminaries who have a great deal of import to say. They include Tina Brown, Larry Gagosian, John Lahr, Twiggy, Adam Gopnik, Hilton Als, Calvin Klein, and Avedon’s lovingly insightful son, John Avedon. James Martin, the executive director of the Richard Avedon Foundation, takes us deep into the labyrinth of Avedon’s archives, where we see the work that resulted from the 16,000 sittings he did from 1944 until his death in 2004. That’s a long time, but it still averages out to one sitting per day, Monday through Friday, for 60 years. The movie also tells the tragic story of his mentally ill sister, whom he revered, and traces his troubled marriages.

“Avedon” opens with an old clip of Richard Avedon, fixing that piercing bird-eye look of his into the camera as he explains, “I can say very little about photography. I follow no rules, no laws, and no advice.” He’s totally serious about that. We then get a montage, set to the funky martial beat of Spoon’s “I Turn My Camera On,” of Avedon portraits of the famous. John Lennon. Orson Welles. Michael Jordan. Elizabeth Taylor. Groucho Marx. Twiggy (in mid-air). Anjelica Huston. Marilyn Monroe lovingly embracing Arthur Miller. Truman Capote. J. Robert Oppenheimer.

All the images look timeless. (Avedon’s lighting makes it look as if each one was taken yesterday.) Every subject looks like what we imagine them looking like, and also different from how you’ve ever seen them. More direct and unfiltered, more intimately themselves. That’s the Avedon touch, but the magic element is that we can’t see how he achieved it. The technique is invisible. There’s a part of one that thinks, “Okay, just set up a camera and put some black-and-white film in it. Place a famous person in front of it. Encourage them to relax and ‘be themselves.’ And voilà! I could be Richard Avedon.”

He probably would have said you’re not wrong.

He wasn’t very interested in the technique of photography. Talk to him about f-stops and he would glaze over. His technique was in the way he approached a session. Iin the film, Isabella Rossellini says that other photographers tend to shoot and shoot and shoot, but Avedon would shoot an image every so often, then tell you to think of something, then shoot again, and end up with a total of maybe 10 shots. He himself explains that if you’ve booked someone for an hour, you don’t have to shoot for an hour. He says that he wastes half an hour having coffee, and then, maybe five minutes before the hour is over, he and the subject will go over to the white paper background, and the magic happens.

The white paper! It has its source in a remarkable story. Avedon was born in New York in 1923, and when he was a kid he says that walking down a street he would blink for a moment and keep his eyes closed, to keep what he saw alive in his head. It was his way of taking pictures before he’d even gotten a camera. He entered the U.S. Merchant Marines when he was 19, securing a place in the photographic department, where he took maybe 100,000 portraits of enlisted men and other workers, all to use for ID cards.

This was the photographer’s equivalent of peeling potatoes on KP duty. But Avedon began to notice something. Shooting these military mug shots against a white background, he encountered “thousands of baffled faces looking into the camera,” and he saw how the photographs revealed these people. That’s one of the startling things about police mug shots. (John Waters: “Everybody looks better under arrest.”) We see a handful of the ID photos that Avedon took, and there it is: the Avedon style. He saw that each face was different, and needed nothing else to be different. So he took what fate handed him — a thankless military assignment — and turned it into a vision. The white background equalized everything. It became his way of making each individual stand out as an individual, with literally nothing to get in the way.

He became a head-on existential classicist, one who echoed Diane Arbus’s eye for highlighting the aspect of a person that was somehow off. The famous Avedon shot of the model Lauren Hutton with her head thrown back and her breast popping out looks as perfectly composed as a Picasso sculpture. But it was totally spontaneous; it just happened. And that’s true of all the famous and iconic gazes Avedon’s portrait subjects gave him. Those looks weren’t planned (how could they have been?).

At the tail-end of a session in 1953, Charlie Chaplin pranked the camera by holding devil horns over his grinning face, and that became one of the most famous images Avedon ever shot. Images like that happened…and then became frozen in the hive mind’s eye. But as “Avedon” explains, part of what he was always recording was the ineffable dance between subject and artist, using each subject to express something about himself. (Chaplin wouldn’t have made those devil horns for anyone else.)

By the time of the 1957 Hollywood musical “Funny Face,” which paired Audrey Hepburn with Fred Astaire as a magazine photographer pointedly based on Avedon, he was already becoming a legend, and he began to use his power as part of his method as an artist. In his 1957 shoot with Marilyn Monroe, pegged to “The Prince and the Showgirl,” the trick was: How could you photograph Marilyn — or, rather, how could you photograph her the way Avedon wanted to photograph her — when she had come prepared, more than anyone else alive, to be photographed? The shoot took an entire day, and it’s full of joyous photographs of Marilyn in full splendor, but the iconic shot that emerged from it was taken at the end of the day, when she let down her guard for 10 seconds, and kind of drooped, and looked a bit sad, and we glimpse, in that photograph, the real Marilyn — not necessarily the forlorn Marilyn, but the full woman behind the image. You see Norma Jean grown up.

“Avedon” is full of great stories and privileged glimpses into how Avedon created some of his most famous images. And, of course, he also photographed the unfamous, like the Oklahoma oil workers he shot in 1980. He used his impish nature to coax fashion models into leaping through the air for his models-in-motion photographs. He waited around for seven weeks in Vietnam to photograph the war’s American generals, and devised singular ways to shoot the Chicago 7 and the denizens of the Warhol Factory and the residents of the new American West, in a series that was so raw and authentic no museum in New York wanted anything to do with it. (He was that far ahead of the curve,) We also hear the story of how he photographed his aging father and used that process to reconcile with him. Avedon supported his own ritzy lifestyle by doing advertising work (including the Obsession perfume TV spots that were like surrealist mini-films), but for him it was never a compromise. And we see how that could be the case. There is, over 60 years, a breathtaking continuity between his portrait work and his fashion work and his commercial work that makes it all inseparable. That’s because every photograph Richard Avedon ever took was striving for the same thing: to open your eyes.

The premise of James D. Parriott's 1985 superhero/comedy series "Misfits of Science," which featured a young Courteney Cox, will sound very familiar to fans of Marvel Comics' X-Men titles.

The series follows a human scientist named Dr. Billy Hayes (Dean Paul Martin), who has taken an interest in what he called "human anomalies," which are people who have been born with a variety of unpredictable superpowers. In the show's pilot episode, he assembles the main cast of powerful castoffs with the intention of studying them, but also helping them come to terms with their supernatural abilities.

The team was small but mighty. It included Dr. Hayes' lab partner, Dr. Lincoln (Kevin Peter Hall), who can shrink for a limited period. Additionally, Mark Thomas Miller plays "Johnny B" Bukowski, a rocker who can suck up electricity and who wears shades to cover his glowing eyes. He can throw lightning bolts.

Most strikingly, Courteney Cox, future star of "Friends" and "Scream," plays Gloria, a troubled teen with "Carrie"-like telekinetic powers. "Misfits of Science" wasn't Cox's first credit: She had appeared in an episode of "As the World Turns" back in 1975, when she was still a kid, and famously danced with Bruce Springsteen in the "Dancing in the Dark" music video in 1984. "Misfits of Science," though, was the first time Cox starred in a TV series.

"Misfits of Science" is not currently available to stream, but resourceful media hunters will be able to find episodes online easily enough. It's a whimsical, lighthearted show that prodded at the conventions of superheroes as much as it vaunted them. It's definitely worth a glance.

"Misfits of Science," it should be noted, only lasted for a single 16-episode season, so it's okay if you don't remember it.

The series does have something of a superhero legacy, as R. Timothy Kring, a.k.a. Tim Kring, wrote an episode of "Misfits of Science," and he would go on to create the hit series "Heroes," the first season of which remains a must-watch (the later seasons, though, less so). Kring's "Misfits" episode, called "Twin Engines," is about a young man (Joel Polis) who is plagued with uncontrollable visions. It's eventually revealed that he was sharing the visions of his long-lost twin brother.

Indeed, the entire show was based on a long series of super-powered shenanigans and investigations. Mostly, the protagonists get involved in run-of-the-mill spy plots. The main characters aren't costumed vigilantes, but they do seem to operate as freelance spies. Famed drummer Mikey Jones appears in the show's pilot as a character with freezing powers named Ice Man. According to Giant Freakin' Robot, that character was abandoned after Marvel Comics "raised objections" over its own Ice Man character. And speaking of Marvel, Courteney Cox's character was very similar to the X-Men's Jean Gray.

Some fun trivia: Shortly after the time "Misfits of Science" was being made, Dr. Lincoln actor Kevin Peter Hall (who played the original Predator in John McTiernan's sci-fi action classic) was in contention to play either Data or Geordi La Forge on "Star Trek: The Next Generation." /Film has previously talked about all the finalists for the roles, and Hall eventually lost the roles to Brent Spiner and LeVar Burton, respectively.

As mentioned, "Misfits of Science" was not successful. Thanks to low ratings, the series was canceled before all of its episodes could even air, leaving one of them unaired in the United States.

Courteney Cox continued to work undeterred, of course. In 1987, she appeared in the low-budget, high-profile Cannon adaptation of "Masters of the Universe," a movie so goofy, it has been subjected to a "How Did This Get Made" oral history. That same year, she began playing the recurring role of Lauren Miller on the hit sitcom "Family Ties." By 1994, she was appearing in the comedy "Ace Ventura: Pet Detective" and began playing one of the six lead roles on "Friends." "Misfits of Science" was her first starring role, but it certainly wasn't her last.

The 10 Best Survival Movies of All Time, Ranked

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Technically, a whole bunch of movies are about survival, but a line was drawn here to look at the ones really focused on surviving. Basically, no horror or action movies where people are obviously fighting to stay alive the whole time, because if you included anything with life-or-death stakes, then you'd probably find yourself with more “survival movies” on your hands than non-survival movies.

The ones below are mostly adventure/drama films, with some creeping into other territories, but as long as characters are isolated and battling the elements (more so than specific disasters) in one way or another (and maybe some other threats, too, at least on the side), then they're worth considering as survival movies. And they're high-quality, too, and definitely worthy of being considered among the best survival movies ever made.

Rescue Dawn takes place during the Vietnam War, so maybe it’s more of a war movie early on, but the focus is definitely on survival and less about the usual stuff you'd find in more straightforward war movies. It’s based on the real-life experiences of a pilot named Dieter Dengler, and what happened when his plane got shot down over Laos, with him having to battle the elements and coming into contact with forces who make him a prisoner of war, too.

There’s time spent on surviving time in a POW camp on top of having to survive (and trek through) jungle terrain, so Rescue Dawn is certainly grueling, but by design. It’s one of the better non-documentaries Werner Herzog has directed, though he did also cover the same story in a documentary, and that film’s pretty great as well (it was made about a decade earlier and was called Little Dieter Needs to Fly).

This is one you sort of have to mention, because it’s probably one of the first things most people think about when they hear the term “survival movie,” but yes, it’s Cast Away. There’s a desert island, there’s just the one guy stranded on it, and he has to stay alive on that island for an extended period of time. It’s all very stripped back in terms of the premise, but there are things beyond it being just literally a guy and an island for nearly 2.5 hours.

Still, the bulk of the movie is that. And a little under 2.5 hours is long, but Cast Away stays interesting for most of that runtime. It’s one of the good Robert Zemeckis films, which is always nice to see (when he’s on, he’s on, but he definitely isn't always on, y’know?), and it’s also got one of many strong Tom Hanks performances at its center, so that dynamic director/actor duo ensures the movie’s pretty good for the most part.

Cast Away but it’s Mars instead of an island: that’s The Martian. Kind of. Someone is alone for a good chunk of the movie, and they have to figure out ways to survive their predicament while also needing to think about how they might actually get out of the situation they're in, with help from anyone else being limited, at least at first.

The Martian works well as a crowd-pleaser, not exactly doing a ton to rewrite the survival sub-genre, but hitting all the beats in a more-than-solid manner.

It’s also one of a fair few movies that features a character played by Matt Damon trying to get home in one way or another, so that’s possibly worthy of note. And The Martian works well as a crowd-pleaser, not exactly doing a ton to rewrite the survival sub-genre, but hitting all the beats in a more-than-solid manner and, overall, ending up being a pretty difficult-to-dislike sort of film.

If you're picking up a theme here, with stories about one person battling the elements or otherwise being in a stressful situation while alone, then congrats. But also not really congrats, because it’s obvious. That’s a lot of survival movies. And that is also All Is Lost, though maybe this one goes a step further because the only cast member listed is Robert Redford.

The closest thing to a second character here is the boat he’s on throughout the film, and even then, he’s not a particularly well-developed character, not even having a confirmed name or anything. Nothing else is really relevant, though. There’s been an accident, the boat is sinking, and he doesn’t want to drown. That’s enough for All Is Lost, because it’s genuinely quite gripping throughout, and it’s one of the best of the late-era Robert Redford movies, too (he does a lot considering he was nearing 80 at the time of this film’s release, though there is one other survival movie he starred in decades earlier that’s also worth mentioning… more on that one in a bit).

What do you know: Buried is about a guy who gets buried. No clickbait with this title, that’s for sure. Ryan Reynolds is that guy, and he gives a surprisingly good performance here in a movie pretty much devoid of anything funny or otherwise light-hearted, since he’s stuck in a coffin the whole time, well and truly, you know, buried, and he has a limited amount of time to try and contact someone who can then work out where he is.

He’s really just equipped with a phone and a lighter, so he’s calling people, stressing out, and exploring any option he can for the 90 minutes of oxygen he has (approximately), and that is also the length of the movie. So, Buried plays out basically in real-time, and it’s remarkably intense for all of those minutes, successfully stretching out that very memorable coffin scene from Kill Bill: Vol. 2 into a feature-length film.

This is a tricky one to talk about, because Deliverance is a deeply unpleasant movie, but it takes a little while to get to that territory, and then also, discussing that stuff feels like it could be a bit much on top of it possibly involving saying too much, in terms of the narrative and all that. But then again, it’s more than half a century old at this point, so you probably know at least a little about this movie and why it’s notorious, even if you’ve not actually seen it.

If you don’t mind the idea of having a bad time with a good movie, it’s likely worth watching. Deliverance does make you feel the desperation of its main characters uncomfortably well, and there’s undeniable skill involved here with it all building the way it does. And a movie being this old and still feeling shocking in many ways is quite impressive when you think about it, too.

Just over 40 years before All Is Lost, Robert Redford also starred in Jeremiah Johnson, which is a different sort of adventure/survival movie, sure, but both are great, in any event. Jeremiah Johnson definitely isn't as minimalist, since there are a few other characters here, and the main character isn't as restricted, not being stuck out at sea and instead having to survive in and around the Rocky Mountains.

It’s also set back in the mid-1800s, very much being a Western, but one focused on survival, and then there is some additional conflict in the movie involving some other people in the area the titular character lives. Still, not a lot of story. It’s not minimalist compared to All Is Lost, but placed against most other more “conventional” movies, it’s very light on story, but not in a bad way. It’s interesting to watch something that’s kind of a hang-out movie, but lots of it is just the one guy, rather than a film where you get to see, like, a group of characters hang out and everything.

Take the setting and the chilly Western feel of Jeremiah Johnson and add in the grit/violence of Deliverance, and you'd find yourself with something that might be honestly kind of close to The Revenant. Okay, not perfectly, but those itches scratched by those films get scratched here, and also, both those movies from the early 1970s have revenge play some kind of role in their respective stories.

The Revenant is as much a revenge movie as it is one about survival, with one man seemingly staying alive against all odds so he can brutally murder the man who wronged him. That’s a simple way to break down such a long film, yet that is pretty much what The Revenant boils down to. It tells that straightforward story well, though, all the while doing some interesting things thematically and feeling hard to fault in terms of its technical aspects and also regarding many of the performances it features.

Of all the notably uplifting movies out there, 127 Hours could well be the most disturbing, but it has to be disturbing and grisly before it can get uplifting. It feels right, in other words, and while “that” scene that everyone knows might be nauseating to watch without context, if you see it in the movie, at the point at which it happens… well, sure, it’s still nauseating, but it’s also undeniably triumphant.

This one came out the same year as Buried, and it’s similarly claustrophobic, all the while also being much more visually unpleasant and then, somehow not paradoxically, also a whole lot less depressing. 127 Hours is about as good as the more mundane survival movies out there get, because it’s easy to imagine yourself in this situation, get some kind of understanding about the pain involved, yet also then find some inspiration in how far a real-life person was willing to go to survive.

Gravity is not based on a real-life story, but it certainly feels real while you're watching it, and it’s also grounded in enough ways to make it a bit difficult to classify as a science fiction movie. It’s about survival in space, but the disaster, in effect, isn't too outlandish, and then it also doesn’t happen very far into space, by any means, with Earth always very much in view, but still undeniably difficult to get back to in one piece.

Also, Gravity is very heavy on spectacle for many of its scenes, though, things are also personal and character-focused, to some extent, with enough time to spend on the characters even with a short runtime because there are so few characters. Some people find parts of Gravity silly and maybe a little overly sentimental, too, but it is so spectacular and laser-focused on survival throughout that it feels worthy of being ranked as high as it is here. If you can get on board with what this movie’s doing both technically and emotionally, it really is one of the most thrilling and involving films out there.

DC has a surprisingly rich history of replacing Bruce Wayne as Batman. Famously, Terry McGinnis took up the cowl in Batman Beyond, proving pretty resoundingly that Gotham’s dark future could thrive under the shadow of a new Dark Knight, and he’s far from the only one.

The main DC comic continuity also has seen plenty of familiar faces pull on Wayne’s cowl, too: Dick Grayson, Tim Drake, and Damian Wayne have all taken a turn wrestling with the weight of the bat symbol. Even Jim Gordon and Alfred have temporarily become Gotham’s masked protector, while Jace Fox followed Terry McGinnis as a legitimate heir before shifting his operation to New York City. In other words, the supposed sanctity of Bruce Wayne as Batman has been interrupted a number of times.

The most notorious example, though, came in the wake of Bruce Wayne’s most famous defeat at the muscular hands of Bane in DC’s mid-'90s Knightfall arc. DC has long discussed the impact of being Batman on Bruce, and how heavy the cape really is, but it comes into focus most prominently when he steps aside, and Knightfall showed that cost better than any story.

Following the brutal climax of Batman: Knightfall - Part 1, the upcoming release of Batman: Knightfall - Part 2: Knightquest will officially hand the cowl to Bruce Wayne’s most infamous replacement. And while passing the torch often feels like a triumphant rite of passage, Knightfall is actually a terrifying warning about what happens when the wrong man tries to play god.

The ending of Knightfall: Part 1 delivered one of the most brutal cliffhangers in DC history as Bane systematically tore Batman apart and then snapped his spine. Played with dark relish by Michael Mando, the hulking DC villain was the real deal, and that climax was the perfect way to shatter the illusion of Batman’s invincibility. He did it to prove his superiority, as well as finally overcoming his fear of bats.

That same moment has been shown on screen before, of course, as Christopher Nolan partially adapted Knightfall into The Dark Knight Rises to send Christian Bale’s Batman spiraling to his lowest point of the trilogy. Tom Hardy plays a very different Bane, of course, and the twist in The Dark Knight Rise’s ending changes him further, but the image of the giant brute snapping Batman over his knee was still iconic.

Interestingly, Nolan’s final Batman movie actually used Batman’s vulnerability as the way in to tell a very different story than Knightfall. For Nolan, Bruce passing the torch (to Joseph Gordon-Levitt’s Blake) was important to underline the idea that Batman as a symbol was more important than Bruce Wayne’s destructive commitment to his duties. Nolan chose to give his Batman a happy ending by freeing him after Bane’s victory over him, but in Knightfall, the terrifying inverse of Nolan's philosophy is true.

The original comic storyline - and 2026’s excellent and faithful R-rated animated adaptation - confirms instead that Batman is not an off-the-shelf symbol that anyone can simply step into. The cowl requires restraint, empathy, and psychological discipline, and Bruce Wayne is very much presented as the only viable option to keep Gotham safe. Including from Batman.

When Bruce is paralyzed and forced off the streets, you might naturally expect that Dick Grayson would step up as his natural heir. But Bruce deliberately overlooked him, partly because DC wanted to protect the successful separation of Nightwing, but also for narrative reasons. They were somewhat estranged for a start, but Bruce also saw an opportunity to further redeem Jean-Paul Valley, the former assassin, by giving him the Batman mantle.

Big mistake. Batman’s broken legacy being added to the volatile background of Azrael was easily one of Bruce’s most idiotic moves. He was a brainwashed child killer, conditioned to kill by the System, and Batman’s strict moral code and no-kill rule simply doesn’t fit with how he’s built. He modifies the Batsuit into one of the coolest versions ever, thanks to DC’s desire to parody dark 1990s extreme antihero tropes, and he effectively becomes a monstrous, heavily weaponized mirror of Batman, instead of Valley changing himself.

As Knightfall Part 2 will show, Valley’s extreme measures and aggressive style did deliver results, and he’s able to stand up to Bane in his new suit, after initially suffering a defeat that drives him to make the improvements. But it all comes at a cost, and in contrast to Nolan’s message in The Dark Knight Rises, it becomes clear that Bruce Wayne brings something to being Batman that stops the hero becoming Gotham’s nightmare.

THR Visionary: 5 Janusz Kaminski Shots

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The cinematographer, this year’s THR Visionary Award honoree, always lets film follow function, choosing a visual style to match the story being told.

Try to find a signature Janusz Kaminski “look” and you’ll come up empty. Yes, he likes backlighting and has a penchant for smoke and film-noir style. But the Polish-born cameraman, winner of the 2026 THR Visionary Award for excellence in cinematography, has spent three decades making images that bend to the story rather than the other way around.

Kaminski’s guiding principle is that form follows function. With lenses, film stocks, color palettes and camera movement chosen to serve what each film needs to tell its story. The result is a body of work remarkable for its originality and visual diversity. Here are five of Kaminski’s most original visions. Steven Spielberg’s Holocaust drama was shot in stark black-and-white. The choice wasn’t nostalgic but a way of conjuring newsreels and documentary record, with a handheld immediacy that keeps the audience in the room with the horror. Kaminski’s hard, contrasty lighting, harsh shadows and drifting smoke owe a debt to German Expressionism and postwar European cinema. Against this monochrome, the lone splash of color, the little girl’s red coat, lands as one of the most devastating images in modern film. It won him his first Oscar.

For the Omaha Beach landing and the war that follows, Kaminski set out to make combat look like frontline footage rather than a Hollywood spectacle. Colors are leached out to a washed, metallic palette. He stripped protective coatings from his lenses to invite flare and a rawer, less polished image, and used a narrow shutter angle that makes explosions and bullet strikes snap with a jittery, staccato clarity. Handheld cameras and spattered lenses put the viewer on the sand with the soldiers. The look was so influential that it reshaped how war was filmed for a generation, and it earned him a second Oscar.

In Julian Schnabel’s adaptation of Jean-Dominique Bauby’s memoir, written by a man paralyzed but for one eyelid, the camera becomes that eye. Kaminski opens the film from Bauby’s first-person perspective: blurred focus, tilted frames, sudden blinks and a claustrophobic narrowness of vision that gradually widens. As Bauby’s imagination takes flight, so does the imagery. Soft, luminous pastels and sunlit memories of Paris and the seaside offer escape from the hospital room. It’s an exercise in subjective cinematography that earned Kaminski an Oscar nomination.

If Saving Private Ryan rejected glossy spectacle, War Horse embraced it on purpose. Spielberg and Kaminski drew on the sweeping, painterly look of classic Hollywood Westerns and epics, with John Ford and David Lean in mind. Anamorphic widescreen frames showcase burnished golds, glowing skies and silhouetted figures against vast landscapes, giving the First World War tale a storybook romanticism before the mud and horror close in. It’s lush, deliberately old-fashioned and entirely suited to a story told through the eyes of a horse. It was another Oscar-nominated turn.

For Spielberg’s tense thriller about the aftermath of the 1972 Olympics massacre, Kaminski looked to the gritty, paranoid cinema of the 1970s. The film has a grainy, slightly faded texture with muted, earthy colors, and frequently uses zoom lenses, a hallmark of the era’s thrillers, to create a sense of surveillance and unease. The images feel period-authentic without becoming pastiche, and their rough-edged restraint keeps the focus on the moral toll of the mission.

What has become clear in the ten years since Rogue One is that people don't actually want "gritty, realistic, and adult" stories, they want killing, one-liners and gratuitous demonstrations of strength for its own sake. They saw the form of Vader's hallway scene and loved it, while ignoring all the meaning and substance behind it.

Again, that scene belongs in Rogue One, and it fits with Vader's overall character, but, even through that lens, it's still misunderstood. People see that and think Vader is a badass, but he really isn't; he's just super strong and easily manipulated.
