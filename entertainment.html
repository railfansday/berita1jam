If you’ve already taken a lap through our gift guide and still need more ideas, we’ve got you. All three of us editors here at Climbing have selected our top picks for gifts this year, from gear we’ve got our eyes on, to accessories in the climbing zeitgeist right now.

Some of these editor gifts picks make perfect mini gifts for stocking stuffers or for that climber partner who you want to thank for giving you the perfect soft catch this past year. Other picks make a bigger impact, from a super lightweight harness to a great gym bag. Most of our picks are great for a variety of climbers, whether it’s your new boyfriend who you hope to convert into a climber, or your mentor who’s been at it for eons.

We hope these gift ideas make your holiday shopping as painless as a splitter hand crack so you can spend more time training or climbing, and less time scrolling through endless product pages and catalogs.

Originally designed and hand-sewn by Nate Barton—the Missoula-based climber who ran the meme account @beta.sprayer until 2024—the Lolo Hoodie V1 ($169.99) is a functional midlayer for cold-weather climbing and the flagship product of the fledgling brand Lolo Hardware. Its large, zippered chest pocket was originally intended to help ice climbers warm up their hands between pitches.

Having pretty much lived in this hoodie for the past three winters, I’ve found the chest pocket far superior to any sort of pant pocket for storing my phone, gloves, and snacks. Plus, with my items against my chest, I never have to worry about my phone freezing or my car keys accidentally slipping out during a chimney. From Joshua Tree in January to Canyonlands in November, this is a comfy and durable hoodie for the coldest days and nights of climbing.

It’s 2026, folks: If you’re still using mined chalk instead of seawater-sourced chalk, this is your call to finally make the switch. Climbing’s feature on the hidden environmental impact of chalk showed how the vast majority of magnesium carbonate comes from mines in China, where magnesite factories poison the water and soil of nearby residents.

For climbers with pierced ears, a pair of carabiner earrings is a customizable accessory that never gets old. Recently, I’ve been getting tons of compliments on my pair of silver, locker draw earrings (yes, they actually screw and unscrew!) that I bought at the Yosemite Climbing Museum in July. Those are only available at the museum, so if you’re not currently near Mariposa, California, here are a few other great places to buy carabiner earrings online:

After breaking a cross-loaded carabiner in a scary trad fall, I now always add some sort of tape or rubber band to keep cam carabiners in place. I wrote about my DIY solution a couple of years ago, but now I simply slide on Avant Climbing’s Flip-Stop Cam Carabiner Stabilizers ($22.50 for a 10-pack). The Flip-Stop makes for easy cam clipping when mega pumped. But unlike my rubber band hack, it also keeps the cam sling open for clipping a long draw into. Crucially, like a quickdraw’s dogbone, the Flip-Stop prevents carabiners from rotating onto the dangerously weak X-axis. Nothing ruins the holidays like a pair of broken ankles.

The new Arc’teryx Alpha SL Harness ($180) may weigh less than the gift wrap paper you’ll hide it in. At an absurdly light 78 grams (2.7oz), the Alpha SL is for those going ultralight in the mountains, whether that’s an alpine rock romp or a big ski traverse. This harness is also surprisingly comfortable to rappel in, thanks to its uber-stiff Aluula-composite construction. And it fits a range of body types thanks to its adjustable leg loops and five waistbelt sizes. Just don’t set it down unattended on a glacier—it’ll blow away in the slightest gust of wind.

The Patagonia Airshed Pro Pullover ($169) might have been built for mountain running, but I wear mine year-round on all sorts of objectives, from sunny rock climbs to cold north faces. The Airshed Pro is a pseudo-windlayer: decently breathable for high-output activities, yet stills blocks the wind and has a water-repellent coating. The result is a shirt equally at home skiing on a glacier or rambling up a gusty rock ridge.

A note on sizing: Since the Airshed Pro was originally designed for runners, the cut is quite slim. I size up to facilitate a wider range of motion.

I have been loving the Black Diamond Solution Hoody ($299) as a light fall belay jacket and even for cold rock climbing when I’m too chilly to shed my jacket. The Solution manages to be super lightweight, movement-friendly, and breathable, yet warm. This is largely because the specific PrimaLoft insulation used in this jacket is made with technology developed by NASA for astronauts. Here on earth, it also works great. It honestly does feel like being wrapped in warm air.

The pocket real estate is also generous, and it’s nice that the whole thing packs into the left hand pocket so you can easily stash it in your pack without a cam or crampon tearing into the insulation. I’m also looking forward to using this as a mid-layer below a shell for ice climbing this winter.

Unless you’ve been living under a rock (which many of our readers hopefully have), you’ve heard about the new film about the weed plane that crashed into a Yosemite lake in 1976. What followed was a tragicomic climber gold rush. Now, to mark the 50th anniversary of the incident, you can sport one of several new t-shirts or hats ($29.95-$44.95) memorializing this infamous moment in Yosemite climbing history. All proceeds support the Yosemite Climbing Association and the Yosemite Climbing Museum.

Have I used one? No. Do I desperately want one? Yes. As a 5’2” climber, I’m often just a few inches short of being able to make the clip from a good stance. I’ve heard wonderful things about the stiffy Kong Panic Draw ($54.95-$59.95) that lets you clip bolts otherwise out of reach. Maybe 2027 will be the year I finally gift myself one! If there’s a short or easily panicked sport climber in your life, I bet they would be stoked to get this as a present. Or you could make one for them DIY-style yourself.

The oversized, durable Metolius Climbers Tote ($64.95) has become my go-to gym bag. It easily fits my personal gear plus a 60m rope, or four sets of personal gear (shoes, harness, and chalkbag for me, my husband, and my two kids). Despite having stored weathered climbing shoes zippered inside for too long, this bag miraculously doesn’t stink yet either. It stows away nicely in gym cubbies (whereas my previous Cotopaxi tote would sort of spill out) and is easy to carry by hand or shoulder. This is a great mid-sized gift for the climber in your life, or you could fill it with other gear you’re gifting for a bigger impact.

Note on price: While this tote isn’t on sale at REI, co-op members get 20% off one full-price item through Nov. 30, so this is a good one to save on.

I receive a lot of climbing books and galleys to review and consider for coverage. Only some of them are so good that I can’t put them down. That’s how I felt about Erratica, a new collection of true stories from Moab-based, life-long climber Brian Laidlaw. Rare is the read that both keeps you attentive and entertained, yet also evokes deeper reflection. With Erratica, Brian manages to do just that. Pick up a copy ($20) at Moab’s Back of Beyond bookstore, or find one online anywhere books are sold.

Indian Actress and Director Nandita Das, Syrian Star Taim Hassan to Be Honored by Doha Film Festival

Indian actress, director and social advocate Nandita Das and Syrian star Taim Hassan are set to be honored by the Doha Film Festival with Creative Excellence Awards during the event’s opening ceremony on Thursday.

Das has acted in more than 40 feature films, starting with Deepa Mehta’s “Fire,” which depicts a relationship between two women in an Indian household. She’s subsequently worked with top Indian directors such as Mrinal Sen, Shyam Benegal, Adoor Gopalakrishnan, Rituporno Ghosh and Mani Ratnam. Her directorial debut “Firaaq” — about the aftermath of the 2002 sectarian carnage in Gujarat, where 3,000 Muslims were killed — played at Toronto in 2008 and went on to travel widely and score many awards. She’s been a member of the main jury at Cannes Film Festival twice, in 2005 and 2013. Hassan made his breakthrough playing young ruler Fatimid Caliph Al-Adid in Syrian director Hatem Ali’s historical epics “Al-Zir Salem” (2000) and “Saladin” (2001). He has led all five seasons of hit Arabic TV show “Al Hayba,” about a family of arms smugglers working the border between Syria and Lebanon, a role that has made him a household name across the region and reached international audiences on Netflix. Hassan’s more recent work includes the show “Taj” (2024), in which he plays a boxer resisting the French occupation of Syria; hit political drama “Under the Seventh Ground” (2025); and thriller series “Mawlana” (2026).

As previously announced, Yemeni-Scottish filmmaker Sara Ishaq’s timely drama “The Station” — which looks at the impact of Yemen’s civil war on the country — is set to open the revamped event’s second edition, which will run Nov. 19-27 in the Qatari capital.

First reactions for the gritty dark comedy have officially arrived ahead of its limited theatrical release.

Critic Zach Pope shared his initial thoughts after watching The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth for the first time and stated that the movie "is a PURE cinematic trifecta of Tarantino’s signature chaos, Fincher’s meticulous attention to detail, and Brad Pitt’s effortlessly charismatic Cliff Booth." Pope continued and shared that:

"All wrapped into a stylish love letter to 1970s Hollywood cinema that reminds us sometimes all you need is a laid back, wildly entertaining misadventure with a character you just LOVE spending time with. That just might turn into insanity.

But Elizabeth Debicki STEALS the entire movie. What a performance."

So many things I want to talk about but don't want to spoil anything."

“We had been talking about going out there to sea, sit on real boats and shoot on Imax,” cinematographer Hoyte Van Hoytema explains.

A large amount of the film was shot on the open ocean, where Nolan, his cast and crew spent four months capturing how hard the journey would have been as they endured crashing waves. And while the climactic moment ended up being shot in Los Angeles, Nolan didn’t spare any expense in pulling off the sequence, relying instead on old-fashioned filmmaking.Speaking with Variety for Inside the Frame, Van Hoytema explains the scene provided a challenge: “How to replicate something and make it feel visceral and real? Van Hoytema’s chief lighting technician and gaffer Adam Chambers built light boxes that would emulate thunder and lightning. I was thinking, if you can see everything around you, that’s going to look fake.”

A CGI storm didn’t feel right because the entire crew had been meticulous about keeping everything as grounded and as experiential as possible. Van Hoytema says the team built overhead lighting rigs that were approximately 60×30 feet, hanging on cranes above the ship. “It meant that you would see flashes that progressively move from behind them to the front of them, as it opens up the sky. “What you see is short bursts and there’s just darkness or a little bit of a glow. It’s like trying to see this relentless endless sea, this weather, this saltwater and they’re just so close to death. It’s extremely claustrophobic at the same time.”

When it came to framing Odysseus, Van Hoytema was wired in to keep him in place on the gimbal. His Imax camera on his shoulder was also wired in.

6 Superhero Movies From the 2000s That Are 10/10 Masterpieces

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The 2000s were huge years for superhero cinema—arguably the biggest for the landscape of all time. It was in this decade that superhero movies, as audiences know them today, were defined, and the standards were set. The years 2000 to 2009 are some of the most significant years and helped birth some of the most popular franchises in cinematic history as a whole.

Sam Raimi's Spider-Man series, the X-Men franchise, the Marvel Cinematic Universe, Christopher Nolan's The Dark Knight series and so much more all got their big breaks in this era. Without these abundantly important 10 years, superhero movies would not even be close to where they are today. Their significance wouldn't even be close to what they are, and events like Avengers: Endgame most likely would not have ever happened or had the success they did without these foundational projects that are, to simply state, masterpieces.

Spider-Man is the most profitable superhero on the planet—he has been for quite some time now—and that meant that no one was surprised that, when the technology was finally there, he'd get a live-action movie. Come 2002, fans were finally treated to Sam Raimi's Spider-Man, which seriously rocked the world. Sure, X-Men came out just a few years earlier, but Spider-Man really helped drive home and fully prove that comic book characters could work on the big screen and the Hollywood landscape.

This 2002 flick was an all-time smash hit and proved that superhero movies could be genuinely amazing financial investments for big studios, with the project grossing an astounding about $825 million worldwide, with a phenomenal $114.8 million opening weekend. It was the first film to ever cross $100 million in its opening weekend and if that's not proof of how great this movie is, then what could be? The performances across the board from especially the likes of Willem Dafoe are wonderful, the directing from Raimi shows how unashamed he was in the source material, and the CGI work was revolutionary for the times.

The first Blade movie was a big moment for comic book movies, being one of the first ones to ever be rated-R and be a success. So, when it came time for the sequel, Blade II, the stakes were high. Thankfully, legendary director Guillermo del Toro and leading man Wesley Snipes, delivered a project that was a great success. It turned the franchise from what the franchise was in Blade to a high-speed, thrilling, gothic, and all-around fire flick.

Guillermo del Toro continues to be a masterclass of a director here with his emphasis on practical effects, making the more horror-based elements of the project sell better than they ever could in CGI. Snipes brings his everything to the role—as per usual—and keeps Blade feeling like a character that the audience genuinely wants to keep watching.

The first X-Men movie was incredibly important. It established these amazing characters and began to send a great message about the effects of discrimination and bigotry. Mutants became the talk of the town, and Hugh Jackman stepped into the boots of Wolverine for the very first time. However, it's highly agreed upon that the sequel, X2: X-Men United, is the superior film for a plethora of reasons.

This is, without a doubt, the Star Wars: Episode V — The Empire Strikes Back for the X-Men franchise. The stakes are raised significantly, and the themes and messaging are more prominent than ever before. It has one of the best opening scenes in a superhero movie—Nightcrawler (Alan Cumming) infiltrating the White House to attack the President (Cotter Smith), brings more classic X-Men characters to the forefront, has a great villain in William Stryker (Brian Cox), and so much more.

2008 was arguably the biggest year in superhero cinema for quite some time because of two major movies: The Dark Knight and none other than the beginning of the MCU itself, Iron Man. Brought to life by the amazing Jon Favreau in the director's chair and Robert Downey Jr. as the Armored Avenger, this 2008 project birthed one of the most popular and profitable cinematic franchises ever put to screen—one that still runs in massive success to this very day.

This served not only as a major comeback for Downey Jr.'s career, but was a huge step in CGI for filmmaking, as well. Iron Man looks perfect in pretty much every shot, and is still looked back on to have better-looking CGI than some modern Marvel films. The biggest standout, however, is the performance from Downey Jr. He solidifies himself at Tony Stark and continued to play him for over 10 years after the release of Iron Man, now even returning to the MCU again in 2026—albeit as a new character, Doctor Doom. This movie is the majority reason the superhero movie landscape has become what it is today.

Tim Burton’s 1989 film Batman, of course, revolutionized the character of Batman and comic book movies as a whole. Before then, the general public’s awareness of Batman was mostly associated with the campy, comedic Adam West Batman, but Burton changed that and presented a dark, heavily stylized version starring Michael Keaton. He then followed it up with Batman Returns, which many still consider to be one of the best Batman films ever.

Warner Bros., however, felt it was too dark and got rid of Burton for the third film, replacing him with Joel Schumacher. Keaton followed Burton out the door, not wanting to continue as Batman without him. Batman Forever, starring Val Kilmer, was the result, and while that film has its defenders, it felt like a different franchise and a different Batman altogether. The same can be said for Batman and Robin.

Then in 2005 came Batman Begins, directed by Christopher Nolan and starring Christian Bale. Taking a far more grounded approach to the character, Batman Begins turned Batman’s origin into an epic film saga, where it had only been relegated to brief flashbacks in previous films. Nolan topped himself with The Dark Knight by putting Batman in the center of a gritty crime drama with Heath Ledger’s Oscar-winning turn as the Joker. For a second time, a Batman film revolutionized comic book films. Nearly 20 years later, many still cite The Dark Knight as the greatest comic book movie ever made.

Unfortunately, Nolan’s third Batman film wasn’t nearly as good as the first two. Perhaps because of Ledger’s untimely death before the release of The Dark Knight, Nolan at first seemed disinterested in a third film, and it is generally understood that he had to rework the story he had in mind to remove the Joker from its narrative. Regardless of the reasons why, The Dark Knight Rises is a deeply uneven, disorganized film that most view as the low point of Nolan’s Batman, if not an outright bad film (I tend to think it is quite bad).

After Nolan’s trilogy concluded, Ben Affleck became the next Batman, in both Batman v. Superman: Dawn of Justice as well as the various versions of Justice League. Regardless of what you think of these films — I personally find them insufferable — they were deeply divisive. Despite an Affleck-led Batman film being rumored for a time, we never got him in any standalone films, much less three of them.

In 2022, The Batman offered new hope. While some were skeptical of Pattinson as Batman, he proved to be a really solid take on the Dark Knight, which prioritized Batman’s skills as a detective, something missing from previous Batman films. He was complemented by Paul Dano’s frightening take as the Riddler and Colin Farrell’s scene-stealing Penguin.

Reeves also presented the best-ever take on Gotham City. While Burton’s Gotham was bold and interesting, neither of his films made Gotham feel like a real city. Nolan’s Gotham, with its unconcealed usage of Chicago and New York City, had little personality at all. But Reeves’ Gotham felt like a broken city that desperately needed a Batman to come to its rescue, with production combining parts of Chicago and Scotland to give the city a more naturalistic, gothic vibe. The film’s nearly three-hour runtime only assisted in the viewer’s immersion into Reeves’ Gotham.

It’s just too bad that his take on the character will be cut short before we get a proper trilogy.

Syfy’s 5-Season Zombie Series Breaks All The Storytelling Rules

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Even the most diehard fans of zombie stories will struggle to deny that the genre can get repetitive. Across zombie TV shows, movies, books, games, and graphic novels, survivors tend to face the same exhausting routine: find safety and shelter, watch it eventually fall to the undead, then hit the road to find another refuge and begin the cycle anew. The specifics may change from story to story, but the genre still over relies on this tried-and-tested narrative loop.

From George A. Romero’s Night of the Living Dead in 1968 to modern hits like The Walking Dead, zombie entertainment has repeatedly built suspense around supposedly secure shelters becoming death traps. It's an effective setup, but one that can soon feel stale to experienced genre fans. That’s why one Syfy series should be celebrated for finding so many new ways to keep its undead apocalypse moving. Z Nation, the five-season saga that made zombies fun again, throws storytelling tradition out the window and gives its survivors much bigger problems than finding their next temporary sanctuary.

Following survivors escorting Murphy (Keith Allan), a potential source of a zombie cure, across America, Z Nation turns saving humanity from the undead into a spectacularly messy road trip. The cult classic series offers a refreshing alternative to the likes of The Walking Dead and The Last of Us, especially for viewers exhausted by the relentless misery that tends to define the genre’s small-screen entries. While its campy performances and B-movie sensibilities might initially discourage viewers more used to prestige TV, beneath the cheap thrills sits one of the most subversive zombie shows ever made.

Initially, Z Nation looks ready to follow The Walking Dead’s familiar blueprint. After an opening involving Murphy receiving an experimental vaccine before getting bitten by zombies overrunning a military facility, the Syfy show drops viewers into a familiar-feeling survivor camp in upstate New York that feels plucked out of literally any zombie TV show. Murphy’s arrival and the reveal of his apparent immunity creates a central mystery from the same trope The Last of Us is built on, and the immediate setting suggest a The Walking Dead-like lengthy struggle to protect a precarious home. That’s when Z Nation pulls out the rug from under smug viewers schooled in zombie stories.

The camp falls almost immediately, and its survivors inherit a military mission to transport humanity’s potential salvation to California. This kicks off a cross-country zombie TV show built around an action-filled road trip rather than finding another home isn’t the main objective. The central characters in Z Nation have somewhere to go and a reason to get there. They’re never simply escaping whatever destroyed their previous shelter. Obstacles like the need to find food, water, and ammo in an apocalyptic world defined by scarcity still remain. However, these take a backseat to the roller coaster plot and the larger-than-life communities and dangerous survivors Murphy and his escort encounter on the way to the West coast.

That structure gives Z Nation room to keep changing, and it makes full use of the narrative wiggle room. The original delivery mission eventually gives way to competing visions of humanity’s future, including efforts to build a society accommodating intelligent undead. Survival remains at the core, just like any great zombie TV series, but the scope means that survival in this instance comes to encompass humanity as a whole. What’s more, thanks to the show’s willingness to embrace wild plot twists and shocking narrative developments, even the equally familiar setup of a savior figure’s journey across an apocalyptic wasteland never feels predictable.

Unlike many of its contemporaries across its 2014-2018 run, as well as many zombie series that followed, Z Nation never feels trapped in the narrative loop of waiting for a fragile period of safety to suddenly collapse. Its forward momentum is relentlessly fast-paced and the trajectory of its narrative arcs are impossible to predict. Every episode can bring a different kind of story and setting, making it the perfect zombie TV show for anyone sick of the formulaicness that often plagues the genre.

Breaking the usual survival narrative cycle most zombie TV shows follow would already make Z Nation refreshing for experienced fans of the genre. However, its storytelling freedom extends well beyond ditching the familiar search-for-safety loop. The cross-country road trip setup would promise incredible variety even in a grounded post-apocalyptic series, but Z Nation is perhaps as far from grounded as it’s possible to be. To say that Z Nation isn’t afraid to get wild is an understatement.

This is a series where a tornado full of zombies is treated as a perfectly reasonable plot development. Elsewhere, a giant cheese wheel becomes a downhill zombie disposal system, and botanical researchers produce a strain of undead-fertilized cannabis whose plant is connected to a plant-zombie hybrid. There’s a zombie tsunami, a giant rolling ball of animated corpses, and even the birth of a zombie baby that parodies Biblical Nativity scenes (complete with undead wise men following a star). These aren’t isolated jokes in an otherwise gritty survival thriller, either. The absurdity is part of Z Nation’s central appeal.

Even Murphy’s immunity refuses to remain a straightforward cure storyline. As Z Nation continues, his changing condition leads to developing the power to control other zombies, blue skin, and a host of other strange abilities. Humanity’s supposed salvation even eventually starts pursuing the creation of his own zombie-hybrid society. The familiar immune-survivor premise becomes a story about a deeply unreliable man deciding whether saving the species is worth it, and if he can really still consider himself human at all.

It doesn’t have grounded horror or the restrained, brooding atmosphere associated with many post-apocalyptic prestige TV shows, but that’s part of Z Nation’s charm. While the effects can be scrappy, and plausibility is something it lets other shows worry about, the Syfy series is still inventive, ambitious, and surprisingly coherent. What’s more, for all its absurdity, it’s not silly. The show is more than capable of taking itself seriously when it needs to. For viewers bored with watching another settlement slowly disintegrate, few zombie shows offer a better antidote than Z Nation.

Can you talk a bit about the themes of totalitarianism and beyond that The Flaw explores?

For me, the totalitarian regimes and everything is just the first level. I wanted the film to act as a strong allegory. You have the regimes, the governments, or society, that is all around us. But I actually think that each one of us is a regime by himself.

We perceive the world through our own eyes and our own perspective, and through our own prejudice. So, by definition, we are a regime by ourselves. Imagine a hunter walking in the woods, and all of a sudden, he sees a deer. So he lifts up his rifle and looks through the scope. At that moment, he sees prey. We meet somebody, and we judge them right off the bat – their sexual orientation, [how they look], how political or non-political they are. During COVID, for example, there were two groups of people: the anti-vaccine and the vaccine people, but nothing in the middle. But for me, that’s the interesting thing: people meet in the middle.

What we were aiming for was a slow change that the characters go through. We wanted to be so slow that you realize that the characters you’ve been seeing from the beginning, all of a sudden, at the end, are completely different characters.

We shot in Greece, and we also shot in Riga in Latvia. Riga was a very important part of the whole process in order to get that feeling of brutalism and all this dark, timeless sensation.

But then we had to combine Greece with Riga, and that was a challenge, so we could get a timeless space and locations where you don’t know they were actually shot in two different countries. The production design team did an amazing job. And lighting and framing were very important for us. I believe that when we shoot, we make conscious choices that actually affect people’s subconscious.

So every shot is there for a reason. Every close-up is there for a reason. We choose what we want to show or what we don’t want to show the audience.

It was amazing to work with all these guys. They are amazing professionals. I actually sent paintings, portraits, to the casting director. My process with the actors once they know the scenes is this. When they ask me what I think about a scene and the characters, I always say to them, “I don’t have anything to say to you. You know the script. You know the scenes. You know your character. Just present your approach.” You need to give actors space so they can breathe on their own.

Richard Sterban, Oak Ridge Boys’ Bass Singer, Dies at 83

Richard Sterban, the Oak Ridge Boys’ bass singer who memorably lent his vocals to their beloved “Elvira” cover, has died. He was 83.

Sterban died Nov. 14 in Hendersonville, Tennessee, due to complications from bronchitis and pneumonia after fighting a battle with cancer, his family and the Oak Ridge Boys announced.

“We appreciate all of your love, support, and prayers. Please continue to lift us up in this difficult time, we find comfort knowing that he is currently face to face with Jesus,” Sterban’s family said in a statement. Born April 24, 1943, in Camden, New Jersey, Sterban began singing at six in Sunday school as a soprano. He sang tenor in his seventh-grade glee club, and upon his eighth grade, his voice dropped to bass.

He studied music at Trenton State College (modernly known as the College of New Jersey), and began singing in a gospel quartet where he met Joe Bonsall, his future Oak Ridge Boys bandmate.

While singing part-time, Sterban joined J.D. Sumner and the Stamps, whom Elvis Presley hired to sing behind him. He then sang and recorded with the King of Rock and Roll, and also appeared in the 1972 concert film Elvis on Tour.

Sterban was then offered the opportunity to join the Oak Ridge Boys after the departure of Noel Fox, which he accepted in 1972. Bonsall joined the group one year later, with Duane Allen, William Lee Golden and Sterban already in the group, which carried on as the faces of the band for five decades.

They performed with Johnny Cash, who helped them sign with Columbia Records in 1973, though it was after opening for Roy Clark in 1975 when they met his manger, Jim Halsey, who would later sign with the group.

They reached success with “Y’all Come Back Saloon,” which reached No. 3 on the country chart in 1977, and led to a string of hits. “I’ll Be True to You” became their first No. 1 song, though 1981’s “Elvira” would become one of their largest successes.

Originally recorded by Dallas Frazier, the Oak Ridge Boys’ version of the track went to No. 1 on the country chart and No. 5 on the pop chart, sold more than two million copies and won a Grammy. Sterban’s bass refrain was a major touchpoint on the song.

The Oak Ridge Boys went on to also become members of the Grand Ole Opry, the Gospel Music Hall of Fame and the Country Music Hall of Fame.

Sterban released his memoir From Elvis to Elvira: My Life on Stage, written with Steven Robinson, in 2012.

Sterban is survived by his wife, Donna; his daughters, Lauren (Tyler) and Tori (Michael); his sons, Rich (Chele), Doug, and Chris; his grandchildren, Mali (Zachary), Nikolas, Tyler (Ryann), Madelyn, Alexandra, Rex, Owen, and Charli Grace; and his great-grandchildren, Salem, Asher, and Theo. He was preceded in death by his mother Victoria Sterban, brother Joseph Sterban and grandson Zachary.

Mikkelsen's razor-sharp cheekbones, intense stare, and European accent instantly code him as "villain" in Hollywood language. When "Rogue One: A Star Wars Story" cast him as Galen Erso, a sympathetic scientist conscripted into building the Death Star, it read as casting against type to American audiences. But look at Mikkelsen's Danish resume and you'll find he's a much more diverse actor than the box that Hollywood has stuck him inside. Amusingly, one of those movies — Thomas Vinterberg's "The Hunt" — stars Mikkelsen as a good man mistakenly persecuted as a villain.

Lucas (Mikkelsen) is a Danish kindergarten teacher. One of his students, his best friend Theo's (Thomas Bo Larsen) daughter Klara (Annika Wedderkopp), has a puppy love crush on him. Obviously, he gently turns her down. Klara then vengefully suggests to the school principal that Lucas exposed himself to or molested her. (In reality, as the movie shows, her crude older brother shoved an iPad displaying porn in her face.) The ugly rumor grows out of control, and Lucas' reputation pays the price.

In "The Hunt," the town rushes to the worst possible conclusion about Lucas. All but a few loved ones like his son, Marcus (Lasse Fogelstrøm), believe the worst from hearsay. But they don't actually know it for a fact (indeed, what ultimately helps clear Lucas is the facts pointing in his favor). The movie is never ambiguous, though; we know from the very beginning that Lucas is innocent and this is all just a terrible tragedy of miscommunication. "The Hunt" also avoids reactionary narratives about scorned women peddling false rape allegations because Klara is a young child. She simply doesn't grasp the consequences of what she did, as is the case with the other preschoolers who echo her claims.

Compare a similar recent film, "Fjord," inspired by a real-life story about a Norwegian evangelical couple whose children were taken from them after suspicions of abuse. Like in "The Hunt," the allegations are implied to stem from leading questions and miscommunication from the kids. But "Fjord" counterintuitively stacks the deck in the parents' favor while trying to present objectivity, perhaps because it knows many left-leaning audiences are inclined to side against the conservative couple and takes perverse enjoyment in testing those sympathies.

A film like "The Hunt" could choose not to reveal Lucas' innocence either way or cast doubt, inviting you to sympathize with a possible monster as an endurance test. But the reality of "The Hunt," seeing an innocent man be branded with a scarlet letter, is also agonizing to watch in its own way. It's a modern iteration on the parable of the feathers in the wind, and how once spoken, even untruthful gossip can't be erased.

As Lucas, Mikkelsen sheds any of the power or menace he projects as a villain in those aforementioned Hollywood movies. "The Hunt" is also a Danish-language film, and Mikkelsen's comfort with his native tongue also makes for an even more seamless performance.

Mads Mikkelsen is an extraordinary actor, and yet the greatest proof of that is him playing a normal man in "The Hunt."

10 Classic Rock Albums To Listen to Before Kicking the Bucket

​​​​​​Joining the choir invisible. Shuffling off this mortal coil. Ceasing to be. Biting the dust. Wearing wooden pajamas. Vacating the earthly meat prison. No matter how it's said, it all means the same thing: death. Death is the great equalizer, the one thing that is inevitable regardless of one's race, religion, income or social status. Every day brings us closer to hopping the twig.

But hey, you're still on this side of the grass, right? Unless you're a well-read zombie, but that seems unlikely (but not impossible... discuss). So you have a choice to make: sit idly by, paralyzed by the thought you may have had your last Slurpee; or make the most of the time you have left. And if — hopefully — you've selected the latter, before kicking the bucket you've got things you want — nay, need — to do, see, and hear. Thankfully, your friends here at Collider have compiled a list that speaks to the latter: 10 rock albums you need to listen to before going belly up, ones that are not only awesome but offer guidance as you prepare for the inevitable. We're dying, literally, to share those with you now.

There comes a point in everyone's life where it's time to drop the charades and get real. That means raw, emotional honesty with others and yourself, and there is no better album that wears emotional honesty on every track like Fleetwood Mac's 1977 classic Rumours. The story behind the album is legendary at this point, recorded as the interpersonal drama among the group was akin to a soap opera, with separations, outright spite and heartbreak, making the atmosphere in the studio, shall we say, tense. A lesser band would have buried that under upbeat pop/rock songs, recording in separate sessions if needed. Not Fleetwood Mac: they owned that energy and put it all on vinyl, creating a timeless masterpiece that is relatable, real, and, in the case of "Don't Stop," even hopeful.

Getting real with ourselves potentially opens up a proverbial Pandora's Box of questions about life, death, time, money, or even one's mental health. While it may not provide any answers to these questions, Pink Floyd, with their iconic album Dark Side of the Moon, sure as all hell wax philosophical about those same themes. Like Rumours, the album has an emotional honesty that resonates only with the fluid cohesion that comes from being a concept album. As a result, Dark Side of the Moon is less a record than it is an experience, one that should be made before going up to the "Great Gig in the Sky."

Speaking of experiences, seeing KISS in concert is one that is an absolute must. Or at least was, given the band gave their last performance at Madison Square Garden on December 2nd, 2023. So if that's on your bucket list, you now only have two options: wait for the KISS virtual avatars to hit town, or listen to their 1975 album Alive! Opt for the latter. Their breakthrough album, consisting of cuts from their first three albums (KISS, Hotter Than Hell and Dressed to Kill), achieves something that other live albums have mostly failed to accomplish: the feeling of being there. Alive! captures the bombastic, take-no-prisoners energy of a KISS live show and puts it on vinyl. Twice, that is, with that raw power requiring containment on a double album. Good luck replicating that, avatars.

The "when" of kicking the bucket is assured. The "how," on the other hand, is another story. There are millions of ways one could go, from horribly tragic to an eternally looping end as a Darwin Award winner. You could, as alluded to in the aforementioned Rumours, even "Go Your Own Way." And if that's the case, as Van Halen cheerfully suggests on their 1984 album, you "might as well jump" (but in all seriousness, please don't — help is always available). The last album with long-time frontman David Lee Roth (until 2012's A Different Kind of Truth) is also, arguably, their best, with "Panama," "Hot for Teacher," "Drop Dead Legs" and "Jump" the standouts. Really, though, the album is so good it doesn't really matter where you "jump" in.

It's ironically fitting that the last album the Beatles recorded before their demise, one that includes "The End," would show up on a list like this, no? As Collider explains, Abbey Road is arguably the greatest record of the 20th century. It's high praise, and even if you may disagree — you shouldn't, it's pretty damn good — then it still stands tall amongst the crowd. Noted as much for its iconic album cover as its tracks, Abbey Road has contributions from all four of the Beatles, with George Harrison and Ringo Starr each providing their strongest entries ("Something" and "Here Comes the Sun" for Harrison, "Octopus's Garden" for Starr). Even the much-maligned "Maxwell's Silver Hammer" is far better than its reputation. The album has a blend of genres that work as a whole, serving as a snapshot of the band at the apex of their creativity.

The Beatles fell apart after recording Abbey Road, while Australian rockers AC/DC could have done the same after the death of original frontman Bon Scott, and even toyed with the idea of disbanding. Instead, they stayed together, with Scott's father encouraging them to do so. They recruited Brian Johnson and set out to record what would be the definitive AC/DC album: Back in Black. It was created as a tribute to Scott, with the all-black cover a sign of mourning, while simultaneously launching a new era for the band. That new era came with a commitment to elemental, arena-ready guitar riffs and straight-forward rock, with a frontman who brought his own energy instead of being a Scott-lite. To say it worked out pretty well is an understatement: Back in Black hit the top of the UK album chart, became the best-selling album that never reached number one in the US charts, and is currently the second best-selling album of all time in music history. There is legitimately not a single track that sucks, from opener "Hells Bells" (which, hopefully, aren't what you hear on the other side) to "Rock and Roll Ain't Noise Pollution."

These classic rock albums are masterpieces from beginning to end.

Another album that showcases the band's mastery over a wide range of genres is Led Zeppelin's Led Zeppelin IV. "Black Dog" and "Rock and Roll" are straight bangers, "Going to California" has an acoustic folk vibe, while "When the Levee Breaks" takes the original blues song by Kansas Joe McCoy and Memphis Minnie from 1929 and reinterprets it as a slow-groove, blues-rock masterpiece. Every member of the band is at their best, which comes through the album's most prominent track: "Stairway to Heaven." The delicate acoustic softness of its opening melds seamlessly into its hard-rock crescendo and a fiery solo from Jimmy Page that arguably stands as his finest, with Robert Plant owning the track with his uncanny vocal range. And hey, when you do say goodbye to this world, it's nice to know you don't need to waste your time finding the elevator to Heaven.

And if you make that trek to the top of the stairway, you'll find "Paradise City," which is also a track from one of rock's all-time greatest debut albums, Guns N' Roses' Appetite for Destruction. Neither the album or the band entered the music world quietly, but rather with a raw, explosive danger. They took the makeup, tights and hairspray that marked the hair-metal era and made it irrelevant overnight, ditching the sexualized, party-time dynamic in favor of a gritty, punk realism. The album balances radio-friendly(ish) tracks like "Sweet Child O' Mine" and "Welcome to the Jungle" with darker cuts like "Mr. Brownstone," making it an album of all killer, no filler. Why waste the time you have left listening to crap?

Knowing that this life is fleeting, you may want to get familiar with the fella that'll be hosting you for eternity after you've bought the farm: God. To that end, you'll want to throw on Stryper's game-changing 1986 album To Hell With the Devil. With it, the holy-roller band did the unimaginable by making Christian rock music actually good. Like, MTV good, where they were in heavy rotation with the likes of Mötley Crue and Poison. They had all the bombast and flash of their secular hair-metal peers, even better than most, with a message that was radically different. "Honestly" is as good a power ballad as you'll ever hear, while standout tracks "The Way" and "To Hell With the Devil" showcase the blistering fretwork of Oz Fox and vocalist Michael Sweet's uncanny pipes. In a word, To Hell With the Devil is heavenly.

As we learned from It's a Wonderful Life, every time a bell rings, an angel gets their wings. Assuming the bells you hear on side B of life aren't one of Hell's, then congratulations, you get your wings! But why wait until you take the big Adios, when you can pick up Aerosmith's second album, Get Your Wings. First, a little context: Aerosmith's self-titled debut in 1973 was not a success, unaided by Columbia's promotional efforts being diverted to tout Bruce Springsteen's debut album Greeting from Asbury Park, N.J. That, and as Tom Hamilton points out, "a lot of people took a look at us and said, 'They're like a Kmart version of the Stones.'" They were given one more chance: "Unless your next album is really, really good, you're not going to be recording artists anymore."

Suffice it to say, Get Your Wings was, is, really, really good. The biggest sin of Aerosmith was that it simply didn't sound like Aerosmith, and Get Your Wings reflected the band's emergence from the blues roots that dominated the first album into their own hard-rock identity. Joe Perry and Brad Whitford's dual-guitars were accentuated by producer Jack Douglas, who also helped frontman Steven Tyler hone his songwriting to reflect the now-iconic, swaggering persona he was developing as a frontman. The album would define the distinct Aerosmith sound from that point on, with tracks like "Same Old Song and Dance," "Seasons of Wither," "Train Kept a-Rollin,'" and the playful sleaze of "Lord of the Thighs" particular standouts.

Adapting J.R.R. Tolkien is never going to be an easy job, particularly when you're dealing with a story that spans thousands of years, introduces entire civilizations, and features enough characters to populate several television shows. That's the challenge The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power has faced since its debut, with the Prime Video fantasy epic juggling storylines involving Elves, Dwarves, Númenóreans, Harfoots, and the increasingly troublesome Sauron. While the series has certainly never lacked ambition, keeping all those moving parts together hasn't always been straightforward. Speaking exclusively with Collider's Maggie Lovitt for an episode of our Behind the Scenes series, The Rings of Power co-showrunner Patrick McKay admitted that the first two seasons occasionally tried to juggle too many storylines, revealing that Season 3 has been deliberately designed to bring the narrative into sharper focus. Rather than continuing to expand the show's enormous collection of characters and subplots, McKay and fellow showrunner J.D. Payne have made Sauron's creation of the One Ring the central event around which everything else revolves. He told Collider:

"You would think that would be one of the real challenges of this show, but actually, the challenge is trying to keep it small. J.D., my co-showrunner, and I have imaginations that we don't know how to stop. This is the season where Sauron makes the One Ring. Everything should tie into that. And if it doesn't, even if it delights us, maybe that's not the time for that story."

The decision to focus the third season around the One Ring also reflects the show's broader approach to adapting Tolkien's Second Age, which contains major historical events separated by enormous stretches of time.

"We looked at the text, we looked at the history of this particular era of the mythology, and there were these series of events that to us felt like they could lead one right into the next, right into the next, and build momentum with an audience the way that a serialized show should," explained McKay. "And then really the work that we do is taking those fixed points, whether it's the forging of the One Ring — that's a big moment this season — or it's Pharazôn bringing a massive Númenórean army to Middle-earth — that's a big moment this season — and there's others. And then it's about, 'How do we make those feel like earned emotional turns in a story rather than fixed historical events, if that makes sense?'" He went on to say:

"We want to bring audiences to it who maybe aren't anticipating those moments or don't know that they're going to come. If you're a Tolkien superfan and a lover of the books and a scholar of the lore, in theory, there's all kinds of things in every episode that you're responding to and picking out that we're dangling there, whether it's an Easter egg or a huge historical moment, like you're saying. But also, for my mother, who's never really gotten Lord of the Rings, it's not her thing, it should play like a novel does, where it's an emotional experience even if it wasn't in Middle-earth."

The Broncos were still willing to trade for Henley, but wanted to give up less. Harbaugh believes physical exams should only be to determine whether or not the player is healthy, not to renegotiate trade terms.

“What happened with the trade? Here’s what I know, from talking to Joe: The Broncos based a pass-fail on a medical grade based on whether we would take less compensation, and we wouldn’t and we didn’t,” Harbaugh said. “In my opinion it’s one of those loopholes that the league should really look into, because it sets a bad precedent.”

Asked if it’s permitted for teams to renegotiate trades after the trade deadline, Harbaugh answered, “It shouldn’t be, but apparently it is. That’s why it’s a loophole, and teams should not have the ability to do that.”

Harbaugh said the Broncos offered to say Henley passed his physical, but only if the Chargers would take something less than the fifth-round pick the Broncos had agreed to.
