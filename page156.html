The Boston Red Sox have made the offseason's first major free agent signing. The Red Sox and first baseman Jake Bauers have agreed to a four-year contract worth a reported $58 million, the team announced on Tuesday.

Bauers, 31, broke out with the World Series champion Milwaukee Brewers this past season, authoring a .266/.389/.484 batting line with 26 home runs that more than doubled his previous career high (12). He was named the NLCS MVP during Milwaukee's title run thanks to a 4 for 14 (.286) effort. Bauers drove in seven runs in the four-game sweep of the Los Angeles Dodgers.

The Red Sox desperately needed a power bat and certainly added one with Bauers. Their 163 home runs this past season were the fifth fewest in baseball and three players -- Wilyer Abreu, Willson Contreras and Jarren Duran -- accounted for 71 of those 163 home runs. Football Association chair Debbie Hewitt is set to inspect documentation in person regarding Fifa’s deeply contentious World Cup private investment proposal, it is understood.

The global governing body dispatched a letter on 2 November to members of the Fifa Council – where Hewitt serves as a Fifa vice-president – offering them the opportunity to examine the files at its headquarters in Zurich.

Hewitt is believed to have taken up the offer, though a firm date for the trip remains unconfirmed. Uefa president Aleksander Ceferin is likewise thought to have agreed to attend.

Relations between Uefa and Fifa president Gianni Infantino broke down over the project, which involved creating a new entity named Fifa Forward Enterprise (FFE) to manage commercial and operational activities for both the men’s and women’s World Cups alongside other Fifa tournaments.

Under the blueprint, a minority shareholding in FFE was to be acquired by private investors, led by Thrive Capital, an entity established by Joshua Kushner, whose brother is the son-in-law of United States President Donald Trump.

The initiative was ultimately scrapped following opposition from Uefa and a pair of other continental confederations.

Hewitt’s career experience in private equity provides her with direct insight into the standard documentation and financial details expected in proposals of this nature.

Fifa issued the invitation after European representatives on the Fifa Council sent a formal letter on 19 October requesting clarity on several matters, including the FFE arrangement and disciplinary proceedings involving Folarin Balogun.

Provisions regarding non-disclosure agreements and the specific terms governing document inspection in Zurich have yet to be finalised.

Earlier on Tuesday, the French Football Federation formally endorsed Philippe Diallo’s bid for the Fifa presidency.

On Monday, Uefa confirmed that the 63-year-old had secured the support of national association delegates across its executive committee and the Fifa Council to challenge Infantino.

Disquiet over the FFE proposals prompted European football’s governing body to promise in August that it would put forward a credible challenger to Infantino.

Diallo’s domestic federation has now unanimously backed his campaign, stating: "World football is going through a pivotal period in its history. The challenges affecting its governance demand clear thinking and responsibility.

"Restoring trust, preserving the universality of football, rebuilding Fifa’s credibility, and placing the development of football in all regions of the world at the heart of its actions are essential issues for the entire international football community.

"By accepting this invitation, Philippe Diallo is responding to a desire to open a new chapter for the governance of world football."

Figures within Uefa had previously hoped that former Arsenal manager Arsene Wenger, who currently serves as Fifa’s chief of global football development, might enter the race, though he is believed to have declined the prospect last week.

Concerns as Arsenal Start Handing Out New Contracts

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Arsenal’s willingness to hand new contracts to key figures such as Declan Rice, David Raya and Jurrien Timber is entirely understandable. These are not squad players being rewarded for loyalty; they are central pillars of a team that has developed into one of the strongest in Europe under Mikel Arteta. Reports indicate that Arsenal are moving towards new agreements with several of those players as they attempt to protect the core of the squad for the years ahead.

The challenge facing Arsenal is that successful teams eventually encounter a difficult balancing act. Keeping a title-winning squad together sounds attractive in theory, but football rarely allows clubs to stand still. At some stage, decisions have to be made about age profiles, wage structures and the next cycle of the team.

The next phase of Arsenal’s evolution could depend as much on who eventually leaves as who stays.

There is little debate around the wisdom of extending Rice’s contract. He has become one of the defining players of the Arteta era and is widely regarded as one of the Premier League’s outstanding midfielders. Timber’s importance has also grown significantly following his recovery from injury, while Raya has established himself as one of the league’s most reliable goalkeepers. Piero Hincapié, another player viewed as central to the club’s future, has reportedly signed a new deal already, though the club are yet to issue an official confirmation at the time of writing.

The logic behind these renewals is obvious. Arsenal have spent years building a squad capable of winning major honours. Allowing elite players to drift towards the final years of their contracts would create uncertainty and expose the club to unnecessary risk.

From a football perspective, securing the futures of those players is straightforward.

The complications arise when the wider picture is considered.

Much of Arsenal’s success has been built on careful squad construction. One of Arteta’s greatest achievements was creating a team that could improve together rather than peak at different times.

That process becomes more difficult when an increasing number of senior players move into their late twenties and early thirties simultaneously.

A cluster of important Arsenal players have contracts which expire in 2028, including Martin Odegaard, Kai Havertz, Ben White, Mikel Merino, Declan Rice, David Raya and Jurrien Timber. Many are either already in their peak years or approaching them.

There is a natural temptation to keep extending deals and retain a successful group. The danger is that a squad can age faster than expected. What appears sensible in 2026 can look problematic by 2029 if too many key players are on expensive long-term contracts at the same stage of their careers.

Elite clubs constantly refresh, they evolve while remaining competitive. Such an approach requires difficult judgment calls.

Supporters naturally focus on the stars they want to keep, but executives are forced to think differently.

Every new contract impacts the wage bill. Every long-term commitment affects future flexibility. Every decision made for one player influences negotiations with others. If Rice receives terms comparable to Arsenal’s highest earners, as has been reported, it will inevitably shape expectations across the dressing room.

That does not mean Arsenal should avoid rewarding their best performers. Quite the opposite. The club are right to secure the futures of players they believe can form the backbone of the team for years to come.

It does mean there will eventually come a point where not everybody can receive the same treatment. Some contracts will be extended, others may not. That is the reality of long-term squad planning.

For now, Arsenal’s contract activity should be viewed as a sign of strength rather than concern. Clubs in decline rarely spend their time trying to protect the futures of their most important players. Arsenal are doing so because they remain convinced that the current project has room to grow.

The more intriguing question is what happens next.

Arteta has already built a team capable of winning the Premier League and challenging for the Champions League. Sustaining that level for another five years may require a different skill altogether. It will require the ability to refresh the squad without weakening it, identify the next generation before the current one begins to decline and make unpopular decisions before they become obvious.

Those decisions are rarely celebrated when they are made – they are usually only appreciated years later; if ever.

Arsenal are entering that phase now. Keeping Rice, Raya and Timber appears straightforward. The real challenge will be determining which members of this successful squad remain part of the next cycle, and which make way for it.

South Africa seized 5 ancient baobabs bound for Georgia, where 8 Kenyan trees died in a billionaire’s park

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

For more than three months, five baobab trees estimated to be 200 to 300 years old lay on their sides in a Durban warehouse parking lot, roots and branches hacked back, waiting to be shipped to the Black Sea nation of Georgia. South Africa’s Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment (DFFE) intercepted the trees on July 17 after their exporter, Pretoria-based Divine Landscapes, could not produce licenses to remove, transport, or export them.

Officials have not named the buyer. But reporting by Daily Maverick traced the trail to the Shekvetili Dendrological Park, a private arboretum on Georgia’s coast founded by billionaire Bidzina Ivanishvili, the country’s former prime minister and the founder of its ruling Georgian Dream party. Ivanishvili has not commented, and Daily Maverick said requests to his lawyer and to Georgia’s embassy in Pretoria went unanswered.

The case tests how far South Africa will go to keep a protected species from becoming a collectible for wealthy buyers abroad. It also left authorities with a practical problem: keeping living evidence alive while a case moves through the courts.

Environmental journalist Tony Carnie got an anonymous tip about the trees, photographed them through a fence at the Africa Global Logistics warehouse in Durban’s Mobeni industrial area, and reported the seizure in Daily Maverick in October. AGL told the publication it only provided logistics services and neither owned nor exported the cargo.

Every baobab in South Africa is a specially protected species under the National Forests Act, which bars anyone from cutting, disturbing, removing, transporting, or exporting one without a license. The DFFE says the five trees were dug up in the Lephalale area of Limpopo province without a provincial permit. It refused Divine Landscapes’ late license application at the end of July and dismissed the company’s appeal on Oct. 27.

The company denies wrongdoing. Its attorneys told Daily Maverick the trees were “obtained from private gardens with the full consent of the respective owners” and said it is seeking judicial review of the appeal decision. The company’s owner appeared in Durban Magistrates’ Court in September, and the case was postponed to January pending further investigation.

Ivanishvili, whose party critics accuse of tilting Georgia toward Moscow, has spent years moving giant trees to Shekvetili. Eight baobabs uprooted on Kenya’s Kilifi coast were bound for the park when Kenyan President William Ruto halted their export and ordered an investigation. The Kenya Forest Service later authorized the shipment, and the trees reached Georgia in 2023.

All eight died. In August 2024, the park confirmed the deaths and blamed delays it attributed to Ivanishvili’s political opponents.

“We also did not want to see a repeat of what happened in Kenya,” Dr. Sarah Venter, an ecologist and founder of the Baobab Foundation, told Daily Maverick.

The seizure comes as South Africa presses for tougher international enforcement. On Oct. 23, parties to the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime, meeting in Vienna, voted 83 to 12, with 27 abstentions, to begin negotiating a new protocol on crimes that affect the environment, including trafficking in wild flora and fauna. South Africa co-sponsored the resolution.

“Environmental crime is organized crime,” Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment Minister David Maynier said in a DFFE statement. Once negotiated, the protocol could give countries shared tools for cross-border investigations, extradition, and recovering criminal proceeds. Trafficking networks are already expanding their reach, as a U.N. report on the global illicit economy found this year, and wealthy collectors’ appetite for rare species is under scrutiny elsewhere, including in India’s trade in trafficked orangutans.

Seized cargo can usually wait in storage. These trees could not. “They’ve been lying on their sides. Their branches and their roots have been trimmed, well hacked, so they’re not in a very vertical position. They’re not in soil. They’re in full sun. There’s no water,” Carnie said in a Daily Maverick video interview.

“The really critical thing is to get them back into the soil as soon as possible to allow them to recover and regenerate, because they are using up stored stem water to survive,” Venter said. A healthy baobab can be up to 75% water.

The rescue was privately funded. Timbavati Private Nature Reserve, which straddles the Mpumalanga and Limpopo provinces, offered to cover loading, transport, and replanting, and Mark Read of the Everard Read Group found an unnamed private funder within 10 minutes of Venter’s call, Daily Maverick reported. On Nov. 11, the trees, each weighing an estimated 17 to 30 tons, began leaving Durban on abnormal-load trucks for the roughly 900-kilometer trip to the reserve.

All five had sprouted leaves and flower buds. “I think we got here just in time and I’m quite positive about their future,” Venter said.

Scientists Discover a Disturbing Link Between Cannabis Use and Earlier Psychosis

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

In studies where psychosis typically emerged at age 30 or later, cannabis users experienced it more than six years earlier, raising questions about cannabis use throughout adulthood.

Most discussions about cannabis and mental health focus on teenagers, but new research suggests the conversation should extend into adulthood. A large analysis found that among people who developed psychosis, cannabis users experienced their first episode an average of two and a half years earlier than non-users. The findings reveal a consistent association, although they do not prove that cannabis caused the difference.

Researchers at UNSW Sydney examined 149 studies involving more than 71,000 people, largely conducted in Europe, North America, and Australia since 2011. The analysis, published in Biological Psychiatry, focused mostly on people with schizophrenia.

Psychosis can involve hallucinations, delusions, disordered thinking, and changes in behavior. It is associated with schizophrenia and other mental illnesses and typically emerges in late adolescence or early adulthood. According to Psychosis Australia, up to 3% of adults experience a diagnosed disorder involving psychosis during their lifetime.

The researchers found that the age difference between cannabis users and non-users grew in studies where psychosis developed later in life. When the average age of onset was between 20 and 24, cannabis users experienced their first episode 1.6 years earlier. That difference increased to 4.43 years in studies with an average onset between ages 25 and 29, reaching 6.3 years when the average onset was 30 or older.

Chief investigator Matthew Large, a psychiatrist and Conjoint Professor at UNSW’s School of Clinical Medicine, said the findings suggest that the effects of cannabis use may accumulate over time.

“We’ve tended to focus on the dangers of using cannabis in adolescence, when the brain is still developing, but the risk doesn’t disappear once you’re in your 20s,” Prof. Large said. “What we’re seeing suggests a cumulative effect, where harm builds up over time, a bit like alcohol or nicotine.”

Interestingly, the researchers found no statistically significant difference in the age of psychosis onset between teenagers who used cannabis and those who did not. However, because psychosis rarely develops before the mid-teens, there is a limited window in which researchers could detect an earlier onset.

“There may be a biological floor, in that there is only so much earlier that onset can be shifted. In younger groups, strong development, genetic or other psychosis risk factors may dominate, making any cannabis-related age shift harder to detect,” Prof. Large said.

The analysis builds on previous research led by Large. A smaller 2011 meta-analysis found that psychosis began 2.7 years earlier among cannabis users, while a 2015 analysis found that psychosis typically developed 6.3 years after cannabis use began. Those earlier studies lacked sufficient data to compare different age groups.

Lead author Carly Stevens, from UNSW’s School of Biomedical Sciences, said the new age comparisons challenge the idea that delaying cannabis use until adulthood necessarily avoids its potential psychiatric risks.

“What we’ve found challenges the assumption that you’ll be fine if you wait to smoke weed until your mid-20s; that’s just not the case,” Ms Stevens said.

“There’s been so much emphasis on adolescents not smoking cannabis, which is important, but we need to broaden the warnings for everyone.”

An important limitation is that the research cannot establish whether cannabis directly causes psychosis or makes it develop earlier. Doing so would require experiments that deliberately expose people to potential harm, which Large said would be unethical. The findings also do not establish how likely an individual cannabis user is to develop psychosis.

“But the consistency of the evidence, particularly the strong effect in older adults who likely have been using cannabis for longer, does support the theory that cannabis plays a causal role in the timing of psychosis onset, and possibly the onset of some cases among people who would not have otherwise developed psychosis.”

For people who do develop psychosis, the timing of their first episode can affect important stages of life. Stevens noted that even a difference of a few years could interfere with opportunities that might otherwise shape someone’s future.

“People may miss out on study, job opportunities, travel and relationships in those years, and treatment can come with physical side effects that can make life harder as well,” she said.

Large believes reducing or stopping cannabis use could offer meaningful benefits if it helps delay psychosis, although the analysis did not directly test that possibility.

“It’s a major illness, which robs you of your shared reality with family, friends and all those around you,” he said. “If we can delay psychosis by even a few years, people will be more likely to reach crucial milestones in education and training, and develop strong support networks, which can protect against psychosocial decline.”

Delaying the onset of psychosis could also ease pressure on healthcare systems. According to Large, psychotic disorders account for up to 3% of health-related expenditure in developed nations.

The researchers believe their findings support clearer public health warnings about the potential psychiatric consequences of cannabis use, even while questions about causation remain unresolved.

“We can’t run direct experiments, so we have to go with the best evidence available,” Prof. Large said. “In my view, the threshold for health warnings about cannabis and psychosis has definitely been met.”

One concern is the expanding use of medical cannabis. Large said he has treated a growing number of people with psychosis who had been prescribed cannabis for conditions where evidence of benefit remains limited. He worries that its medical applications can lead people to underestimate potential risks.

“It’s got this therapeutic halo around it, which blinds people to the risk.”

The increasing popularity of cannabis edibles raises additional questions. Some products can contain higher concentrations of tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the main compound responsible for cannabis’s psychoactive effects.

Large also sees opportunities for harm reduction through cannabis legalization. Although legalization could increase access, government oversight could allow authorities to regulate THC potency, improve consumer education, and introduce requirements aimed at helping buyers understand potential health risks.

“You could tax it, regulate it, and require people to show they understand the risks before buying,” Prof. Large said.

For now, the researchers want to better understand the biological mechanisms that might connect prolonged cannabis exposure with the timing of psychosis. They are calling for more research involving older cannabis users, particularly as medical cannabis and high-potency products become more widely available.

Future studies will also need clearer, standardized measures of cannabis exposure to determine how different patterns and durations of use relate to psychosis onset.

Reference: “Cannabis Use and Age-Related Acceleration of Psychosis Onset: A Meta-Analysis” by Carly Stevens, Hannah Pincham and Matthew Large, 18 August 2026, Biological Psychiatry. DOI: 10.1016/j.biopsych.2026.06.025

Suno Moves Into AI Filmmaking With New Product ‘Visuals’

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The AI company is expanding to video and image generation with its latest launch, describing its mission as a "creative entertainment" brand rather than a strictly musical one.

Suno is pushing past the world of AI-powered music creation and into the world of videos and images with its latest launch, Visuals. With the new tools, users can generate up to 15 seconds of video based on written prompts, music input or images. The product can be used to create music videos, lyric videos or cover art, among other use cases. In a company blog post announcing the launch, the company’s chief product officer, Jack Brody, notes that Suno is a “creative entertainment” company that goes “beyond music.”

“Suno began with a simple belief: everyone is creative. With the right tools, people can make something original and feel the joy of bringing an idea to life,” writes Brody. Last month, Suno launched Speech, its first push past music generation, which allows for users to prompt their way into generating poems, spoken audio and meditations.

“Video had always been the logical next step in our vision, but until recently video generation was slow and too expensive for anyone to create but enterprises,” says Brody. “A new generation of models changes that and opens video generation up to creative entertainment for everyone.”

According to representatives for Suno, Visuals is powered by H3 Move, a model derived from H3 from MiniMax, fine-tuned/optimized for how Suno believes its community will use the product. Unlike other AI visual generators, Suno’s is made to align itself with the structure and story of a user’s musical creations.

The launch of visuals also unlocks new monetization for Suno. Visual credits are treated differently than song credits, and free-tier users cannot generate more than a single five-second video before hitting their credit limit. Any further use requires topping up for more money.

The blog post about Visuals is careful to note the “additional safeguards” added “to protect artists from unauthorized use of their likenesses or works, on top of the ones we already have in place for music.”

These new safeguards include “new text and visual filters intended to block uploads and prompts that try to use or create visual representations of specific artists, public figures, copyrighted works and IP.” The company also scans videos before sharing them with users to ensure they do not violate community guidelines against a range of “harmful content.” All visuals will also be embedded with watermarks before they can be shared off the platform.

“We are pairing these protections with in-platform user education. The first time a Suno user tries to upload a video or image to use with Visuals, we remind them the tool is for original creation, and to confirm they have the rights to any file they are trying to use,” the blog post explains.

Some of the ramifications of abusing the tool can include removing the content, restricting the account or even terminating a Suno user.

News of Suno’s Visuals launch comes about six months after OpenAI discontinued its short-form video generator, Sora, which could also generate music in the background as a byproduct of the service. At the time, copyright owners complained about the platform’s use of protected characters, brands and images. Billboard also found that the platform could, in rare cases, generate clips of copyrighted songs, like Pop Smoke‘s “What You Know Bout Love.”

In recent months, legal action against Suno has mounted. On top of an additional lawsuit from Universal Music Group and Sony Music surrounding alleged copyright infringement, Elvis Presley‘s estate and a class action group, including Jason Isbell, have also recently sued the company over alleged violations of their name, image, likeness and voice.

Suno has argued in lawsuits that its current models do not allow users to include the names of specific artists in prompts, nor produce outputs that mimic specific real voices. The complaint from Elvis’s estate argues that those claims are “unequivocally false.” They included links to videos capturing the use of Suno to play songs that sound remarkably similar to The King.

NASA to Cover 35th SpaceX Resupply Mission Space Station Departure

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

NASA and its international partners are set to receive scientific research samples and hardware as a SpaceX Dragon spacecraft departs the International Space Station no earlier than Wednesday, Nov. 18, for its return to Earth.

Pending weather conditions, the Dragon spacecraft will undock from the Harmony module’s space-facing port at 11:05 a.m. EST, following a command sent by ground controllers at SpaceX’s facility in Hawthorne, California.

NASA’s live coverage of undocking and departure begins at 10:45 a.m., streaming through a variety of platforms. Learn where to watch online:

NASA and SpaceX are monitoring weather forecasts and targeting splashdown off the California coast no earlier than 1:50 a.m. Thursday, Nov. 19 (10:50 p.m. PST Nov. 18). Teams will adjust plans as needed based on splashdown conditions in the Dragon recovery area and will provide updates on splashdown timing when available. NASA will not stream the splashdown but will post updates on its space station blog.

Thousands of pounds of cargo are returning from the space station, including research samples that could advance future space exploration and improve life on Earth. The returning research includes a 3D-bioprinted blood vessel construct scientists used to study vascular aging and cardiovascular disease in space and on Earth. Frozen tumor organoids treated aboard the station also will return, helping researchers identify new ways to improve cancer treatments and advance personalized medicine. Scientists will analyze frozen microbial samples to better understand how antibiotic-resistant bacteria survive, adapt, and evolve in space. In addition, a lighting system will return after more than three years aboard the space station, where researchers tested its potential to support astronauts’ sleep and well-being during long-duration missions.

Filled with more than 6,300 pounds of crew supplies, science investigations, and equipment, the spacecraft arrived at the space station on Oct. 15. It launched two days earlier on a Falcon 9 rocket from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida for the agency’s SpaceX 35th commercial resupply services mission.

For more than 26 years, people have lived and worked continuously aboard the International Space Station, advancing scientific knowledge and making research breakthroughs not possible on Earth. The space station helps NASA understand and overcome the challenges of human spaceflight, expand commercial opportunities in low Earth orbit, and build on the foundation for long-duration missions to the Moon, as part of the Artemis and Moon Base programs, and to Mars.
