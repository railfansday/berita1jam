Bluesky was right all along and will accept your apology any time

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bluesky is a niche Twitter-clone frequently accused of being an echo chamber for progressives. After the blue tsunami of this week’s midterms, however, its inhabitants don’t seem to be echoing themselves so much as the vast majority of American voters.

The sheer scale of Democrats’ victory is staggering. They won control of both chambers of the U.S. Congress, flipping Senate seats in seven states Trump carried in 2024—including Republican stronghold Texas, which now also has a Democratic governor and attorney general. All in all, this year’s midterms represent the biggest electoral shift in state-level politics to hit the U.S. in generations. They also represent a Texas-sized validation of the worldview Bluesky has become notorious for over the past two years.

Although it officially launched in the spring of 2023, Bluesky might as well have been born on election eve in 2024. That was the moment millions of disillusioned Twitter users decamped from a website owned by the largest single contributor to Trump’s reelection. Bluesky quickly became a haven for Twitter expats and grew at a frenetic pace. It went from 9 million users in September 2024 to 15 million by mid-November, eventually reaching its current 47 million.

Because many of Bluesky’s users arrived out of principled opposition to X, formerly Twitter, the two sites have arguably existed in opposition to each other. Both portray their counterpart as out of touch, with Bluesky alleging Elon Musk’s algorithm tweaks have skewed X’s political vortex too far to the right, and X suggesting Bluesky is a bubble of liberals who can tolerate anything except a difference in opinion.

The latter idea seemed to largely prevail. 

In 2025, Washington Post columnists, tech world figures like Mark Cuban, and myriad other pundits dismissed Bluesky as smug, self-righteous, and irrelevant. It was the fantasy sandbox where progressives went to play among themselves, protecting reality dwellers from having to hear their drivel. (“Like a containment dome over a nuclear reactor,” as the writer Josh Barro put it.)

So, how well did Bluesky’s users stay in touch with reality between elections, compared with X’s exponentially larger population? Consider the post-midterms perspective.

Rather than accept the narrative that 2024 represented a rejection of “wokeness” and a political realignment toward Trump, the manifestly trans-friendly Bluesky suggested the election was more a referendum on postpandemic inflation and global anti-incumbent bias. As an emboldened Trump then proceeded to antagonize U.S. citizens and the rest of the world, while making billions for himself in crypto, Bluesky meticulously chronicled the state of play. It became the premier destination for seeing radicalized older white ladies, abysmally attended pro-ICE rallies, and complaints about the lack of mainstream news coverage around massive No Kings protests. Any random day would find endless posts with evidence that, per the motto of the political philosopher and Bluesky power user Olúfẹ́mi O. Táíwò, “We are going to win.” 

Above all, Bluesky proved to be less a bubble than an expression of the multitude of ideological factions that had united under the umbrella of non-MAGAness . . . to argue with one another.

Meanwhile, X’s millions of liberal and progressive folks were often drowned out by an algorithm that seemed to favor right-wing voices and their preferred narratives. A bubble chart from Nate Silver, the political analyst, visually depicted the accounts with highest engagement rates back in April. Its many MAGA-red circles made it look like the blood-filled elevator from The Shining. As a May 2026 study from Northwestern University suggests, X’s algorithm rewards posts that provoke strong reactions, so the loudest and most emotionally satisfying political claims become disproportionately visible. That’s how we got viral fever dreams about James Talarico, the then-senatorial candidate, checking into a mental hospital, something that never in fact happened.

It should come as little surprise that many high-profile MAGA voices on X spent the days leading up to the election incorrectly predicting how it would turn out.

Who looks out of touch and stuck in an echo chamber now?

While X may have been more plugged into the political discourse online for the past two years, Bluesky now appears better attuned to what’s been happening on the ground. Its users were right about pretty much everything. 

They were right that anti-trans ads would have little or no impact on the 2026 election, and that their impact on the previous election had been vastly overstated.

They were right that Graham Platner wasn’t the only Democrat who could beat Susan Collins in Maine, and that Abdul El-Sayed’s association with a controversial Twitch streamer relatively few voters had even heard of was not as big a deal as X users seemed to think. 

And they were right that fleeing X’s algorithm helped them view the world with clear eyes.

If Bluesky is a bubble, then it is currently a champagne bubble awash in celebration.

The festivities will likely last only a matter of hours, though, until infighting explodes around just how the Democrats should spend their newfound political capital.

6 good, bad, and weird changes in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time for Switch 2

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You may have heard by now that Nintendo has remade mighty '90s classic The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time. The reimagining of this 1998 Nintendo 64 adventure game for Switch 2 includes many major and minor changes, not all of them created equal.

With the Ocarina remake on shelves now, I wanted to highlight a handful of differences that stood out to me while playing. Some of them are fun, others are frustrating, and some defy that sort of categorization entirely. Without wasting any more time, let's dive in.

I'm not going to be too specific about this change because the game literally just came out and I don't want to ruin the experience for folks.

That said, you should know going in that Nintendo chose to alter several of Ocarina's legendary boss fights with new attacks, animations, and more. It's one of the most exciting elements of the new game for me, as it adds a sense of novelty to the adventure that is otherwise lacking, owing to the game's status as a remake. Every time a well-known boss fight swerved and surprised me, I had a huge grin on my face.

One of the best puzzle-solving items in the entire Zelda series is the hookshot, a straight-shooting grapple gun that can easily zip Link between points. Ocarina was not the first game to incorporate the hookshot, but it did expand the tool's usefulness with an extended targeting range and a wide variety of surfaces to which the hookshot can attach, such as wooden treasure chests and the rooftops of Kakariko Village.

Unfortunately, that philosophy has not been entirely maintained in the Switch 2 remake. Most grapple points remain intact, but many have been changed from natural-feeling features like tree branches to clearly marked targets. I don't mind that change so much as I mind the fact that the hookshot can no longer grapple onto Kakariko's rooftops. This greatly reduces Link's ability to move around the village and just makes the game less fun for no discernible reason.

In 2005, Resident Evil 4 set a template for third-person, over-the-shoulder aiming cameras that pretty much every mainstream video game nowadays deploys, including the Ocarina remake. When aiming items like the hookshot, bow, or boomerang in the new Switch 2 release, the camera sits behind Link, slightly offset so his character model doesn't get in the way. You can also aim using gyro controls as a nice bonus.

This point of view works just fine and may be more familiar to younger Zelda fans used to the aiming functionality in 2017's The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, however, I do miss being able to zoom in with a first-person lens to examine Link's surroundings up close. It's kind of a bummer in the opening Kokiri Forest area, where you can no longer get a good look at the stick drawing on the trunk of Link's treehouse.

I wouldn't say that rupees (the currency in every Zelda game) are totally superfluous in the original Ocarina of Time. After all, you need 40 of them to bypass an early-game obstacle, and some important mini-games require small sums in order to participate. You can also use them to stock up on supplies like arrows and health potions, but frankly, the original version doled out those things generously enough to render that unnecessary.

While I would say Ocarina on Switch 2 is easier overall, the developers did add some new incentives to hoard rupees and collect Gold Skulltula tokens in order to expand the size of Link's wallet. Specifically, the Great Fairies who provide Link with game-changing upgrades now ask for sizable monetary donations before they'll hand over the good stuff. In the original game, the upgrades were free, but some of them cost hundreds of rupees in the remake.

I like this change because it gives players a reason to more fully engage with optional content and wring as much fun out of the reimagined game as possible.

I talked about this in my review, so I'll keep it brief here, but major story cutscenes in the Ocarina remake have been expanded with full voice acting for every character except for Link. The acting itself is a little cartoonish, which adequately fits the vibe, but also comes across as slightly cheesy at times.

More importantly, though, this makes some cutscenes last longer than they might have if they were broken up by text boxes, as they were in the original. Maybe someone can do a proper stopwatch comparison, but it at least felt like the Ocarina remake seized cutscene control for lengthier periods of time than the N64 version did.

Lastly, Nintendo has opted to lengthen the day/night cycle in the Ocarina remake. It was very short in the N64 original, as you could hardly cross Hyrule Field on foot without the sun going down and horrible skeleton monsters emerging to ruin your travels.

In the Switch 2 remake, it takes much longer for the sun to rise and set, which has benefits and drawbacks. On the plus side, you get more time to enjoy each distinct atmosphere, which gives music lead and sound director Masato Ohashi an excuse to craft new, chilled out nighttime versions of some of Zelda composer Koji Kondo's iconic area themes. Some downsides, however, are that the Hyrule Field drawbridge no longer goes up at night, and it's somewhat more tedious to wait for certain times of day in the few situations where that's necessary.

One last note is that the day/night cycle is now universal, rather than turning off in towns and cities like it did in the original game. That means you get to see NPCs actively move from their daytime positions to their nighttime states in real-time, which is pretty neat.

Despite the long waits for glory, both franchises are among the most successful teams in their respective sports. Although Manchester United have won multiple cup competitions following Sir Alex Ferguson’s departure, since 2013 they have failed to lift the most direct comparison to a World Series or Super Bowl - the Premier League title.

Glazer’s comments have been construed by some angry fans on social media as an acceptance of poor results on the pitch, as long as revenues remain high.

Fans have ramped up their protests against Manchester United’s owners - majority shareholders the Glazer family, as well as minority stakeholders INEOS, who control the club’s sporting operations and are fronted by Sir Jim Ratcliffe.

It’s official, 20-year-old Kimi Antonelli has secured his spot as the youngest F1 Champion in history after just two seasons, but while the dedicated Mercedes driver has been relentless on the track, the speedy Italian has also stayed active outside of his car. Here’s why the history maker’s appetite for adventure is the reason that he never stays still, and how a smart approach to staying active benefits both body and mind.

Kimi Antonelli’s third place in Mexico City on November 1 meant that he’d earned enough points to stave off all other drivers to complete the season as champion, but this overnight success is the result of a mature approach to staying sharp. “I try to do things that keep me active, but won’t tire me out,” explained Antonelli during a recent and telling interview with GQ. “It’s all a way to release mental stress, to just relax and enjoy. Sean Payton says J.K. Dobbins remains "a huge part of what we're doing"

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

It’s been a somewhat strange second year in Denver for veteran running back J.K. Dobbins. As coach Sean Payton tells it, however, everything is going as planned.

“He’s a huge part of what we’re doing,” Payton told reporters on Wednesday regarding whether the team is managing Dobbins’s workload. “So each week we look at — a lot of times who’s the back that’s in there with the hot hand. But he’s been fantastic. So to answer your question: No, that’s not a hot button topic with us here. It might be with you guys, but not with us. So we’re good. We’re good with the rotation.”

Payton is generally using a three-back rotation, with Dobbins joining R.J. Harvey and rookie Jonah Coleman. Still, with Harvey on injured reserve for Sunday’s showdown against the Chiefs, Dobbins got fewer chances than Coleman.

Coleman, who was on the field for 41 offensive plays, had 11 carries for 41 yards and four catches for 63. Dobbins played 13 snaps, getting seven carries for 13 yards and no targets in the passing game.

“Look, it’s always easy to work two [running backs] and it becomes more challenging when you’re trying to work three, but he’s doing a great job,” Payton said of Dobbins.

For the year, Dobbins has 94 carries for 306 yards in eight games, with eight catches for 27 yards. That’s 333 yards from scrimmage — an average of 41.6 per game.

In 10 games last year, before he suffered a season-ending foot injury during a Thursday night game against the Raiders, Dobbins was averaging nearly twice that amount, at 80.9 yards from scrimmage per game.

Through it all, the Broncos have been linked to a potential trade for Colts running back Jonathan Taylor. While the prospect of acquiring a workhorse would cut against Payton’s longstanding approach to use multiple running backs, Taylor’s arrival would surely result in even fewer chances for Dobbins.

Zelda: Ocarina of Time Remake - How To Get A Free Hylian Shield

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time has so many items and pieces of equipment that it's hard to keep track of what is in each game. However, select iconic gear sets are shown as motifs throughout the entire series, like the Master Sword. Another piece of reliable equipment is the Hylian Shield, which, depending on the game, can be easy to get.

In Ocarina of Time, you can buy it outright at one of the first major shops in Castle Town, but 80 Rupees is a big ask for new players who may not yet know the best ways to earn Rupees. Here's how to get one for free without paying the Rupee tax.

Early in the game, before you enter Dodongo's Cavern, Ocarina of Time will constantly remind you that you need a better shield than your initial Deku Shield that you picked up in the opening moments; which burns up on contact with flames.

As a result, you'll want to pick up the Hylian Shield, the classic reliable shield found in multiple Zelda games and one of the most iconic pieces of equipment in the series.

To find it, head to the graveyard behind Kakariko Village. When you enter the village, walk straight ahead and to the right, following the path up to the graveyard. Walk all the way to the back, near the big grave area near the hill. Go behind the grave in the gallery above, and pull the stone until it reveals a hole.

Drop in, and be prepared to either run or battle a formidable foe right away.

Inside the hole is a ReDead, an iconic and terrifying enemy throughout the series, including Ocarina of Time. They can scream to stun you, then grab you to suck your health, so you'll need to approach them carefully. Thankfully, it's easy to run past them, grab the shield in the chest, and then dash out before the ReDead can react.

As Young Link, you can't use the Hylian Shield to its full potential because it's a little too big for him. Link will crouch and hold the shield above him, so it's less useful than the Deku Shield in some situations. However, I would keep it equipped in Dodongo's Cavern regardless, because it won't burn and can help protect you from vertical threats.

You keep the Hylian Shield when you turn into Adult Link. As Adult Link, you can wield the Hylian Shield like a normal shield, blocking attacks from the front.

There is another way out if you can't locate the Hylian Shield in the graveyard for whatever reason, or you lose it somehow. The Bazaar in Castle Town sells a Hylian Shield for 80 Rupees, which you can reduce to 60 Rupees by talking to the guard near the trail to Death Mountain at the edge of Kakariko Village.

While you're there, give the guard the Keaton Mask, the first mask from the Happy Mask Shop.

There are other chests that contain Hylian Shields, but these are the easiest ways to grab them early in the Ocarina of Time remake.

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time’s Changes to the Infamous Water Temple Are Actually Brilliant

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Zelda's most infamous dungeon is no longer a frustrating rite of passage

With The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time’s Switch 2 remake out now, long-time Nintendo fans are in for a lot of warm nostalgia. A trek through the lost woods, duets with Sheik, a return to the Bombchu Bowling Alley — all of those wonderful moments return in a faithful remake that feels every bit as magical as the Nintendo 64 classic. But with all those wonderful moments comes a painful reunion with one of the most hated moments in Zelda history. Yes, you will have to return to the dreaded Water Temple.

Don’t worry! Even though the Ocarina of Time remake is careful not to change too much about the original game, it does make some small tweaks to a dungeon that still haunts a generation. It’s mercifully easier to navigate now thanks to a few minor but smart updates that go a long way.

The Water Temple is the most infamous dungeon in the original Ocarina of Time, so much so that it has been a bit of a running joke for decades now. The gist is that it’s a four-story structure built around a central room, and it’s flooded with water. To access certain areas, you need to find rooms in the dungeon that allow you to change the water level throughout the temple. Easy enough on paper, but it’s a bit of a pain in practice. Due to the limited visual information you get in-game, it requires you to keep a mental map of where each water adjustment point is, as well as how each of the three levels will change the layout of the dungeon. It’s a headache to keep everything straight, thus earning it a reputation as a frustrating rite of passage.

While that structure hasn’t changed much in the remake, Nintendo does make it easier to get through with a few smart decisions. That came at the request of Eiji Aonuma, who directed the original Ocarina of Time. “I had some overdue homework: I wanted to do something about the Water Temple I created 28 years ago,” Aonuma said in a recent Nintendo developer interview. “Since it became well known for its difficulty in the original game, I had the team make some adjustments in this version.”

One of those adjustments just comes down to clearer signposting. Building off the dungeon’s Ocarina of Time 3D redesign, the doors that lead to water controls are now clearly marked with symbols showing you exactly how the water level will change. You no longer have to remember which door leads to the room that drains the room, or which one will get you to floor two.

On top of that, each of the routes are now color-coded. You’ll find variously colored pipes around each of the three doors, and those visually lead you to the control room if you follow them through the dungeon. For instance, when you enter the route to the room that brings the water to its lowest level, you’ll notice that some blue pipes signal that you should float to the water’s surface. Follow that hint and you’ll find that the pipes lead to clearly marked pedestals for you to play your ocarina on. All of that makes it significantly easier to remember exactly where you need to go at all times.

Arguably, the more impactful Water Temple change comes from a top-level control decision that just so happens to make life easier in the dungeon. Part of why the Water Temple is such a pain is because you need to use a few key items throughout it. Your ocarina, Iron Boots, Hookshot, and more all play major roles in solving puzzles throughout the temple. Since the original game only gave you three item slots to work with, you were often left juggling around your item shortcuts in the main menu. It’s a minor pain point, but one that stands out when you’re constantly swapping items.

While the Switch 2 remake still has its own item management quirks, it wisely keeps your ocarina mapped to the D-pad at all times. Your boots get a quick swap button on the down button there too, meaning that you don’t need to waste an item slot on the Iron Boots. With those two necessities free up, you can dedicate your ZR item slot to the Hookshot and only swap to other items as needed with a quick menu on the R button. That decision cuts out any need for pause-menu management, allowing you to move through the dungeon more fluidly.

For the ‘90s purists, these changes might sound a little sacrilegious. As much as players claim to hate the Water Temple, that complaint invariably comes with a fondness for a genuinely memorable bit of frustrating design. “Why shouldn’t today’s youths struggle through the Water Temple like their elders once did?” you may ask. I, for one, welcome the help, just as I support the idea of today’s students having their loans canceled even though I had to pay mine off. The new visual design tweak makes for some sensible signposting that still feels part of the dungeon’s architecture, and the control solution just makes it easier to stay immersed in a well-built dungeon. New generations of players needn’t suffer like us old Zelda fans once did. Swim free, ye tadpoles.

Nintendo’s excellent Switch 2 remake recaptures the soul of a masterpiece
