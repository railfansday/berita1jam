DockCase is no stranger to Kickstarting unique SSD enclosures and docks, with the company’s Visual Smart M.2 enclosure making its way onto our list of the best SSD enclosures. The Mount Ling 2 Pro, however, is gunning for a spot among the best USB-C docks. It’s a dock for handhelds like the Steam Deck and Switch 2, an external enclosure for an NVMe SSD, and a game capture device, all packaged up in a dock that can show off your handheld and expand your port selection.

There are two versions of the dock: the standard Mount Ling 2 and the Pro version. The only difference between them is the internal capture card. The base version is $109 via an early bird deal while the Kickstarter gets underway, and the Pro version is $149. They’ll be priced at $199 and $249, respectively, after the early deals are gone.

It’s the world’s first “smart creator dock,” says DockCase, which is partially true. There are other docks that give you additional inputs, a stand, and capture capabilities with a handheld, and at a lower price. The AVerMedia X’TRA GO, for instance, offers all of that for $160. The differentiator here is the internal M.2 slot that can support up to 4TB of NVMe storage, with points to mount 2280, 2242, and 2230-sized drives. Of course, this external storage only works with platforms that normally support external storage (so, not the Switch or Switch 2).

For PC-adjacent handhelds running Windows or Linux, the Mount Ling 2 Pro is an expensive but useful dock that gives you capture, storage, and port expandability in one attractive package. For the Switch and Switch 2, the usefulness isn’t as clear. You can’t use the SSD with either platform, and Nintendo’s game of whack-a-mole with third-party accessories means a system update could break HDMI passthrough (and, in the process, the capture function of the dock).

DockCase says the Mount Ling 2 Pro supports the Steam Deck, ROG Ally, Switch, Switch 2, and “more,” presumably wrapping up other Windows- or Linux-based handhelds like the ROG Ally X Xbox or Lenovo Legion Go S. Switch and Switch 2 are supported, as well, though getting Nintendo’s handheld to connect properly isn’t always smooth.

The dock connects to your handheld with a USB 3.2 Gen Type-C connection capable of 10 Gbps, and DockCase includes a small, braided 40 Gbps cable in the box, along with two right-angle connectors. That single connection gives you up to 100W through the USB-C port, along with Gigabit Ethernet, an HDMI 2.0 connection (4K@60Hz), a single USB 3.2 Gen 2 Type-A port, and a USB 2.0 port. An additional USB-C connection serves as another USB 3.2 Gen 2 port on the Mount Ling 2, while it becomes the capture card output on the Pro model.

Interestingly, there’s also an optical audio output available. Although I don’t suspect most users will put it to use (HDMI transfers audio, as well), it’s a nice fallback if you plan on using the dock with a more elaborate audio setup. It’s a nice bonus, though I would’ve liked a 3.5mm audio jack as well, considering handhelds no longer have access to that connection.

The dock itself is constructed almost entirely of plastic, and holding it in your hands, you can tell it’s filled with more air than hardware. It’s a sizable package at 8.4 x 7 x 3.5 inches. Outside of the shell, there’s a thick layer of firm silicone to rest your handheld on. There are grooves to provide a natural resting place for just about any handheld, as well as a deep central cut that fits the Switch and Switch 2 specifically.

You can install your SSD at the bottom of the dock, with mounting points for 2280, 2242, and 2230 drives. There's a single 40mm fan internally, as well as venting around the back and side edges, to keep the drive cool. The fan will automatically adjust its speed out of the box, but you’re free to manually adjust the speed or turn the fan off, if you want.

Unfortunately, there’s no room for a heatsink. The face of the drive sits nearly flush against the vented cover on the bottom of the dock. Even if you left the cover off, most heatsinks would push past the rubber feet and prop the dock up.

Setting up the Mount Ling 2 Pro is mostly a matter of plugging everything in and getting on your way. The dock goes through a connecting phase when you hook up a new device, and it’s finicky. Sometimes it would drop one part of the connection, like charging, while picking up another, particularly with the Switch 2. You want to have the dock fully connected before hooking up your host.

The main page of the dock’s screen provides HDMI, SSD, and power info at a glance, which is helpful to quickly check, for example, that the HDMI passthrough is operating at the correct resolution and refresh rate without digging into the settings. A swipe to the right brings up five quick-access settings, which just take you into the settings menu, while a swipe to the right gives you access to volume and fan controls. The volume adjustment on the dock is passed through to the host.

Swiping up instead gives you the full settings menu, which has more to monitor than adjust. You can see the SSD details (including SMART monitors), the status of USB connections, and your HDMI info, andother details about the dock. For settings, there are different HDMI transfer modes, in case you run into a broken handshake, along with power delivery settings if you need to roll back to PD 2.0.

For the dock itself, you have some small control over the fan and built-in lighting. For the fan, you can adjust the speed manually, turn it off, or leave it on the default automatic mode. The lighting settings are surprisingly bare. You can choose between blue, red, or a gradient between them, and that’s it. Otherwise, you can just dim or turn off the lights. If you find the screen and lights distracting, there’s a dedicated button on the back of the dock to turn all the lights off (including the screen).

Although the screen is responsive with minimal lag between swipes and taps, the software running here isn’t perfect. Particularly with the Switch 2, if I connected things in the wrong order, the dock would force itself to reboot, only sometimes picking up the Switch 2 when it came back. DockCase offers firmware updates over USB, and hopefully it gets a patch between units shipped to fix some of these quirks.

The firmware updates are a nice sign, but they bring up an unfortunate reality of creating a device for the Switch 2 without Nintendo’s seal of approval. Both the Switch and Switch 2 are infamously fickle with third-party docks and hubs. A system update could break compatibility, forcing a firmware update.

DockCase plays up Switch 2 support on its product page, and that is a big draw. However, the dock can’t get around the limitations that Nintendo itself has placed on its handheld. You may as well fill the SSD slot with concrete if you’re docking a Switch 2, and docking the device is troublesome. It took multiple emails for me to get the Switch 2 to pass through its HDMI signal at all. You have to connect everything in a specific way, and critically, have the Switch 2 already unlocked when you plug it into the bottom USB-C port; the top port won’t work.

With the Steam Deck, everything was seamless, and although I didn’t have a Windows handheld on hand to try out, I don’t anticipate there will be major issues. DockCase may offer a lot of functionality in a single box with the Mount Ling Pro 2, but it’s all operating over standard connections that are well supported in the Windows ecosystem.

You can use the SSD with the Steam Deck, though you’ll need to head to desktop mode to partition the drive and add a folder for Steam games. The process is similar on Windows handhelds if you’re using some sort of handheld-native overlay.

For the capture portion, it serves its purpose. Critically, DockCase doesn’t have any software for capturing or recording like the mainstream capture cards you’ll find from Elgato and AVerMedia, so you’ll need to use something else like OBS. For any of these types of recording/streaming platforms, the DockCase shows up as a simple video capture device, with resolution and frame rate determined by your settings on the host device (assuming it’s within that 4K60 limit that HDMI 2.0 is capable of).

There isn’t much of a need here for HDMI 2.1, considering the types of devices connecting to the Mount Ling Pro 2, though it would’ve been a nice upside. You can still push the refresh rate higher, just not at 4K. And in fairness to DockCase, the devices it’s targeting really have no business driving a 4K display.

You shouldn’t expect wonders out of SSD performance with the Mount Ling 2 Pro, given that it uses a USB 3.2 Gen 2 connection that’s only capable of up to 10 Gbps. High performance in an external enclosure is the game of Thunderbolt 5 docks with M.2 storage, which can often reach sequential reads above 5,000 MB/s over the 80 Gbps Thunderbolt 5 interface. Here, the connection will bottleneck your speeds, all the way down to typical PCIe 3.0 NVMe SSDs.

Still, in the context of gaming on a handheld, that isn’t the end of the world. It’s nice to have that much storage on tap for a device like the Steam Deck, which is otherwise only expandable with a larger internal SSmicroSDicro SD card.

In CrystalDiskMark, the Mount Ling 2 Pro landed with sequential reads and writes around 820 MB/s, with random performance far lower. We tested with a 2 TB WD_Black SN770, which is a PCIe 4.0 drive that boasts sequential reads up to 5150 MB/s when installed directly in a motherboard. In the Mount Ling 2 Pro, it behaves more like an external SSD like the SK hynix Beetle X31 2TB, naturally hamstrung by the connection.

The Mount Ling 2 Pro does a lot, combining external storage, a capture card, and an attractive dock into a single package that works with both PC handhelds and the Nintendo Switch 2. The SSD performance isn’t great, as is to be expected out of a dock that uses a 10 Gbps USB link for connection, but that downside isn’t much of a concern considering the devices this dock targets.

Although Switch 2 support is a big upside, it’s also fickle. You don’t get access to the SSD, and the connection can fail if you don’t follow the order of operations for connection. The Mount Ling 2 Pro makes sense if you have a Switch 2 and a PC handheld of some kind, but as a pure Switch 2 dock, it doesn’t make much sense. You’d be better off buying an external capture card for use with the official dock.

Ozempic and similar drugs have a surprising benefit that goes beyond weight loss

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

New research suggests that Ozempic and Wegovy may reduce long-term sick leave, benefiting employers and taxpayers without increasing workers' earnings.

Ozempic is best known for lowering blood sugar and body weight. But does better health also mean more time at work and higher earnings? A study from Denmark found that the answer isn’t so simple.

Adults there who started Ozempic spent less time on long-term sick leave in the years that followed. But they were no more likely to hold a job than similar adults who hadn’t started the drug yet, and their income didn’t rise.

The money saved went to employers and local governments, which pay for sick leave in Denmark, instead of to the patients themselves.

Meltem Daysal of the University of Copenhagen and four colleagues, including economists at the ROCKWOOL Foundation and Duke University, carried out the analysis.

Their paper is an early release that the authors may still revise, and it compares patients in national health and tax records instead of assigning anyone to take the drug.

Ozempic, a brand of the drug semaglutide, became available in Denmark in August 2018.

During the study years, it was approved there for adults whose type 2 diabetes wasn’t under control with diet, exercise and earlier medicines.

The team started with 9,794 Danes who began taking Ozempic in its first 17 months on sale.

Comparing them with people who never took it would mislead, because patients who start a new diabetes drug tend to differ from those who don’t.

So the researchers paired each early starter with someone who began Ozempic four years later. Each pair was matched on age, sex, diabetes and obesity, and then on income, education, marriage and how often they saw a family doctor.

The matching produced 7,011 pairs. Until the later starters began their own treatment, they showed what might have happened to the early starters without the drug.

Everyone was between 30 and 59 at the end of 2017. That way, all of them would still be under 65, and more likely to be working, when the study ended.

In the United States, many patients stop these drugs early. One study of American patients with private insurance found that only 40% to 60% were still taking them after a year.

The Danish patients stuck with it. Four years after their first prescription, 75% of the early starters were still filling prescriptions for semaglutide, and most of those who quit did so in the first year.

Ozempic is subsidized in Denmark, where a month’s supply cost 1,117 Danish kroner, about $212, in 2019. A patient’s yearly out-of-pocket spending on subsidized prescriptions was capped at 4,300 kroner, about $681.

Earlier research has often blamed side effects when patients quit. The authors suggested that what patients pay may also play a big part in whether they stay on the drug for years.

In this study, sick leave counted as long-term once a spell ran past 30 days. Before starting Ozempic, the patients spent about 5.5% of their months on long-term sick leave.

Over the four years after the first prescription, that share fell by about 1 percentage point compared with the later starters, a drop of 17.3%. Put simply, long sick leave became about a sixth less common among the people taking the drug.

In the first two years, the early starters spent about 0.8 percentage points less of their time on long sick leave than the later starters. In years three and four, the gap reached about 1.1 points.

Payments from local governments, which cover sick leave after the first 30 days, fell by 19.4% over the same period.

The authors interpreted the decline as evidence of a treatment effect, noting that sick leave had followed similar patterns in both groups before the early starters began treatment.

The decline appeared in nearly every group the team checked when it split patients by sex, age and education. Women had the largest drop, while the estimates for men and for people under 50 were less certain.

The records can’t show exactly why sick leave fell, but the team found some clues in them.

After starting Ozempic, patients were less likely to visit an emergency department and less likely to fill prescriptions for heart and blood vessel medicines.

Earlier randomized trials, including one from 2016, found that semaglutide lowers the risk of serious heart problems such as heart attacks and strokes in high-risk patients. Events like those often keep a person away from work for weeks.

The authors called that a plausible explanation for the drop in sick leave, not a proven one. Their records include no weight or blood sugar measurements, so the team couldn’t track how each patient’s health changed.

Over the same four years, the team found no change in whether patients had jobs, whether they were in the workforce at all, or how much they earned.

The estimates were precise enough to rule out a rise in employment of more than 0.4 percentage points and a rise in income of more than 0.7%.

The authors traced this to the way Denmark pays for illness.

Employers cover the first 30 days of a worker’s sick leave, and the local government takes over after that. For workers who earn below a cap, those payments can replace all of their usual pay.

So when a worker missed fewer weeks, their income stayed about the same. The savings went to employers and taxpayers.

The team estimated those savings at about 4,163 kroner a year for each employed patient, or 1.3% of a year’s earnings. If shorter sick spells fell by the same share, the total would reach 4,766 kroner, about $722, or 1.5% of earnings.

For Denmark’s budget, this means the drug costs the public less than its pharmacy price suggests. Even so, the authors wrote, the savings remain far smaller than what GLP-1 treatments cost in 2026.

Sick pay in the United States is less generous than in Denmark. There, the authors suggested, similar health gains might show up instead as higher earnings, more hours, or fewer people losing their jobs.

A 2026 study of American women who started GLP-1 drugs to lose weight found large increases in employment among those who hadn’t been working.

The Danish authors wrote that the two studies differ in both their setting and their patients.

The team also checked Wegovy, the semaglutide brand approved in Denmark for weight management.

Among 67,736 new Wegovy users, long-term sick leave fell by about 1 percentage point within the first year, and jobs and income again didn’t rise.

Less than a year of records is too short to judge the long run, and the authors treated the Wegovy job and income results with caution.

Whether the gains last for people who take these drugs mainly to lose weight is still an open question.

Answering it will take longer records of weight-loss patients, ideally from countries where a sick day costs workers more than it does in Denmark.

The study appeared as an IZA Discussion Paper.

Early Medical Pain Can Produce a Lasting Memory

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Posted November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Several years ago, I worked with a woman who experienced trauma on the first day of her life. She was born with Rh incompatibility, a potentially deadly condition that occurs when an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive baby and her immune system attacks the baby's red blood cells. This woman required a blood transfusion in which all of the blood in her body was drained and was replaced with transfused blood. This medical procedure saved her life, but she is experiencing lasting psychological adverse effects from the trauma associated with this procedure.

As a medical student, I remember watching an infant with his arms and legs strapped down to a board in order for the doctor to perform a circumcision. As the baby cried and screamed, the doctor performing the procedure said that the young boy would not remember this procedure years later. No anesthesia was used. Was the doctor correct? I have wondered about this for more than 40 years, and I can tell you that my research into epigenetic memory has taught me that he was probably wrong.

As a father, I took my 2-year-old daughter to a routine pediatrician's appointment for a vaccination. She cried after the shot, but otherwise seemed OK. The doctor told us she would probably have a sore leg the next day, but Tylenol would take care of it. Well, the next day, our daughter wouldn't walk on her sore leg. She cried and cried, despite our efforts to manage her pain with Tylenol and Ibuprofen. Well, years later, our daughter grew up to be one of the most courageous women I know. She climbed every 14,000-foot mountain in Colorado—all 58 of them! But she has had to deal with a lifelong fear of needles. If she has blood drawn, she has to lie down because she is likely to pass out if she sees the needle. For years, I have wondered: Is her fear of needles related to that early vaccination?

For much of the 20th century, physicians believed that newborns didn’t feel pain. Infants commonly underwent surgery with little or no anesthesia. That belief began to change in 1987, when a review published in The New England Journal of Medicine showed that newborns can experience pain, and they can also experience powerful stress responses due to the pain.

This raises the question: Can pain experienced early in life create a memory that persists for years? Research involving epigenetic memory suggests it is possible.

Epigenetics refers to chemical modifications that alter how genes are expressed without changing the DNA sequence. These modifications help cells “remember” what has previously happened.

In 2004, a study in Nature Neuroscience showed that rat pups receiving little maternal care developed epigenetic modifications to a gene in the hippocampus that left them with exaggerated stress responses as adults. Five years later, another study found similar changes in the brains of suicide victims who had been abused as children. These studies suggested that early trauma can be remembered by modifications to our cells’ DNA.

Strong evidence in humans linking medical procedures to epigenetic change comes from studies involving premature infants. These babies may undergo about 10 painful procedures per day, including heel lances, needle sticks, intubation, and suctioning.

An Italian study found that very preterm infants exposed to more skin-breaking procedures showed increased changes to the serotonin transporter gene between birth and discharge. Serotonin helps regulate mood, anxiety, and pain. A follow-up study linked these changes to greater negative emotionality at 3 months of age.

These changes may persist. Researchers in Canada found that at age 7, children born very preterm had greater epigenetic changes than full-term peers. Neonatal pain predicted that epigenetic changes were associated with more behavior problems later in life. This same group later connected greater neonatal procedural pain to physical changes in the brains of these children at the age of 8.

Healthy, full-term infants are not immune to these trauma-induced changes. Newborn circumcision is one of the most common surgical procedures performed in the United States, and historically it was often done without anesthesia.

In a 1995 study, researchers noticed that circumcised boys cried longer and showed more pain behavior during vaccinations at 4 to 6 months of age than uncircumcised boys. Their prospective 1997 study confirmed it. Circumcised infants reacted more strongly to routine vaccination months later than those who had received a topical anesthetic before circumcision.

In other words, a single painful event in the first days of life appeared to change the infant’s pain system, and pain relief partly prevented it. Whether epigenetic changes underlie this effect has not been directly tested, but the pattern of lasting sensitization from early pain fits closely with the previously described findings.

Toddlers and school-age children remember procedures as well. A review found that a minority of children develop posttraumatic stress symptoms after surgery, and 12 to 31 percent of children undergoing cardiac surgery develop posttraumatic stress disorder. Medical fear in childhood can also shape how people approach health care as adults.

The evidence is preliminary that these changes are due to epigenetic memory. But it is encouraging to note that epigenetic changes appear to be potentially reversible, and maternal sensitivity can reduce these effects in full-term infants.

Necessary procedures should not be avoided because of fear. But the evidence argues that we should be taking infant and child pain seriously. Pickle Juice Vs Buttermilk: Which Marinade Is Best For Fried Chicken?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

When it comes to down-home Southern cooking, buttermilk and pickles both have a prominent place thanks to their flavors and how they affect the chemistry of cooking. Todd Johnson, Proprietor of Strips Chicken & Brewing, insists that both have a place in fried chicken — although he asserts that buttermilk is the better all-around choice.

"If you're going to marinate your chicken, buttermilk wins," Johnson told Food Republic. "It's gently acidic, so it softens the surface of the chicken without turning it mushy. It's also thick enough to leave a tacky layer that flour clings to. That's the foundation of a craggy, crunchy crust." Just about any marinade can infuse the muscle fibers with moisture and keep it more tender, but it's that combination of subtle acid, sugars, and milk solids that gives it a more well-rounded, heartier flavor and makes it such an essential ingredient in fried chicken. Since it lacks the potency of a pickle brine, it also tends to absorb other seasonings quite well, so you don't have to rely solely on your breading.

While Johnson admits to being "not a fan of pickle juice as a marinade," he noted that it can have its place. "It's already salty and acidic, so it seasons and moistens the meat quickly," he explained. "You'll notice results in a couple of hours instead of overnight." Still, its high acidity means you have a narrower window between tender and mushy chicken, so it's not an ideal choice for beginners. Brine is a pretty potent ingredient, so while it's effective, it also calls for an experienced hand unless you're willing to experiment.

Good fried chicken has three components: meat, breading, and oil. Buttermilk is such a mainstay in recipes because it has a delicious effect on all three of these, promoting flavor, browning, and texture.

One of many major mistakes everyone makes with fried chicken is creating a breading that doesn't stick, and dry ingredients aren't the only contributor to this. "Oil and fat act like a release agent between meat and crust," Todd Johnson explained. "That's why we use fat-free buttermilk." For this aspect, it may seem like pickle brine is the right choice, but Johnson said that its lack of fat comes at the cost of a thinner consistency. Fat-free buttermilk is far more adhesive, giving your dry ingredients a prime surface to cling to, so they won't fall off the meat once they hit the oil.

Johnson conceded that buttermilk has a tendency to "over-brown" if your oil gets too hot, burning the milk sugars and solids, fouling the flavor. This is a pretty hefty disadvantage, but one that can be turned to an advantage with a careful eye. If you maintain Johnson's recommended oil temperature of 350 degrees Fahrenheit, you can toast, rather than burn, those solids, delivering a robust flavor profile focused on a hearty, umami-rich breading. Buttermilk is also a more convenient option, as you can just remove the excess drippings and drop your meat directly into your breading.

When you're looking to make fried chicken on the fly or don't have a surplus of seasoning, there are few options better than pickle brine. Todd Johnson said it's indispensable for achieving that "sandwich shop flavor" that some folks prefer, even if it has its quirks.

While you can always add some salt to your buttermilk, it won't have quite the same effect as using a jar of leftover pickle brine. "It's already salty and acidic, so it seasons and moistens the meat quickly," Todd Johnson clarified. Its extremely high and well-dissolved sodium content loosens up muscle fibers, making them far better at retaining moisture. While it lacks the rich solids and sugars of buttermilk, it brings far more tang, so it'll cut through even the heartiest, most marbled pieces of fried meat quite easily.

The biggest downside to pickle juice in your marinade is inconsistency and lack of other seasoning options. Not all brines are made the same, so unless you work with the exact same brand and product every time, there's always some amount of guess work involved. While pickle juice is one of the most powerful vehicles for flavor, it also leaves very little room for anything more subtle or nuanced. After marinating your meat, you'll also need to carefully pat dry each piece before tossing it in a batter or dry ingredients. Excess surface moisture creates a steam barrier between your chicken and breading, so even a bit left on the surface can ruin your food.
