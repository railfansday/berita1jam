Hackers exploited iOS and Android flaws to infect phones

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Researchers say group may have been Kremlin-sponsored in search of diplomatic targets.

Hackers possibly linked to the Kremlin exploited previously patched iOS and Android flaws in an attempt to hack visitors of multiple websites, including one celebrating the 250th anniversary of the signing of the US Constitution, researchers said Wednesday.

One of the impersonated sites was america250.org, belonging to a non-partisan group of lawmakers, business executives, and at least one former member of the Trump administration. The attackers registered america250[.]holiday and used it as a reverse proxy to america250.org. The imposter website delivered content from the real one that added malicious JavaScript into it.

“We assess that the intrusion set may have deliberately selected this website due to its association with a high-profile national celebration, potentially leveraging the event as a pretext for invitation-themed spear-phishing campaigns,” researchers from security firm Sekoia said.

A second website, theowo[.]club, proxied the website Raffles of London OWO, a luxury hotel physically close to some of the UK’s most sensitive government institutions, including the Ministry of Defence and 10 Downing Street, the official residence and office of the British Prime Minister. Open source reporting indicates that Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy stayed there during a visit on October 14.

“By doing this watering hole [impersonating] the Raffles London at The OWO, the intrusion set may have sought to target diplomatic delegations and high-profile guests through targeted spear-phishing campaigns,” the researchers said.

The attackers also delivered their attacks through compromised websites—including a top Ukrainian gas production company—and a Ukrainian web analytics service that serves thousands of websites. Sekoia said its attribution to the Kremlin is based on the targets’ profiles and similarities to a previous attack attributed to APT29, one of the names the suspected Russian group is tracked under.

The hackers relied on three infection vectors: direct JavaScript injection into traffic from legitimate websites, proxy domains such as america250[.]holiday, and the supply-chain compromise of the Ukrainian web analytics service. In each case, the injected JavaScript was the same. During the first stage, the JavaScript profiled the visitor’s browser and device. The attackers then used that information to deliver one of three zero-click exploit chains, which work without requiring any interaction from the target.

One of the chains was DarkSword, an iOS exploit kit that Google researchers said customers of PARS Defense, a commercial surveillance vendor in Turkey, and at least two other threat actors have used. Sekoia said the more recent campaign used two other exploit chains that weren’t named.

All of the vulnerabilities exploited in the campaign were “n-days,” meaning they had already been patched and attacks worked only on outdated versions of iOS or Android.

This isn't a bug. It's the default.

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 4 • Originally published at randomchaos.us

A machine running OpenAI's Codex wrote about 37 TB to its main SSD in 21 days of uptime. Process and file-level checks pinned the continuous writer to one place: Codex's local SQLite feedback log at ~/.codex/logs_2.sqlite. At that rate the drive takes roughly 640 TB a year, about 640 full-drive writes on a 1 TB SSD. Many consumer drives are rated near 600 TBW, so Codex alone can burn a drive's warranted write endurance in under a year.

The database looks small. It retains about half a million rows and sits around 1.2 GiB. The SQLite AUTOINCREMENT counter tells the real story: it has advanced past 5.5 billion ids, a roughly 10,000x gap between rows kept and rows ever inserted. In one 15-second sample about 36,211 rows went in while the retained count never moved. Codex inserts a batch, indexes it, writes it to the WAL, then prunes inside the same transaction. The file size stays flat and the disk keeps taking writes, which is why drive wear rather than database growth is the first visible signal.

The cause is a single line. The persistent SQLite sink is attached with a global TRACE default, Targets::new().with_default(Level::TRACE), in both the app-server and the TUI. Every target gets persisted at TRACE, so the database fills with logs no one chose to keep. The default log level, not a stray code path, is what writes the terabytes, and every install ships this way.

The high-frequency rows are dependency noise and raw protocol traffic: inotify events (128,764 OPEN events for ld.so.cache, 23,843 for passwd, more for nsswitch.conf and locale.alias), thousands of tokio-tungstenite poll_read and with_context entries, websocket frame fields, and repeated OpenTelemetry mirror rows from codex_otel.log_only and codex_otel.trace_safe. TRACE alone is 70.7% of retained bytes; the two OTel mirror targets add another 25.3%. Dropping those categories removes about 96% of retained log bytes without turning feedback logging off. Raw websocket and SSE payload bodies are also persisted, and the reporter left them out of the public sample because they can contain private conversation content.

A large logs DB does more than wear the SSD. On startup the app-server's SQLite pool can time out (#27741), and a 419 MB logs DB alongside session state has produced 1-to-5-minute cold starts on WSL2 (#28166). One commenter reports that Codex in /goal mode will delete files and folders trying to free space when the disk is full, so a full drive becomes data loss, and a Linux box rebooted while full can fail to log in.

Three PRs merged by late June 2026 and, per the reporter's own Codex, cut persisted logs by about 85%. #29432 stops logging every Responses websocket event and #29457 filters noisy targets, both in 0.142.0; #29599 stops persisting bridged log events in 0.143.0. OpenAI says the series of fixes is in the latest shipping desktop app, CLI, and IDE extension.

Not all of it has shipped. A later comment notes 0.144.1 is missing #31789 through #31792, which appear in 0.145.0-alpha.4 and presumably 0.145.0. A separate root cause, tracked in #32496, has Codex continually rewriting models_cache.json and scope_v3.json for roughly another 1.3 TB a year, well under the logging figure but still idle I/O for nothing. Testing with GPT 5.6 Sol High on macOS, one commenter measured unmitigated churn falling from 33.1 to 7.36 inserted ids per second after the upstream fixes, with a local filter taking it to 0.45.

On a build without the fixes, quit Codex and install a trigger that rejects log inserts outright:

That blocks every insert, feedback logging included, but it stops the writes at once. Several people who built working filter patches could not upstream them: GitHub returned "does not have the correct permissions to execute CreatePullRequest" on their accounts, so the real fixes waited on maintainers either way.

Before and after slider in React, accessible and draggable

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 4 • Originally published at facadeui.com

Roofers, painters, landscapers and remodelers all have the same proof: a photo from before the job and a photo from after. Put the two side by side and the eye wanders. Put them in one frame with a handle the visitor drags and the eye follows the handle. That is why the comparison slider earns a spot on every home services site I build, and why I spent more time on the Before / after slider in Facade than its size suggests. Most of that time went into things you cannot see in a demo: touch, keyboard and screen readers.

The first version of every comparison slider on the internet listens to mousedown, mousemove and mouseup. Then someone opens it on a phone and nothing happens, so touchstart and touchmove get bolted on, and now there are two code paths that drift apart.

Pointer events replace both. onPointerDown, onPointerMove and onPointerUp fire for mouse, touch and pen with the same event shape, and clientX means the same thing in all three. The one extra call that makes dragging reliable is setPointerCapture: once the visitor presses down, the element keeps receiving move events even when the finger or cursor leaves its box. Without it, a fast drag that exits the frame freezes the handle halfway.

fromClientX reads the container's bounding rect and converts the pointer position into a percentage between 0 and 100. That percentage is the only piece of state. Everything else, the clip, the handle position, the ARIA attributes, derives from it.

Listen on the whole frame, not just the handle. Visitors tap anywhere on the photo and expect the handle to jump there. A 24 pixel handle as the only hit target is a usability bug on touch screens.

The after photo fills the frame. The before photo sits on top of it in an absolutely positioned layer, and clip-path: inset(0 X% 0 0) cuts that layer off on the right at the handle position. Dragging changes X and the before photo reveals or hides the after photo underneath.

Clip-path is the right tool because the two images never move or resize. A common alternative is to change the width of the top layer, which stretches the image unless you also counter-scale it, and that path leads to subpixel jitter during a drag. With clip-path both images stay pixel aligned and the browser only repaints the clipped region.

The trick is in one effect: while dragging, the spring jumps straight to the value so the handle follows the finger one to one; when the visitor releases or taps somewhere, the spring eases to the new position. Reduced motion users get the jump in both cases.

Online Course Tutor Retrieval Architecture: Designing Fallbacks That Keep Answers Grounded

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Short answer: use a staged retrieval design with explicit collections, bounded queries, and traceable source context; make each fallback narrower and more conservative than the previous stage.

An online course tutor is a small production system with an awkward failure mode: a plausible answer backed by the wrong lesson is harder to notice than an obvious error. I have been paged for missed jobs and duplicate deliveries, so I treat retrieval the same way I treat queue work: every stage needs a contract, a timeout, and an idempotent handoff.

The contract starts with the answer the student should see. A question about “the gradient descent lab” should return a lesson section, its course and tenant, and a citation anchor. If the first pass cannot satisfy those fields, it must say so and move to a defined fallback. Silent broadening is how cross-course answers leak into a tutor.

Keep ingestion, querying, and citation as separate observable stages. Ingestion normalizes a representative set of lessons, preserves tenant and access-control metadata on every indexed item, and records a stable document identifier. Querying searches the course collection first, with a bounded top-k and a deadline. Citation assembly checks that returned identifiers still belong to the requesting tenant before the language model sees them.

Fallback one can widen within the same course: a lexical match over titles and headings catches an unusual term that embeddings missed. Fallback two can search a curated, tenant-wide collection, but it should require a stronger confidence threshold and return fewer passages. The final fallback is an explicit “I could not find this in the course material” response, with a path for the instructor to add or correct a source.

That last step matters. A tutor that always produces text has no useful failure signal.

I don't let a timeout silently promote a broader search. In one runbook review, the dangerous path was a retry that reused the old broad query after the course index had recovered; the answer looked fine, but its citation belonged to a neighboring course. The fix was a stage token carried through the trace, a maximum of three retrieval attempts, and a final authorization check against the enrollment snapshot. That sequence takes more lines than a single search call, yet it makes a duplicate delivery and a late response converge on the same decision. The tutor either has an approved source or returns no answer.

I model each retrieval result as a record, not a loose chunk:

The check is deliberately boring. A score is not authorization. A high-scoring chunk from another tenant is still unusable, and a low-scoring chunk should not be promoted merely because the first query timed out.

Give every stage a budget and a reason code. For example, course_vector_timeout, course_low_recall, and tenant_lexical_hit are more useful in a runbook than one boolean called retrieval_failed. The application can then alert on a rising fallback rate without paging on a single hard question.

Evaluation needs representative documents and failure cases: short definitions, long lab instructions, duplicated slide text, misspelled terms, and questions that intentionally have no answer. Measure recall for finding the right section and precision for keeping irrelevant sections out. Also test a query whose only matching passage has a revoked enrollment; the expected result is no citation, not a clever guess.

A practical trace records the collection, stage, query hash, result IDs, scores, elapsed time, and the final citation IDs. Do not log raw student prompts by default. Retain enough context to replay a miss while respecting the tenant's data policy.

The write path should be equally explicit. A collection is created before ingestion, vectors are upserted with the metadata needed for authorization, and deletion removes both the item and its collection membership when a course is retired. These are separate state transitions, so a retry worker can safely repeat a transition keyed by the document ID.

The choice is less about a leaderboard score than about who owns the operational boundary. Pinecone is a managed vector service with a focused API. Weaviate offers an open-source and managed path with a broader schema layer. Postgres with pgvector keeps vectors beside transactional course and enrollment data, which can simplify joins and policy checks. Infrai is another option when a team wants one plain REST API and no SDK to install; its discovery surface is public and self-describing, and its wider backend surface can keep adjacent services behind one key.

The catch is that a broad interface does not remove retrieval design work. Use a direct specialist when you need a feature your evaluation depends on, such as a particular hybrid-ranking implementation, tight database transactions, or a self-hosted deployment mandate. Stick with Postgres when the authorization join is the hard part. Choose a managed vector service when your team cannot own index capacity and recovery. Your mileage may vary because corpus shape and enrollment rules dominate the result.

In a real worker, add the document identity and idempotency key required by the discovered schema, retry 429 responses with bounded exponential backoff, and emit the stage trace before acknowledging the queue message. Never send the credential to a presigned URL or expose it to a browser.

I would not use this sample as a complete ingestion client. It is a control-path sketch: discovery supplies the exact schema, while the tutor owns authorization, retries, and citation policy. That division keeps a provider change from rewriting the answer contract.

Start with a course-scoped collection and a strict citation contract. Add lexical fallback only after measuring misses on representative lessons. Add tenant-wide search only with an explicit policy and a higher threshold. Keep a no-answer outcome, because it is the only fallback that cannot invent a source.

Before launch, replay duplicate upserts and deletes, revoke a student's enrollment mid-query, and inject timeouts at each stage. Confirm that every emitted citation maps to the requested tenant and course. Then watch fallback rate, citation rejection rate, and unanswered-question samples as first-class SRE signals.

The Souls of Animals

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every month, I spend hundreds of hours and thousands of dollars keeping The Marginalian going. For two decades, it has remained free, ad-free, AI-free, fully human and alive thanks to patronage from readers. I have no staff, no interns, not even an assistant — a thoroughly one-woman labor of love that is also my life and my livelihood. For an act of resistance to the tyranny of algorithms, try the Marginalian newsletter—undistracted notes on the search for meaning, free, ad-free, AI-free, fully human since 2006. (Here is an example.)

“They do not sweat and whine about their condition,” Walt Whitman wrote of the other animals, “they do not lie awake in the dark and weep for their sins, they do not make me sick discussing their duty to God, not one is dissatisfied, not one is demented with the mania of owning things.”

Here was “the poet of the body and the poet of the soul” holding up a mirror to us creatures inhabiting an animal body complicated by a soul — that organ of want and worry which we ourselves invented to explain why we make art, why we fall in love, why we yearn to converse with reality in prayers and postulates.

It is daring enough to ask what a soul actually is. Carl Jung knew that it defies the substance we are made of: “The soul is partly in eternity and partly in time.” Virginia Woolf knew that it defies our best technology of thought: “One can’t write directly about the soul. Looked at, it vanishes.” It is doubly daring to question the age-old dogma that the soul is the province of the human animal alone. Even as we have incrementally and reluctantly admitted other creatures into the temple of consciousness, we have denied them souls — denied them, because our tools of communication and computation have failed to probe it, an inner life capable of imagination and play, of love and grief, of dreams and wonder. And yet our very language defies our denial: the word animal comes from the Latin for soul.

In 1991, long before we came to consider the soul of an octopus, long before fMRI and EEG studies revealed not only that birds dream but what they dream about, Gary Kowalski took up this daring question in The Souls of Animals (public library) — an inquiry into the “spiritual lives” (and into what that means) of whooping cranes, elephants, jackdaws, gorillas, songbirds, horses, dogs, and cats. At its center is the idea that spirituality — which he defines as “the development of a moral sense, the appreciation of beauty, the capacity for creativity, and the awareness of one’s self within a larger universe as well as a sense of mystery and wonder about it all” — is a natural byproduct of “the biological order and in the ecology shared by all life.” (There are in this view echoes of Kepler, who believed that the Earth itself is an ensouled body, and of myriad native cosmogonies that regard other animals as sources of more-than-human wisdom and emissaries of the numinous.)

Kowalski — a parish minister by vocation, who spends his days praying with the dying, blessing bonds of love, and helping people navigate moral quandaries — celebrates the soul as “the magic of life,” as that which “gives life its sublimity and grandeur,” and reflects:

For ancient peoples, the soul was located in the breath or the blood. For me, soul resides at the point where our lives intersect with the timeless, in our love of goodness, our passion for beauty, our quest for meaning and truth. In asking whether animals have souls, we are inquiring whether they share in the qualities that make life more than a mere struggle for survival, endowing existence with dignity and élan.

Many people think of soul as the element of personality that survives bodily death, but for me it refers to something much more down-to-earth. Soul is the marrow of our existence as sentient, sensitive beings. It’s soul that’s revealed in great works of art, and soul that’s lifted up in awe when we stand in silence under a night sky burning with billions of stars. When we speak of a soulful piece of music, we mean one that comes out of infinite depths of feeling. When we speak of the soul of a nation, we mean its capacity for valor and visionary change… Soul is present wherever our lives intersect the dimension of the holy: in moments of intimacy, in flights of fancy, and in rituals that hallow the evanescent events of our lives with enduring significance. Soul is what makes each of our lives a microcosm — not merely a meaningless fragment of the universe, but at some level a reflection of the whole.

Half a century after Henry Beston insisted that “we need another and a wiser and perhaps a more mystical concept of animals,” for they are “gifted with extensions of the senses we have lost or never attained, living by voices we shall never hear,” Kowalski writes:

Without anthropomorphizing our nonhuman relations we can acknowledge that animals share many human characteristics. They have individual likes and dislikes, moods and mannerisms, and possess their own integrity, which suffers when not respected. They play and are curious about their world. They develop friendships and sometimes risk their own lives to help others. They have “animal faith,” a spontaneity and directness that can be most refreshing… all the traits indicative of soul. For soul is not something we can see or measure. We can observe only its outward manifestations: in tears and laughter, in courage and heroism, in generosity and forgiveness. Soul is what’s behind-the-scenes in the tough and tender moments when we are most intensely and grippingly alive.

By investigating the inner lives of other creatures, Kowalski argues, we are invariably deepening our own:

As [modern] shamans, we are allowed to examine enigmas like “What makes us human?” and “What makes life sacred?” We can ask not only about the mating behavior and survival strategies of other animals but whether they have souls and spirits like our own. The danger here is that we are often in over our heads. But at least we are swimming in deep water and out of the shallows. In searching for answers to such queries, I have found, we not only enrich our understanding of other creatures, we also gain insight into ourselves.

There is an inwardness in other living beings that awakens what is innermost in ourselves. I have often marveled, for instance, watching a flock of shore birds. On an invisible cue, they simultaneously rise off the beach and into the air, then turn and bank seawards in tight formation. They are so finely coordinated and attuned in their aeronautics it is as though they share a common thought, or even a group mind, guiding their ascent. At such moments, I feel there are depths of “inner space” in nature that can never be sounded. And it is out of those same depths, in me, that awe arises as I contemplate the synchronicity of their flight. To contain such depths is to participate in the realm of spirit.

We have invented no greater expression of our inwardness than music — the language of the soul, with its eternal translation between mathematics and mystery. We know that other animals partake of that language — each spring birds sing the world back to life, each summer cicadas serenade the sun with their living mandolin, and when we set out to tell the cosmos who we are, a whale song joined Bulgarian folk music and Bach on The Golden Record.

Birds, Kowalski observes, sing for reasons beyond the pragmatic — their song is “far from a mechanical performance” and “much more complex than a simple cry of self-assertion.” It is music, which is distinguished from noise by an organizing principle of creative intent, and creativity may be the purest evidence of soul. Kowalski writes:

Surprisingly, many birds are relatively insensitive to pitch. But the best singers employ all the elements of tone, interval, rhythm, theme, and variation in complex and highly pleasing combinations. And what is music if not the deliberate arrangement of sound in aesthetic patterns?

Greatly influenced by philosopher Martin Buber’s I-Thou model of relating, Kowalski admonishes against relying on our own frames of reference in assaying what other creatures are expressing and how it is being expressed:

The tempo of life is faster-paced for birds than for people. This is one of the reasons the individual notes in bird song are so short, sometimes distinguishable only with a spectrograph, and why the compositions of birds last a few seconds at most, compared to an hour or more for a human symphony. It is also why birds sing in the upper registers (just as the pitch on a phonograph record rises when played at high speed). To the birds, with a metabolism continually in allegro, human beings must appear to be lazy and dim-brained creatures indeed. Just as our music reflects the rhythm and intensity of our inner life, the music of birds expresses the flash and flutter of their nervous and high-strung existence.

Examining another subset of the creative impulse — visual art — Kowalski cites Desmond Morris’s famous 1950s studies, which found that non-human primates given pens and paints not only became adept at using them with “a distinct feel for symmetry and balance,” but developed individual styles of drawing. He considers what that indicates:

Art arises from a spiritual longing that all people share: to make our mark on the world and to spend our life energy in a work that rises above the mundane, adding grace to existence. We respond to the light of the world around us by giving expression to our own inner light, and when the two are on the same wavelength, the world seems more brilliant and finely focused.

Insisting that such spiritual longings do not belong to human beings alone, he cites an astonishing case study:

In 1982 Jerome Witkin, a professor of art at Syracuse University and a respected authority on abstract expressionism, was invited to view a collection of drawings by a “mystery artist.” The professor was busy at the time, preparing for a traveling exhibition. Nevertheless, he was sufficiently intrigued to accept the invitation.

“These drawings are very lyrical, very, very beautiful,” the professor said when he saw the portfolio. “They are so positive and affirmative and tense, the energy is so compact and controlled, it’s just incredible.”

“This piece is so graceful, so delicate,” he said of one drawing. “I can’t get most of my students to fill a page like this.”

Only after he had finished his professional evaluation did Witkin learn the identity of the artist: a fourteen-year-old, 8,400-pound Asian elephant named Siri who lived in Syracuse’s Burnet Park Zoo. Siri’s keeper, David Gucwa, had seen her tracing lines with sticks and stones in the dust of her cage. Against the wishes of the zoo’s superintendent, who scoffed at the notion of an artistic elephant, Gucwa had given her pads of paper and charcoal, permitting her to express herself more freely.

When Witkin showed Siri’s drawings to a colleague without context — an expert on children’s drawings charing the university’s art education department — she firmly concluded that they were not done by a child. Witkin himself readily likened them to the work of Willem de Kooning, wishing the painter himself could see Siri’s art.

It was this report of Siri that inspired May Sarton — one of my favorite poets and favorite thinkers — to reimagine these reckonings in a poem. (The footnote of credit in Sarton’s collection is how I discovered Kowalski’s book.)

The drawings were abstract, Delicate, Like Japanese calligraphy. When the painter de Kooning Was shown them, he said, “Interesting. Not done by a child, I think, Or if so, an extraordinary child.” “The artist is an elephant, Sir, Named Siri.”

It had once come about That the keeper noticed Her sensitive trunk Drawing designs in the dust. After an argument With the head of the zoo Who laughed at him, The keeper himself Brought large sheets of paper And boxes of charcoal And laid them at Siri’s feet. For an hour at a time In happy concentration The elephant created designs. Like Japanese calligraphy. What artist’s hand As skillful As that sensuous, sensitive trunk?

Two decades after Iris Murdoch found psychological symmetry between art and morality, locating in both “an occasion for unselfing,” Kowlaski turns to the acts of selflessness and compassion that evince a moral faculty — that fundament of a soul. Pelicans and crows, he notes, have been known to care for blind comrades. Darwin himself reported of a band of monkeys coming to the aid of member seized by an eagle, at the risk of their own lives. But nothing renders such morally tinted actions more vivid and more moving than one nineteenth-century naturalist’s account of a misfire.

Working in an era when “collecting specimens” meant killing creatures, he aimed at a tern but only wounded the bird, which fell helplessly into the sea. Immediately, other terns began circling above “manifesting much apparent solicitude,” until two of them dove down toward their wounded comrade. They lifted him up, one at each wing, carried him several yards, and gently put him down before another two picked him up, and so the group took turns carrying him the entire distance to the shore. The naturalist was so moved by this display of compassion and solidarity that, although he was within shot of the rock on which the wounded tern had been rested, he couldn’t bring himself to finish what he had set out to do.

To witness such a scene is to be stilled with wonder and with humility — which, as Rachel Carson so poignantly wrote, “are wholesome emotions, and they do not exist side by side with a lust for destruction.” A generation after her, and well ahead of our still dawning awakening to the ecological and ethical dignity of other species, Kowalaski reflects:

If we are to keep our family homestead — third stone from the sun — safe for coming generations, we must awaken to a new respect for the family of life.

We are kin to, and must be kind to, all creation. Overcoming speciesism — the illusion of human superiority — will be the next step in our moral and spiritual evolution.

To behold such a display of moral feeling with our own eyes is stirring enough, but to be witnessed back by another creature’s eyes is nothing short of a spiritual experience. In a passage that calls to mind Alan Lightman’s transcendent account of looking into the eyes of an osprey, Kowalski writes:

It is difficult to probe the inward awareness of another being. The realm of what one mystic called “the interior castle” is wholly private and wrapped in solitude. But when we look into another’s eyes — even into the eyes of an animal — we may find a small window into that inner sanctum, a window through which our souls can hail and greet one another.

The act of making eye contact with another being presupposes a conscious self behind either pair of peepers: I see you seeing me, and I am aware that you are aware that we are looking at each other.

Perhaps in the end it is not we who have the power to acknowledge or deny the souls of other creatures but other creatures who confer soul-ness upon us. Kowalski writes:

If by soul we mean our sense of self, our identity as particular persons, then our souls are interwoven with those of other living beings… We know ourselves as human, in part, through our relationships with the nonhuman world.

We are rather unsure of ourselves. What distinguishes our species may be this inward anxiety. While other animals may be endowed with special gifts—acute hearing, keen eyesight, incredible speed — human beings are nothing special. This is both a biological and a moral judgment. Lack of specialization makes us highly adaptable, but it also means we have no fixed form or definite identity. Without many inborn instincts to guide us, we as human beings need models for how to live. We need a sense of our own possibilities and limits, and we find them not only in the artificial rules and restraints imposed by human society but in the lessons for living suggested by biology and the earth itself. We are the younger siblings in life’s family — the perpetual neonates of the animal world. In a fundamental way we need other creatures to tell us who we are.

Out of this arises an urgency more than ethical, more than ecological, but existential — nothing less than examining what we are and why we are here at all:

What profit do we have if we gain the whole world and lose or forfeit our own souls? The human race may survive without the chimpanzees, orangutans, and other wild creatures who share the planet. But we will have attenuated the conditions that are necessary for our own “ensoulment”… And when we look into the mirror there will be less and less to love.

There is a glimmering of eternity about our lives. In the vastness of time and space, our lives are indeed small and ephemeral, yet not utterly insignificant. Our lives do matter. Because we care for one another and have feelings, because we can dream and imagine, because we are the kinds of creatures who make music and create art, we are not merely disconnected fragments of the universe but at some level reflect the beauty and splendor of the whole. And because all life shares in One Spirit, we can recognize this indwelling beauty in other creatures.

Couple The Souls of Animals with John James Audubon — who was both a visionary ahead of his time and, like the tern-shooting naturalist, a product of its blind spots — on other minds and the secret knowledge of animals, then revisit Loren Eiseley on the wonder of being alive lensed through a bouquet of warblers and a reflection on signs vs. omens and our search for meaning lensed through a great blue heron.

Fabregas: ‘A wonderful night for Como, but must stay humble’ after 9-1 victory

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Cesc Fabregas admits the 9-1 win in Lens was ‘a wonderful night for Como, but we need to keep our feet on the ground’ as Champions League ambition starts to genuinely build.

The Lariani had won both their home games 4-1 against RB Leipzig and Manchester United, but lost 3-2 to Feyenoord, so everyone was waiting for their first ever Champions League away victory.

What they did not expect was for this to end 9-1 in Lens, with a Tasos Douvikas hat-trick, Nico Paz, Lucas Da Cunha, Marc-Oliver Kempf, Mattia Liberali, Martin Baturina and teenage debutant Matteo Papaccioli on target.

It was noted that Como are fourth in the big Champions League table midway through the league phase of the competition.

“It is also our fourth ever Champions League game, so four is the key number here,” smiled Fabregas on Sky Sport Italia.

“I’m happy, this is football and everything went our way. The lads did well, now we have to prepare for a very important match on Sunday, the ninth in a run between breaks for international duty.”

This was the sixth consecutive victory in all competition for Como, who will face Scudetto holders Inter in Serie A this weekend.

It was a truly historic occasion, as no Italian team had ever scored even seven away goals in the Champions League or its predecessor the European Cup, let alone nine.

“It is a wonderful night for Como, for all our fans and many lads here who are at the start of their careers, but they need to keep their feet on the ground, be humble, keep learning and not think everything will go well from here on.

“They must keep working to continue these performances.”

Douvikas has also now scored in each of his four Champions League games and is the current top scorer in the tournament.

However, Lens scored with a volley on a corner kick, so are yet to keep a single clean sheet in the Champions League.

“I am angry over the goal we conceded, as we changed something in our defending with the three substitutions,” confessed Fabregas.

“Plus it was scored by a former teammate of mine, I played with Ruben Aguilar at Monaco, and I told him I’d never seen you score a goal like that before! It was honestly a great goal.”

These weren’t just nine goals, but almost all of them were well-worked team affairs playing out from the back with a string of passes, the kind of football that Fabregas wants to see.

Now they will play AEK Athens at home, Real Betis away, concluding with Paris Saint-Germain and then visiting Barcelona on January 27.

“When I saw the last two games were PSG and Barcelona, I thought we needed to get as many points as possible in the early fixtures. Nothing is impossible, but these are two teams who are of a superior level to the rest.

“There is a long way to go, those are the last two weeks in January, so all I am focused on is recovering the injured players, because we are missing so many in attack especially.

“There’s a final sprint on Sunday before we will try to rest as much as possible over the break for international duty,” noted Fabregas.

It was noted there are eight left-footed players, with five of them on the field together this evening, and this is something unusual when constructing a team, with only Arsenal perhaps packing the squad with that many.

“I have a weakness for left-footed players. I think they tend to be special, those I played with tended to have a class and technique different to the others, so when playing with or against those, that interested me.

“Having said that, we didn’t plan to get so many left-footed players, it just happened. The most important thing is that they understand the football we want to play here, and today everything went our way.

“I want the whole city of Como to enjoy this moment, but from tomorrow we begin preparing for Sunday.”
