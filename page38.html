The Simulation Gap: Why Physical AI Still Needs Real-World Data

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Simulation, digital twins, and synthetic data can reduce the time, cost, and risk involved in training robots. That has encouraged work on robotics foundation models intended to operate across tasks and industries. A lot of people in our field now assume that with enough simulation, we won’t need much real-world data at all. I’m less convinced, especially as Physical AI moves from controlled research environments into live operations. Analysts now treat Physical AI as its own branch of AI: systems that sense, reason, and act in the physical world instead of producing text or images.

For testing ideas, probing edge cases, and building training simulations, it is indispensable. My concern is with systems built mostly in simulation. They tend to break when they meet a real site. I’d rather treat simulation as a multiplier. Start from a real recording of a robot in an aisle, then use simulation to vary the lighting, clutter, and people around it. One real run becomes many plausible ones; each still tied to something that happened.

A model can do well on the data it was trained on and still fail when something changes. That’s overfitting, and for a robot trained in simulation, the training data is the simulator. Researchers who study the reality gap see it as one of the main barriers to moving robots from simulation into the real world. The gap is difficult to measure, but teams relying heavily on simulation need to track how performance changes under production conditions.

In warehouses, you usually see it as a gap between the demo and the deployment. A robot runs well in one building and struggles in the next. One relies on a large seasonal workforce, the other on a stable crew; one stores small, fast-moving SKUs, the other bulky items.

In a simulator, engineers decide what exists. Objects can be labeled, events logged, and individual variables changed without disturbing the rest of the environment. That level of control makes simulation valuable for testing, but live warehouses are far less observable and far harder to isolate.

Robots share the floor with people, equipment, inventory and business processes, and the site itself keeps changing. Worse, a lot of problems only show up at scale, after you’ve already deployed thousands of robots and made the investment. A navigation model behaves until the season turns and, at one particular hour, sunlight lands on a sensor. Someone parks equipment in an aisle that’s always clear; now it’s a bottleneck. You rarely find any of this in benchmark datasets, but it often decides whether a deployment works.

AI is good at picking up patterns like these, such as when and where congestion builds. None of that shows up unless you’re collecting from live operations. Simulation can also teach a model the wrong lessons, and in manipulation it’s even more obvious. Vision models are very good at cheating, and they’ll happily latch onto a texture or render artifact that exists only in the simulator.

“World models,” which learn how a physical environment behaves and responds to a robot’s actions, are one attempt to address this problem. Nature recently took a close look at this work, and researchers see it as a possible bridge between perception, prediction and action. They don’t escape the problem, though. A world model is only as grounded as the real observations behind it. Today’s best world-model platforms still can’t capture every meaningful interaction in a complex physical environment — and a busy warehouse is full of them.

Synthetic data is most useful when it extends real-world datasets rather than standing in for them. It can add hazardous or low-frequency scenarios, such as a blocked main aisle or a forklift crossing a robot’s path. Many of the biggest Physical AI programs now lean on synthetic data to grow their training sets, move faster and show models rare conditions.

The limits come from how it’s made. Behind every synthetic dataset is an engineer deciding what to model, what to leave out, and which scenarios seem likely. Even sensible choices leave gaps because the resulting data can only represent the conditions included in its design.

Many teams now build on the same open models and published architectures, so the algorithm alone rarely separates one company from another. What differs is the operational data available to train, test, and improve those models. A navigation trace becomes far more useful when you can connect it to what the robot was doing and whether the work is finished on time. Warehouse observations get interesting when they’re linked to inventory, throughput, labor, and performance.

That’s why scale matters. A fleet spread across many sites sees cold-storage aisles, peak-season surges and night shifts that a single pilot never will. A congestion model tuned to one site’s shift change, for example, tells you little about a site that runs around the clock.

Google’s RT-2 showed that mixing web-scale knowledge with robot data can help a robot handle situations it wasn’t trained on. But many real robotics problems are still specific to one domain. A robot can pick up a box perfectly, and the building can still miss its shipping cutoff.

Does a warehouse robot need to understand the physical world? Not all of it. It needs to know warehouses: inventory flow, labor workflows, congestion, equipment, and the site’s constraints.

Domain expertise determines what the system should optimize and which signals are relevant. In a warehouse, that means evaluating the model against operational measures such as throughput and shipping performance, rather than judging it only by a perception or manipulation benchmark. That knowledge matters more as models get more capable.

As I recently discussed in an examination of Physical AI and robotics, I expect most of the industry’s near-term value to come from specialized systems in well-defined domains rather than from general-purpose intelligence. A warehouse operator judges a robot by whether orders ship on time and how many labor hours it saves, so those are the numbers a warehouse AI system should be built and judged on.

The decisive test is operational: place the system in a live warehouse and measure whether it performs across sites, shifts, workflows, and changing conditions. When it falls short, real-world data shows where the model’s assumptions stopped matching the environment.

Dr. Oscar Mendez is Director of AI & Data Science at Locus Robotics, where he leads AI strategy, research, and deployment across a global fleet of more than 22,000 autonomous robots. An award-winning, internationally recognized researcher in Physical AI, robotics, machine learning, and computer vision, he focuses on bringing AI out of the digital world and into operational reality, developing intelligent systems that perceive, reason, and act to deliver greater productivity, adaptability, and operational confidence in large-scale warehouse environments.

‘Root-to-Tip’ Eating: Are You Missing Out on Fiber by Discarding Scraps?

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Eating fruit and veggie scraps may boost fiber intake and cut food waste.

This study from King’s College London found that peels, skins, seeds, and stalks of fruits and vegetables—parts often discarded—contained significantly more dietary fiber than the commonly eaten portions in 10 of 12 produce types analyzed.

In some cases, the fiber content was up to five times higher in these “root-to-tip” components.

These findings, recently published in the International Journal of Food Sciences and Nutrition, are noteworthy, since around 95% of U.S. adults aren’t getting enough fiber. What’s more, around one-fifth of all food produced globally is wasted every year.

The researchers identified 12 fresh produce items that were deemed suitable for “root-to-tip” inclusion:

In the laboratory analysis, they measured the dietary fiber content of both the “root-to-tip” components (skins, peels, seeds, stalks) and the parts that people typically eat.

The key findings of the study showed that in 10 of the 12 produce types, the “root-to-tip” components contained more fiber than the parts typically consumed.

However, broccoli stalks contained 28.4% less fiber than the florets that people usually eat, meaning individuals are already consuming the most fiber-rich part.

Most participants in focus groups saw the nutritional value of the “root-to-tip” approach as more of a motivator than its sustainability.

However, these findings show that a “root-to-tip” approach to many fruits and vegetables could not only support higher dietary fiber intake but also reduce food waste.

Kristin Kirkpatrick, president of KAK, Consulting, LLC, and a dietitian at the Cleveland Clinic Department of Wellness & Preventive Medicine, said that the takeaway isn’t to eat every peel or seed, but to look for safe, simple ways to get more fiber from the foods you’re already buying. Kirkpatrick wasn’t involved in the study.

This applies to some of the plants analyzed in this study, but not all produce. For instance, you can only eat a kiwi that’s peeled. This is still a better choice than no kiwi (or a processed-food snack in its place) at all.

Fiber is important for promoting health and preventing chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, colorectal cancer, and obesity.

“Fiber does much more than support regular bowel movements. It helps regulate blood sugar, supports beneficial gut bacteria, and certain types of soluble fiber can help lower LDL cholesterol by increasing bile acid elimination,” noted Michelle Routhenstein, a preventive cardiology dietitian at Entirely Nourished, who wasn’t involved in the study.

On average, adults are recommended to get 25–30 grams (or more, depending on age) of fiber daily.

Both nutrition experts told Healthline that people generally aren’t getting enough fiber because of their food options or choices.

“Most likely because individuals are not consuming enough plants, and overconsuming processed and ultra-processed foods which are typically very low in fiber,” said Kirkpatrick. “Simply not eating enough fruits and vegetables or choosing white refined grains over whole grains can be why a person’s diet is lacking fiber.”

Routhenstein added, “I think it is important that when we talk about fiber we point out that it doesn’t have to mean completely changing how you eat. I encourage people to incorporate fiber-rich ingredients into familiar, culturally meaningful foods they already enjoy.”

“Start with small increases with foods you enjoy and build gradually to allow your digestive system to adjust.”

It is important to always research any new plant parts before eating them, wash thoroughly, and consult a healthcare professional if you have digestive conditions or concerns.

And remember that not all produce scraps are edible or recommended.

“Any dietary change will be dependent on personal preference. Some individuals may not enjoy or may not know how to add components of a plant they are not used to,” said Kirkpatrick.

She noted that, based on the study’s examples, an easier option might be to add more kiwi peels and seeds. “In fact, kiwi skin is probably the most practical fruit peel to highlight for consumers,” she said.

Routhenstein added, “I think there is a knowledge gap that the skins, peels, from the root to the tip are edible and nutritious, so we need to start there.”

She suggested that one way to start is by eating the skins of apples, pears, and well-scrubbed carrots. Trim and thinly slice broccoli stalks for stir-fries or soups. Sautée beet greens and stems just as you would spinach. Freeze appropriate vegetable trimmings, such as carrot ends and celery leaves, for homemade broth.

“Root-to-tip eating helps reduce food waste by encouraging us to use more of the edible portions of fruits and vegetables rather than discarding them. It also maximizes the nutritional and economic value of the food we purchase while making better use of the water, land, and energy required to produce it,” said Routhenstein.

“The key is finding simple, enjoyable ways to incorporate these parts into meals we already eat.”

I’ve tested several Aiper cleaners over the years, and the Aiper Scuba V3 Ultra feels like a natural evolution of the brand’s previous models. I ran it through repeated cleaning cycles in my 8,500-gallon saltwater pool in Florida, where leaves, bugs, fine debris, and the occasional patch of algae give pool robots plenty to deal with. Setup was super simple: The Aiper app connected quickly, the robot updated without any fuss, and I had it in the water about 15 minutes after opening the box.

What impressed me right away was just how much the Scuba V3 Ultra can do in one cleaning cycle. Plenty of the robotic pool cleaners we’ve tested at Bob Vila handle floors, walls, and the waterline, but only a select few can also skim floating debris from the surface. That matters in my open-air pool, which regularly collects bugs and leaves. Anything the Aiper actually reaches gets cleaned extremely well.

The MicroMesh filter is another highlight. It traps extremely fine material, including algae and sediment that can slip through coarser filter baskets. Cleaning that ultra-fine layer takes a little more effort—I usually spend a few minutes thoroughly rinsing it with a hose and then leave it out to dry—but that’s a fair trade-off for how much fine material it captures. The app is also among the better pool-cleaner apps I’ve used, and I like getting a notification when the cleaning cycle is finished. The robot then floats neatly near the pool edge for retrieval.

Where the V3 Ultra disappointed me was its claim of shallow-area cleaning. Aiper says it can work in water down to about 7.9 inches deep, but my sun shelf is apparently too shallow for it to clean reliably. I still have to brush debris from the shelf into the main pool and let the robot collect it there. It also won’t clean my pool steps. For a $2,300 machine marketed around near-total pool coverage, those are significant omissions—especially since sun shelves have become so common in newer pools.

Still, I’m a fan of the Scuba V3 Ultra overall. My older Aiper cleaner has been running reliably for close to two years, which gives me more confidence in the brand than I’d have from a short-term test alone. The voice assistant is useful without being obnoxious, retrieval is simple, filtration is excellent, and cleaning performance everywhere the robot can reach is phenomenal. I’m still waiting for the robot that will truly clean every inch of my pool, but the Scuba V3 Ultra gets closer than most.

What worked wonders, I found, was the quiet, subtle immersion, especially when exploring dungeons or figuring out solutions I’d forgotten decades ago. I had to study the environment for clues.

That’s the magic ingredient: a throughline in the Zelda games that Fujibayashi says is “the idea of exploration and discovery.”

I’m still unlocking all the little secrets in Ocarina of Time, sinking into the world as I go. And when I play the notes on my Switch, I imagine I’m playing that ocarina. After playing this Zelda game, I wonder what Nintendo could do with that same level of detail for Mario’s return on the Switch 2, almost 10 years after the last new 3D Mario game on the Switch. We might know the answer to that soon enough.

The Supreme Court of Virginia unanimously ruled Wednesday that the stateâs law prohibiting felons like Ginevan from exercising their Second Amendment right runs afoul of the U.S. Constitution and fails the historical tradition of firearms regulations analysis the Supreme Court created in New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen. Virginia permanently bans felons from possessing firearms unless they petition a court to have their right restored.

The ruling reverses the Virginia Court of Appeals’ judgment, which held that the circuit court properly sentenced Ginevan, albeit for different reasons. The circuit court determined that felons are not “the people” referenced in the Second Amendment. Justice Wesley Russell Jr. said the determination was error.

“The fact that the phrase ‘right of the people’ means the same thing in the First, Fourth and Second Amendments is, by our reckoning, sufficient to resolve whether a felony conviction, without more, removes a person from the political community,” Russell said. “Because a felony conviction does not remove a person from ’the people’ referenced in the First and Fourth Amendments, we conclude that, as a matter of language and logic, it does not remove a person from ’the people’ referenced in the Second Amendment."

The Court of Appeals assumed Ginevan was included as part of “the people” but ruled Ginevan’s 2018 conviction for possessing ammunition is a violent felony under state law. The Court of Appeals then examined the nation’s historic traditions and observed that the United States has long barred those judged to pose a danger to the public from possessing firearms. The Supreme Court of Virginia, however, ruled no founding-era law permanently disarmed nonincarcerated people based on a felony conviction.

“Statutes banning convicted felons from possessing firearms as a result of their prior felony convictions are a creature of 20th-century legislatures,” Russell said. “The inability of the commonwealth to offer even one example from the relevant timeframe of a statute barring all felons or even violent felons from possessing even a class of firearms, let alone all firearms and ammunition, is a significant strike against the constitutionality of Code Â§ 18.2-308.2.”

Shawn Weneta, director of government affairs at Cavalier Consulting, who himself regained his Second Amendment right in January, said the laws banning possession come from the Jim Crow era.

“Once we started saying it’s okay to strip people of their rights because of a criminal conviction, and then started, you know, criminalizing Black culture, we became very much desensitized,” Weneta said in an interview.

Executive Director of Justice Forward Virginia Brad Haywood said the felon-in-possession law disproportionately impacts Black people.

“If you’re Black in America, you’re much more likely to have a felony conviction,” Haywood said during an interview. “So I think this has also just a disproportionate positive impact on them.”

In affirming Ginevan’s conviction, the Court of Appeals observed English laws denying firearms to those who declined to join the Church of England or colonial laws disarming Native Americans. Russell ruled the laws aren’t analogous because they disarmed those viewed as disloyal to the state rather than punishing crimes.

Haywood said he doesn’t expect the ruling to impact the restoration of civil rights, like the right to hold public office or serve on a jury. Weneta said he’s interested to see how Virginia’s Democratic-led General Assembly approaches the ruling during the 2027 session. Weneta noted the ruling intersects the interests of criminal justice reform and Second Amendment advocates.

“I think there are quite a few very interesting questions now in front of advocates, you know, Second Amendment advocates, gun control advocates, very interesting questions in front of legislators, and of course a very interesting question in front of the attorney general,” Weneta said.

The Office of Attorney General Jay Jones did not respond to a request for comment. Attorney Elliot Harding said he expects a flood of habeas corpus petitions from people currently serving time for felon-in-possession offenses, but he doubts the ruling will apply retroactively. The ruling is narrow because it applies only to Ginevan, but Harding said he would be interested in bringing a habeas petition for a similar client under the right circumstances.

“This isn’t a broad, sweeping decision that undermines felon-in-possession laws, but it does invite future defendants, particularly those with histories lacking a crime of violence, to raise Second Amendment arguments,” Harding said in an interview.

The ruling doesn’t change the fact federal law prohibits felons from purchasing firearms. Attorneys representing Ginevan did not respond to a request for comment.

NBC Tracking Storm Due To Hit 100th Annual Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade: Will Balloons Be Grounded?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NBC is keeping close watch on a Thanksgiving storm expected to bring high winds and heavy rain to New York City and much of the Eastern U.S.

The network’s coverage of the 100th edition of the annual Macy’s Thanksgiving Day parade will air on NBC and stream on Peacock from 8:30 a.m. to noon ET. The parade remains a significant draw, with last year’s pulling in 34.3 million viewers across NBC and Peacock, its biggest combined audience on record.

Tyler Roys, a senior meteorologist with AccuWeather, said in an interview with Deadline that the second half of the parade seems to be at the most risk of being disrupted. He said the winds will be “near criteria” for grounding balloons – a signature feature of the New York City event, along with marching bands and floats. “They have some decisions to make over the next 48 hours,” Roys added.

NBC’s affiliate in New York expects it to be “a close call” if conditions on the Midtown Manhattan streets fall below city rules mandating that balloons be grounded in sustained winds of 24 miles per hour or gusts higher than 35 mph.

The Nor’easter, a classic storm pattern, is expected to take shape. By Thursday morning, sustained winds are expected to be 12-25 miles an hour with gusts of 20 to 30 mph, increasing over the course of the day.

In honor of NBC’s broadcasting centennial, the parade is set to have 100 large balloons. The “All-Star Broadway Thanksgiving Day Celebration” will feature numerous shows in a 10-minute medley. The balloons have only been grounded once before, in 1971.

About 3 million people watch the parade live, with the Manhattan skyscrapers creating a wind tunnel that can heighten the effects of the weather. In 1997, a wind-blown Cat in the Hat balloon clipped a light pole, sending it tumbling to the street below. The accident injured four spectators, one seriously.

“Our team is closely monitoring the weather forecast with our city and agency partners, and as always, the safety of our participants and spectators is our top priority,” NBC said in a statement provided to Deadline. “Decisions regarding balloon flight will be made on parade morning based on weather conditions and established safety protocols.”

NBCUniversal’s ad sales teams have been pushing the 100th anniversary, and the division said Monday it had established a revenue record for the parade. The company’s holiday programming, which extends beyond the parade to include a Thanksgiving Eve special, Christmas in Rockefeller Center and daily opportunities on Today and The Tonight Show, posted a 30% revenue gain over last year. The company said 50 new advertisers have joined the fold.
