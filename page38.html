Should You Invest $1,000 in Bloom Energy Right Now?

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bloom stock is plunging, but business is booming. Is this the buying opportunity of a lifetime?

By Steven Porrello – Nov 21, 2026 at 7:30PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Bloom Energy ›

Earlier this year, things seemed to be going exactly right for fuel-cell manufacturer Bloom Energy (BE -15.30%).

It's not hard to see why. The race to build AI data centers had stimulated such an enormous appetite for electricity that developers, facing grid bottlenecks and tight construction timelines, simply couldn't afford to wait years for traditional power grid connections.

In that context, new orders for Bloom's fuel-cell systems were coming in hot: First Oracle (ORCL -8.48%) contracted for 1.2 gigawatts (GW) of capacity, then Brookfield Asset Management (BAM -2.53%) expanded its financing framework with Bloom for AI projects from $5 billion to a whopping $25 billion.

Both were significant developments for Bloom's business, and Wall Street absolutely agreed: The stock soared to an all-time high of roughly $350, more than quadrupling in value from where it started the year.

If you take a look at Bloom's stock chart today, however, you'll see a different picture: It has fallen from its high to roughly $168 per share.

That raises the obvious question: What the heck is happening? And, more importantly, should you take this as an occasion to buy Bloom stock?

For starters, Bloom is a fuel cell manufacturer. In simple terms, it makes equipment that let data centers and other customers generate electricity on-site rather than waiting years to connect to the grid. These fuel cells are also a source of (relatively) clean power: Instead of burning fuel, they use an electrochemical reaction to convert fuel (such as natural gas) into electricity. It's a pretty neat concept, and it is especially useful for those who need round-the-clock power.

As mentioned, Bloom stock soared earlier this year as sales of Bloom boxes went through the roof. This hasn't changed. Indeed, third-quarter sales surged 138% year over year to $1.24 billion, prompting management (yet again!) to raise full-year revenue guidance to between $4.4 billion and $4.6 billion. For perspective, its full-year 2025 revenue was about $2 billion.

What has changed, however, is the environment, or more specifically: Wall Street's willingness to pay sky-high valuations for companies associated with the AI boom. Investors have become increasingly concerned about the billions being poured into AI infrastructure, and Bloom, whose future growth is tied to AI spending, has found itself caught in this broader wave of bearish sentiment.

The irony, of course, is that the very numbers that once sent Bloom's stock soaring are no longer enough to reassure investors. But why is that?

If I could capture that shift in perspective in an image, it would be this: Bloom's breathtaking growth this year is like a star's light. It's brilliant to look at, but it tells us more about the past than it does the future. It tells us just how bullish data center developers were on AI infrastructure earlier this year, but it doesn't necessarily indicate how Bloom will fare if that spending slows or stalls.

In other words, Bloom's extraordinary financial results could be a lagging indicator of an AI spending frenzy that is fading, while its stock price reflects anxiety about what will come next.

So, given that Wall Street is concerned about Bloom's future sales, should you reverse course and buy?

Personally, I think this is a great opportunity to pick up Bloom stock. The reaction to concerns over data center construction strikes me as a bit overdone. Even if AI spending slows, the underlying problem Bloom is solving -- an electricity grid struggling to keep up with rising demands -- isn't going away anytime soon. The stock might be volatile today, but for those willing to hold through the volatility, I think today's sell-off offers an attractive opportunity to buy a company with long-term growth potential.

If a Stock Market Crash Is Coming, Warren Buffett Says This Is the No. 1 Move All Investors Should Make

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The right strategy can protect your portfolio against even severe market volatility.

By Katie Brockman – Nov 21, 2026 at 7:30PM EST

10 stocks we like better than S&P 500 Index ›

It's been a lucrative year for the stock market, with the S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) notching more than two dozen record highs in 2026.

However, the headwinds have been coming in strong lately. Volatility within the tech sector has rattled Wall Street, and the tech-focused Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -5.28%) fell into correction territory after plunging nearly 12% from its recent peak, as of this writing.

The bad news for investors is that no bull market can last forever, and it's only a matter of time before we face a pullback. However, Warren Buffett has some timeless advice about how to prepare your portfolio for a potential market crash or recession.

Warren Buffett. Image source: Getty Images.

In late 1999, just four months before the dot-com bubble officially popped, Warren Buffett issued a warning to investors in an essay for Fortune. He emphasized that while new advancements in technology could transform society, that doesn't necessarily make them smart investments.

Using airlines as an example, Buffett noted that although air travel was revolutionary, 129 airlines had gone bankrupt over the previous 20 years. Televisions and radios fell into the same category as "glamorous businesses that dramatically changed our lives but concurrently failed to deliver rewards to U.S. investors."

The dot-com bubble eventually proved Buffett's point. While the internet went on to fundamentally transform the world, countless tech stocks crashed and burned in the early 2000s.

"The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society, or how much it will grow," Buffett explained, "but rather determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage."

While it's impossible to say how the market will fare in the coming months, the dot-com bubble serves as a valuable lesson. Artificial intelligence (AI) as a technology could be revolutionary, but that doesn't mean every AI stock will be a good investment. Inevitably, some stocks will flounder during the next downturn.

The best move investors can make now is to load up on a wide variety of quality stocks with robust business fundamentals. While all stocks are vulnerable to short-term volatility, those with strong competitive advantages are the most likely to recover and deliver long-term growth.

Healthy companies will likely thrive over time. Although the S&P 500 lost almost half of its value between 2000 and 2002, the index soared by more than 200% in the two decades following the dot-com bubble.

If we're headed toward a similar bear market, not all stocks will recover. But healthy companies will fuel decades of growth, and the investors who look past the hype and focus on quality stocks will reap the biggest rewards.

That share, a year’s rent divided by the price, is the gross yield, and it decides whether rent can cover a mortgage. In Zillow’s October 2026 figures, a typical house in the San Diego area rents for about 5% of its value a year, while one in the McAllen, Texas, area rents for 11.2%.

Now figure the mortgage payment on each, with 25% down and a 30-year loan at 7%. A typical McAllen-area house, worth $189,000, rents for about $1,760 a month, roughly $820 more than its $943 payment. A typical San Diego-area house, worth about $1 million, rents for about $4,130, about $860 short of its payment of nearly $5,000.

At those terms, the typical rent is higher than the mortgage payment in all 15 metros below, by about $270 a month in Little Rock up to about $820 in McAllen. That margin still has to pay the property taxes and insurance, plus the repairs, empty months, and management every landlord budgets for, so those costs decide whether a house makes money.

Property taxes come out of the rent every year, and several states on this list tax a rental more heavily than an owner’s home. Texas’s homestead exemptions, which shield part of a home’s value from tax, go only to an owner’s main home, so a rental in McAllen or El Paso is taxed on its full value, the state comptroller explains. Michigan charges a rental up to 18 mills, or $18 per $1,000 of taxable value, in school operating taxes that an owner’s home skips, according to its Treasury Department.

In South Carolina, a rental is assessed at 6% of its value, against 4% for an owner’s home, and pays school operating taxes the owner’s home skips, as Richland County, home to Columbia, explains. Your PC's USB port can charge your phone, even when it's turned off — Here's how

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Your USB ports might have a hidden "Always On" mode that charges your phone with the PC shut down.

Almost all PC USB ports supply enough current to charge a device, but not all continue to do so when the PC is shut down. This convenient feature can also be confusing, since each brand tends to have its own terminology. For example, Lenovo calls it an "Always On" port while Dell uses the term PowerShare. Others stick with icon labels, like a battery symbol or a lightning bolt. The best way to know for sure is to dig into your PC's manual, confirm the markings and learn how to enable it.

Depending on the system, USB standby power might be on by default or be part of an energy-saving setting that cuts power when the PC is off. Your desktop or motherboard documentation should have this information. Fortunately, testing the feature is fairly simple: plug your phone in, turn the PC off and see if your phone continues charging.

If it stops charging, it's safe to assume the setting is off, which you can rectify in the BIOS or UEFI on most Windows PCs. (If your PC never supported the feature, changing settings won't help.) Restart the PC and press the key the startup screen tells you to (usually F2, Del or F10). Look under Advanced, Power or Chipset for a USB power delivery or Always On charging option and enable it. If you see ErP (Energy-related Products) or Deep Sleep settings, make sure they're disabled. Save and exit.

You don't need a computer science or software engineering degree to do this, just a little patience as you wait for the PC to boot up and press the right key. The menu can be confusing at times, but you're unlikely to brick your PC.

For starters, energy-saving features may get in the way. Anything that uses standby power will conflict with firmware settings that cut power to reduce consumption. For instance, if ErP Ready is enabled in select ASUS NUC PCs, USB standby power during shutdown or hibernation is disabled. Check your system's instructions before changing anything.

You also may not have to turn off every single energy-saving feature just to charge your phone when your PC is off. Keep in mind that changing the energy-saving settings won't add post-shutdown charging if the system never supported it to begin with.

Laptops are an entirely different case, since they often come with battery reserve limitations. Depending on the model, USB charging may stop when the laptop's battery hits a reserve threshold. Laptop charging features may depend on an AC adapter after shutdown, and that's a potential restriction that shouldn't be confused with a desktop's standby power settings. Make sure your USB plug is completely in the port and that the cable is reliable, not one that's been sitting in an attic box for the last quarter century.

In this context, "Always On" refers to power availability, but that's not the same thing as "charging only." A supported port can continue to supply power while the computer is off, though the wording shouldn't be taken literally. Firmware settings can disable that power, and the behavior may be different between ports on the same system.

Power and data transfers are also different capabilities. A phone charging while the PC is shut down doesn't mean you can transfer photos from your phone or copy files to it. The specific feature here is all about electricity available via the USB connection.

Another problem is the lack of consistency in names, setup processes and USB port symbols. One system may offer a dedicated USB power setting while another's ports depend on power-saving configurations. Even within a single brand, capabilities and terminology can differ, and manufacturers implement "Always On" USB in drastically different ways.

For example, "Always On" could mean the feature is active after shutdown, during hibernation or while your PC is asleep. It could mean two of them, just one or all three. The feature may come with symbols, color coding, terminology or fine print in a manual, but not necessarily all of the above.

I knew it had become increasingly rare for Hollywood productions to actually be filmed in Los Angeles, but Rob Lowe’s stats for The Floor really put things into perspective. The host of the Fox game show, which is on the 2026 TV schedule this fall season, just got real about why it’s filmed all the way in Ireland rather than at the network’s own lot. And, wow!

Ever since The Floor premiered back in 2023, the game show featuring 100 contestants, a giant LED floor grid and a $250,000 grand prize has been made in Ireland. When Lowe was asked why he shoots it overseas, he said this:

When people want to know how bad the economics are in California, and Los Angeles in particular in terms of the industry that created this town. It is more cost-efficient to find 100 Americans and bring them to Ireland and shoot the game show in Ireland than it is to shoot in the soundstages [on the Fox lot] right outside the window of the offices where we’re trying to figure out what we’re going to do next season.

Yeah, this really illustrates the rough situation in LA right now. When I looked deeper into it, I found out that there are a few factors that probably played into The Floor deciding to go to Ireland rather than stay in Los Angeles. One is the rigid labor regulations in Los Angeles that just don’t apply in Ireland. Another is how much more expensive it would be for the production to board the one hundred contestants during the shoots. And then, there’s the competitive tax incentives Ireland has in place to encourage productions to be there. Lowe continued with these words:

There’s a new California initiative that did pass to help bring production back into LA. And then, there's also a national one that people are talking about. But, that would still only make it competitive with some of the other [markets]. I mean, it’s 40% less expensive, 40. It’s a lot of money.

It sounds like The Floor just wouldn’t be able to exist if it were shot in Los Angeles. There are a number of TV shows that currently shoot in the City of Angels, like Dancing With The Stars, The Pitt, The Rookie, 911, Shrinking and Abbott Elementary, and some incentives in California are starting to help repair the market. However, as Lowe also shared while on Mostly Sheenius, there’s a long way to go before it becomes cost-efficient again. As he put it:

It is definitely trending up, but it is trending up from the basement of where it used to be.

Tons of Los Angeles TV shows recently moved to new locations, such as Shark Tank and American Idol moving to Atlanta and The Masked Singer to New Jersey. It’s also been reported lately (per Deadline) that America’s Got Talent is scouting a new home outside of its longtime home in Pasadena, California.

The Floor airs new episodes on Wednesdays (with the finale airing on December 2). If you’re looking to catch up on the new season, you can do so now with either a Disney+ subscription and/or a Hulu subscription. Lowe’s explanation of why The Floor shoots in Ireland kind of blows my mind, but as a Los Angeles resident, I totally see how expensive it is over here, too. 10 things you should never do on your Android phone

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Avoid major mistakes that make your Android phone less secure and less convenient to use.

If you've used Android for a while, you probably know some of its most important rules. But even with an OS that's been around for more than 15 years, myths and outdated assumptions still persist. Or you might be new to Android and not totally sure how to best take care of your device.

Either way, Android doesn't come with a complete user manual, so we've compiled some of the mistakes you shouldn't make on an Android phone. They include ignoring security features and trying advanced functions without understanding them. Follow these, and your Android phone should last years in good condition.

Why it's bad: Software updates fix known vulnerabilities. Patches in a software update are often responsive, meaning they protect against attacks that are happening, not just theoretical threats. Also, if you use high-security apps like banking and wallet tools, those services won't work if your phone gets too behind on security updates.

Who says so: Security vulnerabilities in Android are reported to Google, and Google shares this information confidentially with Android device makers. You can read about what threats Google considers critical on its Android Security page.

Google doesn't share this information publicly for 90 days, giving vendors time to create proper patches and preventing would-be attackers from taking advantage of the details before people have a chance to install patches. There are a lot of players here: Google is responsible for Android software, then every phone maker adds software to Android devices that needs to be patched. Chip makers like MediaTek and Qualcomm also need to patch the drivers that allow those chips to function properly.

What's the best practice: Install every security update as soon as it appears. Also, open the Google Play Store and make sure to update apps regularly — automatic updates are best.

Why it's bad: When your phone is running too many processes at once, it slows down. That seems obvious, but what's actually happening is your phone is shuttling apps between three internal components: storage, RAM and a special partition of RAM called ZRAM made to quickly store and recall active processes.

When your RAM fills up, it starts compressing processes so it can cram them into the ZRAM partition. When there's no more room in ZRAM for compressed data, your phone enters a state called memory thrashing, where it repeatedly loads data from the ZRAM and then compresses it back in an ongoing cycle. This causes your phone to slow down and heat up as the CPU drains power.

Who says so: The Android Open Source Project offers technical details on the so-called "low memory killer daemon" (the software process that stuffs data into ZRAM), and the problems caused when you let that memory fill up with extraneous data pages.

What's the best practice: Restart your phone regularly to clear the RAM and ZRAM. When you're done with a heavy app like a game, swipe to close it from the app switcher menu (though you don't need to do this constantly for all apps). Finally, uninstall apps that you no longer use to make sure they aren't running processes you don't need. On Android, even if you don't open an app, it may still run and use your RAM in the background.

As another part of core maintenance, make sure to keep enough space free on your Android phone. Running it with maxed-out storage means it has no room for temporary caches and other "helper" data.

Why it's bad: There is no easier way for bad actors to take advantage of security flaws than from apps that come from outside Google's Play Store garden. One common practice is for bad apps to take advantage of your phone's Accessibility controls, which grant privileges for copying your phone's screen and running apps in the background. Google is cautious about approving accessibility access on the Play Store, which is why bad apps are often found on third-party sites.

Who says so: According to Google's security blog, Google Play Protect identified more than 13 million malicious apps from outside the Play Store in 2024 — and those are just the ones the service was able to detect. The standards for Android app security are set by the App Defense Alliance, part of Linux, which forms the foundation of Android's code.

What's the best practice: If you must install apps from outside Google Play, make sure you 100 percent trust the source. A first-party developer you trust, or a security-conscious third-party app market like F-Droid, is better than a random forum or Reddit page. Always use caution outside the Google Play Store.

Why it's bad: If you're asked to disable Google Play Protect for any reason, that's a major red flag. Usually, a scammer will try to convince you to download a phony app by pretending to be your bank or another trusted source. When you try to install the new app, you get a warning from Play Protect, which the scammer tells you to disregard. Never ignore this warning.

Who says so: Play Protect has been a part of Android for almost 10 years, and it gets constant updates that make it more stringent. It's the primary line of defense against dangerous apps you try to install.

What's the best practice: Never disable Google Play Protect, but don't trust it blindly either. Researchers like Bitdefender claim to have found apps that made it to the Play Store and still pose a risk, so you still need to be careful about what you install.

Why it's bad: In the early days of Android, there were good reasons to obtain root access to your phone. You could enable features that were inexplicably turned off, like network tethering on Verizon's early Android devices. It allows you to remove bloatware installed by your mobile carrier, or even load an OS like CyanogenMod that was better than what Samsung or Google provided. Those days are over.

Rooting a phone triggers failures in Google's Play Integrity API, and that's a break that can't heal. You won't be able to use banking or financial apps or any apps that rely on your phone's Strong integrity, a tag that disappears when you obtain root access to your system. Having root access means mistakes or malicious apps can cause much more damage.

Who says so: Even the US government says this is a bad idea. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) warns against rooting your mobile device, listing rooting and jailbreaking a phone as a threat in its Mobile Threat Catalogue.

What's the best practice: There is not enough reason to root your primary Android phone today if you aren't a software developer. Phone features are no longer restricted based on which carrier sold you the phone. And phone makers let you remove (or at least hide) the bloat — you can even remove Google apps that used to be permanently stuck in your app drawer.

If you have an old device that no longer gets updates, rooting and installing a custom ROM can bring new life to it, but the trade-offs aren't worth it on an in-support daily phone.

Why it's bad: Say your new Android phone came with a bunch of apps you don't want. The Settings menu lets you disable them so they disappear, but they're still taking up space and you can't let it go.

So, you found a YouTube video that shows you how to turn on USB Debugging, which lets you manage your phone's files from your PC. You figure this isn't a problem, since you aren't installing anything from a third party or handing your phone to a stranger.

Except those instructions often skip an important final step: remember to turn USB Debugging off. Otherwise, your phone is more vulnerable to attacks using a computer. While Android requires you to authorize the connection when you connect to any PC for the first time, you might not realize what you're doing, or someone could trick you into handing over access. That's why it's important to watch the prompts when connecting to an unknown USB port (more on this below).

There are lots of features on Android that can give you advanced access to your system without being dangerous, as long as you're careful to turn them off when you're done.

Who says so: Google is enthusiastic about Android users trying Developer Mode, but admits "it's essential to understand the implications of each setting." That's a massive understatement, since these options can cause problems if you don't know what they do.

What's the best practice: If you dabble in the Android cauldron, be sure to put out the fire. Disable USB Debugging and any other features that you turned on once the task is done. Follow instructions closely, and don't mess with features you don't truly understand.

Why it's bad: When you access an open Wi-Fi network, you have no guarantees of privacy. Anyone else who joins the network can use free analyzer tools to see what's happening. Almost every modern website uses HTTPS, meaning information you send to a site (like login credentials) is encrypted. But someone snooping on the network would still be able to see which sites you're accessing.

Even if the network seems trustworthy, it's hard to be sure. For example, say you use the password on the wall to connect to a cafe network. Someone could have set up a fake network with the same name and password combination, and your device won't know the difference. It looks for the strongest connection, which might be the person next to you with a sketchy hotspot and Wi-Fi sniffer, not the router mounted above the ceiling.

Who says so: The US Government's Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency said public Wi-Fi networks are one of the biggest threats for high-risk security attacks, though this was in 2012 before HTTPS was as common as it is now. Technology departments at universities like Vanderbilt continue to warn of the risks and how to mitigate them.

What's the best practice: Use a VPN for another layer of encryption on untrusted networks. If you must use public Wi-Fi without a VPN, avoid working with sensitive info like bank details. And if a site doesn't use HTTPS, leave it for when you're at home.

Why it's bad: When you plug into a USB port, you are often connecting your phone to both a power source and a data connection. Your phone will prompt you to trust a new device for data connections, which is where the risk comes in. If you grant that access, an attacker could steal your photos, load malware onto your phone or intercept communications, among other harmful actions.

Who says so: The FCC has issued warnings against plugging your phone into unknown USB ports. While no reports of so-called "juice jacking" attacks have happened in the real world, the technique has been demonstrated multiple times at hacking events (first at the Black Hat security conference in 2013). There's even a USB cable called the O.MG sold by an InfoSec testing company, Hak5, that contains the necessary hardware to pull off a juice jacking attack — for testing purposes, of course.

What's the best practice: When you plug your phone in at an airport, don't accept any pop-up notices that appear. If possible, use a proper wall outlet or a portable charger to eliminate the small theoretical risk of these attacks.

Those are obvious examples of permissions you should be critical of. More important are Accessibility permissions, which allow an app to monitor everything on your screen, monitor your SMS messages (for 2FA codes, for example) and even control other apps without your knowledge. That sketchy to-do list app could open your banking app, change your password and have its way with your accounts, if it has proper accessibility access.

Another permission to watch is Display over other apps. This lets Facebook float Messenger chat heads on top of your home screen and other apps, but could also let a bogus app overlay a fake OK button that gives permission to further abuse. It may look like you're tapping Agree in the Google Play Store, when the button is actually a separate app that will install a nasty payload.

Who says so: Android takes permissions seriously: the latest versions of Android revoke all permissions from apps you haven't used in a long time, just to be safe. Google tells developers to ask for permissions incrementally, and only when a feature is needed, so you have context for why the app is asking.

What's the best practice: You know to be careful with microphone and camera access, but also be aware when an app asks for any unusual permissions. Regularly review your permissions and remove any that are unnecessary (or uninstall apps you never use).

When one is always kept at 100 percent, it increases the wear on internal components and reduces the total charge the battery can hold over time, according to Samsung. That's why modern iOS and Android versions come with battery management tools that keep your phone around 80 percent while you charge overnight, only topping up to 100 percent just before you wake up.

You should also avoid using cheap, off-brand USB cables for charging. Your phone came with a high-quality USB cable that includes a controller chip to make sure the device gets a steady voltage. Cheap USB cables often lack that chip, which can cause your battery to overheat. At worst, it can cause internal electrical damage, though any excess heat is bad for your battery's chemistry. It speeds up reactions and can cause the battery to degrade faster.

Who says so: Most phone makers have a warning page about batteries, with battery utilities recommending that you only charge your phone to 80 percent under normal circumstances. This will extend the longevity of the battery, which is a good idea if you plan on keeping your phone for many years.

What's the best practice: Let Android do its job. Your phone has software that helps manage battery, both for day-to-day life and overall longevity. Don't force your phone to charge past Android's recommended limit all the time.
