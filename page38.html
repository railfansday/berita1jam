Kenyan doomsday sect leader jailed for 200 years over 191 murders

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Paul Mackenzie convicted over the deaths of 190 children and one adult after followers were ordered to starve themselves and their families

The leader of a Kenyan doomsday sect has been sentenced to 200 years in prison for the murders of 190 children and one adult in one of the world’s worst cult disasters in recent history.

The sentence, comprising 100 years for murder by starvation and 100 years by direct homicide, came two weeks after the court in Kenya’s coastal city of Mombasa found the cult leader guilty of the crimes.

Prosecutors said Paul Mackenzie and his Good News International church ran ​a Christian doomsday sect whose members were ordered to starve themselves and their children to death to go to heaven before the world ended.

His accomplices threatened and beat victims to ensure they followed his orders. The emaciated bodies were found scattered in shallow graves and dumped in latrines. Some had been buried alive.

Mackenzie was arrested in 2023 after police received a tipoff, and weeks later authorities began uncovering bodies in shallow graves in the Shakahola forest near the coast in south-eastern Kenya.

In total, more than 400 bodies have been recovered. Many were severely decomposed and only 26 could be identified.

Mackenzie denied murder charges, saying he did not force the victims to starve, but the court rejected that argument.

The Shakahola tragedy has gripped Kenya since the discovery of mass graves in the forest more than three years ago.

It raised questions about how Mackenzie was able to continue operating despite being on the radar of the authorities since 2017, when police searched his premises.

In 2019, authorities shut down Mackenzie’s church and he relocated ‌to the Shakahola forest, police said.

The case has also led to calls for tougher oversight of churches and other religious organisations in a country where religious faith plays a prominent role in public life.

Mackenzie, a former taxi driver, forbade cult members from sending their children to school and from going to hospital when they were ill, describing such institutions as satanic.

Cult members lived in makeshift homes of polythene sheeting and thatch that Mackenzie divided into areas with biblical names such as Jerusalem and Judea.

The plight of his followers began ​to emerge in March 2023, when a man told ​police his brother and wife had starved their children ​to death in the forest on Mackenzie’s orders.

Mackenzie was arrested but released on bail, after which relatives said he brought forward the date ​on which he told his followers the ‌world would end. Police rearrested him and ​started combing through the forest more systematically and began exhuming mass graves.

Last month’s judgment covered 191 deaths while investigations into more than 200 other deaths are continuing.

Justice Kavedza-Mochache said in a hearing lasting more than eight hours that in some cases children were isolated and denied food while their parents watched.

Mackenzie faces other charges over the forest deaths, including those of terrorism, which he has denied.

Prosecutors said that while he was in detention he also directed the deaths of 52 people in Kwa Binzaro village, about 18 miles from Shakahola. Mackenzie has pleaded not guilty in that case.

Fantasy Football Week 9 Fades & Busts: Bad times to continue for Panthers

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Welcome to the ninth of 17 weekly articles spotlighting the fantasy fades of the week! I am your host, Matt Okada, and I'll be bringing you half a dozen potential busts each and every Friday, from now until fantasy football championships.

As a note, just because a player earns a "fade" or "bust" designation doesn't automatically mean they should be benched — it depends on the rest of your roster or the options on your waiver wire. But you can expect them to fall short of expectations (when I get them right). I include a "What to Do" section for every player to help with that.

Without further ado, here is my list for Week 9 of the 2026 season.

Over the first two weeks of the season, Bryce Young was brilliant, throwing for three touchdowns and hitting 24+ fantasy points in both games. Then, he suffered a minor knee injury in Week 3, which he has played through with a brace, limiting his mobility in the pocket. He hasn't thrown three touchdowns in any of Carolina's six contests since, clocking in at just seven TDs and four interceptions over that span.

In the meantime, the Panthers' offensive line has been ravaged by injuries and, in combination, these factors have led to a severe drop-off in fantasy production for Young. He's been pressured on 50% of his dropbacks over the last two weeks and has logged a 35.4 passer rating and 20% success rate against that pressure. Largely as a result, he's averaged just 9.39 fantasy points per game in those two outings.

Now, he draws a matchup with a Broncos defense sporting the league's fourth-highest pressure rate (40.3%) and second-highest quick pressure rate (21.7%), per Next Gen Stats, both of which have increased in recent weeks. Young is likely to be in trouble early and often in this game and is staring down the barrel of another fantasy dud.

What to do ❓ Young is well outside QB1 range and is honestly closer to the back of QB2 range this week. I'd much prefer a streamer like Tyler Shough, Malik Willis, Kirk Cousins or even Michael Penix Jr.

Ollie Gordon II has been shockingly good for fantasy since taking over for De'Von Achane several weeks ago. Most of the fantasy community couldn't even decide whether he'd get the starting job, and he's proceeded to average 18.8 touches per game since the Week 3 takeover and score 13+ fantasy points in four of those five contests. However, I'm pumping the brakes for Week 9.

Coming off his first dud — 6.9 points against the tough Patriots run defense — Gordon has an even tougher matchup in the Lions. Detroit is a massive pass-funnel opponent, meaning that it encourages offenses to throw — allowing a league-high 298.7 yards per game on a league-high 41 attempts per game — rather than run (where it's bottom-10 in YPG and attempts per game allowed). As a result, it's allowing the fourth-fewest fantasy points per game to running backs. And unfortunately, Gordon has essentially zero role in the Miami passing attack. If he's lucky enough to see a goal-line carry or two, Gordon could score a touchdown and eke out a decent fantasy day, but it feels far more likely that he has another medium-volume, low-efficiency day and ends up in single-digit fantasy points once again.

What to do ❓ Gordon isn't an absolute must-sit, especially given the shallow nature of the position. But he's a low-end RB2 at best and I'd definitely lower expectations and avoid him in DFS.

Speaking of the Lions' run defense, they turned Jordan Mason into an absolute dud last week, with just 20 rushing yards on eight carries for 2.0 fantasy points. That brings his total since returning from injury in Week 7 to 88 yards on 23 carries (3.8 per carry) with zero catches and zero touchdowns. Fantasy managers who waited on his return are justifiably frustrated but might see a glimmer of hope in the "green" Bills matchup here in Week 9.

Buffalo has been a good matchup for running backs because its allowed the third-most receiving fantasy points per game, at 11.0, behind only the Dolphins and 49ers. In rushing fantasy points, its much closer to the middle of the pack, and Mason is only guaranteed to see a portion of that production with Aaron Jones Sr. still involved. The Vikings offense has been extremely lackluster all season and they're an underdog against the Bills in what figures to be a relatively low-scoring affair. None of this bodes well for the run-only half of Minnesota's RBBC, so Mason is better suited on the bench until a better opportunity arises ... if ever.

What to do ❓ Mason is well outside RB2 range and I wouldn't even consider him a promising FLEX. You're better off starting committee-backs like Zach Charbonnet or Blake Corum, as they should have better chances at scoring.

Remember everything I said about Bryce Young, the Panthers' O-line and the mess that pressure has created for this passing attack lately? All of that trickles down in a very discouraging way for early breakout candidate, Jalen Coker. He has just three catches on 10 targets all season when Young is pressured and just three catches for 45 yards during this particularly troubling downturn the past two weeks. He had a brilliant game against the Eagles three weeks ago, but Philly is currently the best matchup for wide receivers, so we have to take that 97-yard game with a grain of salt. And it makes sense that pressure and Coker don't get along: he's averaged 11.2 air yards per target and 20.5% of his targets have come on deep passes this season (nearly twice as high as the rate for Tetairoa McMillan).

Meanwhile, Denver has allowed the fifth-fewest fantasy points to wide receivers and the fourth-fewest yards per target on downfield throws (10+ air yards). Just as Young is a fade, Coker is a complementary fade for all the same reasons. I like his upside down the stretch if Young and the O-line can get healthy, but he's a very risky play in the meantime.

What to do ❓ Coker is outside WR2 range and while he's a playable FLEX in a pinch, I'd rather go with a lesser name in a better situation, like Keenan Allen or Stefon Diggs.

It seems like there's a bit of growing optimism about Jaylen Waddle in the fantasy community ... but I'm not entirely sure why. Sure, he caught a touchdown in Week 8 against the Chiefs ... just his second of the year, by the way. He also finished that game with three catches for 39 yards, marking his sixth-straight game with fewer than six catches and fewer than 50 receiving yards. His only truly impressive outing all season came back in Week 2 against the Jaguars — who have proven to be a major pass-funnel defense — when he saw eight catches for 138 yards on 10 targets. But Waddle had 138 receiving yards in his other six games combined entering Week 8 and is averaging just 39.4 per game on the season, even when including that aberration of an outing against Jacksonville.

He's averaging the fourth-fewest yards per target (6.2) among all wideouts with 50+ targets and doesn't have a game over 14 fantasy points in the last six weeks. All told, his floor and ceiling are concerningly low and I see little reason for optimism against the Panthers — even with injuries to their starting cornerbacks — given how spotty and inefficient Waddle's usage has been.

What to do ❓ Waddle falls a couple spots behind Coker in my rankings, so we can apply the same logic for him in Week 9 — WR3 at best, iffy FLEX play, would rather go with Allen or Diggs.

This pains me, as I am a huge Dalton Kincaid fan, and there are a lot of reasons to believe in him rest-of-season — like his position-leading 2.7 yards per route run and his robust-for-a-tight-end air yards share (19.7%). But the Minnesota Vikings are terrifying. They've allowed a league-low 5.5 fantasy points per game to tight ends and have held every single TE they've faced to single-digit fantasy points. Tucker Kraft set the current high-water mark at just 8.0 points, while Sam LaPorta (5.0), Juwan Johnson (3.6), Colston Loveland (0.8) and others have been complete duds in this matchup.

On average, they've surrendered just 3.1 catches for 39.0 yards per game to the position, and have yet to allow a touchdown to a tight end all season. Sitting Kincaid won't be easy, especially given the shallow nature of the position, but it might be necessary given the heavily reduced ceiling against Minnesota.

What to do ❓ Try to find a streamer in redraft if you can, a la T.J. Hockenson, Kyle Pitts Sr., or even Brenton Strange, rather than playing Kincaid against this defense. And stay away in DFS.

Did You Get Blamed for Everything in Your Family?

The first time I learned about the concept of an “identified patient,” it immediately resonated with me. So many of my clients have had the experience of being the “problem” in their family. They were scapegoated, blamed, and gaslit for struggling with their mental health. Often a highly sensitive or neurodivergent child (or both), they felt the family’s turbulence deeply, unable to pretend everything was fine and ignore all the chaos.

The idea of an identified patient is used in family therapy, where sessions involve the whole family, not just the person (often a child or adolescent) who is struggling. It’s common in these sessions for other family members to say, “Well, they have all these problems, but we’re just fine.”

If this resonates with you, it’s likely you were blamed by your family for being “difficult,” “too sensitive,” or “selfish,” because you often got upset as a child. I even hear these descriptions of babies who cried a lot, didn’t sleep through the night, or struggled with colic, as if a baby could somehow control their behavior to be easy or difficult.

It’s true that babies are especially sensitive to their parents’ moods, facial expressions, and tone of voice, so they can react strongly to, say, a depressed mother or angry father. But babies can’t express their discomfort in words, so they cry—their only means of communicating distress. Even older children will struggle to express themselves verbally, as their emotions are so intense and the prefrontal cortex—the brain region we need for complex thought and emotional regulation—remains undeveloped until well into adolescence. That’s why babies and children of all ages are a lightning rod for problems in the family.

This is especially true for kids with highly sensitive nervous systems, because they pick up all the negative energy in a room, whether expressed or unexpressed. It hurts them, like a throbbing headache, invisible but impossible to ignore.

Let’s explore this idea through Jo’s story. Jo* was an unusually sensitive kid who struggled with chronic anxiety as far back as she could remember. “My brother always called me ‘scaredy-cat’ because I was terrified all the time. Dogs, strangers, loud noises—you name it, I was freaked out by everything,” she told me in our first session.

Like so many clients, at first, Jo told me her family was “totally normal.” “I had a regular, happy childhood,” she claimed, despite struggling with a raft of anxiety disorder diagnoses her whole life. When I pointed this out, she initially blamed herself for being too sensitive, too thin-skinned, just too much as a kid.

It took some slow, patient detective work to explore what lay beneath the surface of this “normal” family, which was anything but. As part of my psychoeducation with clients, I explain the idea from schema therapy of “core emotional needs,” which every child has, no matter their culture or country of origin. These include needs for love and a secure attachment, being kept safe and protected from harm, and feeling valued and accepted for who they are, not how they perform.

Jo’s father was a violent alcoholic who took his frustration and rage out on Jo’s poor mother. Tearfully, Jo recalled the “walls shaking” as her dad punched them or trashed their home. Jo would hide under the covers with her younger brother, cradling him in her trembling arms. He was too little to understand, and her dad left the family when he was just five, so the chaos and dysfunction didn’t affect him as much as it did her.

As she admitted to me how awful things had been, Jo began to feel more compassion for the little girl who was so traumatized by her father’s rage attacks. Little by little, we helped transform her self-blame into self-compassion.

Like so many of my clients who were stigmatized by their family, Jo was constantly gaslit by other family members. Even though she rarely saw her father, who continued to drink heavily, he would tell her she was “weak” and needed to “toughen up,” taking zero responsibility for his behavior or role in making her so anxious. Even her brother, whom she tried so hard to protect, said she was “self-centered” and “attention-seeking” for being so anxious all the time. This broke her heart, but she had long just accepted that everyone’s version of their shared reality was true.

We worked hard to change that. I was a tireless cheerleader for Little Jo, that sensitive kid who did nothing wrong, despite the critical family stories. Jo now had two daughters of her own, so I asked her how they would react if their dad smashed up the living room or hit Jo in front of them. She was horrified, knowing how profoundly that would affect them. This helped her see that the family stories told by her dad and brother were neither true nor fair. “You know, I’m starting to see it wasn’t my fault for getting so upset,” she told me one day. “Any kid would have struggled with all that anger and chaos. If anyone’s to blame, it’s my dad, not me.”

For Jo, seeing the truth of her childhood eventually set her free. She learned to treat herself kindly, as well as how to set boundaries and be assertive, even with her overbearing father. When our therapy ended, she was flourishing—loving and accepting herself as “highly sensitive and proud of it.”

If any of this resonates with you, I’m sorry—I know all too well how painful it can be to fill that identified patient role. But I also hope you understand that this was a label given to you by a family desperately trying to avoid responsibility for its own dysfunction.

You may need help from a skilled professional in untangling the knotty story of your childhood, but finally understanding that you were just a sweet, innocent kid in a painful environment might just change your life.

Dallas Wants More Gates At Love Field—And To Give Southwest Airlines A Bigger Monopoly

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Dallas is opening the door to more gates at Love Field airport. Two things stand in the way. Federal law caps the airport at 20 gates, and Dallas has already negotiated a lease that would offer most of the additional gates to Southwest Airlines if the law were to change. Southwest already controls nearly all of the gates there. More room for adding Southwest flights is different than more competition for Southwest. Dallas needs a larger airport and to open itself to competition. The Dallas City Manager raised the prospect of additional gates at an aviation event last week. The airport is undergoing a $2.5 billion redevelopment that doesn’t include more gates. Former American Airlines Chairman and CEO Doug Parker defended the existing arrangement on this week’s Airlines Confidential podcast. His argument rests on the existing five-party agreement, federal law and investments made in reliance on both: “agreements are agreements.” That’s only why changing the rules will be difficult, not a reason to keep limits on competition forever. Why Dallas Can’t Just Build More Love Field Gates Southwest Airlines had to fight to operate from Dallas Love Field when it first launched. It flew its first flight in 1971 after being tied up in the courts. Existing airlines agreed to leave Love Field for DFW airport. Southwest wasn’t a party to that agreement. In 1979, the Wright Amendment was passed to limit competition from Southwest Airlines. Deregulation meant that Southwest could fy beyond the state of Texas, and other carriers feared that the close-in airport they operated from would be more desirable than DFW. The upstart Southwest that fought for the right to fly was mature and pivoted to using government to its own advantage by the time they succeeded in securing the Wright Amendment Reform Act of 2006. It was a compromise among Dallas, Fort Worth, DFW Airport, American Airlines and Southwest Airlines. Southwest gained the ability to sell connecting service beyond the old geographic limits, followed by nationwide domestic nonstop service in October 2014. Love Field’s gate count had to be reduced from 32 to 20. Southwest kept most of the gates. The airport couldn’t offer nonstop international passenger service. Basically Southwest got more flying from Love Field and a monopoly there, while American Airlines got a monopoly on international service from Dallas and a cap on how much service could compete from Love Field. Initially, Southwest effectively couldn’t even fly out of Dallas – Fort Worth offering direct competition with American. The five party agreement specified that any gate Southwest operates at DFW (or another airport within 80 miles) would require giving up a Love Field gate, up to 8 total gates. That restriction ended in 2025. The provision even covered affiliates and flights carrying Southwest’s code, with exceptions for existing DFW–Midway codeshare service. Southwest couldn’t even codeshare with partners who flew to DFW, and could set up a regional affiliate to operate there.
