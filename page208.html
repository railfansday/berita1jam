You don't have to spend much to fix little annoyances in everyday life.

Steve Jobs once said that customers don't know what they want until you show them. And he was right. But he was also lucky because he was showing people iPhones and not, say, rechargeable nose trimmers.

Of course, when Apple launched the iPhone in 2007, Amazon wasn't the global behemoth it's since become. Now there's virtually nothing Amazon doesn't sell, and a lot of it is dirt cheap. In the post-Bezos world, Jobsian marketing principles no longer apply. Customers still may not know what they want, but if it's cheap enough, they'll buy it anyway and then throw it out and buy something else.

Thus begins our quest to assemble a list of cheap gadgets that are worth every penny, which should come in handy around the shopping season. If you treat them right, these may last you for many holidays to come.

It's the middle of the night when you wake up and need to head to the en-suite bathroom. You snap on the light and you're blinded because it's too bright. Or worse, you wake up your spouse, who can't easily get back to sleep even with their favorite sleeping app.

This scenario may sound exaggerated, but if you've ever been there, you know it isn't. Or, to spin it back to Steve Jobs, you just don't realize what you need. In this case, it's a motion sensor light for your bathroom.

Decent motion-activated lights for around $10 that don't require any drilling come in two varieties. They're either battery-powered stick-ons like the Star-Spangled LED Puck Light ($8.99 for 5), or they plug right into a socket. The latter are more like smart night lights. The BN-Link Backlit Motion Sensor ($12.34 for 2), for example, has an auto mode that only activates with motion, plus a night mode that gives a dim light until activated.

In both cases, you get to see at night without waking anybody else up. And you're good to go for years because the bulbs are LEDs and use very little energy.

Those of us driving around without built-in infotainment displays in our cars have tried every so-called solution for mounting our phones: suction cups, clips, vent mounts and more. The more expensive and heartier the holder, the faster they wilt, with one exception: peel-off phone magnets.

This WixGear offering costs $6.99 and is simple to use. First, peel off the film on the back. Stick one on the back of your phone (or preferably your phone case), stick the other on your dashboard, and bring your phone close to snap them together. It shouldn't plop to the ground ever again.

A few caveats here. One, the stickiness of the adhesive makes this a permanent fix, so don't expect to move the magnets around once they're on. Two, we recommend finding a vertical position on your dashboard that's close enough to tap, but not too close to a vent. Extreme temperature changes might lead to your phone slipping, while heat blowing on it is never good.

And three, a phone stuck to your dashboard can easily become dangerous. Don't spend too much time playing with it; you could easily get distracted. Apple has also reported that magnets can influence photo quality in certain cases, so keep an eye on that. All said, for speaker phone calls, Google Maps or playing music in your car, peel-off magnets are a tested fix.

There are two types of people: those who are aware of keyboard gunk and those who have blissfully never observed it. If you don't make a habit of cleaning your keyboard, the first tool you're likely to reach for to remove gunk is a brush. You may, with much practice, reach a state of competency where you can finagle stray brush hairs into the cracks around individual keys and dislodge the detritus of your typing life that way. It works, but you can do better.

A handheld, mouse-sized vacuum cleaner may strike you as effete, and in a sense, it is. But for those who like a clean keyboard, a vacuum is quicker and much more efficient than hunting for crumbs with a brush.

Our recommendation also comes with a nozzle, brush and USB charging cable, amenities that nudge it a bit over the $10 mark. While you can find cheaper options, caveat emptor. Some of them look good but get horrible customer reviews. For best results, disconnect your keyboard from a desktop PC (or shut down your laptop) before you clean.

If you haven't kept up with other forms of computer cleaning, it's also worth learning how to clean your screen the right way.

Yes, it's just a lowly phone stand. But it could be a life-saver. If you doubt us, you've probably never had a last-minute Zoom interview where, in lieu of propping your iPhone up against a fruit bowl, you opted for a grainy, handheld shot that gave your potential employer a bold view of your inner nostrils. That isn't the only reason to invest in an inexpensive, versatile phone holder, however.

Modern versions of iOS and Android can turn your phone into a webcam, which is a great way to reuse an old smartphone. But unless you own a folding phone, you're back at square one.

A budget stand like the Nulaxy solves this problem neatly. Whatever stand you end up with, make sure it's universal so you don't have to buy one for everyone in the house. It should have a gap at the bottom to make room for the charging cable, and remain steady whether in landscape or portrait.

There is perhaps nothing more disturbing than a wax-bejeweled earbud. Actually, there is: having to send your dirty, malfunctioning bud back to wherever you purchased it for a diagnosis of "ear wax accumulation." The minds behind the $6.99 Hyashee earbud cleaning pen are on our wavelength here.

Hyashee's earbud cleaners are geared toward AirPods, though the flocking sponge and brush combo will get the job done for any other brand too. As an alternative, there are other universal options like the Midukit cleaner kit. At $9.99, this comes with everything from the Hyashee Cleaner Kit, plus blobs of purple putty and some brushes. Dirt sticks to the putty so you can get it out that way.

Investing a few dollars in an earbud cleaner could improve the longevity of your buds, which makes a difference if you own a quality pair of AirPods. It could also save you from the disgrace of having to send your grimy headphones to a horrified technician at Best Buy. This applies even more if you've got kids with earbuds of their own.

There always ends up being someone who appears less often in family photos because they're the one behind the camera. To restore balance to your family, consider a remote Bluetooth camera shutter. The ATUMTEK Wireless Camera Remote Shutter is a popular choice; it's $8.99 and comes as a two-pack. It should pair with your phone up to distances of ~30 feet, and is compatible with both Android and iOS.

The Zttopo Wireless Bluetooth Camera Shutter Remote is about the same price. Reviews for both hover around 4.5 stars on Amazon. Just be warned that you'll need a tripod (or at least a stand, as above) before you assemble your first full family portrait.

You might not even need to buy a new flash drive to make this work; rifle around in your desk drawer and if you get lucky, you'll unearth a dust-covered treasure from 2011. If you can't find one — or if this is a stocking stuffer for techy friends or relatives — you can always order something like a basic Sandisk 32GB USB 2.0 drive around $9.99.

Why would you want to turn a USB stick into a portable antivirus? Because it might be all you need to clean up an infected Windows machine without having to call the Geek Squad. There are plenty of other neat ways to use an old USB thumb drive aside from storage, but this is our favorite.

When an attack strikes, it can take over your OS, locking you out of your antivirus. When you boot up an antivirus from a separate disk (your USB drive), you can bypass the infected system and clean it from the outside. ESET SysRescue Live is one example that's bootable from a flash drive.

Considering the expense, why not just buy a digital kitchen timer? They're a lot cheaper and you won't have to bark at Google ever again. If we ever calculated how much time we've lost, as a species, to USB-related inconveniences, we would drag USB's creator before the ICC. His name is Ajay Bhatt, by the way, and he's been hiding in broad daylight reaping the fruits of his evil genius since 1996.

Yes, it's just a cable. But oh, what havoc USB can wreak. The problems are endless, but the source of our grief is usually the same: we have the wrong connector for a device. Our machine could also feature the wrong port for the cable we're trying to use. Or, if we're on an Apple laptop, we probably don't have enough ports. In all three cases, we're stuck.

The usual solution is to buy an adapter, but that can still run into issues. Maybe the adapter only works for one situation, like USB-A to USB-C. Or we shell out way more for a multi-purpose hub that has ports handling a bunch of different connections, including HDMI. Fortunately, there's a middle route that costs a lot less: a USB adapter kit.

This 12-piece IANFAN USB kit, at $8.59, offers a bunch of adapters that should cover any USB compatibility issues you face. If you want a handier solution geared to USB-C compatibility, try the Povdite charging cable adapter kit, which comes with a case and its own charging cable. Whichever you choose, you'll be doing your part in the fight against uncontrolled cable sprawl.

Wireless charging pads present an interesting dilemma for the sustainability-conscious gadget shopper. Will replacing one accessory (a USB cable) with another (a charging pad) actually make your life easier and the planet better? Or will you just feed the yawning maw of your accessory drawer one more tasty meal?

A charging pad still needs to be powered by a cord, so it's not truly wireless. But once you plug it in, you won't have to worry about USB compatibility anymore, as long as your devices all support wireless charging. If you can find a quality charging pad that powers multiple devices, that's even better. But that can get tricky, especially when your budget is 10 dollars.

Cheaper charging pads also might not charge as quickly, as outputs vary. And one station might not be enough for a whole household. That said, if you want to test the waters, this NANAMI Fast Wireless Charger Pad costs $9.99. It works with Android phones, iPhones and even earbuds, with a maximum charging power of 15W.

Canada Goose: 3 Key Takeaways for Long-Term Investors

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Store traffic is the open question: the retail network is where the apparel gains either hold up or stall.

On its second quarter of fiscal 2027 earnings call, held on Nov. 5, 2026, Canada Goose (GOOS +2.17%) announced a 5% revenue increase to 274 million Canadian dollars while expanding its gross profit margins. Long-term investors need to evaluate whether the luxury outerwear maker can build a sustainable year-round business while addressing headwinds in retail store traffic. Three things management said stand out.

Management expanded gross margins through improved sourcing, manufacturing efficiency, and higher pricing, rather than relying on short-term external boosts. These operational upgrades helped expand gross margins even as retail network costs increased.

Expanding beyond traditional heavy parkas creates a balanced business model that reduces reliance on cold weather. Sales growth in lighter apparel and rainwear indicates that retail customers increasingly purchase these products from the brand year-round.

Lower physical store traffic dragged down direct-to-consumer (DTC) comparable sales, which measure performance at locations open for over a year. Better online sales and store conversion rates partially cushioned the drop, but store foot traffic remains a clear challenge.

Wholesale orders are expanding, and management projects steady progress toward its annual profitability targets. Operational upgrades and pricing discipline support long-term margins while leadership tracks store traffic recovery.

Execution now centers on boosting store traffic and improving retail conversion during the peak winter buying season.

Where was Nvidia’s security team?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Outsiders had little reason to notice when Nvidia’s longtime chief security officer and head of product security stepped down in August. Just two and a half months later, CEO Jensen Huang himself left the company over growing questions about his extramarital relationships — starting with an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore data center company that U.S. investigators suspect of funneling Nvidia chips to China, then widening to reports about two separate affairs with Nvidia employees.

At the time, David Reber, who spent four years as CSO and seven at Nvidia overall, said he was leaving for health reasons. “This wasn’t a pivot I had planned. But I know what needs to come first: family and health,” he wrote on LinkedIn in late August, disclosing that he’d been diagnosed with young-onset Parkinson’s more than two years ago.

Reber’s replacement, Saša Zdjelar, previously spent 17 years at ExxonMobil in Houston, leaving as a senior IT manager before spending one year at Salesforce and several years at the cybersecurity company ReversingLabs. Zdjelar didn’t respond to questions Thursday about the transition or what the security team knew.

Nothing public connects Reber’s or Zdjelar’s role changes to the Megaspeed matter, but it seems fair to wonder what Nvidia’s security team knew about the government’s scrutiny of Megaspeed and about Huang’s relationships, and whether anyone raised concerns. Reber didn’t respond to questions Thursday. Nvidia also has not answered our related questions.

Not all of those relationships are the business of the security team. Relationships with subordinates typically fall to HR, legal, and the board. But an affair with an executive at a company under federal investigation is a different animal. (Megaspeed has denied any wrongdoing.) Security specialists say affairs can create vulnerabilities that foreign intelligence services look to exploit.

Catching that kind of exposure isn’t easy. The way intelligence services collect information, much of it through everyday conversation rather than hacking, makes it especially difficult for security teams to identify risks, according to retired senior intelligence officer Nicholas Eftimiades, who says he has trained more than 1,000 corporate security professionals.

Some people inside Nvidia clearly knew parts of the picture. Nvidia’s top lawyer was engaged with the government’s Megaspeed inquiry more than a year ago, The New York Times reported earlier this week. (Nvidia has said it regularly contacts officials.) Reports about Huang’s relationships with employees suggest colleagues also knew of them, even if the behavior is not expressly forbidden by Nvidia’s code of conduct. The Times cited five Nvidia employees who said he was involved with a vice president who reported directly to him, and Reuters, citing two people, later reported a second relationship. What’s unclear is whether any of that reached security.

Even if it had reached Nvidia’s security team, Eftimiades, who wrote Chinese Espionage Operations and Tactics in 2020, said a security team’s resources are often limited. For one thing, they’re often underfunded because, unlike some other cost centers, they don’t drive revenue. It’s astonishingly common for CEOs to answer a security team’s warnings with “not a nickel more than compliance,” he said.

Many companies also define security very narrowly, specialists say. In an opinion piece in The Hill, Don Aviv, CEO of the corporate investigations firm Interfor International, and author Jeremy Hurewitz, wrote, “Too many companies still approach security primarily as a matter of protecting facilities, preventing cyberattacks, and responding to physical threats.” The two argued that “[s]ecurity teams need to pay greater attention to insider threats, including the cultivation of executives and employees by foreign intelligence services, suspicious business relationships, conflicts of interest and attempts to obtain sensitive information through trusted personal contacts.”

Still, some of it is beyond what any team can do. Speaking generally, not about Huang, Eftimiades offered a hypothetical in which a CEO complains that he can’t do something because “I’m fighting a board who doesn’t want to do this.” That tells an intelligence asset who the obstacle is. The service then asks, “Who’s the sticking point here?” and looks for ways into that person’s email and phone.

What a rival intelligence service wants is often less technology than policy insight, including who’s pushing to change export rules and who the biggest blocker is. “Intel world tends to be like cancer,” he said. “Once it gets into the body, it looks to spread and looks to reach out.”

The playbook isn’t new, Eftimiades added, “particularly for China.” Any executive dealing with the country “will tell you that they’ve been approached.” He said there are also “tons of diplomat stories” of U.S. diplomats walking into a hotel room to find a woman on the bed. He said what’s newer compared with Soviet era spying is the relationships these services cultivate now often last years, not weeks.

Technology can augment that human intelligence, Eftimiades said, pointing to IMSI catchers are one example. These devices mimic a cell tower so that nearby phones connect to them. A basic version can cost about $1,000 and fit in a suitcase, he said, though government-grade models cost far more.

Eftimiades says no one knows yet if these were used against Huang, whose phone was presumably hardened such that compromises could be detected, if not stopped altogether. But either way, what Nvidia’s security team knew, and whether it could have acted on it, should become clearer as the board’s independent investigation and federal inquiries proceed.

In the meantime, Eftimiades said he hopes the whole episode is “a wake-up call” for U.S. industry, which he said is mostly “clueless” about China’s tactics. “We have lost trillions [of dollars] in intellectual property,” is his guess. “Whatever we think we’re doing isn’t working.”

As a native Southern Californian, I’ve never seen a white Christmas. The closest I can get without getting on a plane is during Disneyland’s holiday season. Every night, without fail, Main Street U.S.A. and the area in front of “It’s A Small World” become a winter wonderland, and it feels like a fantasy. That is, unless it's raining. I didn’t know before exactly why rain would stop the snow from falling upon Disneyland, but someone just made the reason quite evident.

“Disneyland’s Wintertime Enchantment” makes snowfall in Southern California possible every night at 7:30 p.m. and 8:30 p.m. However, don’t count on it if it’s raining. And FYI, there’s a lot of it coming this week after some notable flooding at Disneyland last month. Here’s why:

A post shared by Molly Evans (@happilyevansafterall)

Now, I hate to ruin the magic, but Disneyland snow isn’t actually snow. It’s something that’s been named "snoap" by fans, because it’s practically soap disguised as snow. It's dispersed by hidden rooftop machines.

According to the Los Angeles Times, Disneyland’s snow is made up of 98% water mixed with a non-hazardous, proprietary surfactant (soap) blend. On any other day, the “snow” evaporates shortly after landing somewhere and leaves no trace on guests or around the park. However, when too much water mixes with it, you get a very soapy (and gross) ground!

As you can see in the Instagram post, when the “snoap” and rain happen at the same time, it creates a major slip hazard for the guests. Therefore, it is usually canceled on rainy days. In this recent instance, the guest reports that snow was accidentally turned on at 10:30 p.m., causing an issue everywhere in the park where snow fell.

Even if the snowfall is stalled by rain, most of the other holiday festivities can still happen during such weather conditions. The firework show will have to be canceled during active rain, heavy cloud cover or high winds, but the projections at “It’s A Small World” will typically still run.

The “It’s A Small World” ride also has a special holiday version if you’re not into that song. The World of Color - Season of Light over at California Adventure can also usually hold up unless rain is paired with severe wind or lightning. And you can meet Santa Claus at Mickey’s Toontown Trolley Barn, which is located indoors.

One of my personal favorite holiday traditions is the A Christmas Fantasy Parade. I just love seeing all the characters in their Christmas-themed outfits, too. Over at DCA, there’s also a holiday event called “Guardians of the Galaxy: Awesome Dance Off!” debuting at Avengers Campus. Disneyland’s holiday-themed entertainment runs until January 6 this year, so there’s plenty of time to enjoy this special time of year.

If you’re not able to make it to Disneyland right now, it’s also a good time to check out the 2026 Christmas movie schedule. A ton are available to stream with a Disney+ subscription!

Florida city cancels St. Patrick's Day parade, citing new anti-DEI law

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis (shown here in October) signed the anti-DEI law that Delray Beach officials say prohibits them from putting on cultural celebrations, like a St. Patrick's Day parade. Alex Wong/Getty Images hide caption

One of the first casualties of a new Florida law that prohibits taxpayer dollars from going toward diversity initiatives is a St. Patrick's Day parade in a beach town that has been celebrating its Irish American community for nearly 60 years.

The city of Delray Beach announced this week that it was canceling its popular St. Patrick's Day festivities in compliance with a new law that takes effect on Jan. 1. The city will also withdraw its sponsorship of LGBTQ Pride celebrations in June, officials said.

The law, backed by Republican Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis is part of the state's ongoing crackdown on diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) initiatives that the governor has called discriminatory and said have "no place" in public institutions.

The city lawyer for Delray Beach, a community in southeast Florida with picturesque beaches and a historic downtown, told the mayor that any government-sponsored celebrations of culture, ethnicity or heritage that are not a national holiday (for example: Independence Day) could run afoul of the new anti-DEI law. So, Mayor Tom Carney said, he made the difficult decision to cancel one of the city's biggest events of the year.

In past years, the parade, an annual event since 1970, wound its way through the downtown core of Delray Beach with spectators lined up several-people deep along the route. Local businesses and organizations sponsored floats and giveaways. School bands played as they marched through the streets. Participants included members of the Ancient Order of Hibernians, the largest and oldest Irish Catholic organization in the country.

"I've said it's engrained in the DNA of Delray and I do mean that. It's one of our signature events," Carney, a Republican, told NPR.

The mayor said he's hopeful that the city can find a path forward — and a way to preserve the event.

If a private sponsor were to volunteer to take over the St. Patrick's Day parade, or the June Pride celebrations, Carney said, the city would gladly hand over the reins and support the continuation of these festivities.

"These types of events we should be celebrating because in this country, people can have these events. In other countries, you can't do this. We have great freedoms here," he said. "And if the community wants something and it's not against public policy then I would support them to do it if they're using private dollars. If we can do it, we should do it."

Carney, who is of Irish descent, said the parade was privately run until about eight years ago — so returning to a private-sponsorship model would be a callback to the parade's origins. His own family, he said, was involved in keeping the parade going during the 1980s and 1990s.

Delray Beach officials said they have been inundated with emails, calls and social media messages from constituents who are angry that the community's decades-long celebration had been axed.

"It was kind of a call to action to me," Carney said. "The community really wants to have it, so we should go ahead and have it, we just need to get it privately funded."

The governor, meanwhile, has accused Delray Beach of weaponizing the new anti-DEI measure to make a political point. DeSantis said the city was "engaging in "malicious compliance" in a message he posted on X following the city's cancellation announcement.

"What they are doing is not mandated by Florida law," the governor wrote. "It's just an attempt to make a political point — a point that legacy media will no doubt try to amplify. We've seen these stunts throughout the years. Rinse and repeat."

The mayor said the city is merely trying to stay out of trouble with the law. The measure, SB1134, asserts that any official that violates the provision could face civil or legal consequences.

"The legislation has unintended consequences. The governor may genuinely believe what he believes and I take him at his word; our city attorney came up with a different conclusion," Carney said. "I'm going to leave it at that."

Netflix's New Firefly Replacement Series Can Be Binged In Less Than 4 Hours

While it has been a long time since Firefly graced the airwaves, Netflix’s new space western is a four-hour binge that offers an ideal alternative to the iconic series. There are few one-season TV shows that have quite as long a half life as the short-lived space western Firefly. Firefly’s 14-episode run was met with mixed reviews and underwhelming ratings, but that wasn’t the end of the space opera’s story.

Firefly’s superb DVD sales, as well as the outspoken fan community online, prompted a movie spinoff in the form of 2005’s Serenity. Even though Serenity wasn’t a box office hit, the movie was beloved by critics, and a massive array of subsequent sci-fi shows acknowledged Firefly as a formative influence. There is SyFy’s space opera Killjoys, which often feels like the show Firefly could have been, as well as 2015’s Dark Matter, which was a love letter to Firefly in tone and premise alike.

In 2016, Jonathan Nolan’s Westworld reboot took Firefly’s mix of a futuristic sci-fi setting and traditional western elements and turned these same ingredients into a darker, more austere dystopian mystery box series. More recently, Netflix’s latest high-profile show has blended western and sci-fi tropes in a fresh, original story that has more than a touch of Firefly to it. An animated show created by LeSean Thomas, Bass X Machina takes place in a steampunk wild west where supernatural threats, giant robots, and gunslinging cowboys vie for dominance.

Within this pitiless milieu, Brian Tyree Henry voices the titular Bass Reeves, a bounty hunter and Civil War veteran. Joining Bass is Tati Gabrille’s Dana, a teenage engineer searching for her uncle who has disappeared, and Cree Summer’s Ahni, a magical mystic. With a superb ensemble voice cast including Wayne Knight and Janelle Monáe, Bass X Machina has no shortage of star power.

However, much like Apple TV’s Murderbot, this sci-fi series isn’t reliant on its impressive cast alone to keep viewers engaged. With epic, immersive battles, constant twists, and a wild mix of genres at its core, Bass X Machina is a truly fresh take on the real-life story of Bass Reeves that is unpredictable, inventive, and brimming with the unlikely fusion of sci-fi and western plot elements that made Firefly so memorable all those years ago.

On the big screen, attempts to mix sci-fi and westerns have often fallen flat. One need only look at expensive misfires like 2011’s Cowboys & Aliens and 1999’s Wild Wild West to know that, for whatever reason, the pairing doesn’t seem to attract interest from audiences at the box office. Moreover, even the kindest critics would struggle to call these movies overlooked classics.

Westerns and sci-fi don’t seem to mix at a practical level when it comes to feature films, at least not on a blockbuster scale. In contrast, Bass X Machina proves that Netflix’s animated series could take the same character who was the subject of a po-faced Taylor Sheridan Western series, Lawman: Bass Reeves, and have him fighting killer machines alongside paranormal mystics in a twisted reinvention of the Wild West.

With stunning imagery that calls to mind Netflix’s earlier genre blender Blue Eye Samurai, Bass X Machina admittedly makes a pretty dramatic visual contrast with Firefly’s deliberately low-tech vision of the far future. However, fans of Firefly’s fast-paced action, sharp dialogue, themes of found family, and truly original mix of the two genres will find plenty to love here.

Only time will tell if Bass X Machina will receive a second season, or if the show will suffer the same ignominious fate as Firefly as well as sharing its atypical mix of genres. However, for now, Netflix’s Bass X Machina is an ideal substitute for any Firefly fans who still miss the influential show.
