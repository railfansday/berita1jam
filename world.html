How Berlin's Arab queers are building their community

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

As the co-founder of the ADIRA Drag Festival, Lebanese performance artist, drag performer, DJ and cultural organizer Hassandra has become an essential part of Berlin's queer artistic landscape.

"Adira" is an Arabic slang term used in Levantine queer culture to describe someone with a strong presence — someone powerful and accomplished.

It is also the name of a Berlin-based collective that curates events and club nights celebrating the diversity of queer Arab* culture. The asterisk signals the inclusion of non-Arab ethnic minorities and people with diverse identities and roots in Southwest Asia and North Africa.

Among the collective's events is the ADIRA Drag Festival, which celebrated its third edition in Berlin in late October.

This year's theme, "Who's Your Diva?!," brought together drag performers paying tribute to legendary figures from across the Eastern Mediterranean who have become queer icons — from Egyptian music legend Umm Kulthum to Turkish star Bülent Ersoy and Lebanese pop icon Haifa Wehbe.

Hassandra, co-founder of ADIRA and the ADIRA Drag Festival, was among the performers, taking on the persona of Lebanese pop star Elissa.

"It is very important for me to bring my Lebanese Arab identity and culture into what I do, because I think it's a very beautiful culture. And there's a lot of campy, glamorous, beautiful divas," Hassandra said.

Elissa's "whole discography is very romantic, raw, soft, kind of like very Adele vibes," Hassandra told DW ahead of the October 30 show. "However, her personality is known to be the extreme opposite. She's very feisty. Very blunt, very unfiltered. Not soft at all as a person." The star is also known for speaking out on political issues in Lebanon. She has publicly voiced support for LGBTQ+ communities in the Middle East, while also frequently sparking controversy across the Arab world with her outspoken positions on other political issues.

Hassandra's ADIRA Drag Festival performance addresses this complexity, ultimately "tapping into her sort of controversial political opinions," the drag artist explained.

This reflects one of the ideas Hassandra hopes to promote with the theme of this year's festival. "As queer people, we tend to idealize a lot of pop divas. But these pop divas are very imperfect in their nature," Hassandra said. The show provides an opportunity to critically revisit these divas' legacy through a more critical lens. "And this sort of critical lens can be taken into the outside world, as in: never follow anyone in life blindly. No one should follow any politician, any political party, any pop diva, any activist blindly."

Born in Beirut in 1994, Hassandra studied theater and stage acting at the Lebanese University before moving to Berlin in 2017. "I would say it was it was just a very sort of impulsive decision at the age of 22. I mean, there's no doubt that the economic situation was really bad in Lebanon and continued to become even worse," the artist said.

In Berlin, Hassandra became increasingly involved in the city's independent art scene and queer nightlife. Drag emerged as another form of self-expression for the actor.

Through performances that combine theatrical intensity, political humor, grotesque aesthetics and moments of tenderness, Hassandra challenges conventional understandings of gender and identity.

Their work explores themes of home, nostalgia, displacement and queer Arab culture. These ideas are also central to ADIRA, which Hassandra founded in 2023 with Syrian-German DJ and cultural producer Zuher Jazmati, also known as xanax_attax.

Through panel discussions, performances and music events, ADIRA fosters collective participation and community-building.

As Islamophobia and homophobia rise alongside the far right, spaces like ADIRA offer a sense of belonging and support for Arab queer communities.

That sense of community has become even more important following the deadly Islamist terrorist attack during Berlin's Christopher Street Day pride parade in July.

In the aftermath, Hassandra felt caught between their different identities. As an Arab queer person, "you fall in this position where then you start receiving a lot of racism," they said.

"And I think this is why it's important, for us queer Arabs, to say we are sad over what happened, but that we also reject any of the racism that comes from that," Hassandra said. "I think we [the Arab community] are being used in a lot of ways as a punching bag for a lot of things that are happening."

Rather than allowing the attack to fuel hatred and racism, Hassandra said, "we should make Germany a safer place for everyone to live in."  "There's place for everyone. It's a huge country," they added.

Divers Spent 7 Years Hunting a Ghost Ship Linked to a Nazi Spy Plot. They Just Found It.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A citizen-led search found the SS Cornwallis—and revived a forgotten Nazi spy story in Maine.

For seven years, a group of volunteer divers scoured historical records and the coast of Maine in search of one of New England’s largest undiscovered World War II shipwrecks. Now the SS Cornwallis has been found. The Nomad Exploration Team located the Canadian merchant vessel, which a German U-boat sank off Mount Desert Rock in 1944. The discovery also helps tell the story of the 43 people aboard the boat who lost their lives in the attack and revives interest in the German spies linked to the sinking.

The team used side-scan sonar to map the ocean floor where they believed the Cornwallis might rest, and they nearly overlooked the 400-foot, 5,458-ton vessel. Some of the group’s most harrowing dives later confirmed that the sonar anomaly really was the lost ship. “It was exhilaration,” said Danny Allan, a founding team member, according to Seacoastonline.

The nine-member, New Hampshire-based team began the search in 2019 with a rough location about 10 miles southwest of Mount Desert Rock. By drawing on historical records, the ship’s blueprints, and local fishermen’s observations, they narrowed the search to three likely sites. Even so, the work wasn’t going to be easy.

“There are big tides up off the Mount Desert area,” team member Jeff Goodreau told the Portland Press Herald. “A lot of weather, a lot of fog … Any way you look at it, the challenge is just getting there, whether it’s a five-hour run from Portsmouth or trailering a boat up there.”

In fact, the team nearly missed the wreck twice. On a first pass within 300 feet of it, they didn’t recognize the sonar image. Later, while they were reprogramming the GPS, they almost skipped past it again when it showed up just 100 feet away. “We would’ve written off that whole section as ‘we already looked there’ and wouldn’t have found it,” Allan said.

Reaching the wreck, nearly 400 feet down, was just as tricky. Team member Don Ferrara called it the scariest dive he’d ever done, and Goodreau described the site as deep, cold, and full of current. Still, “We were over the moon,” Ferrara said of the find.

“You’re the first person to see and touch this wreck literally in 80 years, so you’re ecstatic,” Allan said. “But it’s also extremely sobering because this is a grave. 43 souls lost their lives on this wreck.”

The tragedy happened in the Gulf of Maine as the Canadian ship was heading home to Saint John, New Brunswick, after loading sugar and molasses in Barbados. At 6 a.m. on December 3, 1944, the German submarine U-1230 fired a single torpedo that struck the forward cargo hold, and the Cornwallis sank in less than 10 minutes, according to the team. Only five of the 48 people aboard, most of them Canadian, survived. Fishing boats picked up the survivors, and a U.S. Navy vessel then carried them to Rockland, Maine.

The Cornwallis wasn’t a target the U-boat had been hunting. “The U-boat wasn’t looking for them,” Ferrara said. “They were just there.”

The sinking alerted the U.S. to the U-boat’s presence and prompted an investigation into its mission. By interviewing witnesses in Hancock, the FBI learned about Germany’s Operation Elster (Operation Magpie in English). Just days before the attack, the same submarine had landed two German spies, Erich Gimpel and William Colepaugh, near Hancock Point. Their mission was to gather intelligence in the United States. The pair took a taxi to Bangor, a train to Portland, and then made their way to New York City. In the end, the plot fell apart from within: Colepaugh lost his nerve, confided in a friend, and turned himself in, which led to both men’s capture.

This wasn’t the first time the Cornwallis had been torpedoed. The ship was built in Vancouver, British Columbia, in 1921. In 1942, a torpedo struck it while it was anchored at Barbados, and it partially sank in shallow water. After repairs, it returned to service.

“For the team, finding the wreck was the culmination of years of persistence,” Allan said in a statement, according to MassLive. “For the families and communities connected to the ship, it brings renewed attention to a largely forgotten tragedy.”

The Cornwallis is the latest find for the team, which had already located the USS Eagle 56 and the SS William H. Machen. “I love history,” Allan said. “My father was a history teacher. Other people like to see pretty fish and reefs. For me, I love to go see wrecks.”

What US midterm elections mean for Ukraine, Iran and elsewhere

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Donald Trump's Republicans control the US Congress, but that could change after the midterm elections. The outcome of the ballot will not only impact domestic policy but also conflicts in Europe and the Middle East.

The outcome of the midterm elections will shape US policy for years — and have repercussions around the globe. Decisions made in Washington affect both US adversaries such as Iran and countries such as Ukraine, which is seeking more support from the United States.

President Donald Trump does not have the final say when it comes to deciding where the United States goes to war or which countries get financial or military aid. While the president wields more power in the US than in parliamentary democracies such as Germany, he does not govern alone — even in the land of opportunity.

The House of Representatives and the Senate, the two chambers of Congress whose composition voters will decide this Tuesday, wield considerable influence. They decide on legislation and government spending and also serve as a check on the president and the intelligence agencies.

The president can veto legislation passed by Congress. Congress can override that veto with a two-thirds majority in both chambers. The legislature also handles impeachment proceedings. In addition, the president can only appoint Cabinet members, US ambassadors and federal judges from a list of nominees the Senate approves.

Trump’s Republicans currently hold majorities in both chambers of Congress, making it easier for the president to govern. But recent polls suggest the Democrats could take control of at least the House of Representatives and possibly even the Senate. This could shift the direction of major foreign policy issues.

Joe Biden, a Democrat, was in the White House when Russia launched its invasion of Ukraine. Alongside Europe, the US stood out as a reliable partner, supporting Ukraine with financial aid, military expertise and, above all, large-scale weapons deliveries.

The situation changed abruptly under Trump. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy  famously received a notably frosty welcome during his 2025 visit to the White House. Trump now openly admits that he gets along better with Russian President Vladimir Putin than with Zelenskyy.

The US has suspended sanctions against Russia and reduced financial and military aid to Ukraine. If Democrats win majorities in both chambers of Congress, they could put increased support for Ukraine on the agenda.

"Democrats criticize the Trump administration for not doing enough for Ukraine," Brandon Conradis, a former DW journalist and now an editor at Bloomberg Government, told DW. "They can push legislation that would provide more aid to Ukraine."

If Trump vetoed such legislation, the outcome would depend on the balance of power in Congress. But a two-thirds majority could override his veto with support from some Republicans.

Trump launched the war against Iran alongside Israel on February 28 without consulting Congress. Under the US Constitution, Congress must approve a formal declaration of war against another country. Democrats have repeatedly voted against the war and even passed a resolution, together with some Republicans, calling for an end to the hostilities. The Trump administration has ignored it.

"I don't think Congress will have huge powers here," said Michael O'Hanlon, director of research for foreign policy at the US think tank Brookings Institution, told DW, but added that Congress could perhaps prevent a major escalation by refusing to fund it.

US support for aid projects around the world that help fight Ebola, AIDS and hunger was sharply cut back by President Trump shortly after he began his second term. Among other measures, he shut down USAID, the US agency responsible for foreign development assistance.

Whether a Democratic-controlled Congress could "reactivate" the agency against the president's wishes is questionable. But Congress could push for increased aid spending and investigate the controversial closure of USAID.

"Democrats would have oversight power and use it to scrutinize the closing of USAID," Conradis said. "There will be hearings, and they will drag officials they view as responsible in front of Congress."

Ultimately, the US remains a country with a Republican president. There will be no complete reversal of course. "It's not like if Democrats win control of Congress, everything is going to change. But some things will change because the power dynamics will be different."

Tested: 2027 Genesis GV60 Magma Is a Riot

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Genesis brand’s first performance variant is quick and playful, yet sophisticated.

The Genesis brand’s first performance variant isn’t subtle, particularly when painted in its signature Magma Orange hue. And the ends of its rear wing are turned up, which, to us, gives a devil’s-horns vibe. That’s appropriate, because when driving the Magma, there might as well be a persistent devilish presence on your shoulder whispering, “Push harder.”

The smirks on our faces when driving the Magma are mostly about its immensely swift acceleration, satisfying ride and handling, or the silly-but-impressive 9000-rpm fake engine noises. But the name of this new subbrand also makes us think of “liquid hot magma” (in a Dr. Evil voice, of course).

The GV60 Magma is based on the excellent Hyundai Ioniq 5 N, which introduced the fake shifting and faux engine noises to this electric platform. Peak output is the same 641 horsepower as the Ioniq 5 N, but the Magma gets the full luxury treatment. In fact, the number of Magma-specific touches is staggering for such a low-volume model, including front bucket seats and black suede upholstery with orange stitching—even the key fob dons a Magma Orange shell. Although the front seats are comfortable and supportive, we were surprised to find no lumbar adjustability.

There are also plenty of Magma-specific displays in the gauge-cluster screen that have a chronograph feel to them. They include information such as the temperatures of the two motors and battery pack, as well as the 9000-rpm tachometer integrated into the speedometer display. Then there are all the configuration settings for the chassis and powertrain modes in the center screen. It’s all thoughtfully and tastefully executed. Plus, there are elements from the regular GV60 that still wow first-timers, such as the circular shifter that flips over and looks like a crystal ball when the car is parked.

While the Ioniq 5 N mimics a 7750-rpm four-cylinder with its engine cosplay, the Magma aims higher, instead playing the tune of a 9000-rpm V-6. Is it gimmicky? Absolutely. But it’s also impressively executed. And loud: With the adjustable noise level turned up all the way, the Magma’s interior volume measures 102 decibels—about the same as a Corvette ZR1X—as it rips its way repeatedly to its pseudo-9000 rpm over and over on the way to a 166-mph top speed.

Despite weighing roughly 90 pounds more, the Magma’s performance is all but identical to the Ioniq 5 N’s, with 60 mph arriving in a scant 3.0 seconds flat and a quarter-mile blitz of 11.1 seconds at 126 mph. The two run neck and neck, at least until the Magma’s longer Boost mode (15 seconds versus 10 in the Ioniq 5 N) causes it to pull ahead above 120 mph. Although there’s a Boost button on the steering wheel, helpfully, the Magma’s extra hit automatically engages when you push the accelerator all the way to the floor.

Unlike in the Ioniq 5 N, where using the shift mode significantly slows acceleration, Genesis claims no acceleration difference when using the Magma’s fake-shifting mode. With large power cuts between fake shifts, we were highly skeptical. But we measured it both ways, and the Magma does in fact run all but identical times whether faux-shifting or not.

Wearing newer Pirelli P Zero PZ5 Elect summer rubber, versus PZ4 versions on the Ioniq 5 N, the Magma hung on for 0.97 g on the skidpad, slightly exceeding the Ioniq’s 0.96 g. Braking from both 70 mph and 100 mph was pretty much identical.

While you might think that the Magma is an always-on type of car that never settles down—some performance variants can be like that—we were even more impressed at the layers of sophistication on display here. The ride and handling balance in its middle GT mode is terrific even on choppy, undulating roads. Steering is sharp and satisfying. Driven back to back to back against its most obvious competitors—a Cadillac Optiq-V and a Tesla Model Y Performance—the Magma was hands down our favorite. The only part of its dynamic behavior that we didn’t love is a brake pedal that feels slightly squishy as it blends between regen and the brake pads and rotors.

There were some other negatives too. For one, the GV60 Magma entry point is more than 10 grand dearer than the Ioniq 5 N’s, although the Magma’s $72,145 base price is still a relative bargain compared to plenty of other luxury-brand EVs. At highway speeds, the Magma isn’t luxury-car hushed, though, with obvious wind noise, and it measured no quieter (70 decibels and 25 sones) than the Ioniq 5 N when cruising at 70 mph.

Also, the GV60 has a slightly shorter wheelbase than the Ioniq 5, and rear-seat space suffers slightly accordingly. There’s not a lot of foot room under the front seat, and the rear seating height is rather low as well, pushing occupants’ knees upward. Then there’s range. Compared with the least efficient variant of the GV60 line, the Magma is down a significant 45 miles of EPA range, landing at a 222-mile estimate. But that’s a mixed-driving figure, and in our 75-mph highway-range test, the Magma made it only 200 miles.

Is the Magma answering a question many buyers are asking? Clearly not, as Genesis is selling it only at a limited number of dealers in just three states: New York, New Jersey, and California.

But for those who find their way to a Magma, fun is in store.

Gossamer Gear The Free Tent Review: A Freestanding Double-Wall Tent Under 2 Pounds?

The Gossamer Gear The Free is a one-person, double-wall, freestanding ultralight backpacking tent. Unlike the brand’s traditional trekking-pole shelters, The Free uses a DAC aluminum hub-and-pole system to create a freestanding structure that can be easily repositioned at camp.

Constructed with Gossamer Gear’s exceptionally tear-resistant, PFAS-free nylon fabric, The Free aims to deliver durability and weather protection without unnecessary weight. At just 29.8 ounces, this tent joins the Durston X-Dome 1 and the Nemo Hornet Osmo 1 as one of the lightest freestanding, double-wall tents in the world — and it’s the most affordable of the three, to boot.

But does its minimalist design strike the right balance between weight savings, comfort, and practicality on the trail?

After taking The Free on a section hike of the Continental Divide Trail in northern New Mexico, I came away impressed by its weather-resistant construction and sturdy design. However, its narrow dimensions and some setup quirks left me wishing Gossamer Gear had not sacrificed quite so much interior space in the pursuit of ultralight performance.

I tested The Free during a section hike of the Continental Divide Trail in northern New Mexico, during which the weather was frequently miserable and wet. The conditions gave me an excellent opportunity to evaluate the tent’s weather protection, condensation management, and overall durability.

One of the most noticeable differences between The Free and my previous shelters was its double-wall design. I appreciated being able to avoid the persistent dampness that comes from brushing against a single-wall tent’s interior in wet conditions. In addition, a few nights were particularly windy, and I was impressed by how solidly The Free held up.

However, my overall experience was mixed. I felt that The Free lacked the interior roominess I’ve grown accustomed to in The One. Despite being a relatively small user, I often felt cramped and occasionally claustrophobic.

The Free’s DAC aluminum hub-and-pole system distinguishes it from Gossamer Gear’s traditional trekking-pole shelters. The tent can stand without stakes when pitched with its inner mesh body, making it possible to move the shelter to a different spot before securing it.

Like most freestanding tents, the fly requires staking out the vestible for proper tension, and additional stakes for guylines in less-than-ideal conditions. While the freestanding design sounds wonderfully convenient, I found the initial setup more complicated than expected.

Gossamer Gear developed a custom ultralight shelter fabric called ClearSkies. It combines 15D recycled Robic fabric with a 20D dope-dyed ripstop silicone/polyurethane coating. The company designed this material to improve strength and waterproofing while avoiding PFAS.

The fabric has a reported hydrostatic head rating of 4,923 mm — waterproof enough to withstand heavy downpours and wind-driven rain. The seams are factory-taped.

The fabric is manufactured using a dope-dye process, which incorporates color into the polymer before the fibers are formed. This can improve colorfastness compared with conventional surface dyeing and reduce water and energy consumption during manufacturing.

In the field, the fabric felt reassuringly robust for such a lightweight shelter. During wet and windy conditions, I appreciated the protection it provided without the bulk or weight of a heavier tent.

Although the double-wall construction kept me from directly touching the wet fly, moisture accumulated on its underside, particularly when I kept the vestibule closed to retain warmth overnight. By morning, the fly could be soaked and noticeably heavier.

The fly also sits close to the inner mesh body, helping maximize the available interior space. Unfortunately, that limited separation meant that contact between the inner tent and the wet fly could transfer moisture inside. The Free offers the advantages of a double-wall shelter, but its compact geometry makes condensation management especially important.

The Free’s inner mesh body must be pitched first. Two aluminum poles connect through a central pivoting hub that attaches to the inner tent’s ceiling, creating the tent’s basic structure.

This inner-first setup became a drawback in rainy weather. While I worked to get the fly in place, the mesh interior was exposed to the elements. Since the fly was often still damp from the previous night, assembling the shelter without adding more moisture wasn’t always easy.

The color-coded poles and corresponding corner attachments help guide assembly, but I struggled to keep everything in place while inserting the poles into their designated corners (often with comical results). In breezy conditions, the lightweight tent threatened to slither away before I could finish setting it up. I initially tried holding the corners down with my feet, but eventually resorted to staking them down. That rather defeated the convenience I had expected from a freestanding tent.

The Free is reasonably straightforward once you become familiar with its pole arrangement, but I wouldn’t describe the setup as effortless, especially when the weather is uncooperative.

As I mentioned above, The Free felt more cramped to me than Gossamer Gear’s The One, which I have used extensively. Although the two tents have similar floor dimensions (The Free is even a few inches longer and wider), The Free has a significantly lower peak height and the angle of the side walls feels more restrictive.

The Free has a tapered floor measuring 34 inches wide at the head and 21.5 inches at the foot. At 86 inches long, it’s long enough for most solo backpackers, but the narrowing floor is a significant consideration when choosing a sleeping pad.

My pad fit comfortably, with a little room to spare. However, anyone using a wide sleeping pad should check the dimensions carefully. Per Gossamer Gear’s website, “while The Free’s floor dimensions don’t technically support a traditional 25″ wide pad, it does fit — it’ll just push the side walls a bit. For the best fit, we recommend going with a wide tapered pad instead.”

At 5 foot 7, I could sit upright and even stretch my legs inside. Still, I frequently brushed against the sides or top of the tent. When the fly was wet, those accidental touches could bring moisture into the living space. The compact dimensions are understandable in a shelter designed for minimal weight, but the trade-off was more noticeable than I expected. I often felt confined, particularly when spending extended periods inside during bad weather.

Gossamer Gear lists a maximum user height of 6 feet 2 inches, but height alone doesn’t tell the whole story. Sleeping-pad width, sleeping position, and how much room you want to sit up or move around all influence how comfortable this tent will feel.

The D-shaped doors open into a modest vestibule with enough room for my backpack and a few smaller items. This was useful for keeping gear protected from the weather without cluttering the sleeping area.

My main complaint was the vestibule door’s toggle system. When rolled back and secured, the door tended to hang into the tent rather than staying neatly out of the way. One morning, I cinched it closed with the toggle, only to have accumulated ice fall directly onto my sleeping bag. Magnets or a more effective door-retention system would have made this detail less frustrating.

The Free includes two interior pockets: one near the ceiling and another beside the door. While I appreciated having somewhere to stash small essentials, I found the pockets less useful than expected. Items tended to slide out, making it difficult to keep things securely organized. For an ultralight tent, the storage options are welcome, but I would have preferred pockets that hold their contents more reliably.

The Free impressed me with its sturdy pole structure and durable-feeling fabric. It handled windy conditions well, and the double-wall design provided a welcome barrier between me and the wet fly.

However, the fly’s tendency to collect condensation and its close proximity to the inner mesh deserve consideration. Proper ventilation and careful pitching are important, especially in cold, damp conditions.

This was the tent’s biggest weakness for me. Although I could sit up and move around to a limited extent, the tapered floor and close-fitting walls made the interior feel cramped. Taller users and anyone carrying a wide sleeping pad should pay particular attention to the dimensions.

The color-coded pole system makes the design reasonably intuitive, but pitching the inner tent first can be inconvenient in rain or wind. I also found myself staking the corners during setup to keep the lightweight body under control.

The freestanding structure is useful, but it doesn’t eliminate every need for stakes or make the pitching process as simple as I had hoped.

At 29.8 ounces, The Free is impressively light for a double-wall, freestanding shelter. Its compact packed dimensions make it an appealing option for backpackers who want to keep their pack weight down without switching to a trekking-pole shelter.

The ClearSkies fabric feels like a thoughtful balance between low weight and ruggedness. Despite its delicate-sounding 15-denier thickness, the material is designed for improved tear resistance, and I was impressed by the tent’s overall robustness during my hike.

The use of recycled Robic fabric and PFAS-free materials is a welcome feature. The dope-dyed fabric construction is also designed to improve colorfastness. These choices make The Free appealing to backpackers who want a lightweight shelter without overlooking material considerations.

The Free comes with six aluminum stakes, four guylines, a pole-repair section, a stuff sack, and a pole bag.

The Gossamer Gear The Free makes a compelling case for an ultralight, freestanding backpacking tent. And while it isn’t the featherweight champion when compared to minimalist trekking pole shelters, The Free is still one of the lightest tents in the freestanding double-wall category. At $400, it wins heavily on its value-to-weight ratio. However, its impressive weight savings come with noticeable compromises in livability and convenience.

If minimizing pack weight is your top priority, it deserves a look. If comfort and easy setup matter just as much, I would consider a roomier alternative.

A new book looks at how Marcel Duchamp’s ‘impossible’ love for the sculptor Maria Martins changed his work

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Marcel Duchamp’s final work, Étant donnés (1946-66), an assemblage created in collaboration with Maria Martins © Artists Rights Society/ADAGP/Association Marcel Duchamp

Glacial, calculated, distant, sly: these are among the adjectives used to describe the “godfather of conceptual art” Marcel Duchamp (1887-1968). In the opening chapter of Impossible, a monograph by the prominent Duchamp scholar Francis M. Naumann, the artist appears as a (mostly) reticent introvert. But over the course of 192 richly illustrated pages, that familiar, spectral figure becomes flesh and blood: tender, wistful, even romantic. Duchamp falls in love.

Naumann’s account of the French-American artist’s relationship with the Brazilian sculptor Maria Martins (1894-1973) unfolds through a series of speculative scenes that draw, at times uneasily, on the conventions of the romance novel, interwoven with formal analysis that demonstrates reciprocal artistic influence. The author’s nearly five decades of writing on Duchamp, along with the rigorous notes and chronology included at the back of the volume, help temper any initial scepticism about the balance between projection and documentation. From Duchamp and Martins’s first meeting in March 1943 until their respective deaths five years apart, Naumann argues that the two sustained a profound emotional connection, even if the most intense—and presumably physical—phase of their relationship lasted less than a decade. Despite Duchamp’s evident longing, Martins never left her three children or her husband, a Brazilian ambassador to the US.

What first attracted the two artists intellectually to one another is not fully known. But from early on, Duchamp was no longer indifferent, at least where Martins was concerned. Asked to design an announcement for an exhibition at Julien Levy Gallery in December 1943, he placed a cupid figure atop a chessboard depicting an endgame problem with no way to win. “There is no solution,” the artist had famously said, “because there is no problem”. Now, however, there was a problem. Despite a previous marriage and a long-term relationship with the American artist Mary Reynolds, Duchamp had never been in love before. At this point, 26 years had passed since he made Fountain and 20 since he had declared The Bride Stripped Bare by Her Bachelors, Even (The Large Glass) definitively unfinished. It was the problem of Martins, Naumann suggests, that reanimated Duchamp’s interest in making—shifting the affair from a biographical register into a lens through which to view Duchamp’s late work.

The Impossible, III (1946) by Maria Martins is a comment on her relationship with Duchamp © MoMA, New York

As the relationship develops, Naumann focuses on the lovers’ sculptures, exchanged or produced in parallel, as a metonym for the physical encounters he is otherwise obliged to omit. There is Paysage fautif (faulty landscape), an intimate piece made with his own seminal fluid on plastic and backed with black satin, that Duchamp gave to Martins in 1946. And then her cast bronze The Impossible, III (at the Museum of Modern Art in New York) from the same year, in which two creatures, their heads ringed in tendrils, reach toward one another in a gesture that is at once an embrace and a devouring. Martins was, Naumann writes, “unquestionably referring to the impossibility of her relationship with Duchamp”. The goddess figures borrowed from Brazilian folklore that populated her second show at the Valentine Gallery in New York became increasingly abstract and psychologically charged as her career progressed, perhaps reflecting Duchamp’s own preference for the cerebral over the real, the concept over the image.

There is then no shortage of confusion surrounding Duchamp’s final work, Étant donnés: 1º La chute d’eau, 2º Le gaz d’éclairage (Given: 1. the waterfall, 2. the illuminating gas, 1946-66). Why, after decades of apparent withdrawal, would the celebrated Modernist devote 20 years to a highly illusionistic installation of a nude woman posed in a landscape that recalls Gustave Courbet’s The Origin of the World (1866)—the very tradition of “retinal art” he had long disavowed? Naumann’s retelling, which frames the work as the culmination of their coupling, is in this sense instructive. Martins emerges not merely as muse or model but as collaborator. She lends her body to be cast, but also choreographs its position, advises on process and technique. There is no precedent in Duchamp’s oeuvre for the imagery, yet echoes of Martins’s prints and sculptures persist in both the figure and the vegetal setting. Even after she returned to Brazil and their contact waned, Duchamp retained, through work on the diorama, a means of beholding her body and sustaining their artistic tête-à-tête.

Duchamp’s notebook page inscribed with “That is for Maria”

Interspersed passages from Duchamp’s letters to Martins reveal the force of his affections, rendering it difficult to regard Étant donnés as anything other than an act of devotion, however strange or disconcerting it may appear from the outside. The florid sentiments strain against the claim that it is just another one of his jokes. “At heart, my little one, I am profoundly sad, for I can see all our days passing rapidly and fading away,” he writes. And elsewhere: “I feel totally lost now that we are cut off from each other… This isolation hurts.” Reproduced in one of the final chapters is a notebook page with the words “Please do not throw this on the floor” typed above a hand-drawn heart, filled in with red crayon, and scrawled below it: “that is for Maria”.

Although Martins’s letters to Duchamp are not available to the public, it is said that she kept a photograph of him in her studio, and another on her charm bracelet, alongside a lock of his hair—observations that complicate Naumann’s repeated casting of her as a “femme fatale” who “did whatever she needed to get a man to fall in love with her”. The absence of her subjectivity on the page creates an asymmetry that the text acknowledges yet cannot quite overcome. What she truly made of Duchamp—of his work, their relationship—remains largely unknown.

After her husband’s death in 1965, Martins returned to New York and visited Duchamp, who was by then married to Alexina “Teeny” Duchamp. As Naumann notes, it is possible that he signed and dated Étant donnés then and there, unable to complete it until finally, at long last, they were together again.

Something Was Hiding in This Ancient Roman Capital. It Took a Drought to Finally Reveal It.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Drones caught the ghostly outlines of a 2,000-year-old city—before returning greenery erased them again.

A heatwave wreaked havoc upon all facets of life in Europe to some degree this past summer, but it also provided archaeologists in Switzerland with an interesting opportunity: the chance to view ancient Roman history in an entirely fresh way. With vegetation stripped back and parched, a team researching Aventicum, a once-thriving Roman city, took to the air via drone for a bird’s-eye view of the entire site. From the new vantage point, they discovered things they simply hadn’t ever known existed.

The site in Switzerland’s state of Vaud, which is now known as Avenches, looked different in the unusually hot and dry months of July and August, allowing archaeologists to “observe remains usually invisible underground,” according to a statement from those working at the Aventicum archaeological site. The airborne exploration revealed previously unseen buried structures, redrawing the map of what was once the capital of Roman Helvetica.

“These are the richest discoveries made there in the last 50 years, at least over the last two generations of archaeologists,” said Jordan Anastassov, archaeologist for Vaud and the University of Geneva, according to Reuters.

The archaeologist said less vigorous plant growth occurs atop buried structures. Combine that with a drought-stricken region and wilting vegetation this past summer, and the archaeologists were able to make a number of “observations that complement the data from the excavations in an exceptional way and show the full potential of surface prospecting methods.”

Aerial images not only showed the locations of houses (making up a full neighborhood), expansive villas, and roads, but also highlighted thermal baths and a potential rectangular temple site running nearly 100 feet in length that was lost to history. Some of the buildings appear in sections of the historic city that haven’t been excavated, and the large building sits on what was once thought to be the town’s outskirts.

Founded originally after Julius Caesar conquered local tribes 2,000 years ago, Aventicum had a successful run under Roman rule, eventually succumbing to Germanic invasions, starting in the third century. “We are now asking really interesting questions we wouldn’t otherwise be asking,” said Hugo Amoroso, an archaeologist on the project.

The team hopes on-the-ground exploration of the newly discovered areas will provide fuller detail and a richer history.

The summer heat proved critical in exposing the areas. By September, as temperatures returned to normal and vegetation started to flourish again, many of the newly seen sites slipped from view, although the pools discovered at the bath location were still visible in a struggling alfalfa field. “We aren’t going to wish for droughts,” said Pierre Blanc, director of the site’s excavations, “but we’re going to make the most of this and keep looking.”

The use of droughts to reveal lost sites isn’t a new concept. Going aerial simply helps cover more ground quickly. A post from the tourism website About Switzerland that Anastassov shared on his LinkedIn page noted that a 1976 drought played a role in the discovery of a Roman villa in the nearby municipality of Orbe.

With droughts increasing throughout the world, archaeologists are turning to aerial monitoring as a planned tactic, scheduling surveys systematically to better understand potential excavation sites and more accurately redraw ancient maps, just like at Aventicum.

A college student searching rocky glaciers in Yellowstone finally discovered one of the rarest insects in the world

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A University of Wyoming student discovered three rare ice crawlers in Yellowstone, marking the first known sightings of these cold-loving insects in Wyoming.

In summer 2026, a college student named Will Anderson found three rare bugs called ice crawlers on rocky glaciers in Yellowstone National Park.

As a student at the University of Wyoming, Anderson had spent the whole previous year looking for them without any luck. Ice crawlers are some of the rarest insects on Earth.

Ice crawlers belong to Grylloblattodea, one of the rarest orders of insects in the world. Only 35 species have been described, and many are known only from single localities.

Anderson is a biology major from Casper and a junior in the Wyoming Research Scholars Program. He works with Audrey Lindsteadt, a third-year Ph.D. student in the university’s Program in Ecology and Evolution.

“I am interested in this insect because there is so much not known about them, with their life history, habitat, and distribution all not being fully understood,” Anderson said.

“I also am passionate about and interested in their alpine habitats, which are understudied,” he added.

Starting in late July 2025, the pair surveyed 14 rock glaciers in the Red Mountains next to Heart Lake, in southwestern Yellowstone. They found no ice crawlers, even though Anderson also checked snowfields and set overnight traps.

Most snowfields had already melted by then. The area also had fewer glaciers than ideal, and most of them were small or inactive.

“Despite the close proximity to their known distribution and the plentiful suitable habitat, Gryllos have never been found in Wyoming,” Lindsteadt explained. “Therefore, Will hypothesized the rock glaciers and nearby snowfields that we are studying could have Gryllos.”

On a cold, snowy six-day backpacking trip in late June and early July 2026, Anderson found two ice crawlers near Antler Peak in northwest Yellowstone. Both turned up under rocks he flipped on a large rock glacier.

In mid-July, a mother grizzly and her two-year-old cub bluff-charged the pair and blocked their planned route.

They scrambled up Grey Peak instead and slid down to its two rock glaciers, where Anderson caught a third one. In August, he searched several alpine glaciers in Grand Teton National Park but came up empty.

Over the summer of 2026, he and Lindsteadt walked about 170 miles (274 kilometers).

“The specimens I collected in Wyoming are so far identified to the species Grylloblatta campodeiformis, which is the most widespread North American species, having populations in Montana, British Columbia, and Alberta,” Anderson noted.

He briefly kept two of the insects alive. He could not tell whether they were feeding on the dead insects and plant matter he gave them.

Ice crawlers are wingless insects that stay active in cold conditions, according to North Carolina State University entomologists. Most species are active below freezing and usually die above 50°F (10°C).

They develop through incomplete metamorphosis, from egg to nymph to adult, and their growth is slow. Some may need up to seven years to complete a single generation.

The University of Wyoming describes them as extremophiles that live at temperatures as low as 21°F (-6°C) in humid alpine settings.

By day, they hide under cool rocks near snow and ice. At night, they scavenge, eating mostly other invertebrates, including insects blown onto the snow, along with some plant material.

Little is known of their diet when they live more permanently under the snow, or in the soil through summer and winter.

The order lives in northeastern Asia, Siberia, the western U.S. and Canada. In North America, small, isolated populations occupy mountains and caves in several western states, British Columbia and Alberta.

Rock glaciers resemble alpine glaciers, but a layer of debris covers their ice, insulating and protecting it.

Lindsteadt suspects that ice crawlers prefer rock glaciers to non-glacial talus fields or snowfields because rock glaciers are colder, more humid and more permanent.

“Obviously, with just three species detections, we cannot confidently identify their habitat preferences,” Lindsteadt said. “But they at least point us in a promising direction.”

Yellowstone holds 58 known rock glaciers, and none had been visited or studied before this project. Next summer, the team plans to survey 17 rock glaciers in eastern Yellowstone and revisit others.

Ice crawlers are generally restricted to high-elevation glaciers or low-elevation caves with permanent ice, according to Purdue University entomologists.

The same entomologists warn that many of these habitats are at risk as glaciers and ice fields recede with climate change.

In Anderson’s view, the insects probably play a small part in these ecosystems because of their rarity, but they still belong to the food chains and nutrient cycles of their extreme habitats.

“More importantly, we can use ice crawlers to better understand how these ecosystems work and be more effective managers of them,” he argued.

He is also working on a paper he hopes to publish in a leading alpine or entomology journal.

Anderson will present the findings at the Entomological Society of America’s 74th annual meeting in Columbus, Ohio, on November 8 to 11.

The Bambu Lab H3C is a large-format 3D printer with nine printheads, and it's available now.

Multicolor 3D printing has been around for many years. Like all technologies, manufacturers are constantly looking for ways to push it further than ever before. Bambu Lab announced today the H3C, a large-format 3D printer that uses nine separate nozzles to print not only different colors or types, but also different-sized filaments. It’s designed to allow you to mix and match as you see fit, expanding your creative options beyond the standard.

The Bambu Lab H3C starts at $2,500 and comes with the nine-filament system and Automated Material System. I’ve spent the last few weeks using it, and while I haven’t finished my review yet, I already have quite a few thoughts.

This is not a small machine by any measure. I originally had it on my workbench, but the H3C sits so tall that opening the lid and loading the filament was arduous, even for a 6-foot-1 person like myself. Bambu Lab also sent me a metal rolling cart, sold separately, that lowers the machine to a usable height. That said, it still stands 63 inches tall on the cart, so yeah, it’s a big’un.

Despite its size and complex design, setting it up was simple. I would recommend having a second person around to do it, as it’s cumbersome — but not difficult to plug in all the parts. The AMS 3 that sits atop the machine is different from most of the best 3D printers because the H3C requires it operate. It’s not a separate add-on but an integral part of the machine.

You can tell from every metric that the H3C is designed to be a Bambu Lab flagship. It has all the top-of-the-line hardware, including a heatbed that can reach 120C and an optional but very cool bird’s-eye camera to monitor prints from above. It’s fast, too, with a top toolhead speed of 1,400 mm/s. Of course, that isn’t the print speed, but it does show you the type of speed it could reach if you pushed it to the max.

Printing at high speed does have some disadvantages, though. I have had several print failures on tall prints where the supports or purge tower have fallen due to accidental touches or vibration. The H3C moves fast, prints fast and fails fast if you aren’t careful. Most of those failures occurred at 125% speed, but that’s the standard setting I use for large prints on other machines, and they handle it just fine.

Bambu calls its nine-nozzle system, which heats using conductive heating, the Swaptek system. This isn’t to be confused with another nine-nozzle system you may have heard of: INDX, which uses conductive heating. INDX and Swaptek are extremely similar in execution but differ in a few areas. INDX is purely mechanical, whereas Bambu says its Swaptek system supplies data and power to each tool, allowing the nozzles to start heating faster than those in a standard INDX system — in around 6 seconds per nozzle.

In practice, there isn’t a huge difference between what I’m seeing on the H3C and the Prusa Core One + INDX in terms of speed. The Core One has the advantage that it’s calibrated to avoid a purge tower, and it doesn’t purge as much waste as the Swaptek system. The H3C has its own neat tricks, though: Not only can it print with different nozzle sizes and materials, but it can also print with materials of different diameters.

One of the biggest roadblocks with printing TPU — the very soft, rubbery material that 3D-printed shoes are made from — through an AMS is that it’s too soft to push through the AMS feeder. To combat this, you have to use a harder TPU that isn’t nearly as flexible as you would need for shoes or things that bounce. Adding toolheads capable of printing 2.85mm-diameter materials lets Bambu create TPU that’s more flexible after melting but thicker and easier to feed in the AMS. It works really well.

And it doesn’t stop at TPU, either. You can use 2.85mm PLA with a 0.8mm nozzle to print the infill of a model, while using a 0.2mm nozzle and 1.7mm PLA to print the detailed outer walls. It gives you so many options. The AMS even has two material slots with active heating chambers, so you can print materials that need to be warm and dry, along with other, more shelf-stable materials.

Given the $2,500 price and the H3C’s flagship status, it would be shocking if the print quality were bad. All of the models that didn’t fail due to purge tower collapse look great, and the nine separate nozzles make light work of just about any color project. I love watching this machine print as it swaps across the nozzles at blistering speeds.

Some of them did fail, though, and the H3C was good at catching the errors, pausing the print and letting me know. I chose to keep one of them going despite the failed tower, and it turned out fine, though a little damaged. I had some fun 3D printing my scanned head at life size, using silver for my greying beard — and I love the way it came out.

The CNET test print wasn’t nearly as good as the one the H2C produced, which pretty much maxed out every test, but it wasn’t the worst either. Accuracy was good, and despite the printer’s speed, ringing was kept to a minimum. If you’re looking to go beyond what you thought you could do with a 3D printer, the H3C is the machine to get you there.
