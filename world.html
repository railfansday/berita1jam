5 Funniest ‘Far Side’ Comics About the Meaning of Life

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

If I were forced to find the meaning of life in a daily newspaper comic strip, the first ongoing series I’d probably consult would be Calvin and Hobbes. Despite the fact that Calvin was just a six-year-old, Bill Watterson infused the boy with a deep thoughtfulness and insight that sometimes wrestled with the biggest questions about life, including its very meaning.

My second choice would probably be Peanuts, as Charles Schulz captured that slice-of-life realism better than anyone else. His characters, while not quite as philosophical as Calvin, would sometimes stumble onto something brilliant in the same way that Charlie Brown’s puny tree could somehow perfectly embody the spirit of the Christmas holiday.

I most certainly would not consult The Far Side.

Gary Larson’s single-panel comic strip was no less brilliant than those aforementioned comics, and Larson was no less a genius than​​​​​​​ Watterson or Schulz, but pondering life’s big questions is just not what the guy did. Gary Larson was a gag man, a joke maker, a humorist, not a philosopher. His humor was incredibly dark and maybe even nihilistic at times. The Far Side was smart, often subtle, but not deep and certainly not the kind of outlet where you’ll discern the meaning of existence. It’d be like reading Garfield in the hopes of finding healthy eating habits.

Still, having already located the deeper, meaning-of-life-type comics done by Watterson and Schulz, I thought it'd be an interesting exercise to attempt the same feat with The Far Side. The results only confirmed what I already thought — and loved — about Gary Larson’s humor.

To find Larson’s views on the meaning of life, I thought it would make sense to check out the comics where he depicts God, as there are a number of them. This one pretty much seems to sum up the seriousness Larson has towards the Almighty. His “Acts of God” depicts God putting on performances like a third-rate vaudeville star, doing things like juggling and getting shot out of a cannon. If you compare this with Linus’ sermons from A Charlie Brown Christmas, you’ve got two very different comic strip creators there.

In this comic strip, Larson depicts God at his computer, watching a man walk under a piano, as he’s about to tap the “smite” button on his keyboard. While the previous entry depicted God as a desperate entertainer, this one somehow seems worse as it portrays God as a disconnected, indifferent deity who deals out death with frightening casualness.

And this comic strip takes it to yet another level, going from indifference to outright cruelty, with God adding jerks to the world “just to make it interesting.” While I don’t think the meaning of life can be discovered in this strip, it does make you wonder why are there so many jerks in the world? And if God does exist, why did he create so many jerks?

This one, I think, gets more at what Larson actually thinks about life more than his depictions of God do. It shows two ants sitting on a mushroom, looking up at a starry night sky. One of the ants says to the other, “Just look at those stars tonight. … Makes you feel sort of small and insignificant."

The way I read this joke has a couple of different levels to it. First, I think the surface-level joke is, “haha, they’re ants, their lives are meaningless and they are small.” However, I also wonder if the ants are supposed to represent us. In other words, we’re​​​​​​​ the insignificant little ants staring up at an indifferent universe above.

Maybe there’s more depth to Larson than I gave him credit for.

The way most Far Side comics worked was to have the image work as the setup to the joke and the punchline was the caption below. Because of that, this comic appears to have no punchline at all. It’s just two scientists staring at a complex equation on a chalkboard, with one scientist saying,​​​​​​​ “No doubt about it, Ellington — we’ve mathematically expressed the purpose of the universe. God, how I love the thrill of scientific discovery!”

That isn’t funny and it isn’t the punchline.

This is one of those Far Side comics where you have to go looking for the joke, and I’m convinced the joke is that this complex equation ends in “=0.” Zero, of course, means nothing​​​​​​​, which I’m pretty sure is Larson’s way of saying that there is no purpose​​​​​​​ to the universe and that existence is meaningless — which certainly is a possibility.

Waugh Thistleton Architects set to transform derelict bathing pavilion into Tracey Emin-backed studios and sauna

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Images courtesy of Waugh Thistleton Architects

UK studio Waugh Thistleton Architects is set to transform the Edwardian Westbrook Pavilion in Margate, UK, into an arts centre for British artist Tracey Emin with a cafe, public sauna and changing rooms.

“The project will bring a much-loved seafront building back into use, combining its original purpose with new spaces for art, culture and the local community,” said Waugh Thistleton Architects.

The studio recently won planning permission from the local council to convert the abandoned building, which Emin purchased in 2023, into a facility run by the Tracey Emin Foundation.

The project aims to return the building to its former use as a changing room for swimmers and as a tea room, when it originally opened in 1914.

“Originally opened in 1914, the building provided public changing facilities for sea-bathing and a tea room,” said the studio.

“Following storm damage in the Great North Sea Storm of 1953, subsequent alterations detracted from its original Edwardian character,” it continued.

“The renovated pavilion will accommodate artist studios, an art gallery, clubroom, cafe, sea-bathing changing facilities, a new sauna and refurbished public toilets, restoring its original role as a place for bathing and socialising by the sea.”

Along with making the pavilion a building used by the public again, the studio aims to return the building’s core to its Edwardian look by removing later additions. It will also add glazed facades to the wings on either side of the central pavilion.

The building will be topped by a rooftop terrace, while a public toilet on the site will be renovated.

“The design draws on the proportions and character of the original building, removing later additions and reinstating lost features such as the historic belvederes which top the central tower structures,” said the studio.

“The beachfront facade will see a new glazing system and colonnade to each of the building’s wings, with a new sheltered archway forming a new seating terrace at the building’s heart,” it continued.

“The design seeks to reference the original architecture while providing contemporary needs of a public building. Where original features are references, these are distinguishable architecturally.”

Emin is one of the UK’s best-known living artists. We recently produced a video of a landmark exhibition of her work at Tate Modern.

Recent projects by Waugh Thistleton Architects include plans for the “tallest mass timber office building in the US”.

On ‘psychotic' day of Mavericks big-wave surfing, Kai Lenny flies into another realm

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The surf was so big at Mavericks on Saturday, it was out of the question to grab a conventional surfboard and paddle out to the waves on your own power.

You would get systematically crushed, beaten back without mercy by unrelenting lines of water.

This was strictly a tow-in day, surfers literally towed by Jet Ski from the far outside into an unbroken wave from behind, then letting go of the rope in (ideally) perfect surfing position as it begins to crest.

The approach sounds easy - "like your grandmother could do it," the locals often say. The problem on Saturday was that once you let go of that rope, you were alone on a 50-60-foot face with a nasty, life-threatening element that had some of the world's best surfers backing off.

The 60-foot factor was stunning in itself, comparable to only a few days in the past, many of them unsurfable due to wildly contrary conditions. This wasn't one of those days - except for a prevailing southwest wind that sent 6-foot chops running up the face.

As longtime photographer/water-safety legend Frank Quirarte put it, "If guys could pull off their bottom turns, they had to knife through those waves just to keep from eating s-."

Bianca Valenti understood the crisis all too well. On the biggest wave of her life, almost exactly two years ago at Mavericks, she tried to negotiate a chop and was sent lurching forward into a soul-shattering wipeout. And that was just a single chop on a relatively clean day. Saturday's surf meant veritable mine fields of "southwest saddles," as veteran big-wave rider Steve Dwyer described it. "Coming straight at you from the opposite direction."

Valenti, who has surfed big waves only once over the past two years due to injuries, wisely backed off. "Way too gnarly," she said. "Psychotic. I'm just now rebuilding my confidence, and I was not feeling that."

Kai Lenny rides a wave at Mavericks after flying in from Maui, putting on a show that bordered on the surreal. (Sachi Cunningham / For the S.F. Chronicle)

She was hardly alone. Some of the most decorated big-wave surfers in the world, including Billy Kemper and Justine Dupont, took only a mild stab before concluding it was far too dangerous.

"And these are people who feel they're ready for 100-foot surf," said Grant Washburn, who has been surfing and documenting Mavericks since the early '90s. "But not these waves. They simply decided not to do it. I was surprised how many people actually caught a few waves."

Familiar Mavericks names were charging hard: Peter Mel, Ryan Augenstein, Lucas (Chumbo) Chianca, Jamie Mitchell, JoJo Roper, Alo Slebir and especially El Granada surfer Luca Padua, clearly "the man" among Mavericks regulars.

It's rare to see a "goofyfoot" (right foot forward) tearing up a right-breaking wave at that size, because you give up plenty of vision and perspective with your back to the wave. But Padua trains like a madman with Laird Hamilton, the greatest of all big-wave surfers. And he reminded me of Laird - as in, "you just don't see that" - when he carved one bottom turn into a straight-up rocket to the top, where the impossibly thick lip looked certain to crush him - then snapped that top turn into a straight-down descent that looked too chaotic to possibly handle.

Sailing comfortably into the channel, he broke into a smile.

But here's the crazy part: Luca wasn't the show on Saturday, nor were the series of heroic Jet Ski rescues of wiped-out surfers heading for the inside rocks. It was Kai Lenny, who flew over from Maui and staged a performance so far and above the rest, it bordered on the surreal.

Lenny needs no introduction. At 34 he's unquestionably the greatest big-wave waterman in the world, a master of foiling, kite surfing, windsurfing, stand-up paddling and any-size surfboards, but he loves nothing better than being towed into giant waves at Jaws (his home break), Nazare (Portugal) and Mavericks.

He's been to Half Moon Bay many times, always with distinction, but nothing like the spectacle he unleashed on Saturday. He's a decidedly humble fellow, quick to champion the glory of others, but when a CBS News video crew interviewed him in the water on Monday, he said, "It was really treacherous with big side chop. That's like speed bumps on a racetrack. Fortunately I didn't suffer the consequences like many others did.

"I was out for 10 hours straight and caught 45, 50 waves."

Most everyone felt just terrific about snagging one or two, but 50?

Kai Lenny routinely used the choppy water to launch himself into aerial 360s, landing in ideal position to continue on his way. (Sachi Cunningham / For the S.F. Chronicle)

Incredibly - that word truly applies here - he routinely used that choppy water to launch himself into aerial 360s, landing in ideal position to continue on his way. They were all the same, each as shockingly precise as the last.

"All day long, he did that," Dwyer said. "I could not believe what I was seeing. He was toying with it out there. I was like, who f-ing does this? He is the one percent of the one percent." (For the record, Chianca is also an aerial master in huge surf, but not quite at Lenny's level.)

Valenti spent a good deal of time that day driving a Jet Ski, to watch and help out however she could, "and to have a front-row seat for Kai Lenny," she said, "that was a real pleasure."

"What was cool," said Quirarte, who has long been in charge of water patrol at big-surf venues, "is everybody worked together to make sure the lineup stayed safe. The whitewater went on for days, so somebody could easily have gotten lost. But nobody got missed after a wipeout. No injuries."

It's significant to note that four women were in the water: Valenti, Dupont, Maui's Katie McConnell and Half Moon Bay lifeguard Zoe Chait. None of them had much to do with Saturday, but Chait continued her steady progression into the big-wave elite when clean, 40-foot-plus surf turned up on Monday.

"I towed it super-clean in the morning with (Montara standout) Jason Stark, who's part of my big-wave team with Ion Banner," she said on Wednesday. "A real confidence boost. Then I paddle-surfed it for five hours. Got a couple nice ones, took a couple on the head, just really getting back into the big-wave realm.

"And it was so cool at the end of the day," she said. "Almost everyone was gone, and just me out there with Kai, Luca and a couple others."

Washburn was part of that scene, and he found it essential. About to turn 59, he's quite comfortable taking a pass on days like Saturday, but he's invariably out there to man a Jet Ski, shoot some photos or grab some more typical Mavericks waves.

"I had never seen the bluffs so crowded as Saturday," Washburn said. "Just hundreds of people there to watch. Felt like the Pumpkin Festival, and a little scary, the way chunks of coastal cliffs are falling into the ocean all up and down the coast. We've got a lot of rain coming, so be pretty careful if you want to watch Mavericks from the edge of a bluff. I'd be super nervous standing there.

"But late Monday afternoon felt like long ago at Mavericks. There were like six of us, nobody but the best in the world and us. The weather was funky but the water was so nice (66 degrees, warmer than anyone could recall in a Mavericks season), it felt like we were somewhere else. Then the wind would switch, and the lighting got really beautiful. We always talk about how good we had it back then, but honestly, at the right moment, it's still like that."

Cambodia’s Repression Hangs Over Francophonie Summit

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image French President Emmanuel Macron (left) with Cambodian Prime Minister Hun Manet during the 20th Francophonie Summit in Phnom Penh, November 15, 2026. © 2026 Chalinee Thirasupa/Reuters

As world leaders recently gathered in Phnom Penh to celebrate the 20th Francophonie Summit with the theme of peace and shared prosperity, French President Emmanuel Macron took to the podium to call publicly for an investigation into the killing of former Cambodian lawmaker and French citizen Lim Kimya. It was a rare public rebuke of the Cambodian government by a foreign leader.

A gunman killed Kimya in Bangkok, Thailand in January 2025. Kimya had served as a parliament member with the opposition Cambodia National Rescue Party before its 2017 dissolution.

A Thai court convicted the gunman, Ekkalak Paenoi, in October 2025 and issued arrest warrants for two Cambodian suspects, Ly Ratanakraksmey and Pich Kimsrin, both of whom have connections to high-ranking Cambodian officials. The Cambodian government—like many of its neighbors in Southeast Asia—is increasingly persecuting activists abroad.

The Cambodian government has yet to acknowledge the arrest warrants or start an impartial investigation. Both men remain at large. France has issued Interpol Red Notices for them on charges of complicity in premeditated murder.

Macron’s raising Kimya’s murder during a joint press conference with Prime Minister Hun Manet sends a strong message that the French government is taking Cambodian authorities’ attacks on activists abroad seriously. The Cambodian government should heed the call and independently and impartially investigate the murder.

But political repression by the Cambodian government runs deeper than one case. Ahead of 2027 local elections, the authorities are escalating their crackdown on the long suppressed political opposition. Young activists in particular, such as the five imprisoned Mother Nature Cambodia environmental activists, have been punished with baseless treason charges and lengthy prison sentences. Concerned governments should publicly and privately urge Cambodian authorities to release the more than 100 Cambodians currently imprisoned for exercising their fundamental freedoms.

Human Rights Watch was among the local and international organizations that pressed Cambodian authorities to release the Mother Nature activists and others ahead of the Francophonie Summit. The ruling Cambodian People’s Party, which exerts complete control over the country’s courts, was apparently unbothered by these efforts.

Other foreign leaders should amplify Macron’s message and publicly call on Cambodia’s government to reopen the space for the political opposition and respect the fundamental rights of all Cambodians, both at home and abroad.

Switch 2 Is Getting an All-new Gamecube Controller

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

8BitDo has a wireless take on a beloved gamepad

Super Smash Bros. fans, this one’s for you. Peripheral maker 8BitDo just revealed the GC Bluetooth Gamepad, a wireless spin on the GameCube controller meant for Nintendp Switch, Switch 2, and Windows PC. It’s available in the GameCube’s iconic purple-and-orange color palette and features the "classic GC design and iconic button layout," according to its product description. The controller can connect wirelessly via Bluetooth 5.0 or a 2.4 GHz adapter, and has wired support via a USB-C cable. All three connection types work with the original Switch and Switch 2, while only wired and 2.4 GHz wireless connections work on PC. The controller goes for $49.99 and ships on Dec. 10, 2026.

Nintendo released its own wireless GameCube controller for the Switch 2 last year. It was closely modeled after the original gamepad, with a few small changes, like additional buttons for better compatibility with the Switch 2.

The most obvious reason that both Nintendo and 8BitDo created modern versions of the GameCube controller is that the Switch 2 supports certain GameCube games through a Nintendo Switch Online subscription. Specifically, the service has beloved titles like The Legend of Zelda: The Wind Waker and F-Zero GX. You can even play Wario World on there, for some reason.

Beyond that, GameCube controllers have remained surprisingly relevant over the years thanks to Super Smash Bros. fans. The controller is still popular because many players first got into the series through Super Smash Bros. Melee on the GameCube, which maintains an active tournament circuit. Nintendo even officially re-released the original wired GameCube controller for the Wii U as a tie-in for Smash 4. The company later released a USB adapter that lets the original GameCube controller work with the original Switch when playing games like Super Smash Bros. Ultimate.

Another reason many players likely want a modern wireless GameCube controller is to play emulated games on PC. While reviews described Nintendo’s GameCube controller for the Switch 2 as faithful to the original, players found it wasn’t compatible with the original Switch or Windows by default, limiting its use cases. 8BitDo’s version of the gamepad works with both of these platforms. Additionally, the 8BitDo version costs $20 less than Nintendo’s official release. We’ll find out whether the third-party peripheral matches the feel of the beloved GameCube controller when it launches next month.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The US is exploring whether Ukraine would cede parts of Donetsk province still under its control in return for NATO membership or protection. Experts warn the idea is risky.

The US has asked Ukraine whether it would be willing to cede Donetsk province to Russia in return for NATO membership. Together, Donetsk and Luhansk constitute the so-called Donbas region. Russia currently controls most of Luhansk province, and large swathes of Donetsk, though Ukraine continues holding a number of major cities there, including Kramatorsk and Sloviansk.

So far, no concrete proposal had been put forward, according to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He said he would publicly discuss the matter once the US and NATO member states had agreed on the plan, stressing that he could not make any such decision "on his own." Zelenskyy also said any potential agreement would go ahead "without recognizing Russia's occupation of these territories."

The Ukrainian leader said the US was talking about guarantees similar to those enshrined in Articles 4 and 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty, though did not envision US troops on Ukrainian soil. Article 4 provides for consultations among member states in the event of imminent threats, while Article 5 stipulates that an attack on one state is considered an attack on all. The US, however, has said it pictures bilateral guarantees for Ukraine similar to those established with Israel and certain Middle Eastern countries.

Russia has yet to comment on these proposals, with President Putin's spokesperson Dmitry Peskov stating that "we maintain our position that, in the interest of the peace process, it is not appropriate to disclose details of the talks between us and the Americans."

Experts interviewed by DW are skeptical of the land-for-security-guarantees idea, objecting that the US cannot uphold such an arrangement on its own.

"It seems to me that this is what happens to normal diplomacy when it's been run by real estate agents who are used to selling people something that doesn't really exist," Oleksandr Kraiev of the North America-Israel Research Program at Ukraine's Ukrainian Prism think tank tells DW. He says that at least half of NATO's member states have reservations about Ukraine's accession.

"We'll just end up with a second Budapest Memorandum, it'll just carry a different name," Kraiev told DW. The original 1994 Budapest Memorandum stipulated that Russia, the US and UK respect Ukraine's independence and sovereignty in its existing borders and refrain from the threat or use of force to change its territorial integrity. Ukraine in return committed to giving up all nuclear weapons.

The Budapest Memorandum, however, did not contain any commitment by the UK or US to defend Ukraine in the event of an attack.

Most experts interviewed by DW are critical of the idea of holding a referendum to let ordinary Ukrainians decide whether their country should hand over full control of Donetsk province in exchange for NATO membership.

This is because ceding territory would not only violate Ukraine's constitution and harm its national interests, but would also undermine a world order "in which borders cannot be redrawn by force," Oleksandr Khara of Ukraine's Centre for Defence Strategies told DW. "It doesn't matter whether the citizens of Ukraine vote for or against such a proposal," Khara said, adding that any such referendum would set "a very dangerous precedent."

A survey by the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology (KISS) conducted between October 8 and 16 found that 61% of respondents categorically reject withdrawing troops from Ukrainian-controlled parts of the Donbas, such as cities like Kramatorsk and Sloviansk, even in exchange for US and European security guarantees. 35% of respondents said they would begrudgingly accept a land-for-security deal.

Latin America's mega-prisons: Can El Salvador's model work?

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

El Salvador's mega-prison has become a model for governments across Latin America seeking to combat organized crime. But experts question whether the approach is effective, sustainable or compatible with human rights.

Around 15,000 inmates and a capacity of up to 40,000, prison sentences of up to 1,000 years, strict security measures and an extreme isolation regime. Staged images show inmates with shaved heads and tattoos being subdued by heavily armed guards.

These are just some of the facts circulating about El Salvador's Terrorism Confinement Center (CECOT), a symbol of President Nayib Bukele's security policy and a possible model for other Latin American countries seeking to combat organized crime.

More than a dozen governments in the region have proposed building similar "mega-prisons," although most would have a far smaller capacity. A range of experts consulted by DW question the effectiveness and sustainability of these initiatives.

De facto mega-prisons — that is, overcrowded prisons with excessive inmate populations — existed in Latin America long before Bukele, says criminologist Jose Luis Perez Guadalupe, former president of the National Penitentiary Institute and former interior minister in Peru. Lurigancho Prison in Lima, for example, designed to hold 3,000 inmates, has housed nearly 11,000 for decades.

But ever since CECOT was launched in 2023, the concept of a "mega-prison" has been primarily linked to maximum-security facilities. For Olga Espinoza, an academic specializing in prison systems at the School of Government of the University of Chile, this association is problematic because it implies that the entire prison population requires "extremely severe" punishment.

According to Espinoza, placing the entire prison population in maximum-security facilities would amount to inefficient and extremely costly "over-intervention." These facilities require substantial investments in infrastructure, technology, specialized staff, anti-corruption measures and social reintegration programs.

It would also be ineffective because "international evidence indicates that when people with different profiles are placed in the same space, there is a high risk of the spread of criminal behavior," the Chilean expert adds.

Several criminal groups across the region even have their roots in overcrowded prisons: Tren de Aragua in Venezuela; Los Lobos, Los Choneros and Los Tiguerones in Ecuador; and Primeiro Comando da Capital (PCC) and Comando Vermelho (CV) in Brazil.

Even when the proposal involves simply building large-scale facilities, investing in a single structure may seem attractive given the difficulties of developing public infrastructure in Latin America.

However, "there is insufficient evidence to support the claim that building mega-prisons is more cost-effective," says Renato Rivera, a senior analyst at the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime (GI-TOC).

The United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners, adopted in 1955, "the recommendation has been to build medium-sized prisons housing between 1,000 and 2,000 inmates rather than mega-prisons," explains Perez Guadalupe, a professor at the Universidad del Pacifico in Lima.

"Mega-prisons, apart from being impersonal, can become extremely dangerous," says Guadalupe.  As an example, the GI-TOC's Rivera highlights the widely reported prison massacres in Ecuador in 2021 that took place in the country's largest and most populated facilities.

Experts acknowledge that mass incarceration has coincided with a dramatic decline in violence in El Salvador and has strengthened the public's perception of safety. Despite reports of arbitrary arrests, abuses and more than 500 deaths in state custody in the country, announcements of new mega-prisons project an image of a strong state confronting organized crime across the region.

However, they warn that this political and symbolic impact does not guarantee sustainable results. Achieving them would require a "clear commitment" to external oversight, human rights protections and social reintegration programs, which governments have yet to demonstrate, Espinoza says.

Perez Guadalupe is particularly critical of the Salvadoran model, describing it as "pure propaganda." He believes the images released from CECOT violate human dignity. He also argues that the state must demonstrate "moral superiority over those who break the law."

Although transnational organized crime represents a growing challenge across the region, the Peruvian criminologist considers El Salvador's case exceptional.

Following the mass arrests carried out under the state of emergency introduced by Bukele, the country has one of the highest incarceration rates in the world. CECOT was designed to permanently detain members of gangs such as MS-13 and Barrio 18, which are considered extremely dangerous.

However, the reality of other Latin American prison systems is different. The question is not where to lock people deprived of their liberty away forever, but rather "how we want them to leave: worse or better," Perez Guadalupe argues, pointing out that most will eventually return to society.

In theory, the official alternative is provided by the UN Nelson Mandela Rules for the treatment of prisoners, updated in 2015. They establish principles for modern prison management, including respect for human dignity, an absolute prohibition on torture and degrading treatment and social reintegration as a central goal of imprisonment.

In practice, Latin America has developed innovative approaches in this direction. These include the APAC model in Brazil, which operates facilities without guards, weapons or violence; Conflict Prevention and Resolution Committees in Argentina; and the Alternative Criminal Justice Center in the Mexican state of Sonora.

Experts also point to the need for maximum-security prisons for specific inmate profiles, but on a smaller scale and with more precise classification of the prison population.

Finally, they emphasize that prisons, including mega-prisons, primarily address the consequences of organized crime rather than its root causes. Poverty, social exclusion and a lack of opportunities require public policies focused on employment, education and social integration to reduce the conditions that enable crime to expand in one of the world's most unequal regions.

Harvard GSD students propose alternatives to Trump's arch

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Images by Jeremy Dvorak and Jake Geitner courtesy The Cultural Landscape Foundation

Landscape architecture students at Harvard’s Graduate School of Design have designed two proposals for Memorial Circle in Washington, DC, the site of US president Donald Trump‘s proposed triumphal arch.

Both designs feature expansive pools of water that cover the current vehicular roundabout at Memorial Circle, and emphasise connectivity to the city’s historic processional axis and straight sightlines to the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington Cemetery.

The students, Jeremy Dvorak and Jake Geitner, worked with Washington DC non profit organisation The Cultural Landscape Foundation (TCLF) on the designs. The aim was to engage the public in the design process, as the team views the government did not properly open the arch up for review.

“By recognising Memorial Circle’s implicit expressions of monumentality, this pair of counter proposals asserts design’s capacity to critically engage history, accommodate public life, and advocate on behalf of our shared cultural landscape,” said Dvorak and Geitner.

The project began after Dvork and Geitner attended a seminar at the GSD taught by TCLF founding partner Charles A Birnbaum.

“As graduate students in landscape architecture, we took Birnbaum’s course to ground our emerging practices in critical historical inquiry and public participation,” they said.

“The current administration’s assertion of authority over national memory presented an opportunity to put these convictions into practice.”

The first proposal treats Memorial Circle as “a void along the historic axis between Washington and Arlington”. The designers chose to fill the central roundabout circle, currently covered by grass, with a low-lying pool of water.

A natural stone perimeter surrounds the pool and is inscribed with words of “belonging and unity”.

It’s design is “restrained” according to the team, and primarily meant to be viewed from a vehicle while passing through the traffic circle.

The second proposal does away with the traffic circle entirely and instead inserts a straight thoroughfare to Arlington Memorial Bridge.

Two circular fountains would be located on either side of the road. Additionally, a large pool of water, mirroring the perimeter of the former traffic circle, would fill up the site during commemorative events when the bridge is closed.

It draws upon previous historical proposals for Memorial Circle and seeks to pay homage to the precedent of the site.

“[The fountain] geometries arise from the site’s historic proposals, combining concentric circles, intersecting alignments, and formal planting into a composition that acknowledges history whilst creating its own present identity,” said the designers.

“In this way the landscape becomes a physical record of its own change.”

Trump’s proposed arch was announced late last year with renderings by US studio Harrison Design. It features gilded statues and a neoclassical design and has been dubbed the “Arc de Trump” – a play on the Arc de Triomphe in Paris.

Construction on the arch was set to begin in September with the government barreling ahead, but it was halted in early November by Judge Tanya S Chutkan.

Trump has claimed the arch will serve as a “military complex” with snipers in an attempt to use national security as a pretext for construction.

Many take issue with the arch blocking views to adjacent Arlington Cemetery, including members of the Kennedy family, who filed a lawsuit to block construction, claiming that the arch would cast shadows over the grave of president John F Kennedy.

The US Commission of Fine Arts (CFA), which reviews projects in the capital city, approved the project in May.

The proposals also follow recent efforts by the legislature to change Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act, which requires federal agencies to consider the impact of proposed projects on historic properties.

Earlier in November, protesters gathered in Washington DC in opposition of the administration’s proposed rewrite of these protections. They are couched within the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) bill recently introduced to Congress.

South Africa seized 5 ancient baobabs bound for Georgia, where 8 Kenyan trees died in a billionaire’s park

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

For more than three months, five baobab trees estimated to be 200 to 300 years old lay on their sides in a Durban warehouse parking lot, roots and branches hacked back, waiting to be shipped to the Black Sea nation of Georgia. South Africa’s Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment (DFFE) intercepted the trees on July 17 after their exporter, Pretoria-based Divine Landscapes, could not produce licenses to remove, transport, or export them.

Officials have not named the buyer. But reporting by Daily Maverick traced the trail to the Shekvetili Dendrological Park, a private arboretum on Georgia’s coast founded by billionaire Bidzina Ivanishvili, the country’s former prime minister and the founder of its ruling Georgian Dream party. Ivanishvili has not commented, and Daily Maverick said requests to his lawyer and to Georgia’s embassy in Pretoria went unanswered.

The case tests how far South Africa will go to keep a protected species from becoming a collectible for wealthy buyers abroad. It also left authorities with a practical problem: keeping living evidence alive while a case moves through the courts.

Environmental journalist Tony Carnie got an anonymous tip about the trees, photographed them through a fence at the Africa Global Logistics warehouse in Durban’s Mobeni industrial area, and reported the seizure in Daily Maverick in October. AGL told the publication it only provided logistics services and neither owned nor exported the cargo.

Every baobab in South Africa is a specially protected species under the National Forests Act, which bars anyone from cutting, disturbing, removing, transporting, or exporting one without a license. The DFFE says the five trees were dug up in the Lephalale area of Limpopo province without a provincial permit. It refused Divine Landscapes’ late license application at the end of July and dismissed the company’s appeal on Oct. 27.

The company denies wrongdoing. Its attorneys told Daily Maverick the trees were “obtained from private gardens with the full consent of the respective owners” and said it is seeking judicial review of the appeal decision. The company’s owner appeared in Durban Magistrates’ Court in September, and the case was postponed to January pending further investigation.

Ivanishvili, whose party critics accuse of tilting Georgia toward Moscow, has spent years moving giant trees to Shekvetili. Eight baobabs uprooted on Kenya’s Kilifi coast were bound for the park when Kenyan President William Ruto halted their export and ordered an investigation. The Kenya Forest Service later authorized the shipment, and the trees reached Georgia in 2023.

All eight died. In August 2024, the park confirmed the deaths and blamed delays it attributed to Ivanishvili’s political opponents.

“We also did not want to see a repeat of what happened in Kenya,” Dr. Sarah Venter, an ecologist and founder of the Baobab Foundation, told Daily Maverick.

The seizure comes as South Africa presses for tougher international enforcement. On Oct. 23, parties to the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime, meeting in Vienna, voted 83 to 12, with 27 abstentions, to begin negotiating a new protocol on crimes that affect the environment, including trafficking in wild flora and fauna. South Africa co-sponsored the resolution.

“Environmental crime is organized crime,” Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment Minister David Maynier said in a DFFE statement. Once negotiated, the protocol could give countries shared tools for cross-border investigations, extradition, and recovering criminal proceeds. Trafficking networks are already expanding their reach, as a U.N. report on the global illicit economy found this year, and wealthy collectors’ appetite for rare species is under scrutiny elsewhere, including in India’s trade in trafficked orangutans.

Seized cargo can usually wait in storage. These trees could not. “They’ve been lying on their sides. Their branches and their roots have been trimmed, well hacked, so they’re not in a very vertical position. They’re not in soil. They’re in full sun. There’s no water,” Carnie said in a Daily Maverick video interview.

“The really critical thing is to get them back into the soil as soon as possible to allow them to recover and regenerate, because they are using up stored stem water to survive,” Venter said. A healthy baobab can be up to 75% water.

The rescue was privately funded. Timbavati Private Nature Reserve, which straddles the Mpumalanga and Limpopo provinces, offered to cover loading, transport, and replanting, and Mark Read of the Everard Read Group found an unnamed private funder within 10 minutes of Venter’s call, Daily Maverick reported. On Nov. 11, the trees, each weighing an estimated 17 to 30 tons, began leaving Durban on abnormal-load trucks for the roughly 900-kilometer trip to the reserve.

All five had sprouted leaves and flower buds. “I think we got here just in time and I’m quite positive about their future,” Venter said.

Instead of wading through decades of defense bureaucracy and drawn-out congressional appropriations, USAFA secured its next-generation aerobatic aircraft in just 10 months, cutting through red tape with the help of a private donor group led by Andrew McKenna.

The T-19A is a derivative of the commercial GB1 GameBird. By skipping the traditional procurement, leadership bypassed the budget overruns and timeline slips that have paralyzed programs like the Boeing T-7A Red Hawk. The lightweight plane is purpose-built for agility thanks to its designer, German aerobatic champion Philipp Steinbach. He is a co-founder of Game Aerospace Composites in Bentonville, Arkansas, which produces the off-the-shelf civilian variant certified to pull a staggering load of ten G-forces.

The Kestrel went from concept to delivery in 10 months using $3 million in private donations, whereas the Red Hawk has spent over a decade in development using billions in taxpayer funds and still faces multi-year delays. The T-19A will fill a major gap in modern USAFA flight training by giving cadets early exposure to high-G, unlimited aerobatics and complex energy management before they ever step into Undergraduate Pilot Training.

The T-19A, or GB1 GameBird, is the only American-built two-seat aircraft approved for Unlimited-category competition aerobatics. Its all-carbon monocoque airframe lacks traditional mechanical wing-attach joints, relying on a continuous composite spar cured inside an autoclave. Under the hood, a 583-cubic-inch Lycoming AEIO-580-B1A flat-six piston engine delivers 303 horsepower (226 kW) to a four-blade composite MT-Propeller system. An inverted oil and fuel system keeps the engine running under sustained negative-g flight and zero-g push-overs. Cadets command the airframe from tandem seats equipped with Garmin G3X Touch glass displays, combining edge-of-envelope stick-and-rudder forces with modern electronic flight screens before stepping into standard turboprops.

To make the transition, USAFA pulled six active-duty instructor pilots from the 306th Flying Training Group to stand up the first training cadre. These officers will shape a new syllabus to train cadets and establish a demonstration and competition team. The initiative comes as the wider military struggles with a persistent shortfall of roughly 2,000 operational pilots, forcing leadership to seek shortcuts to build stick-and-rudder skills earlier in pilot development.

The Academy's existing fleet jumps from unpowered TG-16A gliders and basic T-53A/T-41D piston trainers straight to the UPT's T-6A Texan II turboprops after graduation. USAF Chief of Staff General Ken Wilsbach and Academy Superintendent Lt. Gen. Paul Moga secured the four airframes thanks to a $3 million donation coordinated by philanthropist Andrew McKenna. The Kestrel will act as an intermediate high-performance step, teaching cadets how to manage high power-to-weight ratios, rapid throttle response, and aggressive energy management. Standard trainers are built for stability and prohibit extreme maneuvers. Because the Kestrel is certified for unlimited aerobatics, instructors can deliberately put cadets into inverted spins, vertical stalls, and disorienting attitudes, teaching them instinctive recovery skills that directly prevent real-world loss-of-control accidents.

The Air Force is facing a persistent pilot deficit and needs to push students through UPT faster without lowering standards. By giving top cadets advanced stick-and-rudder proficiency during their junior and senior years at the Academy, they arrive at UPT needing fewer remedial rides and moving through the syllabus faster. That limits intense flights to roughly 45 minutes, requiring ground crews to refuel and turn the planes around quickly between sorties. Moving cadets from unpowered gliders into a 200-knot (370 km/h) airplane is also a major operational adjustment. 5 Legendary Rock Bands With Only One Perfect Album, Ranked

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It can take years for a rock band to finally get radio play, but for a lucky few exceptionally talented acts, all it takes is one album to make a lasting impression. It's not every day that a band comes together, puts out a single project, and then leaves it all behind. Whether it's a side project, an inability to maintain the same chemistry beyond the debut, or simply a matter of unfortunate circumstances, there's something fascinating about bands with only one album to their name.

Arguably, a one-album discography can represent some of the most honest work in a band's career, seizing its artistry at its rawest. There's also the pressure of making that one record count, leaving listeners with something truly memorable. Without further ado, here are five of the greatest rock bands with only one perfect album, ranked.

Following the massive MTV success of Enema of the State and Take Off Your Pants and Jacket, it was only a matter of time before fans (and their record label) expected Blink-182 to follow up with another hit-making album built around their tried-and-true three-power-chord formula. But for members Tom DeLonge and Travis Barker, they needed an outlet to explore heavier sounds and darker subject matter—something that contrasted with Blink-182's rambunctious energy, immature antics, and crass humor. Enter their side project, Box Car Racer. Originally conceived as a creative outlet during a break from touring, the short-lived project gave DeLonge and Barker room to branch out musically and experiment with a different sound, something they would continue exploring in later Blink-182 releases and with DeLonge’s future band, Angels & Airwaves.

Inspired by DeLonge's interest in post-hardcore bands like Fugazi, Refused, and Quicksand, Box Car Racer ditches the fast, chord-driven riffs he was accustomed to playing in favor of heavier guitar textures, distorted riffs, and drop-D tuning—all channeled into their self-titled album. While traces of his pop-punk roots remain unmistakable, the band isn't chasing catchy hooks designed to dominate radio airplay. Instead, explores darker themes ranging from apocalyptic destruction to Freemasonry—definitely a far cry from singing about teenage break ups and personal insecurities.

The Los Angeles hardcore punk scene owes a lot to The Germs. Their original run may have lasted only four years in the late '70s, but the band helped lay the groundwork for the scene, leaving an influence that would carry on for decades. The brainchild of lead vocalist Darby Crash and guitarist Pat Smear (who would later play with Nirvana and the Foo Fighters), The Germs drew inspiration from a surprisingly wide range of artists, including Iggy Pop, The Ramones, and The Sex Pistols. Their only studio album, GI, is regarded as one of the earliest full-length hardcore punk records and is notably produced by Joan Jett of The Runaways.

GI proves that hardcore's unrestrained chaos doesn't have to come at the expense of structure or artistic expression. Rather than reducing the genre to theatrical antics and one-minute bursts of noise with barely intelligible lyrics, The Germs showed that hardcore could be just as expressive and musically thoughtful as any other genre. Hardcore punk may have been at the heart of their sound, but it was their frenetic aggression, both on record and during notoriously chaotic live performances that sometimes got them kicked offstage, that cemented their status as legends of the first generation of Los Angeles punk bands.

Their time in the industry might be short-lived, but The La's got the world singing in karaoke thanks to their hit track "There She Goes," which has become an anthem for many romantic comedies. For a bunch of Brit lads making jolly, Merseybeat sing-along tunes à la The Kinks, The Who, and The Rolling Stones, The La's were surprisingly perfectionistic. This not only became a huge obstacle during the production of their debut album but also put a strain on the band's relationships, eventually contributing to their breakup. It's quite a contrast for a band whose repertoire sounds so raw and organic to have gone through a recording process filled with rejected takes, endless re-recordings, and rotating producers.

While The La's weren't happy with their self-titled debut album (which took a total of three years to record), its sound became a blueprint for Britpop, thanks to its emphasis on guitar-driven music and 1960s British rock influences. The album's legacy can still be heard in the music of Oasis, Arctic Monkeys, and Fontaines D.C. While it's a shame The La's never released another studio album, they at least had the chance to create something that would stand the test of time. The recording process may have been a pain, but the fruits of their labor are unmistakable.

Them Crooked Vultures takes the term "supergroup" to another level, bringing together a trio that's a match made in rock heaven: Josh Homme of Queens of the Stone Age, John Paul Jones of Led Zeppelin, and Dave Grohl of Nirvana and the Foo Fighters. Their sound feels like a more laid-back take on Homme's work with his own band, but with a catchier, more modern rock edge. The project took years to come together, with the trio making its debut in 2009. All three musicians bring their own playing styles to the table, but somehow, everything clicks.

Their self-titled album is a real treat for rock fans, especially when you consider the heavyweight talent packed into this tight trio. There's no shortage of heavy guitar riffs, but the album isn't all about loud guitars and flashy musicianship. It also throws in funk influences, piano arrangements, and plenty of catchy vocal hooks from Homme. There's a little bit of everything here, and that's what makes the record such a familiar yet fresh listen. Rather than sounding like three rock legends simply showing off what they can do, Them Crooked Vultures brings their individual strengths together without the need to overpower each other.

Britain's biggest troublemakers have long sparked debate over whether they're true punk pioneers or just cynical posers who were more interested in the lifestyle than the music itself. It doesn't help that bassist Sid Vicious reportedly could barely play his own instrument, with Steve Jones having to step in and re-record the bass tracks. Still, whether you buy into their authenticity or not, there's no denying that the Sex Pistols left a lasting mark on anti-establishment culture. Their rebellious attitude was a sharp contrast to Britain's prim and proper image, particularly the kind of regal etiquette expected in a society still shaped by its monarchy.

Their only studio album, Never Mind the Bollocks, Here's the Sex Pistols, is pretty much the epitome of their bad-boy attitude. Dropped by record labels, plastered across tabloids, and met with concert cancellations, the album attracted plenty of public scrutiny for its outrageous content. Few songs were as controversial as "God Save the Queen" and "Anarchy in the U.K.," both of which captured the spirit of punk at its most rebellious. The album was packed with political undertones, and at a time when censorship was still rife in an uptight British society, the Sex Pistols came in swinging with a sound that refused to follow the rules and lyrics that could give the royal family a headache.

Plants mix different breeding strategies to survive in a world with changing climates and fewer bees

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Simulations show plants that reject their own pollen and plants that self-fertilize can coexist for ages, swapping dominance without settling.

Apple trees have a strict biological rule for reproduction. They reject their own pollen. That means they need a completely different type of apple tree nearby to produce fruit.

While many wild plants rely on this exact same partner system, plenty of other plant species have evolved to drop the rule entirely, allowing them to successfully fertilize themselves.

A population holding both kinds was supposed to be passing through on its way to choosing one rule or the other.

In computer simulations of evolving plants, the mixed populations didn’t get there. With nothing in the environment changing, the two kinds kept trading dominance for as long as the simulations ran. Neither kind took over.

The researchers call it a volatile mixed mode. A mixed population in the wild that looks unstable, they wrote, may be in a lasting state of its own.

Tamar Friedlander, a senior lecturer at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem’s Robert H. Smith Institute of Plant Sciences and Genetics in Agriculture, led the work with Amit Jangid.

Ohad Noy Feldheim of the University’s Einstein Institute of Mathematics is the third author. Everything here happened inside a model.

In an interview with Earth.com, Friedlander said the result broke a habit of thought. Biologists expect that when a population has several options, some are better than others, she said.

Over the long run, the population is supposed to reach the better one and stay there. Earlier models had the mix fading away or holding steady at fixed proportions.

“Thus, it was very surprising that we didn’t get either of these under a large parameter regime,” she said.

The populations, in her words, “not only did not settle to either of the ‘pure’ strategies (only self-fertilization or only outcrossing) but also did not settle at fixed proportions of these strategies.”

The rejection system works like a lock and a set of keys. The female side of a flower makes a toxin that stops a pollen tube from growing. Each pollen grain carries proteins that disarm the toxins of other mating types but not its own.

About 40 percent of plant families use some version of this, including wild tomatoes, potatoes, apples, pears, citrus, and coffee. Washington State University‘s tree fruit site has a primer on why orchards need two varieties.

The system can break. A mutation that silences the toxin, or adds a key that fits the plant’s own lock, produces a plant that can fertilize itself.

That plant gains a sure route to seeds when pollen is scarce, but it passes on two sets of genes to each seed instead of one.

It pays for that with inbreeding. Seeds from self-fertilization carry more harmful mutations, and more of them fail.

The plants that reject their own pollen pay a different price. Their pollen is useless on every plant of their own mating type, so a type that grows common finds fewer partners.

Friedlander’s team simulated populations of 500 plants for 100,000 generations, discarding the first 50,000 as a warm-up.

Each plant carried two copies of the gene set, and each copy held one toxin and nine keys. Every protein was written as a string of 18 amino acids that could mutate.

Two dials set the outcome. One was how badly self-fertilized seeds suffer from inbreeding. The other was how loosely the keys fit the locks, which decides how many mating types a population can hold.

With heavy inbreeding costs and loose-fitting keys, every plant ended up rejecting its own pollen. With light costs, every plant ended up self-fertilizing.

In between lay the third state, where the share of self-rejecting plants rose and fell for the rest of the run and the number of mating types swung with it.

Types tended to vanish several at a time, in what the authors describe as avalanches. What triggers those collapses, they wrote, is still obscure.

A shortage of pollen was expected to push populations toward self-fertilization, since a plant that can pollinate itself doesn’t need a visitor. The model found the opposite.

When cross-pollen was scarce, the range of settings in which self-rejection won grew at the expense of the mixed state. Friedlander said the explanation lies in how the two sexes compete.

When pollen is plentiful, every female is sure of a partner and only the males compete, so female proteins evolve mainly to avoid matching their own pollen.

When pollen is scarce, females must compete too, which makes them easier to match and raises the number of mating types a population can support.

Researchers often treat self-fertilization as a plant’s backup plan for when cross-pollination fails, she told Earth.com. Self-fertilizing plants do turn up at the edges of a species’ range, where partners are few.

The move from rejecting self-pollen to accepting it has long been treated as a one-way street. A plant that gives up the rule usually sheds the flower traits that attract insects and purges some of its harmful mutations.

Across much of the model’s settings, though, the end state didn’t depend on the start, and a population that began self-fertilizing could evolve the rejection system from scratch.

The exception was the loosest-fitting keys, where the mutation needed for the reverse trip was so rare that the wait became impossibly long.

The model also held the cost of inbreeding fixed, so it describes the short term, before prolonged self-fertilization purges enough mutations to change the odds.

Mixed populations exist in the wild. Long-term studies that track one population’s proportions over time are lacking, the authors wrote, and those are what would test the prediction.

The model also predicts that mixed populations carry fewer and shorter-lived mating types than purely self-rejecting ones, and more inbreeding written into their genomes. Both could be checked in plant genomes.

Asked which population she would most like to watch, Friedlander pointed to wild tomato species growing in South America.

She also said the model doesn’t apply directly to orchards, where trees are genetically identical and do not evolve. Self-rejecting crops do depend on pollinators, she added, and a decline in them could cut yields.

A warming climate and fewer pollinating insects both change the pressures on how a plant reproduces, the authors wrote.

Whether real populations respond by picking a side, or by swinging between the two for generations, is what a long field study of those wild tomatoes could begin to answer.

The full study was published in the journal PRX Life.

All the Award-Winning Photos From Nature Photographer of the Year 2026

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

All the Award-Winning Photos From Nature Photographer of the Year 2026

The Nature Photographer of the Year 2026 winners have been unveiled, showcasing the world’s best nature, wildlife, and landscape photography.

This year’s competition received 27,108 entries from photographers in nearly 100 countries, a record-breaking number for the longtime photo contest.

Finnish photographer Hannu Ahonen bested stiff competition to earn the title of Nature Photographer of the Year. Ahonen’s award-winning photo, “The One that Left and the One that Stayed,” is an extraordinary image of a water lily leaf positioned alongside its icy duplicate.

“It is not often that a photograph of a plant is crowned the overall winner of a major photography competition, but the jury could not overlook this fascinating image by Hannu Ahonen,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom. “First and foremost, the photograph captures a unique situation: a water lily leaf with an almost perfect black-and-white replica of it lying next to it in the water.”

Ahonen’s winning photo tells a compelling and unusual story. The living water lily leaf is positioned on the left, while the ice that formed on top of it and slipped off sits delicately to the side, blown away slightly by the breeze. Even the veins from the leaf are visible in the icy shell.

Nature Photographer of the Year adds that the photo reveals the “marvelous beauty of nature” while embodying its transient character. The sheet of ice will soon melt away, joining the water that keeps the lily alive in the first place. It’s a symbolic photo at a time when climate change is impacting all life on Earth, especially its ice.

“The winter ice had arrived, but it remained snowless for a long time. Some of the pond’s lily pad leaves began to crumble on top of the ice. The wind carried a few of them away, revealing delicate, frosty ornaments left behind. I eagerly captured this natural phenomenon, something I had never witnessed before,” Ahonen explains.

Ahonen has an inspiring story. He has ADHD and lives in a wilderness home in Finland with no carbon footprint. Rarely venturing more than a couple of miles from his home, he spends much of his time behind the camera in his yard, using photography as a spiritual escape and creative outlet.

Alongside the overall winner, Ahonen, the competition’s jury also selected winners across nearly a dozen categories. There are also special prizes for youth photographers and an accomplished portfolio. Ahonen took the top spot in the Plants and Fungi category, so that category is not included below.

French photographer Lucas Mugnier-Lavorel won this year’s Birds category for his environmental portrait of a Magellanic woodpecker in Patagonia.

“Environmental portraits of wildlife can make us feel closer to the animals despite being farther away from them, when done right. This image of a Magellanic woodpecker in its gorgeous habitat was a perfect example. It stood out from thousands of submissions because of its understated sense of place, beauty, simplicity, and timing,” says judge Steffen Foerster.

“Overall, it was a very successful reminder of how the portrayal of nature in its literal state can still be the most powerful of photographic expressions,” Foerster concludes.

British photographer Paul Goldstein’s foggy photo of a giraffe earned the top prize in the Mammals category.

“Quiet images can be easy to overlook in the fiercely competitive mammal category, one of the largest in NPOTY. This ethereal image of a giraffe peeking out of the mist revealed its strengths gradually. Initially a little disorienting, it offered just enough of a visual puzzle to keep the viewer engaged until the image resolved,” Foerster explains.

Judges tapped Marcus Kuan’s macro photo of a surprisingly rich and diverse little ecosystem alongside a pitcher plant in the Other Animals category. The Singaporean photographer impressed the panel with his composition and lighting.

“The lighting, colors, and unexpected gathering of species immediately caught our attention. Kuan beautifully captured a world where crustaceans, amphibians, and carnivorous plants cross paths,” explains jury member Louis Guillot.

“As judges, we were drawn to the possibilities unfolding within this single frame. Could the pitchers be digesting invertebrates? Might the frog be exploring a potential egg-laying site, while the crab continues its daily search for food among the plants?” Guillot continues.

Photographer J Fritz Rumpf from the United States won this year’s Landscape category with a silhouetted photo of Iguazú Falls in Argentina.

“What immediately caught our attention was the incredible light and atmosphere in this photo. The longer we looked, the more fascinating it became. There is so much to discover: the last light of day illuminating the mist rising from Iguazú Falls, the silhouettes of trees and rocks, the birds circling above, the sunlight streaming through an opening in the rock at the center, and the subtle waterfall on the left,” says jury member Ilse Koks.

Another American photographer, Matt Pérez, won the Underwater category with a striking photo of a turtle beneath a collection of newt eggs.

“Pérez’s photograph immediately drew us in. As a jury, we wanted to linger over it, exploring the details and wondering what we were seeing,” remarks Guillot. “Newt egg masses decorate the submerged branches like baubles on a Christmas tree, framing the scene and guiding our eyes towards the pond turtle with its piercing yellow eyes.”

Guillot adds that the scene looks “otherworldly,” which is a very apt description.

Chinese photographer Xuzhong Liu’s abstract drone shot of a winding landscape ticks all the right boxes. It has interesting patterns, striking colors, and a very pleasing composition.

Liu captured the image in a wetland area in China and says the scene strongly reminded him of peach blossoms he had previously photographed—hence the title, “Peach Blossoms in the Snow.”

“This photograph has everything that can make a drone shot interesting and fascinating. First and foremost, there are beautiful shapes and subtle colours, but there is also a sense of alienation. What am I actually looking at, and what is the scale of the landscape in the photograph?” asks the chair of the judging panel, Theo Bosboom.

Czech photographer Petr Bambousek’s winning photo depicts a sobering scene of a Malayan sun bear resting inside a garbage incinerator.

“The winning image stood out for its striking depiction of the impact human activity has on local wildlife,” explains jury member Lana Tannir. “The sleeping Malayan sun bear inside a garbage incinerator creates a powerful visual contrast, showing how habitat loss and human waste increasingly force wild animals into close contact with human environments.”

Tannir says Bambousek’s photo is “unexpected and unsettling.”

Hungarian photographer László Tóth won the Black and White category for his striking, high-contrast, abstract photo.

“This photo really stood out to us. At first, we were not quite sure what we were looking at. It sparked some wonderful discussions among the jury and made us look even closer,” says Ilse Koks.

As for what the photo actually depicts, it’s a thin layer of snow on top of a frozen lake, shaped by the wind.

This photo might be familiar to PetaPixel readers. Norwegian photographer Audun Rikardsen recently won an award for a similar underwater portrait of a polar bear peering down through a hole in the ice.

“Just when we thought we had seen about every kind of polar bear image there is, along comes this shot taken from inside a hole in the ice looking up at the bear,” says Steffen Foerster. “Once you realize what is happening, you start thinking about how this shot could possibly have been taken. Then it dawns on you that it seems almost impossible, no matter how.”

This special category, “De Lage Landen” (which translates to “The Low Countries”), celebrates photos captured in the low-lying region of northwestern Europe, including the Netherlands, where Nature Photographer of the Year is based. This year’s winning photo is by Hungarian photographer Enikő Kálovics.

“The winning image stood out for its ability to transform a familiar landscape into something dreamlike and unexpected,” says Lana Tannir. “The combination of the tiny lacewings, the autumn forest, and the purplish night sky creates a delicate, atmospheric interpretation of the nature of ‘De Lage Landen.'”

American photographer Parham Pourahmad, who was also recently celebrated in the 2027 North American Nature Photography Association (NANPA) Showcase, won this year’s Youth category with a lovely monochromatic photo of a coyote beneath a large tree.

“In ‘Tree of Life’, Pourahmad captured a striking scene that could comfortably hold its own in the adult categories,” says Guillot. “Black and white was an excellent choice, defining the coyote’s silhouette and revealing the majestic oak’s intricate network of branches. Even within this wider scene, the coyote’s downward gaze is clear. Pourahmad explained that it was searching for rodent prey, a small detail that brings the photograph to life.”

Pourahmad has also been named Youth Photographer of the Year.

The Fred Hazelhoff Award celebrates a portfolio of nature photos, rather than a single image. This year’s portfolio winner is German photographer Simone Baumeister.

“In the portfolio category, the bar is set considerably higher for participating photographers than in the individual categories. Whilst a single fantastic photograph is sufficient there, in the portfolio category you need a whole series of outstanding images that not only complement one another but also fit together well in terms of style and theme. Simone Baumeister has succeeded brilliantly in this with her portfolio on eagle owls in the city,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom.

Baumeister has been fascinated by owls for as long as she has been interested in nature photography. The eagle owl’s story is a positive one, in contrast to the plight of many species across the globe. The species, once on the brink of extinction in Germany, has made an incredible recovery. Its resurgence is thanks largely to major conservation efforts and extensive reintroduction programs.

“It was perched in twilight on a church, right below the street lamps, moving cars, and a string of people passing by, with music blaring from a pub right on the church square. Not a soul took notice of the impressive bird with a wingspan of 1.7 meters flying up the steeple, sitting enthroned right within everybody’s view,” the photographer says of the first time she saw an eagle owl in a city.

Ever since then, she has been consistently excited by eagle owls and keen to photograph them in many different places. Observing and photographing them in various cities inevitably morphed into a dedicated project.

Alongside the category and special award winners above, the judges also selected runners-up and highly commended photos, all of which can be seen on the Nature Photographer of the Year website and below.

rune bosse puts living trees on life support in a vision of ecological collapse

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

equipped with respirators and artificial lighting, rune bosse's 'tree lungs' imagines a future where nature itself may need life support.

Danish artist Rune Bosse places living trees on artificial life support in Lung Trees, an installation presented at the Danish Architecture Center in Copenhagen as part of its Age of Nature exhibition, which ran through September 2026. Each tree stands inside a transparent, inflated plastic enclosure, and is surrounded by mechanical equipment and artificial lighting. With their branches reaching toward overhead lamps, each tree seems to have been removed from the forest and placed under intensive care.

The installation imagines a future in which trees might depend on technology to survive, even as humans continue to depend on the ecological systems that trees help sustain. It reverses a relationship we tend to take for granted. Forests have long been described as the lungs of the planet, absorbing carbon dioxide through photosynthesis and releasing oxygen. Here, Bosse gives those lungs a mechanical counterpart, and suggests a world in which the natural systems supporting human life might themselves require artificial support.

The installation brings together living vegetation and an exposed network of machines. Transparent plastic sheeting stretches over metal frames, enclosing the trees while allowing visitors to see the soil and branches inside. Artificial lights illuminate the foliage, while equipment supplies air to the enclosed environment. The temporary structures have the practical appearance of makeshift greenhouses, although their association with medical equipment gives them a more unsettling character.

In a related 2025 presentation at Berlin’s Neue Nationalgalerie, titled Lungetræ / Lungenbaum, Bosse made the breathing movement particularly visible. A living tree and a section of forest floor were enclosed within a flexible PVC membrane attached to a metal framework. At regular intervals, a respirator pushed air into the structure, causing the plastic to expand and slowly collapse around the tree. The movement resembles the inflation of a giant lung, giving physical form to the invisible exchange of gases between a tree and its surroundings.

Bosse’s interest in trees extends well beyond the gallery. Alongside his training at the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts, the artist studied forestry and nature management, developing a practice that follows the growth of living plants over time. At Oremandsgaard, an estate in southern Denmark, he has established an outdoor forest laboratory where he experiments with shaping trees as they grow. Some are bent into unusual forms, while others are guided to grow together. Their final shapes emerge over years, influenced by the conditions of the surrounding landscape.

This work grew partly from his frustration with bringing natural materials into museums, where plants often have to be cut, dried or preserved before they can be exhibited. His outdoor experiments allow trees to remain alive and continue changing long after the artist’s initial intervention. Lung Trees approaches the same relationship from a different direction. Inside the gallery, keeping the trees alive becomes part of the artwork itself, exposing the equipment and artificial conditions that make their temporary existence possible.

The installation touches on an uncomfortable contradiction in the way humans approach ecological preservation. By enclosing the trees and supplying them with artificial conditions, Bosse creates an image of protection that also suggests dependency. The machinery appears to care for the living organisms, yet the entire arrangement places their survival within a system designed and operated by humans. The trees remain living participants in the work, although their relationship with the surrounding environment has been fundamentally altered.

Bosse first developed Lungetræer in 2019, and its more recent presentations give the work a continuing relevance amid growing concerns about biodiversity loss and the deterioration of ecosystems. The installation offers a speculative glimpse of what technological preservation might look like when ecological conditions can no longer support life on their own. Its most unsettling quality comes from the simplicity of the scene.

A living tree stands inside a plastic chamber while a machine pushes air around it. The visitor watches the artificial lung expand and contract, confronted with the possibility that the relationship between humans and forests could one day become this fragile.

Isabel López-Quesada honored the historical essence of a 19th-century Menorcan retreat while introducing vibrant colors and an elevated ease.

For Spanish designer Isabel López-Quesada, summers tend to revolve around Biarritz, in southwestern France, where she has a home. Still, she always carves out time for Menorca, regarded as the most laid-back of Spain’s Balearic Islands. That vibe suits the ELLE Decor A-list designer, whose signature style is luxurious but unfussy, harmonizing old and new with breezy refinement.

Typically, López-Quesada goes there to relax and spend time with friends, but around five years ago she received a call from a Bilbao family who asked her to refresh their vacation home outside Mahón, Menorca’s capital. An old farm property, or finca, the five-bedroom residence required extensive updates. López-Quesada was steadfast in preserving such historical elements as a well and donkey stables, which she transformed into an indoor-outdoor bathroom with a patio and marble tub. “It was important to respect the style of Menorca,” she says. “We were very careful with the materials, so it all goes together and conveys the sentiment I want the house to have.”

López-Quesada founded her namesake studio as a precocious 20-year-old Madrileña 40 years ago. Initially inspired by her mother’s yen for decorating and the work of celebrated Spanish designers such as Paco Muñoz and Fernando Benjumea, López-Quesada garnered wide attention in the early 2000s, when she converted an old Madrid wax factory into an atelier and a luxurious home for her family, filled with antiques and contemporary pieces. Soon she had interest from clients all over the world. Today López-Quesada’s 12-person team includes one of her three adult children, daughter Isabel Llanza. Five years ago the pair co-founded the lifestyle brand Isita Home, selling products they make themselves or discover on their travels.

The Menorca project began by gutting the house’s interior and rebuilding it “in an old style,” López-Quesada says. Central to it all was adding custom windows and doors to each room so the island’s golden light could flood the spaces. Vaulted and beamed ceilings were left intact or were recreated. Reconfiguring the floor plan, López-Quesada eschewed a proper dining room in favor of an eat-in open kitchen. “Most of the time you are eating outside,” she says.

With Menorca’s unrelenting summer sun, bougainvillea-draped pergolas were a must for leisurely alfresco lunches and dinners. Swaths of white lantana and lily of the Nile border the terraces, while “the pool reflects the sky like a mirror and provides a refreshing feel,” López-Quesada says.

Her collaborator on the gardens was Spanish landscape architect Luis González-Camino. “My vision was to design a water-efficient Mediterranean garden, divided into distinct yet connected ‘garden rooms’ for different seasons and times of day,” he says. González-Camino preserved the property as a natural Mediterranean woodland, with wild olive trees providing cover.

Indoors, López-Quesada installed French terra-cotta floor tiles chosen for their aged look. The three fireplaces were all handcrafted in traditional Menorcan style—a little imperfect, painted white—and decorated with vintage tiles the designer keeps in her Madrid warehouse.

She offset the white ceilings and lime plaster walls with vibrant textiles and tilework in blues and greens inspired by Menorca’s native palette. An upstairs bedroom is anchored by an iron four-poster bed with a blue-and-white-striped canopy; its bath has a shower lined with lapis-colored zellige tiles. Doors, which across Menorca are customarily painted in bold shades, here are finished in verdant hues.

Custom cabinets are designed with whimsically patterned cutouts for airflow, since humidity is a major factor. “On the islands, if you close the cupboards completely, mushrooms will grow inside,” López-Quesada says.

She arrayed the spaces with a lively mix of vintage and new furnishings, emphasizing organic materials: rattan chairs, bamboo shades, Moroccan rugs, lots of linen and cotton fabrics in spirited florals or stripes. “If I use a heavy piece, then the rest is lighter,” she says. The tableware maintains the blue and green theme, including pieces from Isita Home and hand-blown glassware from neighboring Mallorca.

In the end, the owners asked López-Quesada to redo their home in Spain’s Basque region as well. It’s one of several projects she currently has across the country, including reimagining a hotel in Barcelona, and she’s also designing homes in Paris, Shanghai, and the Dominican Republic. Many of her projects are with repeat clients. “That’s how it works,” the designer says. “It’s like a chain.”
