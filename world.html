Stellantis is also withdrawing from Formula E as part of efforts to redirect racing expertise toward the likes of Alfa Romeo and Maserati.

Alfa Romeo and Maserati each have plenty of history they can leverage. Getting American buyers excited to snap up what's current or excited about what’s next has proven much harder, though. Now, a retreat by Stellantis from motorsport could give both brands the engineering attention they need.

On November 23, Stellantis announced that it was ending its participation in the World Endurance Championship at the end of this year; next year, it'll end its efforts in Formula E once the season is over. Its new priorities are customer racing and high-performance vehicle development. Alfa Romeo and Maserati are explicitly named as beneficiaries of that expertise.

Perhaps most notably, Stellantis intends to retain and redeploy affected employees, including into performance-car development. That could put people accustomed to solving problems at the highest end of motorsport into roles where they help customers enjoy their cars on public roads.

There's certainly plenty for these brands to do on that front. Alfa Romeo sold just 2316 vehicles in the United States through September, down 52 percent from the same period last year. Its third-quarter total was 569 cars across the Giulia, Stelvio, and Tonale. Whatever affection Americans might have for the badge, it isn’t translating into much showroom traffic.

Maserati, meanwhile, has fresh material to work with; its updated North American lineup includes a 385-hp Grecale V-6, and 582-hp Trofeo versions of the GranTurismo and GranCabrio. Those are solid offerings, but when it comes to marketing, there's plenty of room for the brand to grow.

Of course, there’s a sizable financial caveat: Stellantis hasn’t disclosed expected savings from the withdrawals or promised an accompanying increase in, for example, American marketing spending. It reported a €453 million ($497 million) net loss and negative industrial free cash flow of roughly €4 billion ($4.4 billion) in the third quarter. Any freed-up cash will have plenty of competing demands. That said, it's great to see that Stellantis directly emphasizing that the end of the WEC and Formula E programs will mean more resources go to Alfa and Maserati production cars—and “high-performance models” at that.

ABRUZZO'S TORRE ZAMBRA MAKES HISTORY IN WINE SPECTATOR'S TOP 100

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Ranked n. 40, Colle Maggio Montepulciano d'Abruzzo 2024 achieves the highest placement ever for an Abruzzo wine in the prestigious ranking.

VILLAMAGNA, Italy, Nov. 24, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Torre Zambra, the historic family-owned winery in Villamagna, Abruzzo, has earned international recognition with its Montepulciano d'Abruzzo Colle Maggio 2024, ranked No. 40 in Wine Spectator's prestigious Top 100 Wines of 2026. Scoring 92 points, the wine is the sole representative of Abruzzo in this year's ranking and achieves the highest placement ever for a wine from the region in the history of Wine Spectator's Top 100 Wines of the Year.

The 2026 list includes 22 Italian wines, primarily from Piedmont and Tuscany. This milestone reflects a new chapter for Torre Zambra, a family winery whose roots date back to 1910. Founded in 1961 by Laurentino De Cerchio, the estate is now led by fourth-generation family member Federico De Cerchio, who has spearheaded a comprehensive renewal of the winery, combining its heritage with a contemporary vision focused on quality, sustainability, and international growth.

Today, Torre Zambra oversees more than 40 hectares of vineyards farmed according to organic agricultural practices in the hills of Villamagna. Located between the Adriatic Sea and the Maiella mountain range, the area offers particularly favorable conditions for Montepulciano d'Abruzzo, the region's signature red grape.

"Being recognized among the world's top 100 wines is a tremendous source of pride, but, above all, it confirms that we are moving in the right direction," says Federico De Cerchio. "We have invested significantly in bringing new energy to a winery that has been part of our family for generations, with quality and an international outlook at the heart of everything we do. Our goal is to demonstrate that Abruzzo can compete at the highest level while remaining true to its identity and authenticity."

The Wine Spectator recognition follows an exceptional year of critical acclaim for the winery. Torre Zambra was named Up-and-Coming Winery of the Year in Gambero Rosso's Vini d'Italia 2026. Its Villamagna DOC Classico 2024 received the prestigious Tre Bicchieri distinction in the 2027 edition of the guide, marking the winery's third consecutive year earning the honor.

Further underscoring the winery's consistent recognition on the international stage, Torre Zambra has earned strong ratings from the leading wine publications, including James Suckling, which awarded 94 points to Colle Maggio Montepulciano d'Abruzzo Riserva DOC 2025 and 92 points to Piana Marina Trebbiano d'Abruzzo DOC 2025. Vinous awarded 93 points to Piana Marina Trebbiano d'Abruzzo 2024 and 91 points to Pecorino Poggio Salaia 2025. Wine Spectator also recognized the winery's portfolio, awarding 94 points to Piana Marina Trebbiano d'Abruzzo DOC 2024 and 93 points to Villamagna DOC 2024.

Beyond winemaking, Federico's vision extends to showcasing Abruzzo through hospitality and gastronomy. The newly opened Torre Zambra RESORT, set amid the vineyards of Villamagna, offers visitors an opportunity to experience the region's landscape and culture. Its Ristorante Contemporaneo reinterprets traditional Abruzzese cuisine through a modern approach, celebrating local ingredients and pairing them with the winery's wines.

Through its continued investment in winemaking, hospitality, and its local community, Torre Zambra is strengthening its position as an ambassador for Abruzzo, bringing the region's wines and distinctive character to an international audience.

Founded in 1910, Famiglia De Cerchio traces its roots to Vincenzo De Cerchio, who planted the family's first Montepulciano and Trebbiano vines in Villamagna, Abruzzo. Following the destruction of the vineyards during World War II, his son-in-law Laurentino De Cerchio rebuilt the estate, and in 1961, the first vintage of Torre Zambra was bottled. Today, fourth-generation family member Federico De Cerchio carries on the family's commitment to native Italian grape varieties, sustainable farming and terroir driven wines. The group's estates include Torre Zambra and Idi di Marzo in Abruzzo, along with Pajaru and Peri Peri in southern Italy, with a focus on sustainable and organic vineyard management.

Two Menswear Darlings Just Made the Most Covetable Boots of 2026

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Say you wanted to create the perfect pair of vintage-style military boots. First, you’d need a designer with impeccable taste and encyclopedic knowledge of vintage workwear; then you'd need a bootmaker with the requisite skills to produce it at the highest level. If everything went right, the result would be something like the Modèle 1917, a new collaboration between famed Parisian creative director Gauthier Borsarello and renowned Canadian bootmaker Viberg.

In basic terms, the Modèle 1917 is a pair of boots based on the French Army’s 1917 “brodequin,” a design used by soldiers in the trenches of WWI, available in soft brown suede (Mud) and glossy textured black leather (Pavement). But there’s nothing basic about them. The design comes courtesy of Borsarello, a top-tier collector of vintage workwear and the creative mind behind Gersaint Paris and L’Etiquette magazine. Viberg, a third-generation, family-owned boot specialist, got its start making footwear for forestry crews in British Columbia, and it still builds every pair in its Vancouver Island workshop. While it doesn’t make boots for smokejumpers and loggers anymore, old-school shoemaking techniques like a channeled insole Goodyear welt and stitchdown construction ensure their footwear retains its heirloom-level durability.

The backstory of the “brodequin” begins with the Mdl. 1893, a hobnail-soled marching boot created for French infantrymen in the late 1800s that remained in use until the 1950s. In the First World War, the brodequin’s iron-studded soles and sturdy uppers proved ideally suited to the muddy conditions of the trenches, and American troops soon adopted them in place of their inferior M1904 Marching Shoes. “To me, it’s the blueprint for many combat boots across different countries after WWI,” says Borsarello. “I love the idea that France inspired others after fighting side by side in the trenches. They’re very simple, yet instantly recognizable at the same time.”

For all of its strengths in the field, the original brodequin—whose nickname pays tongue-in-cheek homage to a medieval torture device—wasn’t what you’d call a comfortable boot, especially by modern standards. “They were so stiff that they were almost impossible to walk in,” Borsarello admits. This created something of a design challenge.

To stay as true as possible to the original, Borsarello and Viberg designed the boots on a period-correct “pied haricot” (bean-shaped) last, with a cotton twill lining, solid brass hardware, and reinforced panels that support the midfoot so the forefoot can move freely. To make the Modèle M1917 something you’d actually want to wear, they replaced the hobnailed soles with rubber ones and swapped the original roughout leather for supple American-tanned Horween. The result is a pair of boots that are as comfortable as they are durable, not to mention surprisingly versatile. In other words, exactly what you’d expect from a meeting of these formidable talents.

Watch the ‘Project Hail Mary’ VFX Supervisor Explain Why Having Puppeteers on Set Was Worth All the Extra Work

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The making of “Project Hail Mary” was a miracle on par with the outer space rescue mission that Ryan Gosling is tasked with pulling off. Phil Lord and Chris Miller’s adaptation of Andy Weir’s bestselling novel manages to be epic without ever losing its intimacy, as Gosling traverses the galaxy and saves the world with only a very lovable rock acting alongside him. The final product was a complicated blend of practical effects, on-set puppetry, and a healthy dose of VFX magic.

Incorporating so many production elements creates more work for all involved. The puppetry team lead by James Ortiz had to fit into very small spaces, which frequently meant they showed up in the frames. Visual Effects Production Supervisor Mag Sarnowska and her team were tasked with removing all of the puppeteers. But as she explained during the VFX panel at IndieWire’s Craft Roundtables, it was a small price to pay for the way it enhanced Gosling’s acting performance. Iraq’s Museums Are Finally Opening Their Doors Again

In 2022, Iraq’s National Museum in Baghdad reopened after being closed for three years following political unrest. Then in 2024, the museum closed again, this time for renovations. Now, it’s scheduled to open on December 1.

The Art Newspaper reported that the museum will open with several new galleries, such as the Abbasid and Islamic galleries. There will also be specially curated wings.

The museum will display artifacts including winged bulls (lamassu) and stone carvings depicting legendary Assyrian monarchs, ancient clay tablets and historical prisms recording legal codes, calligraphic friezes, stone carvings, glassworks, and mosaics that have been recovered and restored. According to the newspaper Iraqi News, the museum’s doors are opening to domestic visitors, international tourists, and researchers from around the world. Netflix's 8-Part Hard Sci-Fi Series Is Already Renewed For 2 More Seasons

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

While there are a lot of great original sci-fi shows to come out of Netflix over the years, only one has preemptively avoided a major industry issue with its two-season renewal. It’s a tale as old as time. Viewers are only just getting into a new series, often an ambitious genre show with sci-fi, fantasy, or horror elements, when it is abruptly canceled despite critical acclaim.

From HBO’s one-season wonder Lovecraft Country to the iconic space western Firefly, there is no shortage of promising shows that were unexpectedly ended early by shock cancellations. Often, this leaves their storylines frustratingly unfinished, and fans end up feeling short-changed no matter how much money might have been spent on the budget of these shows during their runs. One need only look at Prime Video’s fantasy series The Wheel of Time for another example.

While the Robert Jordan adaptation was consistently critically adored throughout its three-season run, the show’s abrupt ending arrived long before the series had adapted the entire literary saga to the small screen. As such, what had felt like three increasingly promising seasons of fantasy TV ended up seeming instead like a lot of buildups to nothing. This is why it is so exciting to hear that Netflix’s 3 Body Problem has already avoided this issue with a preemptive two-season renewal.

Based on Chinese novelist Liu Cixin’s trilogy of books Remembrance of Earth's Past, 3 Body Problem’s title came from a real-life Newtonian physics problem. Fittingly enough, 3 Body Problem’s plot focused on a group of scientific researchers from different disciplines, dubbed the “Oxford Five,” coming together to investigate the mysterious deaths of their fellow academics. Eventually, the group unmask a secret conspiracy involving a cult and alien fleets that are preparing to invade earth.

Only, that’s not quite an accurate summary, since 3 Body Problem’s depiction of first contact with extraterrestrial life isn’t as simple as invasions or attacks. Unlike the earlier underrated TNT sci-fi series Falling Skies, 3 Body Problem’s extraterrestrials aren’t straightforward sentient, corporeal creatures, and their plans for earth aren’t as obvious as world domination or the intentional, malicious destruction of human civilization.

Instead, 3 Body Problem’s story merges the past, the present, virtual realities, and layered versions of its story told from different perspectives to create a rich, complex character-driven saga. 3 Body Problem is undoubtedly an odyssey, mixing the ambitious scope of earlier genre shows like The Walking Dead, The Expanse, or Game of Thrones with the narrative complexity of Westworld or Lost.

Despite this ambition, 3 Body Problem doesn’t end up overextending itself in its first outing, and the show is now guaranteed to make it to the end of its source story without needing to rush through the plot at a rate of knots. Where earlier Netflix sci-fi shows like Stranger Things expanded their once-modest stories to become ever more commodious and elaborate, 3 Body Problem was always a complicated show.

Luckily, however, Netflix renewed 3 Body Problem for seasons 2 and 3, ensuring the show has sufficient screen time to adapt the remainder of the source novel series. This is particularly reassuring for fans of Remembrance of Earth's Past since, while the novel was celebrated at the time of its release, Liu Cixin’s books were also thought to be unfilmable due to their unique structure.

3 Body Problem needed to reorder events and rejig characters, while still maintaining the same central plot, and pulled this off with aplomb in season 1. However, it is only since Netflix’s renewal that viewers can be reassured 3 Body Problem will be able to stick the show's landing.

‘we are tiny ecosystems’: iris van herpen on learning to collaborate with nature

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

exploring the hidden intelligence of natural systems, the dutch designer reflects on living materials, experimentation, and the future of design ahead of her upcoming sculptural commission with maison perrier-jouët at design miami.

‘The extraordinary possibility is that we are not discovering intelligence, but discovering how much intelligence we have been unable to perceive,’ says marine biologist David Gruber, whom Iris van Herpen quotes during our interview. The idea resonates throughout the Dutch designer’s work, which repeatedly looks beyond the boundaries of the human body to explore the complex systems connecting people, materials, and other forms of life. Through collaborations with scientists, biodesigners, and artists, van Herpen investigates how these relationships might reshape the way we create. Her upcoming collaboration with Maison Perrier-Jouët, presented at Design Miami, continues this exploration, drawing on the vibrations and energy of microorganisms living beneath the soil of the champagne house’s vineyards to develop a new visual language (find designboom’s previous coverage here).

‘I look at the body beyond our physical boundaries, as something liquid, tiny parts of a large blue planet,’ van Herpen tells designboom. ‘We tend to perceive ourselves as individuals, but we are tiny ecosystems, existing in a complex symbiosis and entwinement with countless other forms of life, both inside us and around us.’ The designer sees technology as a way to expand human consciousness, opening up possibilities for understanding how the body relates to the natural world beyond its physical boundaries. Our senses, she points out, allow us to experience only a small part of reality, while other species communicate through systems that remain largely beyond our understanding. ‘More than 8 million species share our planet, we only understand the language of one,’ the designer highlights.

Van Herpen’s understanding of the body also shapes how she approaches couture, using the medium to explore the connections between human life and the wider natural world. ‘Couture can become an extension of our senses, inspiring us to move beyond the idea of the human being as the center of everything, towards an awareness of ourselves as part of a huge, vastly complex, living intelligence,’ the designer notes. Her work often draws on natural phenomena, but its relationship with nature goes beyond visual references. She is interested in how the processes and behaviors of living systems might influence the way garments are conceived and made.

For the Sympoiesis collection, van Herpen collaborated with biodesigner Chris Bellamy on a living look inhabited by 125 million bioluminescent algae, Pyrocystis lunula. The organisms emit light in response to the wearer’s movement, creating an interaction between the human body and another biological community. ‘Instead of shaping a material towards my ideas/vision, we had to learn about and understand the needs and rhythms of these living organisms,’ she explains. ‘It was for the first time that we were not “just” constructing a look, but instead, we had to nurture a living community.’

The algae required maintaining specific levels of light, humidity, and temperature, as well as attention to their circadian rhythms. The whole experience influenced how van Herpen thinks about collaboration and authorship. The title Sympoiesis, meaning ‘making together’, reflects how living things can work together to create something. ‘We need to stop seeing ourselves as separate from nature, embrace our interdependence (for both our physical and mental well-being), and create and act with the awareness that we are nature,’ she says. For designers, she believes, this means thinking beyond the materials they use and finding ways to make the creative process and its results contribute to nature’s cycles.

Her experiments bring her into contact with forces that cannot be entirely anticipated. For Sonic Starquakes, developed in collaboration with Dale R. Sulak, Iris van Herpen began with a relatively simple dress that was charged inside a particle accelerator and cryogenically preserved. The process left it as a metastable reservoir of energy containing billions of trapped electrons. In the nights before the show, the dress began to discharge, producing branching flashes of lightning that etched microscopic hollow channels into its three-dimensional structure. ‘The artwork you see is not controlled or created by me or anyone else; it was the pure force of lightning that created the 3-dimensional detailing,’ she tells us. The result went beyond what she had originally imagined, but this is precisely what the fashion designer seeks through experimentation. ‘That is why I invite chaos and the unknown into my everyday in the atelier.’

In contrast to the constant demand for new collections, van Herpen follows a slower, research-led approach that she describes as ‘slow fashion’. Her collections consist of one-off haute couture pieces, with a single look taking anywhere from four to ten months to complete. Research collaborations with scientists, artists, engineers, and architects can take one to two years, particularly when they involve developing new materials. She now presents one show annually to give these processes more time to develop. ‘Time is the new luxury,’ she points out. Much of this material research also focuses on sustainability, although for van Herpen the value of taking time lies equally in allowing unexpected discoveries to develop.

Living organisms have developed the ability to grow, adapt, repair themselves, and participate in cycles of matter and energy over billions of years. The challenge, in her view, is for human invention to learn from that complexity. ‘Some tend to think that technology is more advanced than nature. The opposite is true; nature remains infinitely more complex than current technology,’ she shares with deisgnboom.

Her interest in working with nature also carries into her upcoming collaboration with Maison Perrier-Jouët, which will be presented at Design Miami. The project draws on cymatics, using vibrations and energy generated by microorganisms beneath the vineyard soil as the basis for a series of visual motifs. The work continues van Herpen’s exploration of natural processes that usually remain unseen. It also connects to her wider ideas about the future of design, from materials that can grow, regenerate, and biodegrade to architecture and cities that support the ecosystems around them.

Iris Van Herpen believes designers, architects, and engineers have much to learn from the way nature works. ‘Nature has spent billions of years evolving extraordinary systems of intelligence, adaptation and symbiosis. There is so much we can learn from these processes,’ she believes. In fashion, this could mean garments that respond and adapt to their environments, grow through biological processes, and eventually return safely to an ecosystem. ‘I am convinced that the next revolution in design will cause a shift from controlling nature to collaborating with her,’ the Dutch fashion designer argues.

Between Moonlight and Aurora: Photographing East Greenland After Dark

Photographing East Greenland at night is an exercise in preparation, observation, and adaptation. The landscape is immense, the conditions can be demanding, and the opportunities to make a particular image may be limited to a few minutes. Towering icebergs, drifting sea ice, fog, low clouds, and the changing Arctic sky create an environment in which light can completely transform the way a scene is perceived. Yet having spectacular scenery in front of you does not necessarily mean you will come away with a strong photograph. The challenge is to recognize what makes a moment worth capturing, understand how the available light interacts with the landscape, and make the right decisions before that moment disappears.

During a recent photography expedition in East Greenland, hosted by Secret Atlas this last October 2026, I spent several evenings leading the photographic project targeting enormous icebergs illuminated by the rising moon, alongside the Northern Lights appearing above the surrounding fjords and ice. These two subjects offered very different photographic challenges and a particular one-of-a-kind night operation.

Some photographs begin long before the camera is taken out of the bag. The moonrise series was one of those projects. Across four days, I followed the changing opportunities to photograph the rising moon among the enormous icebergs of East Greenland. The objective was not simply to record the moon above the horizon, but to find a relationship between the moonlight, the architecture of the icebergs, the sense of exploration, and the surrounding landscape.

The first opportunity to photograph the sequence came around 8 p.m. on day three of our expedition, when the timing and the available light finally began to align. In the Arctic, twilight can retain considerable ambient light even as the moon rises. This transition creates an interesting balance: the landscape may still hold detail, while the moon begins illuminating the ice from a different angle.

A rising moon and a monumental iceberg are impressive subjects individually, but putting them together is not enough. Their positions, relative sizes, illumination, and separation from the background all influence how the image is read. Anticipating these relationships is essential, particularly when the window for making the photograph is short.

Photographing from a Zodiac adds another dimension to the process. Polar bear presence in East Greenland limits our possibility to operate on land during low-light conditions. Unlike working from stable ground, the shooting position is not necessarily fixed, and even small movements can affect the framing, particularly when using a long focal length.

The objective was to find a viewpoint from which the iceberg formations could be separated visually and the rising moon incorporated without losing the sense of scale. Icebergs are difficult subjects to photograph because their dimensions can be hard to communicate.

Focal length plays an important role in communicating scale. A wide-angle lens can emphasize the surrounding environment, while a longer lens can compress the scene and bring distant formations visually closer together.

Exposure requires similar care. A gradually brightening moon against a dark sky can make it difficult to preserve highlights without losing detail in the shadows. Rather than trying to make every part of the photograph equally bright, I aim to retain the texture of the illuminated surfaces while allowing darkness to remain part of the image, preserving my highlight and shadow information.

The Northern Lights create a very different set of challenges. The moon’s position can be anticipated, allowing me to plan around a relatively predictable source of illumination. This is great for subject illumination, though distracting when the moon appears in the frame. The aurora, by contrast, can change rapidly in brightness, shape, color, and movement.

From our main vessel, we navigate through the fjord in search of a good-looking iceberg to frame our shot. We carefully position the ship to achieve the greatest possible stability while maintaining the flexibility to adjust our orientation depending on where the aurora is expected to appear. The moment the Northern Lights emerge, close coordination via VHF radio with the bridge team becomes essential. As we prepare to photograph from the ship’s outer deck, the vessel is brought to a complete standstill, reaching zero speed over ground. This allows us to capture the scene with maximum stability, even using exposures of around half a second, while the iceberg and the dancing aurora create the perfect natural composition.

Aurora photography involves a series of compromises between shutter speed, aperture, and ISO. A longer exposure collects more light, which can help when the display is faint, but it can also blur the finer structures of a rapidly moving aurora. A shorter exposure preserves more detail in the curtains and patterns but requires enough light to produce a usable image. There is no universal exposure for the Northern Lights. The right settings depend on the brightness and speed of the display, the available ambient light, the lens, and the amount of detail I want to retain. I adjust according to the behavior of the sky rather than relying on a fixed recipe, as shown below with this powerful curtain of light.

As the sun began to set, we left the comfort of our main vessel and ventured out aboard our Zodiacs for a reconnaissance ride through the surrounding waters. Our mission was to explore the landscape, identify one or two particularly striking icebergs, and select the perfect setting for what we hoped would become an unforgettable night of aurora photography. Capturing the Northern Lights is as much about patience and preparation as it is about being in the right place at the right time. With exposures ranging from half a second to four, five, or even six seconds, stability becomes essential. Even the slightest movement can compromise an image. Out here, surrounded by the vastness of the polar landscape, finding a way to create a stable photographic platform would be one of the greatest challenges of the night.

Our idea was to return after dark, launching the Zodiacs from the main vessel and navigating through the freezing waters in search of newly formed sea ice near one of the selected icebergs. This young ice, approximately one centimeter thick, offered the possibility of carefully positioning the bow of a Zodiac against its edge, allowing us to wedge ourselves into place and create the stability required for long exposures. Finding a sufficiently wide area of newly formed ice would also give us the freedom to reposition ourselves as the auroras shifted and danced across different parts of the sky. But this was about more than simply finding a stable platform. It was about finding the right perspective, in pitch darkness surrounded by the freezing Greenlandic waters.

When planning an operation like this, I always look for ways to approach an iceberg while maintaining a safe distance and shooting from as low an angle as possible. I want the iceberg to become a powerful element in the composition, rising beneath the aurora and giving the image a strong sense of scale and depth. The newly formed sea ice often adds another dimension to the scene. Its delicate patterns, textures, and natural leading lines can guide the viewer’s eye toward the main subject, transforming an already extraordinary landscape into a compelling photographic composition.

By 11 p.m., it was time. We left the main vessel and began our night mission. Alongside my teammates Signe-Maria and Myriam, I led our group of photographers toward the giant icebergs and the newly formed sea ice. Behind us, the ship’s powerful searchlight illuminated our chosen iceberg, providing a guiding beacon across the darkness as we made our way toward our destination. Once we reached the area, we carefully positioned the Zodiacs against the edge of the newly formed ice. Every movement had to be deliberate. We wanted to achieve the stability needed for photography while maintaining a safe distance from the iceberg and the surrounding ice.

Fortunately, we had prepared for this moment long before leaving the ship. During the day, we had already assembled, adjusted, and practiced our photographic setups, making sure everyone knew exactly what to do. Now, under the darkness of the polar night, there was no time to waste. We secured our tripods against the Zodiac’s pontoon and tilted them forward, carefully adjusting their position to keep every part of the Zodiac out of the frame. One by one, our photographers settled into their compositions, pointing their cameras toward the magnificent icebergs.

Everything was ready. I picked up the radio and called the ship’s bridge team, asking them to switch off all the lights. And suddenly, everything changed. The powerful beam that had guided us across the darkness disappeared. We were now completely immersed in the polar night, resting on the newly formed sea ice, facing a gigantic iceberg beneath an extraordinary celestial spectacle. Above us, twenty billion stars seemed to fill the sky. A half-moon gently illuminated the frozen landscape, revealing the magnificent shapes and textures of the iceberg, while the auroras began dancing overhead, bringing movement and color to an otherwise silent world.

For a moment, everything seemed to stand still. There we were, floating between the ocean and the ice, surrounded by one of the most extraordinary landscapes on Earth. Cameras were clicking, shutters were opening and closing, and each long exposure was capturing a fleeting moment of this ever-changing spectacle. It is difficult to describe the feeling of witnessing such a scene. You can plan every detail, prepare your equipment, study the conditions, and carefully orchestrate the entire operation, but nature always has the final word. And when everything finally comes together, the experience goes far beyond photography. That night, we were fortunate enough to experience something truly exceptional. A moment in which preparation, teamwork, adventure, and nature converged beneath the dancing auroras. The photographs will remain on our SD cards, but the feeling of being there, surrounded by the icebergs, the stars, and the silence of the polar night, will remain with us forever.

There is a tendency to describe photographs of the Arctic as the result of being in the right place at the right time. Luck certainly plays a role. The moon must rise in a favorable position, the weather must allow the landscape to be seen, and the aurora must develop in a way that creates an interesting composition. None of these conditions can be guaranteed. But being present when a spectacle occurs is only part of the process. The photographer must recognize the potential of the scene, decide where to position the camera, choose a focal length, balance the exposure, and determine which elements belong in the frame. Preparation does not control nature; it makes it possible to respond when an opportunity appears.

This is the best type of wireless earbuds for audio output

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

What design actually delivers the best sound?

Wireless earbud design has evolved in leaps and bounds. Some of the earliest models would occasionally lose sync between each ear, never mind questions like battery life or sweat resistance. Now, the best wireless earbuds can last an entire workday and will survive the sweatiest gym sessions. You can even use the AirPods Pro 3 as hearing aids.

Our question today is this: What design actually delivers the best sound? The industry has evolved to support many different form factors, but not all of them are actually geared towards fidelity. Some may prioritize maximum comfort, particular environments or simply a consumer price point. Make the wrong choice, and the results can be extremely disappointing.

Considerations include the type of eartips, how buds secure to your ear, and noise cancelation features. We'll also explain when it's worth making some sacrifices, since vanilla audio may be better than nothing at all.

When it comes to form factor, you'll want to begin with in-ear (sometimes referred to as in-canal) earbuds. These provide several major benefits to audio quality over open-ear designs, beginning with passive noise cancellation. You're blocking most of the outside world, although some noise will inevitably seep through. This positioning also maximizes the perceived volume of each bud, which can help you catch more of the nuances in your movies and music. You should even experience better bass response because the buds are directly rattling your ear and the air inside it.

From here, further narrow your search to models with foam or silicone eartips. These boost passive cancellation as well as bass response, the latter by trapping frequencies inside a space with increased air pressure. For this reason, it's vital to find tips that form a tight fit. You can sometimes upgrade to third-party tips if the ones bundled with your buds aren't doing the job. Keep in mind that while foam is preferable for sound quality, it's less than hygienic for workouts because it's harder to clean.

Speaking of fit, it's also important to choose buds that lock in as securely as possible without becoming painful to wear. When buds are loose, they can break that ever-so-important sound quality seal, even if you have great tips. You don't necessarily need buds with an external clip, but those may help if you want both quality and the freedom to sprint or do hanging crunches.

You'll also want a design with active noise cancellation (ANC). Passive cancellation merely isolates sound. ANC generates "anti-noise," which partly or completely neutralizing the outside world. This is essential not just for hearing quieter elements in your audio, but also for eliminating distractions such as plane engines or nearby conversation.

Some people intentionally choose open-ear or bone conduction earbuds, despite sacrificing significant bass output and noise cancellation. The reason is simple: situational awareness. If you're running or cycling outside, for example, you can't afford to block out ambient noise because you need to be able to hear an engine, horn, siren or yell warning you of imminent danger. Many in-ear ANC products (like AirPods) compensate by offering "transparency" modes, but of course, it's hard to beat direct exposure to your environment.

Bone conduction buds can sometimes operate in conditions where other designs are unusable, such as underwater. Those benefits go out the window with models that add air-based drivers into the mix to improve bass. All bone conduction buds do, however, deliver many frequencies straight to your inner ear, allowing them to remain "pure" despite mixing with ambience.

You'll probably recognize immediately whether these sacrifices are essential. When in doubt, it's better to default to in-ear ANC buds. There's just no way of recovering the lost fidelity if you opt for something else.

10 Thriller Books That Hooked Me From the Opening Chapter

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

At any given moment, Scott Simone has at least three books jockeying for his attention. He’ll never turn down a campus novel with a literary bent, nor will he hesitate to add yet another mystery to his Leaning Tower of Pisa-inspired to-be-read pile — which is built upon the bedrocks of too many short story collections, how-are-these-real true crime books, and classics waiting to be cracked open again. Topics that have him ripping through pages the fastest include closed-circle murders, unhappy professors, life-questioning philosophical deep dives, so long as they feature sharp sentences, a dark sense of humor, and at least a little malice.

He’s spent so much time in book shops over the years he is, to his wife’s chagrin, continuing to build a library that doesn’t quite respect the dimensions of the apartment meant to contain it.

Some thriller writers seem terrified you'll put their book down, so they throw everything at you: a murder on page one and an abduction on page three, all while a massive conspiracy begins to unravel. And that’s all before introducing who’s involved. Eventful, sure, but there's something almost desperate about all that hoopla. It’s as if the writer's afraid one quiet page will send readers straight back to their phones.

The best thriller writers have a little more faith in their stories. They're in no great hurry to show their hand, allowing the reader to become acquainted with a character worth following. Of course, there's nothing wrong with opening on a murder or throwing somebody into immediate danger, but the excitement alone can only take a story so far. What matters is that the writer gives us time to find our footing, to become familiar with the world we're entering and curious about what lies beyond it. By the end of that first chapter, we might have little idea where the story is headed, but we're more than happy to go along for the ride.

A woman burning her father’s corpse in the garden is a shocking way to start a novel, but Liz Nugent somehow makes it relatable in the opening pages of Strange Sally Diamond. Sally's father constantly joked that she should put him out with the trash when he died, so she does. Yet the way she recounts it, with the practicality of someone listing yet another household chore, you understand it. It's an absurd explanation, but Nugent delivers it so matter-of-factly that you begin to appreciate Sally's reasoning.

That's what makes Nugent's introduction so effective: everyone else sees a 42-year-old woman who's done something appalling. The police have questions and turn Sally into a suspect. The neighbors are horrified and turn Sally into a monster. Sally is confused by all the hubbub. The reader is at an advantage here, having spent some time in Sally’s mind and following her peculiar train of thought, understanding her in a way nobody else does. The twists and the shock that follow only deepen that early compassion for a character that, on the surface, could be easily written off as psychotic.

When you’ve got revolvers and shotguns pointed at your head, it usually spells endgame. Lee Child flips that script when introducing Jack Reacher in the opening of Killing Floor. Reacher is simply eating breakfast as four police officers descend on a Georgia diner. But he doesn’t fly off into action, doesn’t protest or attempt to escape: he quietly finishes his eggs and tucks five dollars under his plate. Reacher, in fact, is so composed that the police, all that firepower be damned, begin to look like the nervous ones.

It's a clever way to introduce a man who'll eventually become one of fiction's most formidable tough guys. Child could have staged some spectacular show of force, but instead he lets Reacher's observations do the work: he sizes up the officers and considers their tactics. And just when we're getting comfortable with the idea that Reacher is an innocent man caught in an unfortunate situation, Child drops a revelation: he hasn't killed anybody in this town. He hasn’t killed anyone in some time now. It’s the exclamation point to the chapter, a way of announcing the police may have arrested the wrong man, but he’s not a harmless one.

Sébastien Japrisot begins A Trap for Cinderella in just about the most non-thriller way possible: lulling the reader into a fairy tale. Mi and Do grew up together. The former is the ideal of the pair: beautiful and wealthy, the symbol of adoration for everyone around her. The latter is intelligent but poor, always getting the short end of the stick. But then Japrisot reveals that Do has invented this little fairy tale herself. She resents Mi, so she’s conveniently cast herself as Cinderella. It’s presented in such a way that we’ve no idea how much of what we've read is true.

That quiet beginning is a trap in itself as the novel takes a dark turn, with a fire at a villa on the French Riviera. The blaze leaves one woman dead and another so badly burned that she loses her memory. The survivor doesn't know whether she's Mi or Do, and the fire may have been deliberate, meaning she could be either the victim of an attempted murder or the person who planned it. Japrisot has already shown how easily a story can be twisted by the person telling it, and then allows his protagonist to reconstruct her entire life from what other people tell her.

Jean-Patrick Manchette’s opening to Fatale is a bit of a red herring. Almost the entire first chapter follows a man named Roucart on a hunting trip. We become overly familiar with his protruding belly and his deep regret at giving up smoking. It's the sort of attention you'd expect a writer to give someone we'll be following for the next few hundred pages. So when an attractive young woman appears in the woods, we assume she's wandered into Roucart's story. But she takes a shotgun off her shoulder and shoots him dead.

Ultimately, it’s clever the way Manchette makes us almost as susceptible to this woman as Roucart. He was so enthralled by her appearance that he didn’t for a second recognize the danger. We, too, dismiss the danger, sliding past the fact that she just committed a cold-blooded murder because we're so intrigued by her composure, so curious about who she is. She arrives in a provincial town where the wealthy residents have plenty of dirty little secrets: they're a vain, greedy, thoroughly unpleasant lot, and watching her exploit their weaknesses becomes something of a pleasure. Before long, we're almost rooting for a woman we know is capable of murder.

Many a thriller makes revenge look easy, the only logical next step in the grieving process. It can look a lot different when you’re staring it square in the eye. That's the predicament Kōtarō Isaka gives a grieving father in the opening of Bullet Train. Yuichi Kimura boards a Japanese high-speed train intent on killing the teenager who pushed his six-year-old son off a rooftop. He's got the boy's seat number, a gun fitted with a silencer, and every reason to want him dead, but not the confidence. So when he sees his target, an innocent-looking schoolboy, he hesitates. The boy doesn't.

And that's how Isaka introduces one of the novel's central ideas: Having a perfectly good reason to do something doesn't mean you're remotely able to pull it off. It's bad enough that Kimura's been outsmarted by a teenager, but worse, the boy finds his humiliation entertaining. And Isaka has plenty more fun at the expense of his characters as the train fills with professional killers, all convinced they know what they're doing. They're a dangerous bunch, sure, but for all their cleverness and carefully laid plans, they spend an awful lot of time making fools of themselves.

Jo Nesbø warps a symbol of winter wonderland into one of holiday horror in the opening pages of The Snowman. Sara Kvinesland meets her lover at his house, leaving her young son waiting in the car. She's nervous about someone catching them, so when her lover notices a face staring at them through the bedroom window, they both panic. But it's only a snowman, its pebble eyes fixed on the house. Except, snowmen don't build themselves — somebody went to the trouble of putting it right outside that window.

Nesbø lets that thought linger as Sara returns to her car, where her son is trembling behind locked doors, seeing something that's scared him half to death. Sara's got other things on her mind: she's worried about her affair and keeping up appearances. Her son can barely get her attention, even when he tells her they're going to die. And that's where Nesbø really gets under your skin: the boy knows something's wrong, but Sara, the one person who really should be listening, is too distracted to notice. There's no murder and no grisly discovery; just a snowman standing outside a house, and already we’re hooked.

Thirteen Hours opens at 5:36 a.m. with a woman running for her life. We don’t know why, or who’s chasing her, and we don’t find out. Deon Meyer leaves her, exhausted and terrified, desperate to escape the men who seem to want her dead. Meyer cuts to Detective Benny Griessel, who's being dragged out of bed by a phone call about a body outside a church. And the man’s got problems of his own: an estranged wife, another woman he slept with recently, and a 156-day desperate attempt to stay sober.

It's almost infuriating how casually Meyer abandons the woman, but that frustration only makes us more desperate to get back to her. Griessel, of course, hasn't the faintest idea she exists, and Meyer takes his time introducing the detective, giving us a man whose considerable professional experience is at odds with the mess he's made of his personal life. Each time Meyer takes us away from the woman, we're left worrying about what's happening to her while we're gone. And all the while, the clock keeps ticking, whether we're watching the woman running or not.

An ex-con in a stolen car isn't exactly the person you'd entrust with an 11-year-old girl's safety, but Jordan Harper has us begging her to climb inside in the opening of She Rides Shotgun. You see, a prison gang has ordered the murder of Nate McClusky and his family, and he's racing to get his daughter Polly before the killers do. But Polly doesn't know: all she sees outside her school is a hulking man covered in tattoos and scars, a father she barely remembers and who's supposed to be behind bars.

Harper puts us in an uncomfortable position, knowing that the man Polly fears might be the only person capable of protecting her. And he plays with that contradiction throughout their uneasy reunion. Polly is painfully shy and bullied at school, carrying a teddy bear for comfort. Nate looks like he could frighten just about anybody. But as they're driving away, a police car passes, and Polly catches something on her father's face she'd never expected to see: fear. Now this intimidating man doesn't seem so invincible, and that's what makes the opening so compelling.

It's a familiar enough scene: a mother worried about a son who's out past his curfew. But Gillian McAllister takes that moment of parental anxiety to the extreme in the opening of Wrong Place Wrong Time. Jen is waiting up for her 18-year-old son, Todd, watching from the window of their Liverpool home as he finally comes down the street. She's relieved to see him, until a stranger approaches. Todd pulls a knife and stabs the man three times, and she hasn't the faintest idea why.

It's a horrible thing for a parent to witness, made worse by the realization that the child you've spent 18 years raising might be someone you don't know at all. McAllister gives Jen a chance to go back and find out. We watch as the clocks move backward through the rest of the novel, allowing her to revisit the days leading up to the murder. She knows who wielded the knife but not what drove him to it. This literal trip down memory lane allows her to scrutinize the years she's spent raising him, revisiting conversations and decisions she'd long since forgotten.

It's easy to imagine what you'd do if someone broke into your house: grab the children, call the police, find something to defend yourself with. But Tracy Sierra makes those plans laughably optimistic in the opening of Nightwatching, in which a mother hears footsteps on the stairs. She entertains just about every possibility until a stranger appears at the top of the stairs, so tall he nearly hits his head on the ceiling. Even then, she struggles to accept what's happening.

Sierra makes us endure every second of that hesitation, making the mental cycle of decisions seem visceral. She thinks of screaming, but doesn’t want the intruder to know she’s there. She thinks about moving, but doesn’t want to wake the children. All the while, her mind also keeps latching onto the most random of details, from the man's dry sneakers to the light falling across the landing. There's something convincing about the way she thinks, grasping at anything that might help her understand the situation while struggling to do something about it.

GTA 6's Autosave Is Ruining The Game, But Not For The Reason You Think

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

GTA 6 fans say they're losing many hours of progress, but that's not the whole story

Rockstar's Grand Theft Auto 6 might be the best-rated game of all time, but that doesn't mean players love every single thing about the open-world shooter. Some drawbacks, like the game's wonky controls, can be adjusted. Others, players are simply stuck with. One of the biggest complaints is that GTA 6's autosave feature simply isn't good enough.

The issue began attracting attention after high-profile incidents involving Twitch streamers like JasonTheWeen, who lost 12 hours of progress after accidentally turning off his PlayStation 5. It's reasonable to expect that closing a game suddenly might result in some lost progress, but losing 12 hourswas so frustrating that the streamer burst into tears on camera.

Most people aren't losing half a day's worth of progress. Early reports suggest that streamers accessing the game early via the "New Zealand trick" are encountering unforeseen issues with the workaround. Still, many GTA 6 fans were incensed enough about lost playtime to complain about the autosave feature​​​​​​​ on social media.

"4 hours gone, no autosave, no save file..." one fan wrote on a gaming Facebook group. "What the actual! A billion dollar game and this has broken already!"

"I lost 10 hours to their​​​​​​​ dumbass save system," one Redditor alleged.

"What genius came up with this save feature," another complained.

The problem is partially due to a knowledge gap. GTA 6 does autosave​​​​​​​, typically at the start of a mission or at a checkpoint​​​​​​​. If a player elects to spend hours between missions shopping or cruising the streets, GTA 6 won't autosave any of that game time. Players should regularly pause the game and save manually.

The issue isn't just infrequent saving.​​​​​​​ Progress in GTA 6 is slow-going compared to previous games.​​​​​​​ It's harder to steal cars now, and the map is much bigger, so getting anywhere takes time. The roads are narrower, so it's easier to get into a car crash. Many players find themselves struggling with the game's idiosyncratic controls. It's easy to aggravate an NPC by pressing the wrong button, which can set off a chain of unwanted events. Loading an earlier save to undo the damage might also mean losing significant progress.

Many of GTA 6's systems mimic real life, and the autosave issue can make the quiet yet rewarding gameplay feel tedious. Even if a player loses just 20 minutes, during that time they could havewoken up​​​​​​​, worked out, drunk a protein shake, showered, chosena new outfit, and answered some texts​​​​​​​. They spoke to the neighbor, picked up a new quest, and biked to the grocery store. While buying bread,​​​​​​​ they accidentally pulled out a gun and scared the cashier. The worker called the police.

It wouldn't take that long to do it all over again from an earlier save file​​​​​​​, but repeating everything would be a major bummer. The player could try to outrun the cops — but then getting back to what they intended to do might take twice as long. That's assuming that nothing goes wrong during the chase, which is a tall order in the world of GTA.

That propensity for chaos might explain why Rockstar's current system works the way it does. If GTA 6 saved more often, players would be perpetually stuck with the haywire consequences of their actions. There must be a middle ground, though.​​​​​​​ The game could autosave more frequently, across multiple temporary save files. That tweak still wouldn't change GTA 6's focus on the monotony of day-to-day life, which is what fans are truly struggling with.

The likelihood of an update here seems slim since Rockstar has used a similar system for saves across many of its games​​​​​​​. Perhaps the best GTA 6 fans can do is save often​​​​​​​ — and learn to embrace the pandemonium.

In Sammy Baloji’s Hands, ‘Objective’ Materials Reveal New Truths About African History

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In the mid-2000s, after he completed his master’s degree in information sciences and communication at the University of Lubumbashi, Sammy Baloji started working with the French Institute. The director of the institute, also based in Lubumbashi, was interested in colonial architecture and cultural heritage history, and he collaborated with academics researching those topics.

Baloji’s work for the French Institute mainly entailed photographing events and later working with researchers from France. But he was already on the way to becoming the artist he is today. Related Articles Actor Says He Removed His Mother's Art from Venice Biennale Installation as a Pro-Israel Protest French Artist Claude Lévêque Ordered to Stand Trial for Rape and Sexual Assault of Minors

Working at the French Institute, he recalled in an interview with ARTnews, “really shifted my perspective, in terms of outlining photography or research, because I was interested in the way that [the researchers] were addressing the city or buildings or cultural heritage from a historical and objective perspective. I was mainly interested in people’s experiences, the testimonies of people who are living [in Lubumbashi like] myself.”

The two opposite approaches opened up the question of how he could “use the so-called scientific objective materials and bring them close to the human experience, to the architecture of local sites.”

The inquiry led the artist to make one of his first significant projects, Mémoire (2006), a photography collage series of colonial-era black and white archival images and colored prints showing contemporary Lubumbashi in the Katanga province, which is rich in mineral resources including copper, uranium, lithium, diamonds and cobalt.

Baloji’s work for the French Institute had allowed him to observe the social, environmental and political impact of mining in Katanga. Now, using a combination of photography and research, he was able to underscore how Belgian colonization had shaped the Democratic Republic of Congo, whose history of extraction he has continued to explore.

Works continuing to build on the themes of Mémoire can currently be seen in two high-profile showings. Baloji is making his debut US solo with “When Extraction Becomes Form It Speaks In Borrowed Tongues,” at the Wexner Center for the Arts in Columbus, Ohio, on view through February 14, 2027. The exhibition explores how Europe’s decorative arts and architecture still include elements of Congo’s colonialist history and the extraction of natural resources. It takes a critical look at the impact on land, people and cultural forms, speaking to complicated relationships between Central Africa, Europe, and the Americas.

Concurrently with the Wexner Center show, Baloji’s work can be seen at the Venice Biennale, both in the main show, curated by the late Koyo Kouoh, and in the national pavilion of the Democratic Republic of Congo. In the main exhibition at Venice, Baloji is showing three sculptures and 16 collage prints. Two of the sculptures reference works from a 19th-century Belgian colonial museum and the national collection built in the 1970s by the former President of the Democratic Republic of Congo, Mobutu Sese Seko.

“I started to realize that the colonial system was not just about controlling territory but also occupying societies and imposing new rules,” said Baloji, who splits his time between Brussels and Lubumbashi.

In the Wexner show, Baloji is exhibiting Suspension (Debris of History, Matters of Memory), a commission conceived with architect Gloria Cabral and in collaboration with art historian Cécile Fromont. Drawing from the design of historical Kongo cloth, the suspended large-scale sculpture is made of mining and construction waste, the latter from recent work at the Wexner Center. Meanwhile, a new tapestry called Rethreaded Indies, made by Baloji and Fromont, addresses the French Old Indies tapestry series (1637–1730), produced at the royal Gobelins manufactory in Paris, connecting that with Kongolese weaving. The works use textile and architecture to correct misinformation about African histories, showing how European colonization has shaped the continent’s narratives, often for the worse.

“I would say my interest in his work is how he weaves these histories together, and in a way that I find extremely compelling,” said Julieta González, the head of visual arts at the Wexner Center and the curator of the artist’s exhibition. Baloji’s work, she added, “is important because it weaves together so many historical events and situations but with materiality that speaks to the particular situation he’s critiquing, which is the legacies of colonialism” in the Democratic Republic of Congo. The show she curated, she said, offers the public “an insight into [the artist’s] very research-oriented practice and how it unfolds in so many different mediums [which includes] video, sculpture and photography.”

Baloji was born in 1978 in Lubumbashi and grew up with, in some sense, limited knowledge about the history of Belgium’s colonization of the Democratic Republic of Congo. (Connecting that and resource extraction in Katanga with global or political history came later following reports of Congo supplying Iran with uranium, which the Central African country denied.) In secondary school, Baloji cofounded a collective that read and shared books from libraries and schools that they had access to. He was also drawing at the time, producing some of his first artworks.

His parents did not encourage his plan to study at the Fine Art Academy, so for his master’s degree, he studied information sciences and communication, which happened to align with the artistic practices he was interested in. Ironically, or maybe coincidentally, the course Baloji pursued to appease his parents so he could make his own career choices ended up being formative to his practice.

Baloji is also known for more than just his art. In 2008, he cofounded the Lubumbashi-based Picha Art and Research Center and the Lubumbashi Biennale, whose ninth edition, cocurated by Congolese-Belgian curator Sorana Munsya and Ethiopian-Eritrean-Italian artist Jermay Michael Gabriel, opened in October. The art space and biennial are platforms that help expand access to the arts and support local artists with needed infrastructure, especially because of limited state support.

Camille Ostermann, an independent art consultant who worked on Congo’s national pavilion at the Venice Biennale, said, “I’ve admired [Baloji’s] collaborative practice [since the early 2010s]: works co-authored with historians, exhibitions built around artists he invites. But it was at the Lubumbashi Biennale, which Picha organizes, that I saw this spirit at work. In a city shaped by mining, by what is extracted and carried elsewhere, Picha does the opposite: it builds, gathers, gives back. Many artists told me their paths had been changed by Picha and Sammy’s mentorship. Like Ibrahim Mahama in Tamale or Yinka Shonibare in Lagos, Sammy works internationally while staying rooted in Lubumbashi, deliberately keeping the two connected: what he builds abroad can open doors for others at home.”

Picha Center and the Lubumbashi Biennale are also, in some ways, an extension of Baloji’s research and practice. The biennial, he said, can start conversations about extractivism and ecological crisis, as well as talk about “the impact of international and economic interests on the environment and societies.” It was a statement that could easily have been applied to many of his artworks.

Distributed LLM Training Across Metros: Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Traditionally, training state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) relies on a monolithic cluster of accelerators, where all devices are collocated and tightly coupled via high-bandwidth to constantly synchronize gradients. However, physical and cost constraints make scaling a single slice increasingly difficult.

To scale further, we can connect multiple compute slices across geographically distributed datacenters. However, when training across multiple datacenters over wide-area networks (WAN), standard data-parallel training suffers from severe network stalls because slow WAN transfers cannot be fully overlapped with computation.

To address this, the Google DeepMind and Google Research teams developed DiLoCo (Distributed Low-Communication), a distributed optimization algorithm designed to make training resilient to these low-bandwidth, high-latency connections. In this blog post, we discuss the core concepts of DiLoCo, how it is implemented in MaxText (Google's open-source JAX-based LLM training framework), and the performance benefits it brings to large-scale LLM training on Google Cloud.

DiLoCo was introduced by Google DeepMind in the paper DiLoCo: Distributed Low-Communication Training of Language Models. Instead of synchronizing gradients across all devices at every single training step (as in standard Data Parallelism), DiLoCo partitions training across M independent compute groups, referred to as learners, and allows each learner to perform many local optimization steps independently before communicating across groups.

Despite communicating significantly less frequently (by a factor of H, e.g., 1-2 orders of magnitude less) than standard synchronous training, DiLoCo has been shown to achieve comparable convergence and model quality.

The Vanilla DiLoCo described above reduces total communication volume by a factor of H, however, all learners still transmit their weight deltas for the entire model all at once at every H-th step. On a thin network pipe, that full-model transfer creates a large periodic stall every H steps. This issue is alleviated by Streaming DiLoCo, which is described in the next section.

In multi-slice training, communication within a single slice is handled by high-speed Inter-Chip Interconnects (ICI). When scaling to multiple slices, synchronization across slices must traverse the Data Center Network (DCN) or, if the slices are geographically distributed, wide-area networks (WAN).

Within a single datacenter, DCN bandwidth is high enough that JAX and XLA can often hide standard Data Parallel all-reduce collectives by overlapping gradient communication with backward-pass computation. Across datacenters or congested links, however, bandwidth drops and latency rises sharply. Standard DP stalls at every step waiting for global gradient synchronization, collapsing Model FLOPS Utilization (MFU).

Streaming DiLoCo eliminates this synchronization stall by partitioning the model's parameters into P disjoint fragments. Over the course of H steps, every single fragment in the model is synchronized exactly once, achieving the exact same outer-loop cadence as vanilla DiLoCo without spiky bandwidth utilization.

Using a roofline model (similar to the JAX Scaling Book), we can calculate the network bandwidth required to prevent communication stalls for a model with ∣Θ∣ parameters (at b bytes per parameter, e.g., b=2 for bfloat16) and per-step compute time tstep=tfwd+tbwd. For example, the standard Data Parallel algorithm transfers 2∣Θ∣b bytes per sync via an all-reduce collective, which is a distributed reduce-scatter where each node reduces a different part, followed by an all-gather so all participants receive all parts. To achieve zero communication stalls, we overlap the reduce-scatter of the gradients with the backward pass, which produces them and takes tbwd time. Therefore, the peak required bandwidth is Bdp ≈ 2∣Θ∣b / tbwd.

On ‘psychotic' day of Mavericks big-wave surfing, Kai Lenny flies into another realm

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The surf was so big at Mavericks on Saturday, it was out of the question to grab a conventional surfboard and paddle out to the waves on your own power.

You would get systematically crushed, beaten back without mercy by unrelenting lines of water.

This was strictly a tow-in day, surfers literally towed by Jet Ski from the far outside into an unbroken wave from behind, then letting go of the rope in (ideally) perfect surfing position as it begins to crest.

The approach sounds easy - "like your grandmother could do it," the locals often say. The problem on Saturday was that once you let go of that rope, you were alone on a 50-60-foot face with a nasty, life-threatening element that had some of the world's best surfers backing off.

The 60-foot factor was stunning in itself, comparable to only a few days in the past, many of them unsurfable due to wildly contrary conditions. This wasn't one of those days - except for a prevailing southwest wind that sent 6-foot chops running up the face.

As longtime photographer/water-safety legend Frank Quirarte put it, "If guys could pull off their bottom turns, they had to knife through those waves just to keep from eating s-."

Bianca Valenti understood the crisis all too well. On the biggest wave of her life, almost exactly two years ago at Mavericks, she tried to negotiate a chop and was sent lurching forward into a soul-shattering wipeout. And that was just a single chop on a relatively clean day. Saturday's surf meant veritable mine fields of "southwest saddles," as veteran big-wave rider Steve Dwyer described it. "Coming straight at you from the opposite direction."

Valenti, who has surfed big waves only once over the past two years due to injuries, wisely backed off. "Way too gnarly," she said. "Psychotic. I'm just now rebuilding my confidence, and I was not feeling that."

Kai Lenny rides a wave at Mavericks after flying in from Maui, putting on a show that bordered on the surreal. (Sachi Cunningham / For the S.F. Chronicle)

She was hardly alone. Some of the most decorated big-wave surfers in the world, including Billy Kemper and Justine Dupont, took only a mild stab before concluding it was far too dangerous.

"And these are people who feel they're ready for 100-foot surf," said Grant Washburn, who has been surfing and documenting Mavericks since the early '90s. "But not these waves. They simply decided not to do it. I was surprised how many people actually caught a few waves."

Familiar Mavericks names were charging hard: Peter Mel, Ryan Augenstein, Lucas (Chumbo) Chianca, Jamie Mitchell, JoJo Roper, Alo Slebir and especially El Granada surfer Luca Padua, clearly "the man" among Mavericks regulars.

It's rare to see a "goofyfoot" (right foot forward) tearing up a right-breaking wave at that size, because you give up plenty of vision and perspective with your back to the wave. But Padua trains like a madman with Laird Hamilton, the greatest of all big-wave surfers. And he reminded me of Laird - as in, "you just don't see that" - when he carved one bottom turn into a straight-up rocket to the top, where the impossibly thick lip looked certain to crush him - then snapped that top turn into a straight-down descent that looked too chaotic to possibly handle.

Sailing comfortably into the channel, he broke into a smile.

But here's the crazy part: Luca wasn't the show on Saturday, nor were the series of heroic Jet Ski rescues of wiped-out surfers heading for the inside rocks. It was Kai Lenny, who flew over from Maui and staged a performance so far and above the rest, it bordered on the surreal.

Lenny needs no introduction. At 34 he's unquestionably the greatest big-wave waterman in the world, a master of foiling, kite surfing, windsurfing, stand-up paddling and any-size surfboards, but he loves nothing better than being towed into giant waves at Jaws (his home break), Nazare (Portugal) and Mavericks.

He's been to Half Moon Bay many times, always with distinction, but nothing like the spectacle he unleashed on Saturday. He's a decidedly humble fellow, quick to champion the glory of others, but when a CBS News video crew interviewed him in the water on Monday, he said, "It was really treacherous with big side chop. That's like speed bumps on a racetrack. Fortunately I didn't suffer the consequences like many others did.

"I was out for 10 hours straight and caught 45, 50 waves."

Most everyone felt just terrific about snagging one or two, but 50?

Kai Lenny routinely used the choppy water to launch himself into aerial 360s, landing in ideal position to continue on his way. (Sachi Cunningham / For the S.F. Chronicle)

Incredibly - that word truly applies here - he routinely used that choppy water to launch himself into aerial 360s, landing in ideal position to continue on his way. They were all the same, each as shockingly precise as the last.

"All day long, he did that," Dwyer said. "I could not believe what I was seeing. He was toying with it out there. I was like, who f-ing does this? He is the one percent of the one percent." (For the record, Chianca is also an aerial master in huge surf, but not quite at Lenny's level.)

Valenti spent a good deal of time that day driving a Jet Ski, to watch and help out however she could, "and to have a front-row seat for Kai Lenny," she said, "that was a real pleasure."

"What was cool," said Quirarte, who has long been in charge of water patrol at big-surf venues, "is everybody worked together to make sure the lineup stayed safe. The whitewater went on for days, so somebody could easily have gotten lost. But nobody got missed after a wipeout. No injuries."

It's significant to note that four women were in the water: Valenti, Dupont, Maui's Katie McConnell and Half Moon Bay lifeguard Zoe Chait. None of them had much to do with Saturday, but Chait continued her steady progression into the big-wave elite when clean, 40-foot-plus surf turned up on Monday.

"I towed it super-clean in the morning with (Montara standout) Jason Stark, who's part of my big-wave team with Ion Banner," she said on Wednesday. "A real confidence boost. Then I paddle-surfed it for five hours. Got a couple nice ones, took a couple on the head, just really getting back into the big-wave realm.

"And it was so cool at the end of the day," she said. "Almost everyone was gone, and just me out there with Kai, Luca and a couple others."

Washburn was part of that scene, and he found it essential. About to turn 59, he's quite comfortable taking a pass on days like Saturday, but he's invariably out there to man a Jet Ski, shoot some photos or grab some more typical Mavericks waves.

"I had never seen the bluffs so crowded as Saturday," Washburn said. "Just hundreds of people there to watch. Felt like the Pumpkin Festival, and a little scary, the way chunks of coastal cliffs are falling into the ocean all up and down the coast. We've got a lot of rain coming, so be pretty careful if you want to watch Mavericks from the edge of a bluff. I'd be super nervous standing there.

"But late Monday afternoon felt like long ago at Mavericks. There were like six of us, nobody but the best in the world and us. The weather was funky but the water was so nice (66 degrees, warmer than anyone could recall in a Mavericks season), it felt like we were somewhere else. Then the wind would switch, and the lighting got really beautiful. We always talk about how good we had it back then, but honestly, at the right moment, it's still like that."

The US is exploring whether Ukraine would cede parts of Donetsk province still under its control in return for NATO membership or protection. Experts warn the idea is risky.

The US has asked Ukraine whether it would be willing to cede Donetsk province to Russia in return for NATO membership. Together, Donetsk and Luhansk constitute the so-called Donbas region. Russia currently controls most of Luhansk province, and large swathes of Donetsk, though Ukraine continues holding a number of major cities there, including Kramatorsk and Sloviansk.

So far, no concrete proposal had been put forward, according to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He said he would publicly discuss the matter once the US and NATO member states had agreed on the plan, stressing that he could not make any such decision "on his own." Zelenskyy also said any potential agreement would go ahead "without recognizing Russia's occupation of these territories."

The Ukrainian leader said the US was talking about guarantees similar to those enshrined in Articles 4 and 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty, though did not envision US troops on Ukrainian soil. Article 4 provides for consultations among member states in the event of imminent threats, while Article 5 stipulates that an attack on one state is considered an attack on all. The US, however, has said it pictures bilateral guarantees for Ukraine similar to those established with Israel and certain Middle Eastern countries.

Russia has yet to comment on these proposals, with President Putin's spokesperson Dmitry Peskov stating that "we maintain our position that, in the interest of the peace process, it is not appropriate to disclose details of the talks between us and the Americans."

Experts interviewed by DW are skeptical of the land-for-security-guarantees idea, objecting that the US cannot uphold such an arrangement on its own.

"It seems to me that this is what happens to normal diplomacy when it's been run by real estate agents who are used to selling people something that doesn't really exist," Oleksandr Kraiev of the North America-Israel Research Program at Ukraine's Ukrainian Prism think tank tells DW. He says that at least half of NATO's member states have reservations about Ukraine's accession.

"We'll just end up with a second Budapest Memorandum, it'll just carry a different name," Kraiev told DW. The original 1994 Budapest Memorandum stipulated that Russia, the US and UK respect Ukraine's independence and sovereignty in its existing borders and refrain from the threat or use of force to change its territorial integrity. Ukraine in return committed to giving up all nuclear weapons.

The Budapest Memorandum, however, did not contain any commitment by the UK or US to defend Ukraine in the event of an attack.

Most experts interviewed by DW are critical of the idea of holding a referendum to let ordinary Ukrainians decide whether their country should hand over full control of Donetsk province in exchange for NATO membership.

This is because ceding territory would not only violate Ukraine's constitution and harm its national interests, but would also undermine a world order "in which borders cannot be redrawn by force," Oleksandr Khara of Ukraine's Centre for Defence Strategies told DW. "It doesn't matter whether the citizens of Ukraine vote for or against such a proposal," Khara said, adding that any such referendum would set "a very dangerous precedent."

Report highlights how colleges can make common-sense changes to help students stay on track to graduate.

INDIANAPOLIS, Nov. 18, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Millions of students depend on federal financial aid to afford college, but until now, there has been little national evidence on how many struggle to meet satisfactory academic progress (SAP) requirements and lose access to that aid. A new national evaluation from Lumina F https://www.luminafoundation.org/oundation and Mathematica provides the first comprehensive national look at how SAP requirements are implemented, how many students lose financial aid eligibility, and what colleges can do to better support students.

The report found that 13 percent of undergraduates, or more than 1.8 million students, failed SAP requirements in spring 2025. More than half of those students (54 percent, or approximately 970,000 students) lost eligibility for federal financial aid for the following term.

To maintain federal financial aid eligibility, students must maintain a minimum grade-point averageâtypically at least a C averageâand complete enough courses to graduate within a certain timeframe. The findings show that colleges can do more to help students understand these requirements, stay on track academically, and maintain their financial aid eligibility.

The consequences were especially pronounced at two-year institutions, where 22 percent of students failed SAP, compared with 8 percent at public four-year institutions and 5 percent at private four-year institutions. Financial aid administrators also most often identified first-generation, low-income, and first-year students as groups that struggle to meet SAP requirements, underscoring the need for strong advising and other support systems for student success.

"For the first time, we have a national picture of how many students are losing financial aid eligibility because they aren't meeting satisfactory academic progress requirements," said Ira Nichols-Barrer, principal researcher at Mathematica. "The scale is much larger than previously understood, but colleges have opportunities to help more students maintain their aid and stay enrolled through clearer communication, earlier intervention, and greater awareness of the appeals process."

Appeals can preserve aid eligibility, but many students never apply

Researchers found that only about one-quarter of students who failed SAP submitted an appeal, even though approximately 75 percent of appeals were approved. All participating institutions offered an appeals process, pointing to an opportunity for colleges to better inform students about their options and help them navigate the process.

Colleges have room to strengthen SAP policies and student support

Federal rules set baseline requirements while giving institutions discretion in how they implement SAP. Yet most institutions are not taking advantage of existing flexibility that could help students stay on track, such as gradually increasing GPA and course-completion requirements during students' early years while still meeting federal standards.

Colleges can also provide earlier warnings, simplify the appeals process, and better coordinate academic advising with financial aid offices so students receive consistent guidance.

"Students who fall behind academically need clear information and support to get back on track. Losing financial aid can make that much harder," said Janai Raphael, strategy officer at Lumina. "Colleges have options within the existing rules to help more students keep their aid. That starts with reaching students earlier, explaining their options, and making sure they know how to appeal."

The findings also have implications for Lumina's Goal 2040, which aims for 75 percent of the U.S. labor force to hold a college degree or other credential of value by 2040. Helping more students maintain financial aid eligibility and complete their education is an important part of reaching that goal.

El Salvador's mega-prison has become a model for governments across Latin America seeking to combat organized crime. But experts question whether the approach is effective, sustainable or compatible with human rights.

Around 15,000 inmates and a capacity of up to 40,000, prison sentences of up to 1,000 years, strict security measures and an extreme isolation regime. Staged images show inmates with shaved heads and tattoos being subdued by heavily armed guards.

These are just some of the facts circulating about El Salvador's Terrorism Confinement Center (CECOT), a symbol of President Nayib Bukele's security policy and a possible model for other Latin American countries seeking to combat organized crime.

More than a dozen governments in the region have proposed building similar "mega-prisons," although most would have a far smaller capacity. A range of experts consulted by DW question the effectiveness and sustainability of these initiatives.

De facto mega-prisons — that is, overcrowded prisons with excessive inmate populations — existed in Latin America long before Bukele, says criminologist Jose Luis Perez Guadalupe, former president of the National Penitentiary Institute and former interior minister in Peru. Lurigancho Prison in Lima, for example, designed to hold 3,000 inmates, has housed nearly 11,000 for decades.

But ever since CECOT was launched in 2023, the concept of a "mega-prison" has been primarily linked to maximum-security facilities. For Olga Espinoza, an academic specializing in prison systems at the School of Government of the University of Chile, this association is problematic because it implies that the entire prison population requires "extremely severe" punishment.

According to Espinoza, placing the entire prison population in maximum-security facilities would amount to inefficient and extremely costly "over-intervention." These facilities require substantial investments in infrastructure, technology, specialized staff, anti-corruption measures and social reintegration programs.

It would also be ineffective because "international evidence indicates that when people with different profiles are placed in the same space, there is a high risk of the spread of criminal behavior," the Chilean expert adds.

Several criminal groups across the region even have their roots in overcrowded prisons: Tren de Aragua in Venezuela; Los Lobos, Los Choneros and Los Tiguerones in Ecuador; and Primeiro Comando da Capital (PCC) and Comando Vermelho (CV) in Brazil.

Even when the proposal involves simply building large-scale facilities, investing in a single structure may seem attractive given the difficulties of developing public infrastructure in Latin America.

However, "there is insufficient evidence to support the claim that building mega-prisons is more cost-effective," says Renato Rivera, a senior analyst at the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime (GI-TOC).

The United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners, adopted in 1955, "the recommendation has been to build medium-sized prisons housing between 1,000 and 2,000 inmates rather than mega-prisons," explains Perez Guadalupe, a professor at the Universidad del Pacifico in Lima.

"Mega-prisons, apart from being impersonal, can become extremely dangerous," says Guadalupe.  As an example, the GI-TOC's Rivera highlights the widely reported prison massacres in Ecuador in 2021 that took place in the country's largest and most populated facilities.

Experts acknowledge that mass incarceration has coincided with a dramatic decline in violence in El Salvador and has strengthened the public's perception of safety. Despite reports of arbitrary arrests, abuses and more than 500 deaths in state custody in the country, announcements of new mega-prisons project an image of a strong state confronting organized crime across the region.

However, they warn that this political and symbolic impact does not guarantee sustainable results. Achieving them would require a "clear commitment" to external oversight, human rights protections and social reintegration programs, which governments have yet to demonstrate, Espinoza says.

Perez Guadalupe is particularly critical of the Salvadoran model, describing it as "pure propaganda." He believes the images released from CECOT violate human dignity. He also argues that the state must demonstrate "moral superiority over those who break the law."

Although transnational organized crime represents a growing challenge across the region, the Peruvian criminologist considers El Salvador's case exceptional.

Following the mass arrests carried out under the state of emergency introduced by Bukele, the country has one of the highest incarceration rates in the world. CECOT was designed to permanently detain members of gangs such as MS-13 and Barrio 18, which are considered extremely dangerous.

However, the reality of other Latin American prison systems is different. The question is not where to lock people deprived of their liberty away forever, but rather "how we want them to leave: worse or better," Perez Guadalupe argues, pointing out that most will eventually return to society.

In theory, the official alternative is provided by the UN Nelson Mandela Rules for the treatment of prisoners, updated in 2015. They establish principles for modern prison management, including respect for human dignity, an absolute prohibition on torture and degrading treatment and social reintegration as a central goal of imprisonment.

In practice, Latin America has developed innovative approaches in this direction. These include the APAC model in Brazil, which operates facilities without guards, weapons or violence; Conflict Prevention and Resolution Committees in Argentina; and the Alternative Criminal Justice Center in the Mexican state of Sonora.

Experts also point to the need for maximum-security prisons for specific inmate profiles, but on a smaller scale and with more precise classification of the prison population.

Finally, they emphasize that prisons, including mega-prisons, primarily address the consequences of organized crime rather than its root causes. Poverty, social exclusion and a lack of opportunities require public policies focused on employment, education and social integration to reduce the conditions that enable crime to expand in one of the world's most unequal regions.

This Is the MVP of the Lord of the Rings Trilogy

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Peter Jackson’s Lord of the Rings trilogy has many heroes, as it perfectly captures the spirit of J.R.R. Tolkien’s classic books. Maybe you cheer whenever Aragorn (Viggo Mortensen) walks on screen, or laugh as Gimli (John Rhys-Davies) and Legolas (Orlando Bloom) compare kill counts. One character, however, quietly stands out from the crowd. He may not have any fancy titles or extravagant weapons, but his pure intentions and devotion to leaving a better place mean that Samwise Gamgee (Sean Astin), Frodo’s gardener-turned-companion, becomes the most valuable member of the Middle-earth saga.

Throughout all three movies, he is the unassuming constant of the mission, even if he wasn’t intended to be part of the Fellowship. Sam is Frodo’s (Elijah Wood) gardener and closest friend, who eavesdrops on his conversation with Gandalf (Ian McKellen) about the One Ring’s power. Discovered by the wizard, Sam is ordered to accompany them to Rivendell where Frodo volunteers to take the Ring to Mordor, after which Sam volunteers to accompany him.

From there, on their epic journey across Middle-earth, Sam is Frodo’s protector, nurse, counselor, and friend. As the Fellowship divides into separate groups, the two friends stick together through unimaginable horrors. As Gollum (Andy Serkis) slithers into their lives trying to trick the Ring out of their possession, Sam defends them from his schemes even as Frodo refuses to see the subterfuge. His humility, bravery, and commitment are key to achieving their goal and saving the world.

A former child star transitioning into adult roles, Astin put everything into the performance that would define his career. He had played underdogs before in films like The Goonies and Rudy, but in Sam, he took that spirit to a different level. Keeping a watchful eye over Frodo, his mission is laid out at the closing of The Fellowship of the Ring, where Frodo seeks to head off alone with the Ring. “Go back, Sam. I'm going to Mordor alone,” he says, to which Sam replies: “Of course you are. And I'm coming with you.” Venturing across a deep river despite not being able to swim, he tearfully declares: “I made a promise, Mr. Frodo. A promise. ‘Don't you leave him Samwise Gamgee.’ And I don't mean to. I don't mean to.”

That’s what makes him so special, and so vital to Frodo’s mission. Sam is a good man with a pure heart, and as Frodo slowly loses himself to the seduction of the Ring, his friend remains a lighthouse in the darkness, a reminder of who he is and why he should cling to hope. Even when all seems lost, he reminds his friend: “There's some good in this world, Mr. Frodo. And it's worth fighting for.” Frodo may have to carry the Ring, but in more than one way, Sam carried him.

The theme at the heart of the trilogy is temptation. The rise of Sauron came about because Isildur could not resist the power of the One Ring, and his allies grew from men who craved the influence it promised. The heroes of this story do not seek fame and fortune, preferring a quiet, anonymous life. Aragorn, the rightful king of Gondor, shies away from his destiny, while Gandalf refuses temptation because he is aware of what he would be capable of with the Ring on his finger. Frodo himself doesn’t seek adventure, but puts his body on the line to do what’s right.

If one accepts that the good people in Tolkien’s books are the ones who cannot have their head turned by power, then Sam is the ultimate example of good. The Ring tries to tempt him, but he is one of the few characters who gives it back willingly, because he is not fighting for an empire, or for vengeance, or anything other than friendship. His humble dreams are as large as the garden that he wishes for in Hobbiton, because he is someone who follows his heart, not his desires. He is the greatest hero of them all because he wishes to protect the home and people that he loves, not take it over.

No actor received an Oscar for their performance in the Lord of the Rings trilogy, with only Ian McKellen earning a nomination. Retrospectively, there is an argument that Sean Astin deserved to be the actor to win that accolade for the ultimate supporting performance. He is the heart and soul of Jackson’s fantasy masterpiece, and the more times you watch the films, the more evident that becomes.

Harvard GSD students propose alternatives to Trump's arch

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Images by Jeremy Dvorak and Jake Geitner courtesy The Cultural Landscape Foundation

Landscape architecture students at Harvard’s Graduate School of Design have designed two proposals for Memorial Circle in Washington, DC, the site of US president Donald Trump‘s proposed triumphal arch.

Both designs feature expansive pools of water that cover the current vehicular roundabout at Memorial Circle, and emphasise connectivity to the city’s historic processional axis and straight sightlines to the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington Cemetery.

The students, Jeremy Dvorak and Jake Geitner, worked with Washington DC non profit organisation The Cultural Landscape Foundation (TCLF) on the designs. The aim was to engage the public in the design process, as the team views the government did not properly open the arch up for review.

“By recognising Memorial Circle’s implicit expressions of monumentality, this pair of counter proposals asserts design’s capacity to critically engage history, accommodate public life, and advocate on behalf of our shared cultural landscape,” said Dvorak and Geitner.

The project began after Dvork and Geitner attended a seminar at the GSD taught by TCLF founding partner Charles A Birnbaum.

“As graduate students in landscape architecture, we took Birnbaum’s course to ground our emerging practices in critical historical inquiry and public participation,” they said.

“The current administration’s assertion of authority over national memory presented an opportunity to put these convictions into practice.”

The first proposal treats Memorial Circle as “a void along the historic axis between Washington and Arlington”. The designers chose to fill the central roundabout circle, currently covered by grass, with a low-lying pool of water.

A natural stone perimeter surrounds the pool and is inscribed with words of “belonging and unity”.

It’s design is “restrained” according to the team, and primarily meant to be viewed from a vehicle while passing through the traffic circle.

The second proposal does away with the traffic circle entirely and instead inserts a straight thoroughfare to Arlington Memorial Bridge.

Two circular fountains would be located on either side of the road. Additionally, a large pool of water, mirroring the perimeter of the former traffic circle, would fill up the site during commemorative events when the bridge is closed.

It draws upon previous historical proposals for Memorial Circle and seeks to pay homage to the precedent of the site.

“[The fountain] geometries arise from the site’s historic proposals, combining concentric circles, intersecting alignments, and formal planting into a composition that acknowledges history whilst creating its own present identity,” said the designers.

“In this way the landscape becomes a physical record of its own change.”

Trump’s proposed arch was announced late last year with renderings by US studio Harrison Design. It features gilded statues and a neoclassical design and has been dubbed the “Arc de Trump” – a play on the Arc de Triomphe in Paris.

Construction on the arch was set to begin in September with the government barreling ahead, but it was halted in early November by Judge Tanya S Chutkan.

Trump has claimed the arch will serve as a “military complex” with snipers in an attempt to use national security as a pretext for construction.

Many take issue with the arch blocking views to adjacent Arlington Cemetery, including members of the Kennedy family, who filed a lawsuit to block construction, claiming that the arch would cast shadows over the grave of president John F Kennedy.

The US Commission of Fine Arts (CFA), which reviews projects in the capital city, approved the project in May.

The proposals also follow recent efforts by the legislature to change Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act, which requires federal agencies to consider the impact of proposed projects on historic properties.

Earlier in November, protesters gathered in Washington DC in opposition of the administration’s proposed rewrite of these protections. They are couched within the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) bill recently introduced to Congress.

WoW: Forever Exploit Lands Streamers In Hot Water After Farming Thousands Of Gold

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A lucrative World of Warcraft: Forever dungeon-farming exploit that netted some players hundreds of gold per hour has been hotfixed, and Blizzard is taking action against accounts that (temporarily) walked away with thousands in loot.

For the dedicated few who have already reached max level in the new version of Blizzard's MMORPG, a new endgame dungeon, Blackmaw Hold, proved to be quite lucrative. In the dungeon, a room with 30 non-elite enemies proved to be the perfect spot for area-of-effect gold farming, since it's easy to access.

As detailed by Wowhead, the farm was as simple as running to the room in question, killing all the mobs, looting them, resetting the dungeon, and repeating. Not only did the enemies drop anywhere from 25 to 50 silver each, but they also dropped items that could be sold either to a vendor or on the auction house for even more profit.

And some players, including prominent streamers, did just that during Forever's second weekend. One streamer, Ziqo, who was previously in hot water for accepting illegal gold during the game's beta, revealed he made 4,000 gold from the farm. On stream, Ziqo named other streamers who did the same and said his only regret was choosing to sleep for four hours instead of farming longer.

On places like the WoW: Forever subreddit, players worried about the effect such a huge amount of questionably obtained gold would have on the MMO's economy, especially since the game has been out for less than two weeks. Many called on Blizzard to take action against accounts, including streamers, who benefited from the farm.

Blizzard issued a hotfix on November 16, making the enemies from Blackmaw Hold no longer drop loot. In a forum post the following day, Blizzard explained what went wrong and why it was taking action. While Blizzard said finding good farming spots is part of old-school WoW, the enemies players were farming in Blackmaw Hold were incorrectly dropping loot that would normally be reserved for elites, an obvious mistake.

Players who ran the dungeon a few times and "benefited organically from the extra loot did nothing wrong" and don't need to worry, Blizzard said. However, a few "hundred players worldwide" spent "significant time exclusively farming that group of enemies."

Those players are now in more serious trouble. Blizzard locked their ability to engage in Forever's wider economy following the exploit, and said it is removing gold they earned through "exploitative gains." Additionally, "significant abusers" of the exploit will be handed account suspensions.

"As always, we advise all players to refrain from abusing game mechanics that are clearly unintended," Blizzard said. "We will continue to take action against intentional abuse."

The Blackmaw Hold farm wasn't the first gold controversy in Forever, and it certainly won't be the last. During the game's beta, streamers were caught accepting suspicious amounts of gold and encouraging their communities to illegally buy gold on their behalf. As a result, Blizzard issued suspensions, made in-game changes, and made it clear it would crack down not only on accounts selling illegal gold, but also on those buying or accepting it.

Farming exploits aside, Forever's launch has been relatively smooth, though Blizzard can't seem to decide how much experience players should get in dungeons. Despite Forever's launch breaking WoW concurrent-player records, it still wasn't enough to get the MMO nominated for Best Ongoing Game at The Game Awards 2026.

South Africa’s Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment (DFFE) intercepted the trees on July 17 after their exporter, Pretoria-based Divine Landscapes, could not produce licenses to remove, transport, or export them.

Officials have not named the buyer. But reporting by Daily Maverick traced the trail to the Shekvetili Dendrological Park, a private arboretum on Georgia’s coast founded by billionaire Bidzina Ivanishvili, the country’s former prime minister and the founder of its ruling Georgian Dream party. Ivanishvili has not commented, and Daily Maverick said requests to his lawyer and to Georgia’s embassy in Pretoria went unanswered.

The case tests how far South Africa will go to keep a protected species from becoming a collectible for wealthy buyers abroad. It also left authorities with a practical problem: keeping living evidence alive while a case moves through the courts.

Environmental journalist Tony Carnie got an anonymous tip about the trees, photographed them through a fence at the Africa Global Logistics warehouse in Durban’s Mobeni industrial area, and reported the seizure in Daily Maverick in October. AGL told the publication it only provided logistics services and neither owned nor exported the cargo.

Every baobab in South Africa is a specially protected species under the National Forests Act, which bars anyone from cutting, disturbing, removing, transporting, or exporting one without a license. The DFFE says the five trees were dug up in the Lephalale area of Limpopo province without a provincial permit. It refused Divine Landscapes’ late license application at the end of July and dismissed the company’s appeal on Oct. 27.

The company denies wrongdoing. Its attorneys told Daily Maverick the trees were “obtained from private gardens with the full consent of the respective owners” and said it is seeking judicial review of the appeal decision. The company’s owner appeared in Durban Magistrates’ Court in September, and the case was postponed to January pending further investigation.

Ivanishvili, whose party critics accuse of tilting Georgia toward Moscow, has spent years moving giant trees to Shekvetili. Eight baobabs uprooted on Kenya’s Kilifi coast were bound for the park when Kenyan President William Ruto halted their export and ordered an investigation. The Kenya Forest Service later authorized the shipment, and the trees reached Georgia in 2023.

All eight died. In August 2024, the park confirmed the deaths and blamed delays it attributed to Ivanishvili’s political opponents.

“We also did not want to see a repeat of what happened in Kenya,” Dr. Sarah Venter, an ecologist and founder of the Baobab Foundation, told Daily Maverick.

The seizure comes as South Africa presses for tougher international enforcement. On Oct. 23, parties to the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime, meeting in Vienna, voted 83 to 12, with 27 abstentions, to begin negotiating a new protocol on crimes that affect the environment, including trafficking in wild flora and fauna. South Africa co-sponsored the resolution.

“Environmental crime is organized crime,” Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment Minister David Maynier said in a DFFE statement. Once negotiated, the protocol could give countries shared tools for cross-border investigations, extradition, and recovering criminal proceeds. Trafficking networks are already expanding their reach, as a U.N. report on the global illicit economy found this year, and wealthy collectors’ appetite for rare species is under scrutiny elsewhere, including in India’s trade in trafficked orangutans.

Seized cargo can usually wait in storage. These trees could not. “They’ve been lying on their sides. Their branches and their roots have been trimmed, well hacked, so they’re not in a very vertical position. They’re not in soil. They’re in full sun. There’s no water,” Carnie said in a Daily Maverick video interview.

“The really critical thing is to get them back into the soil as soon as possible to allow them to recover and regenerate, because they are using up stored stem water to survive,” Venter said. A healthy baobab can be up to 75% water.

Timbavati Private Nature Reserve, which straddles the Mpumalanga and Limpopo provinces, offered to cover loading, transport, and replanting, and Mark Read of the Everard Read Group found an unnamed private funder within 10 minutes of Venter’s call, Daily Maverick reported. T-19A Kestrel: Meet The US Air Force Academy's New Unlimited Aerobatic Aircraft For Cadets

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

On November 12, four new composite planes swept low over the Cadet Wing assembled at the US Air Force Academy in Colorado. The flight marked the official arrival of the T-19A Kestrel light trainer at Davis Airfield in Colorado Springs. Instead of wading through decades of defense bureaucracy and drawn-out congressional appropriations, USAFA secured its next-generation aerobatic aircraft in just 10 months, cutting through red tape with the help of a private donor group led by Andrew McKenna.

The T-19A is a derivative of the commercial GB1 GameBird. By skipping the traditional procurement, leadership bypassed the budget overruns and timeline slips that have paralyzed programs like the Boeing T-7A Red Hawk. The lightweight plane is purpose-built for agility thanks to its designer, German aerobatic champion Philipp Steinbach. He is a co-founder of Game Aerospace Composites in Bentonville, Arkansas, which produces the off-the-shelf civilian variant certified to pull a staggering load of ten G-forces.

The Kestrel went from concept to delivery in 10 months using $3 million in private donations, whereas the Red Hawk has spent over a decade in development using billions in taxpayer funds and still faces multi-year delays. The T-19A will fill a major gap in modern USAFA flight training by giving cadets early exposure to high-G, unlimited aerobatics and complex energy management before they ever step into Undergraduate Pilot Training.

The T-19A, or GB1 GameBird, is the only American-built two-seat aircraft approved for Unlimited-category competition aerobatics. Its all-carbon monocoque airframe lacks traditional mechanical wing-attach joints, relying on a continuous composite spar cured inside an autoclave. This gives the plane an operating empty weight of roughly 1,290 pounds (585 kg) while enabling symmetric flight loads of up to 10-G at its aerobatic maximum limit.

Under the hood, a 583-cubic-inch Lycoming AEIO-580-B1A flat-six piston engine delivers 303 horsepower (226 kW) to a four-blade composite MT-Propeller system. An inverted oil and fuel system keeps the engine running under sustained negative-g flight and zero-g push-overs. Cadets command the airframe from tandem seats equipped with Garmin G3X Touch glass displays, combining edge-of-envelope stick-and-rudder forces with modern electronic flight screens before stepping into standard turboprops.

To make the transition, USAFA pulled six active-duty instructor pilots from the 306th Flying Training Group to stand up the first training cadre. These officers will shape a new syllabus to train cadets and establish a demonstration and competition team. The initiative comes as the wider military struggles with a persistent shortfall of roughly 2,000 operational pilots, forcing leadership to seek shortcuts to build stick-and-rudder skills earlier in pilot development.

The Academy's existing fleet jumps from unpowered TG-16A gliders and basic T-53A/T-41D piston trainers straight to the UPT's T-6A Texan II turboprops after graduation. USAF Chief of Staff General Ken Wilsbach and Academy Superintendent Lt. Gen. Paul Moga secured the four airframes thanks to a $3 million donation coordinated by philanthropist Andrew McKenna. Meanwhile, the USAFA paid $400,000 in operational funds for initial tooling, spares, and flight gear, along with a projected annual operating budget of $1 million to maintain the fleet.

The Kestrel will act as an intermediate high-performance step, teaching cadets how to manage high power-to-weight ratios, rapid throttle response, and aggressive energy management. Standard trainers are built for stability and prohibit extreme maneuvers. Because the Kestrel is certified for unlimited aerobatics, instructors can deliberately put cadets into inverted spins, vertical stalls, and disorienting attitudes, teaching them instinctive recovery skills that directly prevent real-world loss-of-control accidents.

The Air Force is facing a persistent pilot deficit and needs to push students through UPT faster without lowering standards. By giving top cadets advanced stick-and-rudder proficiency during their junior and senior years at the Academy, they arrive at UPT needing fewer remedial rides and moving through the syllabus faster. Additionally, building an aerobatic competition team around the Kestrel will push the selected cadets to fly under public scrutiny with the precision and discipline they will further refine in operational flying squadrons.

Operating the Kestrel at the USAFA in Colorado Springs comes with real performance trade-offs. The Academy's Davis Airfield sits at an elevation of 6,572 feet (2,003 meters), and summer heat often makes the air feel like it is over 9,000 feet (2,743 meters). That means the T-19A's non-turbocharged engine loses roughly 22% to 26% of its power. That thinner mountain air requires longer takeoff runs and leaves pilots with less excess engine power during demanding maneuvers.

Weight limits create another hurdle for daily operations, as the Kestrel can weigh up to 2,200 pounds (998 kg) for standard point-to-point flights. Its maximum takeoff rating for extreme 10-G aerobatics only applies at 1,940 pounds (880 kg) or less. Subtracting the plane's 1,290-pound (585 kg) empty weight leaves just 650 pounds (295 kg) for people, gear, and fuel. Once an instructor and a cadet strap in, only enough weight allowance remains for 25 to 30 gallons (95 to 114 liters) of fuel.

That limits intense flights to roughly 45 minutes, requiring ground crews to refuel and turn the planes around quickly between sorties. Moving cadets from unpowered gliders into a 200-knot (370 km/h) airplane is also a major operational adjustment. Because rapid spins and heavy G-forces can easily cause motion sickness or make new pilots lose track of which way is up, instructors must design a step-by-step program that gradually introduces cadets to the plane's capabilities.

Built entirely from carbon fiber, the T-19A brings modern composite design to a general aviation field long dominated by traditional steel-tube frames. Even compared to the newer carbon-fiber Extra NG, the Kestrel weighs about 110 pounds (50 kg) less at an empty weight of 1,290 pounds (585 kg). Its wing and fuselage tanks also hold 81 gallons (307 liters) of fuel, giving it a cross-country range of over 1,000 nautical miles (1,852 kilometers).

In the air, the Kestrel reacts far faster than almost all other military trainers. The USAF's primary turboprop trainer, the Beechcraft T-6A Texan II, rolls at 180 degrees per second and handles forces between positive 7.0-G and negative 3.5-G. The Kestrel more than doubles that agility, rolling at 400 degrees per second while withstanding up to positive and negative 10-G.

Unlike older aerobatic planes, the Kestrel avoids the sluggish handling caused by a heavy nose-mounted radial engine. Its horizontally opposed Lycoming responds instantly, without the twisting-torque side effects of a radial engine during aggressive maneuvers. That instant throttle response also translates into blistering acceleration and vertical climb performance. Its power-to-weight ratio allows the Kestrel to take off in just 680 feet (207 meters) and climb at 4,200 feet per minute (1,280 meters per minute).

This sharp handling marks a major departure from the Academy’s existing T-53As, which are militarized Cirrus SR20s built to train for instrument flying rather than agility and dogfighting. The Kestrel bridges that gap, letting cadets practice inverted flight, vertical spins, and high-G recovery before they arrive at active-duty pilot training bases to begin the pipeline to the cockpit of a warplane.

The T-19A's performance derives from its lightweight carbon-fiber fuselage built around a single, continuous spar that runs straight through the cabin floor. By dropping heavy metal wing joints, Game Aerospace saved weight while also making the 25.26-foot (7.70-meter) span wings strong enough to withstand extreme loads up to 15-G.

Up front, the engine spins a 74.8-inch (1,900 mm) German-made MT-Propeller built from a compressed wood core wrapped in a tough composite shell with stainless-steel leading edges. Because standard engines lose oil pressure when flying upside down, the Kestrel uses a specialized inverted oil system that keeps oil flowing smoothly between 55 and 95 psi (3.8 and 6.5 bar) at temperatures up to 245°F (118°C), even during violent tumbling maneuvers.

Instead of flexible steel cables that can stretch under heavy loads, the pilot's control stick connects to the wings and tail via rigid metal pushrods mounted on sealed bearings for instant, slop-free response. Small aerodynamic paddles beneath the wings help balance the large carbon ailerons so the stick stays light at speeds up to 175 knots (324 km/h), while tiny fins along the wing surface keep airflow smooth and prevent sudden wing stalls down to 60 knots (111 km/h).

5 Legendary Rock Bands With Only One Perfect Album, Ranked

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It can take years for a rock band to finally get radio play, but for a lucky few exceptionally talented acts, all it takes is one album to make a lasting impression. It's not every day that a band comes together, puts out a single project, and then leaves it all behind. Whether it's a side project, an inability to maintain the same chemistry beyond the debut, or simply a matter of unfortunate circumstances, there's something fascinating about bands with only one album to their name.

Arguably, a one-album discography can represent some of the most honest work in a band's career, seizing its artistry at its rawest. There's also the pressure of making that one record count, leaving listeners with something truly memorable. Without further ado, here are five of the greatest rock bands with only one perfect album, ranked.

Following the massive MTV success of Enema of the State and Take Off Your Pants and Jacket, it was only a matter of time before fans (and their record label) expected Blink-182 to follow up with another hit-making album built around their tried-and-true three-power-chord formula. But for members Tom DeLonge and Travis Barker, they needed an outlet to explore heavier sounds and darker subject matter—something that contrasted with Blink-182's rambunctious energy, immature antics, and crass humor. Enter their side project, Box Car Racer. Originally conceived as a creative outlet during a break from touring, the short-lived project gave DeLonge and Barker room to branch out musically and experiment with a different sound, something they would continue exploring in later Blink-182 releases and with DeLonge’s future band, Angels & Airwaves.

Inspired by DeLonge's interest in post-hardcore bands like Fugazi, Refused, and Quicksand, Box Car Racer ditches the fast, chord-driven riffs he was accustomed to playing in favor of heavier guitar textures, distorted riffs, and drop-D tuning—all channeled into their self-titled album. While traces of his pop-punk roots remain unmistakable, the band isn't chasing catchy hooks designed to dominate radio airplay. Instead, explores darker themes ranging from apocalyptic destruction to Freemasonry—definitely a far cry from singing about teenage break ups and personal insecurities.

The Los Angeles hardcore punk scene owes a lot to The Germs. Their original run may have lasted only four years in the late '70s, but the band helped lay the groundwork for the scene, leaving an influence that would carry on for decades. The brainchild of lead vocalist Darby Crash and guitarist Pat Smear (who would later play with Nirvana and the Foo Fighters), The Germs drew inspiration from a surprisingly wide range of artists, including Iggy Pop, The Ramones, and The Sex Pistols. Their only studio album, GI, is regarded as one of the earliest full-length hardcore punk records and is notably produced by Joan Jett of The Runaways.

GI proves that hardcore's unrestrained chaos doesn't have to come at the expense of structure or artistic expression. Rather than reducing the genre to theatrical antics and one-minute bursts of noise with barely intelligible lyrics, The Germs showed that hardcore could be just as expressive and musically thoughtful as any other genre. Hardcore punk may have been at the heart of their sound, but it was their frenetic aggression, both on record and during notoriously chaotic live performances that sometimes got them kicked offstage, that cemented their status as legends of the first generation of Los Angeles punk bands.

Their time in the industry might be short-lived, but The La's got the world singing in karaoke thanks to their hit track "There She Goes," which has become an anthem for many romantic comedies. For a bunch of Brit lads making jolly, Merseybeat sing-along tunes à la The Kinks, The Who, and The Rolling Stones, The La's were surprisingly perfectionistic. This not only became a huge obstacle during the production of their debut album but also put a strain on the band's relationships, eventually contributing to their breakup. It's quite a contrast for a band whose repertoire sounds so raw and organic to have gone through a recording process filled with rejected takes, endless re-recordings, and rotating producers.

While The La's weren't happy with their self-titled debut album (which took a total of three years to record), its sound became a blueprint for Britpop, thanks to its emphasis on guitar-driven music and 1960s British rock influences. The album's legacy can still be heard in the music of Oasis, Arctic Monkeys, and Fontaines D.C. While it's a shame The La's never released another studio album, they at least had the chance to create something that would stand the test of time. The recording process may have been a pain, but the fruits of their labor are unmistakable.

Them Crooked Vultures takes the term "supergroup" to another level, bringing together a trio that's a match made in rock heaven: Josh Homme of Queens of the Stone Age, John Paul Jones of Led Zeppelin, and Dave Grohl of Nirvana and the Foo Fighters. Their sound feels like a more laid-back take on Homme's work with his own band, but with a catchier, more modern rock edge. The project took years to come together, with the trio making its debut in 2009. All three musicians bring their own playing styles to the table, but somehow, everything clicks.

Their self-titled album is a real treat for rock fans, especially when you consider the heavyweight talent packed into this tight trio. There's no shortage of heavy guitar riffs, but the album isn't all about loud guitars and flashy musicianship. It also throws in funk influences, piano arrangements, and plenty of catchy vocal hooks from Homme. There's a little bit of everything here, and that's what makes the record such a familiar yet fresh listen. Rather than sounding like three rock legends simply showing off what they can do, Them Crooked Vultures brings their individual strengths together without the need to overpower each other.

Britain's biggest troublemakers have long sparked debate over whether they're true punk pioneers or just cynical posers who were more interested in the lifestyle than the music itself. It doesn't help that bassist Sid Vicious reportedly could barely play his own instrument, with Steve Jones having to step in and re-record the bass tracks. Still, whether you buy into their authenticity or not, there's no denying that the Sex Pistols left a lasting mark on anti-establishment culture. Their rebellious attitude was a sharp contrast to Britain's prim and proper image, particularly the kind of regal etiquette expected in a society still shaped by its monarchy.

Their only studio album, Never Mind the Bollocks, Here's the Sex Pistols, is pretty much the epitome of their bad-boy attitude. Dropped by record labels, plastered across tabloids, and met with concert cancellations, the album attracted plenty of public scrutiny for its outrageous content. Few songs were as controversial as "God Save the Queen" and "Anarchy in the U.K.," both of which captured the spirit of punk at its most rebellious. The album was packed with political undertones, and at a time when censorship was still rife in an uptight British society, the Sex Pistols came in swinging with a sound that refused to follow the rules and lyrics that could give the royal family a headache.

Plants mix different breeding strategies to survive in a world with changing climates and fewer bees

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Simulations show plants that reject their own pollen and plants that self-fertilize can coexist for ages, swapping dominance without settling.

Apple trees have a strict biological rule for reproduction. They reject their own pollen. That means they need a completely different type of apple tree nearby to produce fruit.

While many wild plants rely on this exact same partner system, plenty of other plant species have evolved to drop the rule entirely, allowing them to successfully fertilize themselves.

A population holding both kinds was supposed to be passing through on its way to choosing one rule or the other.

In computer simulations of evolving plants, the mixed populations didn’t get there. With nothing in the environment changing, the two kinds kept trading dominance for as long as the simulations ran. Neither kind took over.

The researchers call it a volatile mixed mode. A mixed population in the wild that looks unstable, they wrote, may be in a lasting state of its own.

Tamar Friedlander, a senior lecturer at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem’s Robert H. Smith Institute of Plant Sciences and Genetics in Agriculture, led the work with Amit Jangid.

Ohad Noy Feldheim of the University’s Einstein Institute of Mathematics is the third author. Everything here happened inside a model.

In an interview with Earth.com, Friedlander said the result broke a habit of thought. Biologists expect that when a population has several options, some are better than others, she said.

Over the long run, the population is supposed to reach the better one and stay there. Earlier models had the mix fading away or holding steady at fixed proportions.

“Thus, it was very surprising that we didn’t get either of these under a large parameter regime,” she said.

The populations, in her words, “not only did not settle to either of the ‘pure’ strategies (only self-fertilization or only outcrossing) but also did not settle at fixed proportions of these strategies.”

The rejection system works like a lock and a set of keys. The female side of a flower makes a toxin that stops a pollen tube from growing. Each pollen grain carries proteins that disarm the toxins of other mating types but not its own.

About 40 percent of plant families use some version of this, including wild tomatoes, potatoes, apples, pears, citrus, and coffee. Washington State University‘s tree fruit site has a primer on why orchards need two varieties.

The system can break. A mutation that silences the toxin, or adds a key that fits the plant’s own lock, produces a plant that can fertilize itself.

That plant gains a sure route to seeds when pollen is scarce, but it passes on two sets of genes to each seed instead of one.

It pays for that with inbreeding. Seeds from self-fertilization carry more harmful mutations, and more of them fail.

The plants that reject their own pollen pay a different price. Their pollen is useless on every plant of their own mating type, so a type that grows common finds fewer partners.

Friedlander’s team simulated populations of 500 plants for 100,000 generations, discarding the first 50,000 as a warm-up.

Each plant carried two copies of the gene set, and each copy held one toxin and nine keys. Every protein was written as a string of 18 amino acids that could mutate.

Two dials set the outcome. One was how badly self-fertilized seeds suffer from inbreeding. The other was how loosely the keys fit the locks, which decides how many mating types a population can hold.

With heavy inbreeding costs and loose-fitting keys, every plant ended up rejecting its own pollen. With light costs, every plant ended up self-fertilizing.

In between lay the third state, where the share of self-rejecting plants rose and fell for the rest of the run and the number of mating types swung with it.

Types tended to vanish several at a time, in what the authors describe as avalanches. What triggers those collapses, they wrote, is still obscure.

A shortage of pollen was expected to push populations toward self-fertilization, since a plant that can pollinate itself doesn’t need a visitor. The model found the opposite.

When cross-pollen was scarce, the range of settings in which self-rejection won grew at the expense of the mixed state. Friedlander said the explanation lies in how the two sexes compete.

When pollen is plentiful, every female is sure of a partner and only the males compete, so female proteins evolve mainly to avoid matching their own pollen.

When pollen is scarce, females must compete too, which makes them easier to match and raises the number of mating types a population can support.

Researchers often treat self-fertilization as a plant’s backup plan for when cross-pollination fails, she told Earth.com. Self-fertilizing plants do turn up at the edges of a species’ range, where partners are few.

The move from rejecting self-pollen to accepting it has long been treated as a one-way street. A plant that gives up the rule usually sheds the flower traits that attract insects and purges some of its harmful mutations.

Across much of the model’s settings, though, the end state didn’t depend on the start, and a population that began self-fertilizing could evolve the rejection system from scratch.

The exception was the loosest-fitting keys, where the mutation needed for the reverse trip was so rare that the wait became impossibly long.

The model also held the cost of inbreeding fixed, so it describes the short term, before prolonged self-fertilization purges enough mutations to change the odds.

Mixed populations exist in the wild. Long-term studies that track one population’s proportions over time are lacking, the authors wrote, and those are what would test the prediction.

The model also predicts that mixed populations carry fewer and shorter-lived mating types than purely self-rejecting ones, and more inbreeding written into their genomes. Both could be checked in plant genomes.

Asked which population she would most like to watch, Friedlander pointed to wild tomato species growing in South America.

She also said the model doesn’t apply directly to orchards, where trees are genetically identical and do not evolve. Self-rejecting crops do depend on pollinators, she added, and a decline in them could cut yields.

A warming climate and fewer pollinating insects both change the pressures on how a plant reproduces, the authors wrote.

Whether real populations respond by picking a side, or by swinging between the two for generations, is what a long field study of those wild tomatoes could begin to answer.

The full study was published in the journal PRX Life.
