Magnus Ditlev Sets New Iron-Distance World Best With 7:19:17 at Ironman Florida

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Magnus Ditlev set the fastest-ever Iron-distance time at the 2026 Ironman Florida (Photo by Sean M. Haffey/Getty Images for IRONMAN) (Photo: Getty Images for IRONMAN)

One month after a heartbreaking DNF in Kona, Magnus Ditlev (DEN) rode away from the field at Ironman Florida and never looked back. His 7:19:17 took 1:47 off the previous men’s iron-distance world best that Sam Laidlow (FRA) set at Challenge Roth earlier this year.

Florida was a chance at redemption for Ditlev, who had come agonizingly close to winning the Ironman World Championship in Kona. He’d taken the lead late on the bike and looked on course for the win until he abruptly came to a stop on the run with roughly 30 minutes of racing left.

However, ahead of Kona, Ditlev said he was putting up career-best numbers across the swim, bike, and run at his training camp in Colorado. In Florida, he finally got to reap the rewards of what fitness could produce all the way to the finish line.

Ditlev came out of the water in fifth, right on the heels of Ben Kanute (USA) and Rudy von Berg (USA). He took his time in T1 and started the bike nearly a minute down, but it didn’t take him long to ride back to the front. And then, just past mile 10, he made his move. No one could go with him, and they wouldn’t be able to stay remotely close to him either.

By the time Ditlev came off the bike, he was 11 minutes clear of a chase group that included Sam Long (USA), von Berg, and Kanute. His 3:51:16 was the fastest bike split ever recorded at an Ironman, nearly two minutes quicker than the 3:53:12 Robert Kallin (SWE) rode at Ironman Kalmar in August.

Off the bike, he once again took his time in transition. But transitions were the only slow part of Ditlev’s day. Once through T2, he picked up another minute on the chasers over the opening 10 miles. He hit halfway in 1:15, on pace for a 2:30 marathon and well within reach of Laidlow’s 7:21:04. With such an unassailable lead, one can assume he must’ve known the record was within reach to keep such an aggressive pace.

Ditlev eventually closed with a 2:33:31 marathon, crossing the line in 7:19:17. Von Berg took second, 14 minutes back, securing the early Kona slot and podium he’d come for. Matt Hanson (USA), meanwhile, ran a remarkable 2:28:07 to beat Long to the final podium spot, but still finished 16 minutes behind Ditlev.

With confidence, it turns out, Delap is a very different player.

Guéhi’s form, meanwhile, is becoming a major concern for City. With Dias wary of Xaver Schlager running to his right and potentially becoming a passing option, it was surely Guéhi’s responsibility to close Delap down. 5 Most Ambitious LitRPG Books of All Time, Ranked by Goodreads

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Thanks to Matt Dinniman's Dungeon Crawler Carl becoming a legitimate phenomenon around the globe, a lot of casual and genre readers are starting to latch onto the massive worlds of LitRPG. Essentially taking a role-playing, progression type of game and bringing it into a literary form, LitRPG has become one of the hottest genres around. He Who Fights With Monsters has started to pick up a ton of steam in the wake of Dungeon Crawler Carl's success, and may very well be the next to get a screen adaptation. But there are so many LitRPG books and series out there to explore, and a lot of them are nothing like the ones you've already heard about or read.

There are some wildly ambitious LitRPG novels out there, each bringing something bold and new to a space that is doing nothing but expanding and evolving by the minute. Some show off what a story like Dungeon Crawler Carl would look like if the protagonist had their children with them for the dangerous event. Others take readers to remote farms on faraway planets as protagonists try and live peaceful lives. There's a genuinely great LitRPG series where the main character is literally a tree.

LitRPG writers have ambition in spades, delivering some awesome stories to check out that you may not be too familiar with. Below, we've picked out five of the genre's most ambitious, and ranked them according to the rating on Goodreads.

This eight-book Isekai adventure from author SpaizZzer just concluded earlier this fall, and it has stood for nearly a decade as one of the most unique stories in the LitRPG space. If you're not familiar, the main character of the series is a tree, and that premise was turned into eight very popular, highly loved books, which feels like a miracle in and of itself.

The first book of the series begins with a man named Matt being reborn into a fantasy world, which is the usual start to many LitRPG and/or Isekai stories. However, when Matt is reborn, he comes back as a sentient tree, unable to move from his spot. He quickly realizes there is more to his situation meets the eye, as the tree becomes the central battleground in a demon incursion, and Matt (aka TreeTree) needs to use his evolving powers to aid the heroes that find themselves beneath his branches.

So many LitRPG stories focus on adults in situations where they become players in some sort of dangerous adventure or death game, forcing them to level up and adapt to save themselves, and potentially their entire worlds. Erin Ampersand's Apocalypse Parenting series makes that entire concept devastatingly personal as it puts a regular mom in the midst of battle — right alongside her three young children.

The name really says it all. Apocalypse Parenting flips the script on the genre by exploring what it would be like to lead your own kids through the kind of crazy, other-worldly scenarios that are common in Dungeon Crawler Carl and countless other LitRPG novels. It makes for not only an emotional journey, but also one that makes you think a lot about the logistics that might plague most characters you encounter in stories like this.

The Wandering Inn is one of the biggest names in the world of LitRPG, and it has a little bit of everything. The story of Erin Solstice has some slice-of-life vibes to it, as she creates a safe harbor for creatures of all kinds, but it's also filled with a ton of great fantasy elements along the way. It checks so many boxes, which is ambitious in its own right, but it's the sheer size of The Wandering Inn that has it on this list.

The Wandering Inn is one of the longest written works in history, with 20 individual installments released over the last eight years. And those individual novels are often so long that the print versions, which finally started seeing the light of day this year, are actually splitting the books into multiple parts. For example, the original Wandering Inn book was made into two print novels consisting of 640 pages and 784 pages, respectively. It takes a lot to make a story interesting for this long, but Pirateaba has done just that.

There are a lot of slice-of-life LitRPG books out there, focusing less on world-ending battles or death games, and more on level systems can bring to the "slower" aspects of living. Seth Ring's Battle Mage Farmer series is the best of both worlds, delivering on the slice-of-life story without abandoning the bigger tales and stakes of the genre.

John Sutton's tale of trying to build a quiet life as a farmer on the outskirts of an empire comes as he tries his best to avoid the war and devastation all around him. He's at conflict from the jump, wanting nothing more than a simple existence but knowing that he's destined for a lot more. It's a brutally relatable conundrum he finds himself in, and it makes Battle Mage Farmer one of the more intriguing LitRPG series out there.

Speaking of warriors living out their days on hopefully peaceful farmland, Beware of Chicken is a layered story that takes a different approach to nearly every kind of tale you'll find in LitRPGs. It's also not at all what many are expecting from progression fantasy, but that's a big reason why it stands out.

The book begins with the protagonist waking up not only in another world, but in the body of a celestially powerful martial arts master. Instead of taking up the conflict that his former body host was a part of, the new Jin decides he'd rather take his powers home and start a farm. It's relaxing, hilarious, and constantly bubbling over with the threat of life outside the homestead.

So far, there are five books in the Beware of Chicken series (the sixth arriving early next year), and the 4.45 Goodreads rating on the first installment is the lowest of the entire series.

Ultron Confirmed Who Marvel's Strongest Avenger Is, But Fans Won't Like The Answer

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Ultron settles the debate and reveals which of the Avengers is truly the most powerful. The debate over who deserves the title of the strongest Avenger has fueled decades of intense arguments in and out of universe. Strength in the Marvel Universe is rarely a simple measurement of how much weight a hero can lift, after all. The answers vary drastically depending on the battlefield, the status of each hero's power, and whether weapons and energy-based power can be weighed equally against sheer physical strength. Scarlet Witch can neutralize Thor using her magic, but Swordsman can technically take down Scarlet Witch if he catches her with her guard down.

Marvel Comics and the Marvel Cinematic Universe have presented several conflicting answers to this question. Wanda Maximoff has reshaped reality and rewritten the lives of everyone in the universe, while Hyperion is strong enough to stop clashing universes using his bare hands, and Sentry effortlessly rips Ares' body in half. The MCU playfully turned this debate into a running joke in Thor: Ragnarok, where Tony Stark’s computer security recognizes Hulk as the "strongest Avenger" while relegating Thor to "Point Break." And yet, Thor and Captain Marvel quickly claim the title in Avengers: Infinity War and Avengers: Secret Wars.

The "strongest Avenger" debate has many possible answers, one of which gains more authority when supported by one of the Avengers' biggest villains.

Avengers: Twilight takes place in a possible future where superheroes have been outlawed following a catastrophic event known as H-Day, which resulted in countless deaths and the destruction of Boston. During a televised discussion, an older Steve Rogers and James Stark, Tony Stark's son, revisit the long-running debate about which Avenger deserves to be called the strongest. The young Stark points out that the individual responsible for the highest death toll was the Hulk, who had been placed under Ultron's control. When discussing the incident, Stark confirms Hulk is the strongest Avenger, as Ultron chose to weaponize the Green Goliath rather than Thor or Captain Marvel to destroy the city.

James Stark's assessment measures strength through destructive potential over isolated displays of power. Thor is a literal god who has fought entities like Hela and Surtur, and Captain Marvel can singlehandedly defeat entire alien armies, but Hulk offers a terrifying level of strength that rises indefinitely alongside his rage. Once Ultron gained control of Banner's alter ego, the robot turned one of the Avengers' best defenders into a living weapon capable of destroying an entire city as a starter feat. While Thor, Captain Marvel, and even Iron Man could technically stop Hulk at his base level, nobody can stop the Green Goliath once he crosses a certain anger threshold.

James Stark's declaration that the Hulk is the strongest Avenger in Avengers: Twilight #1 becomes increasingly difficult to dispute. The most famous example is World War Hulk, when the Green Scar returns to Earth in search of vengeance against the Illuminati and illustrates how he could singlehandedly defeat all of Earth's superheroes in a gladiator tournament. A similar demonstration takes place during Incredible Hulks #632, when Hulk's transformation into Worldbreaker threatens to unleash enough energy to destroy North America. Besides, the Illuminati choose to banish the Hulk to outer space precisely because they understand that he's one of the biggest threats to Earth, and their preemptive decision ends up backfiring.

It's been seen on the page and debated for years: which classic Marvel Comics team would win in a straight-up fight? The Avengers or the X-Men?

More recent Marvel stories have taken this terrifying concept even further. In the new Ultimate Universe, Earth-6160, a considerably weaker Hulk oversees horrific experiments on Gamma Island and leaves the resistance team seemingly dead. On Earth-616, the Infernal Hulk defeats the Avengers, the Fantastic Four, and the X-Men, and he transforms many of them into monsters for his army. Parallel to The Infernal Hulk, Avengers: Armageddon explores a similar nightmare with Thunderbolt Ross, whose Red Hulk transformation turns the longtime Hulk hunter into the harbinger of a potential World War III and destroys an entire city in Europe, just like Avengers: Twilight's Ultron-manipulated Hulk.

Earth's churning mantle may have created the Great Unconformity

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Explaining the Great Unconformity, where Cambrian deposits sit on top of older rocks.

In the Grand Canyon, there is a line in the rock where time seems to have gone missing. Ancient crystalline rocks mark its bottom, while sandstones laid down as life started to diversify during the Cambrian explosion mark the top. “It’s called the Great Unconformity, and it’s a gap in the geologic record,” says Nicolas Flament, a geoscientist at the University of Wollongong, Australia. “This gap can be anywhere between 200 million years, which is not that impressive, to more than a billion years.”

In a recent Nature study, Flament and his colleague Santosh Dhungana argue that this gap was carved with help from deep within the planet. According to their models, slow currents of hot rock in Earth’s mantle pushed entire continents up for hundreds of millions of years, letting glaciers grind them down and then let them sink, allowing fresh sediments to bury the scar. The largest hole in the rock record might be a fingerprint of processes happening deep in the planet’s interior.

The oldest sediments resting on the Great Unconformity in North America are between 635 and 482 million years old. Geologists have traced it across most of North America and parts of northern Europe and northern China. “Something seems to have happened, in particular in North America,” Flament says.

The leading explanation was glaciation. Between roughly 720 and 635 million years ago, glaciers reached the tropics, and some scientists think the entire planet froze over, a scenario called Snowball Earth. “Ice sheets are very good at grinding, scouring, scraping the surface,” Flament explains. He said that glaciers are about 10 times more effective than rivers at eroding rock away. But if the whole planet froze, the Great Unconformity should be found everywhere. And it’s not.

Other explanations included rifting, pulling and stretching of Earth’s crust, and a global drop in sea levels. But rifting acts mostly on the edges of continents, and a sea level change should have affected all continents alike. “There’s no doubt that there was extensive glaciation. There’s no doubt that there have been sea-level changes throughout time. The question is, was that enough to put the Great Unconformity puzzle together?” Flament says.

A fourth idea held that the mantle itself was moving continents up and down, a process called dynamic topography. “It had been proposed, but it hadn’t been quantitatively tested,” Flament says. So, his team set out to do so.

From roughly 100 to 200 kilometers beneath the surface down to nearly 3,000 kilometers, Earth’s mantle slowly convects. “An analogy would be a lava lamp, where some stuff comes up and some stuff comes down,” Flament explains. “Cold rock sinks and hot rock rises.”

The mantle is solid, yet a piece of rock can slowly travel from the surface to the deep Earth over some 200 million years. That motion tugs on the surface. Where cold slabs of ocean floor sink into the mantle at subduction zones, the ground gets pulled down; where hot mantle rises, it gets pushed up. The amplitude of this up and down motion is still debated, though. “The short of it is several hundreds of meters, and it’s still to be worked out exactly what that [precise value] is,” Flament says.

Dhungana and Flament ran six simulations of mantle convection going back 1 billion years, driven by reconstructions of how tectonic plates moved over that time. Then they grouped regions where the ground rose and fell in similar ways to find where the unconformity should have formed, and where it should have survived to the present. Flament, however, says the starting configuration of these simulations remains uncertain.

Magnetic records in ancient rocks reveal how far continents were from the equator, but not their longitude. “It’s possible that some continents may be in the wrong hemisphere,” Flament admits. The ancient ocean floor, long since recycled into the mantle, had to be invented entirely from scratch in the models. Still, the simulations produced a consistent story.

Laurentia, the ancient core of North America, had little or no subduction along its margins between about 930 and 510 million years ago, much like Africa today, so it sat above some rising mantle and was pushed up. Five of the six models showed this pattern of Laurentia seeing long uplift followed by sinking in the Cambrian. For northern Europe and North China, this pattern showed up in four.

But the uplift alone, Flament’s team concluded, was too gentle to strip away enough rock to create the Great Unconformity.

“The uplift could create a tilt for the whole continent, but it’s not great at creating a steep slope,” Flament says. The missing piece of the puzzle, he argues, were glaciers that ground through rock even on low ground and gentle slopes. The models show the uplift was still building when Snowball Earth hit. “This gentle uplift that’s continuous, and then that glacial erosion that comes on top, work together,” Flament explains. “They’re not coupled. They just happen to coincide in time.” When the continents sank in the Cambrian, seas flooded in and buried the eroded surface.

The models made by Flament’s team also explain why the gap is so obvious in North America but hard to find elsewhere. Compared with records of sediment preserved on each continent, North America and Eurasia show one long rise followed by one long fall, which the researchers interpret as the ideal recipe for the Great Unconformity.

South America and Australia, on the other hand, went through two cycles. “This would form potentially two unconformities, and then the Great Unconformity would be more difficult to see,” Flament says. Africa was a different case altogether—hemmed in by mountain belts at the time, its erosion and sediment were shaped by the mountains rather than by the mantle beneath.

But if the mantle goes through such up and down cycles on a regular basis, the remaining question was why we can see only one Great Unconformity, and not many.

The team says the Great Unconformity is unique in the geological record because of a combination of a rise-and-fall cycle lasting over 900 million years, a near-global glaciation that happened at the right moment, and rock records that get harder to read farther back in time.

Flament suspects there may be an older candidate, tied to a glaciation 2.4 billion years ago, but rocks that old lack the fossils that make younger sediments easier to date. “Having life in sediments makes it a lot easier to have a sense of time,” he says. His work, however, may help explain what happens right after the Great Unconformity formed.

The intense erosion that carved the Great Unconformity washed rock material into the oceans, possibly fertilizing them just before the Cambrian explosion, when calcium became abundant in seawater. “That’s when life learns to mobilize calcium and create skeletons and shells,” Flament says. He admits, though, that mantle dynamics likely wasn’t the only process that enabled the Cambrian explosion. “I think it would be misleading to say the mantle set the pace here. But I think it was one of the forces that worked together.”

‘Everyone knows why we lost,’ JD Vance points to the Iran war — which has already cost at least $38 billion and 19 American lives

While votes were still being counted in the U.S. midterm elections, administration figures in Washington were already inviting speculation over how the results might be connected to the conflict with Iran.

On the morning after the election, Vice President JD Vance wrote on X that the major reason for the dramatic loss for Republicans was the American public’s opposition to the war. “Everyone knows why we lost. And any honest conversation about where we go from here must start there,” he wrote, in reference to the ongoing conflict that began with U.S.-Israeli airstrikes against Iran in late February 2026.

While it is rare for a vice president to make such a critical proclamation about their own administration’s policy, President Donald Trump later stated that he did not disagree with Vance’s appraisal of the role that frustration with the Iran war played in voters’ minds.

But whether this changes Trump’s view on next steps in the ongoing conflict remains unclear. Reporting over the past few weeks has indicated that the U.S. military has been actively preparing options and increasing capabilities in the Persian Gulf region in the event the president directs a resumption of hostilities.

As an expert on Middle East affairs and a former senior official at the National Security Council during the first Trump administration, I’m aware that months of conflict have failed to bring Iran to its knees, force regime change or militarily defeat a perceived threat to U.S. assets and allies. Escalating now is unlikely to change those realities – and it carries the additional risk of further damaging Republicans in the eyes of Americans voters as they turn toward the 2028 presidential race.

Even if Trump were to ratchet up the conflict, moreover, it’s unclear how much wiggle room he’ll have come January facing a Democratic Congress almost uniformly opposed to the war.

Part of the issue facing Iran war hawks in Washington now is that the conflict has caused serious setbacks for the regime in Tehran without satisfying the officially stated strategic reasons for going to war.

They argue that pulling back now – as welcome as that might be for Americans struggling with the associated rising cost of fuel – may allow Iran to rebuild before the conflict is “won.” Moreover, it would render the military and reputational costs already sunk by Washington as wasted.

Among the achievements of this war touted by the Trump administration has been the death of former Supreme Leader Ali Khameini, along with the killing of scores of senior leaders within the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps – including individuals like the former head of Iran’s National Security Council Ali Larijani. Significant elements of Iran’s air defense and radar network, ballistic missile and drone production, and storage and launch sites have also been destroyed. And Iran’s already outdated navy and air force has been further degraded.

Meanwhile, the U.S.-imposed naval blockade against Iranian-linked shipping in the Persian Gulf cut off a crucial lifeline for Iran’s economy. And additional sanctions and economic penalties imposed by Washington have served to further devalue Iran’s currency and deprive Iran of additional financial links to the international banking system.

Yet for all the U.S. successes in degrading the Iranian military by limitless aerial bombardment and the significant financial pressure against the Iranian economy, an obvious endgame remains elusive.

Despite weakening the Iranian government – now led by Ali Khameinei’s son, Mojtaba, and a new echelon of senior IRGC commanders – the Islamic Republic remains intact. Moreover, it still possesses enough ballistic missiles and drones to hold the region at risk, while continuing to impose significant control over its population.

Meanwhile, U.S. military spending continues to rise. In September, the U.S. Congressional Budget Office assessed that the military costs of the Iran War had already totaled US$38 billion. Since then, that number has only increased. And that is to say nothing of the associated global economic costs that have resulted from Iran’s de facto closure of the Strait of Hormuz – a key shipping route for oil and gas.

Should Trump decide to change course on Iran, he – and others – may think that the money, effort and lives of 19 American servicemen killed in the conflict would have been spent with no obvious objective achieved. Yet continued military expenditure with no strategic endgame could only magnify those same costs.

To Iran, the midterm results have not changed its public position. Mojtaba Khameini has issued statements directing the IRGC to widen the conflict in the region and perhaps beyond should the U.S. and Israel attack again. At the same time, a statement attributed to him indicates that hostilities between the United States and Iran will persist regardless of the balance of party political power in Washington.

This raises questions about the prospects for diplomacy. During the summer, the U.S. and Iran agreed to an initial 14-point memorandum of understanding that, if finalized, could have brought a permanent end to the hostilities. But since the 60-day clock on that agreement lapsed, no parallel diplomatic agreement has emerged.

Even with the major shift in the U.S. political landscape, it remains unclear whether Trump will resume operations against Iran before the new Congress is sworn in. The messy election result in Israel – which has left the pro-war, and potentially outgoing, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyanu with potentially more reason to double-down in Iran – only complicates matters.

The U.S. military certainly appears to be ready to engage in a new round of attacks against Iran if so directed, and Iran is ready to retaliate. But given the shortcomings in the U.S. inventory – with munitions and missile and air defense capabilities depleted by eight months of war – escalation is something Trump and his inner circle will have to consider with caution.

Iran might force the issue by engaging in asymmetric attacks in the region or beyond that they have not attempted already. Or it could launch a coordinated offensive with its partners in the region – notably the Houthis in Yemen, who have become more active in their own conflict with Saudi Arabia in recent months.

But save an event that the Trump administration perceives is giving it cover to act, there are no clear indications emerging from Washington as to which way it will move.

Vance diagnosed a widely perceived political problem for Republicans: Voters don’t want more war. But what he noticeably didn’t mention was a practical solution. The Middle East conflict – and the United States’ role in it – are, as such, locked in a stalemate.

America and Iran have both borne significant political, military and economic costs. A decision not to pursue an endgame would leave those sunk; doing so may only increase them.

Phantom Blade Zero doesn't follow the RPG trend of having weapons in different tiers that you replace with newer ones. However, to upgrade these weapons, you'll need resources specific to the weapon type, similar to how you choose your skills. This means you can upgrade different weapon types, but because each type has finite resources, you need to choose which weapons go best together.

To upgrade your weapons in Phantom Blade Zero, you'll need to use the bells you use to upgrade abilities and fast travel around the map. At the bell, select Upgrade Equipment to open your inventory and choose what you want to improve. Hold the prompted button (X on PS5) to upgrade the weapon you choose.

From this menu, you can choose from your Main Weapons, Phantom Edges and Accessories, and they all get upgraded similarly. Choose the item you want to upgrade, and it will show you what resources you need. Although the resources differ, they all require Remnant Dust, just like upgrading your abilities.

For regular weapons, there are three different weapon types to choose from: One-Handed, Two-Handed and Dual-Wield. To upgrade these, you'll need Forged Metal, Twinned Metal and Broadplate Metal, respectively. You'll also need Solvent, which applies to any weapon you're upgrading.

However, as you level your weapons higher, you'll need to improve the materials, too. For example, for one-handed weapons, you can use Forged Metal to upgrade up to level 10, but you need Polished Forged Metal to upgrade from level 10 to level 20. We typically think of the Moon as a static, floating object that has remained the same for basically its entire lifetime, with an occasional asteroid impact serving as an exception. That understanding is grounded in physics - the Moon should have cooled rapidly after forming when a huge protoplanet hit the early Earth around 4.5 billion years ago. But, some lunar material returned from the Changâe-5 mission was notably formed only around two billion years ago, yet appeared to be volcanic in origin. So how did a world that was supposed to have settled down without volcanism remain hot enough to create these materials more than two billion years after its violent birth? A new paper from Miao Chen of the Chengdu University of Technology and his colleagues, and recently published in the journal Planet, offers an explanation - tidal heating.

Letâs first set the stage of the Moonâs creation. But when Changâe-5 returned some lunar basalts from Oceanus Procellarum back to Earth, laboratory dating showed them to be only around two billion years old. Basalts are only formed in active volcanoes, or other environments with similarly high pressures and temperatures. Environments that, at least according to traditional geological theory, shouldnât have existed on the Moon two billion years ago.

So instead the authors turned to gravity - specifically to a phenomenon called tidal heating. This happens when a celestial body is squeezed, stretched, and kneaded as it travels in an elliptical orbit around a larger parent body. Perhaps the most famous example of this in the solar system is Io. This moon of Jupiter is constantly erupting due to the tidal squishing it undergoes during its orbit around the largest planet.

All of which means that Earth itself exerted huge rhythmic tidal forces on the Moon' s interior during the course of its orbit.

When the research team plugged a more realistic 3D representation of the mantle setup into a convection simulation, they noticed that a ton of energy was directed specifically at two symmetric regions along the lunar core-mantle boundary.

These regions, which they named Tidal Heating Anomaly Zones (THAZs) , absorbed a huge amount of energy. This seemed to be the driving force behind much of the Moonâs geological history. Billions of years later, long after the tidal engine has calmed down, those stranded pockets of mantle melted to produce the basalts seen in the Changâe-5 samples.

Reading Lovecraftian horror feels like reading an ancient forbidden text. There is a timelessness to H.P. Lovecraft's display of the "fear of the unknown" that transcends the page and immerses one into complete existential dread. Yet, despite feeling ancient and timeless, Lovecraftian horror rarely translates well to the screen.

There are not even a handful of worthy direct adaptations of H.P. Lovecraft's stories. Quite a few cosmic horror movies and shows, however, have succeeded at loosely drawing from his works. One of them is Netflix's greatest sci-fi hit, Stranger Things. To clarify, Stranger Things, as a whole, is not a Lovecraftian horror show. It is arguably not even horror.

However, in its first 8 episodes, Stranger Things draws heavily from Lovecraft's chilling Eldritch lore and his ambiguous descriptions of monsters and ancient gods. Lovecraftian horror is certainly one of the many literary influences that ultimately shaped Stranger Things. However, the Netflix sci-fi show's heavy inclination towards cosmic horror in season 1 just cannot be ignored.

The origins of cosmic terrors in every Lovecraftian story stem from humanity's hubris. For instance, in H.P. Lovecraft's seminal short story From Beyond, an ambitious scientist makes an electronic device that allows humans to perceive what lies beyond our scope of accepted reality. With this, he unknowingly allows interdimensional creatures from alternate dimensions to perceive and manipulate humans.

Something very similar unfolds in Stranger Things season 1.

Dr. Martin Brenner and Hawkins National Laboratory open "The Gate" in an attempt to "expand" human consciousness and weaponize it to spy on the Soviets. Little do they realize that they have exposed humanity to an unknown dimension. Just like the terrifying co-existing but hidden dimensions in Lovecraftian horror, Stranger Things' "Upside Down" is not merely hell ruled by the devil.

Instead, it is more of an ancient and toxic "non-Euclidean" plane that mirrors Hawkins. Just like the sunken city of R'lyeh from The Call of Cthulhu, even the "Upside Down" is cold, dark, endless, and incomprehensible.

Almost throughout its run, Stranger Things season 1 also avoids revealing the true nature or form of the Demogorgon. It is packed with horrifying ambiguous imagery where Will attempts to communicate with his family from the "other side" while struggling to escape the monster's grasp.

Similar to Lovecraftian protagonists, even Stranger Things characters like Joyce feel so helpless against the cosmic indifference of their world that they start losing their minds.

In its final arc, Stranger Things season 1 also gives viewers and its characters an illusion of control. It establishes that the power of friendship seemingly saves the day and Will and his family live happily ever after. But just before Stranger Things season 1 ends, Will realizes that control and autonomy were mere illusions. With this, Stranger Things subtly mocks human authority just like Lovecraftian stories.

The Duffer Brothers have previously cited H.P. Lovecraft's stories as an influence on Stranger Things (via EW).

Going into season 2, Stranger Things manages to retain some of its cosmic horror aspects. However, at the same time, it makes its terrors and scares a little too "tangible." This marks the show's departure from cosmic horror. As it progresses, Stranger Things bends genres and even covers coming-of-age themes in latter seasons.

Towards the end, the Netflix sci-fi show feels more like a conventional supernatural thriller than a groundbreaking take on Lovecraftian horror tropes. Perhaps, this explains why, in hindsight, most viewers are kinder towards Stranger Things' earlier chapters and more harsh when it comes to its divisive season 5.

(Tokyo) – The Hong Kong High Court on November 5, 2026, sentenced Joshua Wong, a well-known democracy activist, to 10 years in prison on baseless national security charges, Human Rights Watch said today. Two months of the sentence will run concurrently with his current national security sentence. Wong has been in custody since November 2020, when he was 24, and faces imprisonment until late 2036, when he will be 40.

The Hong Kong government should immediately and unconditionally release Wong. Foreign governments should denounce the sentences against him and other wrongfully detained pro-democracy activists and impose targeted sanctions on officials responsible for Hong Kong’s growing repression.

“The harsh sentence on baseless charges against Joshua Wong is cruel and unjust,” said Elaine Pearson, Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “Young people promoting social change are an asset for any vibrant society, but the Hong Kong government puts them behind bars.”

To keep Hong Kong society subdued, and to reduce the chance of an international outcry, the Hong Kong government appears to have adopted the tactic of stacking charges against influential pro-democracy leaders to ensure their prolonged detention, Human Rights Watch said. Other notable Hong Kong figures who have faced similar tactics include Jimmy Lai, founder of Apple Daily, and veteran democracy activists Lee Cheuk-yan and Albert Ho.

The court sentenced Wong, 30, under the National Security Law for “conspiring to collude with foreign forces.” The authorities accused him of “conspiring” with the activist Nathan Law and other “unknown” individuals between 2016 and November 2020 to urge foreign governments to sanction Hong Kong and Chinese government officials as they advocated for Hong Kong’s “democratic self-determination.” 

The prosecution relied heavily on the international lobbying and media advocacy that Wong and Demosisto, the political party Wong co-founded in 2016, carried out before the National Security Law took effect. Wong initiated online petitions, spoke to international media, and met with foreign officials to draw attention to human rights abuses in Hong Kong. He urged governments to sanction Chinese and Hong Kong officials responsible for abuses and to stop selling weapons to Hong Kong police that they use to carry out rights violations.

Wong had mostly stopped these activities before the National Security Law became effective. Demosisto disbanded on June 30, 2020, and Wong resigned from the group. However, the government quoted social media posts by Wong as ostensible evidence that his alleged “conspiracy” with Law to commit “foreign collusion” continued after the law took effect. In these posts, Wong said he “would not change his views” and that he would “continue to seek support from the international community” in his personal capacity, while Law and others abroad would continue their international advocacy.

The government also cited Wong’s subsequent opinion pieces and media interviews, though none directly called for sanctions, which the National Security Law specifically prohibits. It also cited his reposts of Law’s posts broadly calling for sanctions against Chinese officials and Hong Kong police.

The court ruled that Wong’s offense was of a “grave nature.” The National Security Law sets prison terms of 3 to 10 years for “foreign collusion,” or 10 years to life for offenses deemed to be of a “grave nature.” Wong formally pleaded guilty on September 2. A guilty plea normally results in a one-third sentence reduction. But a sentence reduction cannot bring the term below the statutory minimum.

Because of reporting restrictions on pretrial committal proceedings, when a magistrate determines whether to send a case to a higher court for trial, the public did not know the details of the accusations against Wong until the formal plea. The government enacted a law in 2024 to enable the court, on national security grounds, to deny the defendant’s right to ask the court to lift restrictions on reporting about the case.

By restricting media reporting on the pretrial proceedings in national security cases, the government can control the narrative and undermine the fundamental legal principle of open justice.

Since Beijing imposed the National Security Law on Hong Kong in June 2020, Hong Kong authorities have used the new legal regime—along with existing laws—to rapidly dismantle civil society and detain, imprison, or force into exile hundreds of activists and government critics.

Wong has been in custody since November 2020, when he pleaded guilty to two charges of organizing and inciting an unauthorized assembly linked to the 2019 citywide protest movement. After that, he pleaded guilty to five more charges on separate occasions, including for violating an anti-mask law, participating in an unauthorized vigil for the 1989 Tiananmen Massacre, and breaching a court ban to disclose a police officer’s personal information.

While serving these sentences, Wong was charged with two national security offenses, which carry far harsher penalties. In January 2021, Wong was charged with “conspiring to commit subversion” for participating in an unofficial democratic primary in 2020 and sentenced to another four years and eight months in prison. 

Before Wong could complete these sentences, the authorities brought the “foreign collusion” charge against him in June 2025, ensuring that he would remain in prison.

Wong became one of Hong Kong’s best-known democracy activists as a teenager, after leading a massive 2012 protest against a government educational curriculum widely seen as political indoctrination. He became the global face of Hong Kong’s democracy movement during the 2014 Umbrella Movement and co-founded Demosisto with Law and others in 2016. The party disbanded on June 30, 2020, hours before the National Security Law came into effect.

The National Security Law’s sweeping provisions have been used to punish peaceful protest and expression. Its use against Wong and others violates the right to freedom of expression under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which is incorporated into Hong Kong law through the city’s de facto constitution, the Basic Law, and the Bill of Rights Ordinance.

“Beijing has paid no real price for its increasing repression in Hong Kong,” Pearson said. “Concerned governments should press for the release of Wong and other activists, impose targeted sanctions against implicated officials, and review their relationship with China more broadly in light of the deepening abuses.”

making room for nature: the urban landscapes of kongjian yu

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

what happens when urban landscapes are designed around ecological processes rather than human convenience alone? from restored waterways to constructed wetlands, these projects raise questions about biodiversity, interdependence, and whether cities can become shared habitats for multiple forms of life.

In Landscapes for Adaptation: Evidence from China, architect and Harvard professor Charles Waldheim examines 60 built experiments realized by Chinese landscape architect Kongjian Yu and his practice Turenscape over the past two decades. Analyzing their environmental performance, the book documents how landscape architecture can respond to flooding, climate volatility, and ecological change through implemented projects. These built experiments offer a starting point for exploring a broader question: can cities be designed as shared habitats for humans and other forms of life?

Yu, founder of Turenscape and a leading advocate of the sponge city concept, has spent decades arguing that urban development should work with natural processes. His approach uses landscape as infrastructure, allowing water, vegetation, and existing ecological systems to play an active role in addressing flooding, pollution, and habitat loss.

In Shanghai, Houtan Park demonstrates how landscape architecture can make ecological processes part of the urban fabric. Designed by Turenscape and completed in 2010 for the Shanghai World Expo, the 14-hectare park occupies a former industrial site along the Huangpu River. Once used by a steel factory and shipyard, the land was heavily degraded, while the adjacent river was severely polluted.

The design builds on the industrial past of the site, transforming its existing landscape into a new ecological and public space. A constructed wetland runs through the park, using a sequence of terraces, cascades, and wetland plants to treat polluted river water. The system was designed to treat approximately 2,400 cubic meters of water per day, improving its quality from Lower Grade V to Grade III for non-potable uses. The wetland also forms part of the flood-management strategy of the park, while its planted river edge creates conditions for aquatic and bird habitats. Reclaimed industrial materials, agricultural terraces, and a network of paths connect these ecological functions with the public experience of the park.

Water treatment, flood protection, habitat creation, and public access operate within the same landscape. Houtan Park makes infrastructure visible, allowing visitors to encounter environmental processes that are usually hidden behind concrete walls and underground networks.

A related approach appears in Yichang Yunhe Park in Hubei province. Designed by Turenscape and completed in 2013, the 12-hectare project transforms twelve polluted, abandoned fish ponds into what the practice describes as a ‘green sponge’. The design uses the existing water bodies as the foundation for a system that cleanses polluted canal water and remediates contaminated stormwater. The project relies on regenerative design and minimal intervention. The former ponds become part of a wider landscape of vegetation and water, while paths and elevated walkways allow people to move through the site. Its significance lies partly in the decision to work with an existing, damaged landscape.

In Ningbo, the Yinzhou Central River Transformation explores the potential of restoring an urban waterway. Completed in 2010, the project introduces wetland planting along the riverbanks, alongside walkways and areas for public use. The intervention softens the engineered edges of the river, retaining its role as part of the city’s water infrastructure.

These are some examples of Turenscape’s projects that suggest that ecological urbanism does not necessarily depend on introducing untouched nature into the city, but it can also involve reconsidering infrastructure that already exists: drainage channels, abandoned ponds, industrial waterfronts, and flood-control systems. Such sites become opportunities to restore ecological functions, changing how people encounter water and landscape. However, creating landscapes that manage water and support vegetation is not necessarily the same as designing cities around the needs of other forms of life. In Yu’s projects, wetlands and planted riverbanks help reduce flooding, improve water quality, and provide habitats. Their value also depends on whether other species can thrive there, how ecological systems develop over time, and how well these landscapes are maintained.

In her 2016 book Staying with the Trouble: Making Kin in the Chthulucene, feminist theorist Donna Haraway challenges the idea that humans exist separately from the rest of the living world, describing life as a network of interdependencies in which organisms and environments shape one another. Her concept of sympoiesis, or making-with, offers a way to understand the world as something produced collectively rather than controlled by a single species.

Seen through Haraway’s ideas, Yu’s projects can be understood as more than infrastructure for managing water and flooding. The wetlands and river corridors also create environments where plants, insects, birds, microorganisms, and aquatic species interact. This shifts the focus from what these landscapes provide for people to the wider ecological relationships they support and how these relationships shape urban life.

This does not mean that ecological infrastructure automatically produces a more-than-human city. A landscape can support biodiversity while remaining primarily designed around human priorities. Its ecological value depends on factors such as long-term maintenance, water quality, habitat connectivity, and whether different species can establish and survive. The presence of vegetation or the appearance of a natural landscape is not, on its own, evidence of a functioning ecosystem. There are also social questions to consider. Green infrastructure can improve urban environments, but it does not necessarily address unequal access to public space, the displacement of existing communities, or the uneven distribution of environmental benefits.

Ecological restoration can become part of wider redevelopment processes, sometimes increasing land values and changing who can afford to live nearby. Assessing these projects therefore requires looking beyond their environmental performance to consider how they affect both local communities and other forms of life.

The projects documented in Waldheim’s Landscapes for Adaptation: Evidence from China demonstrate how landscape architecture can contribute to climate adaptation through built, functioning systems. Their significance extends beyond the technical integration of water management and public space. They suggest that cities can be understood as environments shaped by relationships between human activity and ecological processes.

Yu’s work offers a practical foundation for this shift, while Haraway’s thinking invites us to take its implications further. If cities are understood as shared environments, their success cannot be judged solely by how efficiently they protect people from environmental change. It must also be considered in terms of the relationships they allow to develop between different forms of life. The more-than-human city is not necessarily a finished ecological utopia, but an ongoing process of learning how to live alongside the many species and systems that already make urban life possible.

Forests planted to fight climate change may be fueling wildfires

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A global study reveals that planting forests can increase wildfire risk, threatening carbon storage and challenging climate strategies.

There’s something darkly ironic about planting a forest to store carbon and watching it go up in flames.

But new research suggests that planting trees can sometimes increase wildfire risk, threatening the very carbon those forests were meant to store.

A new global study reveals just how much that risk varies across different landscapes.

An international team of researchers from Spain, Chile, and Finland analyzed satellite records spanning more than two decades to track what happens when non-forested land gets converted into planted forests.

Their dataset was enormous: 67,248 patches across the world’s temperate regions, monitored from 2001 to 2023 using MODIS land-cover and fire records.

After accounting for climate, accessibility, and patch size, afforestation was associated with a 26% increase in fire occurrence per hectare per year and a 23% increase in expected burned area when fires did ignite.

“Forest plantations change the fuel conditions in which fires start and spread,” said lead author Irene Repeto-Deudero from the University of Cádiz and the University of Eastern Finland.

“We wanted to understand how these changes differ across landscapes, rather than assuming that tree planting has the same consequences everywhere.”

Those global averages, though, hide enormous variation. The researchers compared fire patterns across four major types of temperate landscapes.

Temperate conifer forests took the worst hit. Fire occurrence there surged 2.7-fold, and the expected burned area roughly doubled.

These landscapes already contain large amounts of vegetation and face increasingly extreme fire weather.

Adding dense plantations can create more continuous fuel, allowing fires to spread more easily.

Mediterranean forests, woodlands, and scrub saw a 38% jump in fire occurrence and a 9% rise in burned area. In absolute terms, the probability of fire per 100 hectares over a decade climbed from 7.8% to 11.0%.

These regions already contend with seasonal drought, flammable vegetation, and frequent human-caused ignitions.

Piling more biomass onto that tinderbox gives extreme fire weather more fuel to work with.

Temperate broadleaf and mixed forests told a stranger story. Fire occurrence actually dropped by 23% after afforestation – but expected burned area grew by 20%.

Fewer fires, in other words, but the ones that did ignite burned larger. That trade-off matters more than it might seem.

Nearly half of the world’s planted forests sit in this biome. Even modest shifts in fire behavior could carry substantial consequences.

Temperate grasslands, savannas, and shrublands were the one bright spot. There, fire occurrence fell 18% with no significant increase in burned area.

“These contrasting responses show why local ecological conditions must guide afforestation decisions,” explained study co-author Alejandro Miranda from the University of La Frontera.

“The same intervention can have very different consequences depending on the vegetation, climate, and existing fire regime.”

Geographically, the increases in wildfire activity clustered in regions that are already under fire stress.

These include western North America, central Chile, southern Europe, southwestern Australia, and parts of South Africa.

In these regions, expanding plantations and increasingly dangerous fire weather could combine to intensify wildfire risks.

The study also highlights a data point that deserves more attention. Plantations are roughly twice as likely to burn as native temperate forests.

And monoculture plantations – single-species stands planted in uniform rows – have been linked to particularly extreme fire behavior.

All of this creates a serious accounting problem for climate policy.

Afforestation has become a flagship mitigation strategy, with temperate regions identified as high-priority planting zones.

But wildfires don’t just destroy trees. They also release stored carbon back into the atmosphere, potentially reducing or even reversing the climate benefits of tree planting.

“These results therefore require that tree planting initiatives consider their exposure to fire,” said Susana Gómez-González from the University of Cádiz.

“As the climate warms, increased wildfire risk may impact the capacity of forest plantations to store carbon in the long term.”

Planted forests provide wood, protect soil, and deliver a range of ecosystem services. The point isn’t to stop – it’s to think harder about where to plant them.

“Plantations are necessary in many cases to produce wood and other ecosystem services, including soil protection,” said Blas Mola-Yudego from the University of Eastern Finland.

“However, their location must be carefully planned to avoid unintended consequences on local fire regimes.”

“Fire risk needs to be considered from the outset, alongside production goals and environmental benefits.”

The findings challenge the assumption that planting more trees automatically delivers greater climate benefits.

In some landscapes, the additional wildfire risk could undermine the carbon gains that plantations are meant to provide.

For policymakers counting on planted forests to help combat climate change, the message is clear: where trees are planted may matter just as much as how many are planted.

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

Uranium cubes reveal why the German nuclear project failed

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required.”

During World War II, physicists on the Manhattan Project worried that the Nazi German nuclear program might achieve the first nuclear bomb before the US and its allies. That’s not what transpired, but historians have debated just how close the Germans came to achieving criticality in their last attempt at building a reactor: the B8 pile. Werner Heisenberg, who led that effort, claimed that they were close and had only needed a little more uranium. But according to a paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences Nexus, that was not the case: the project simply didn’t have sufficient uranium and heavy water to build a working bomb, given the design they were using.

This particular research started in 2013, when University of Maryland physicist Timothy Koeth—a co-author on the new paper—received the gift of a mysterious heavy metal cube from a friend. He instantly recognized it as one of the uranium cubes used by German scientists in their unsuccessful attempt to build a working nuclear reactor. Only 14 such cubes are known to exist today.

In 2019, we reported on Koeth’s quest to track down the cube’s origins, as well as several other similar cubes that had somehow found their way across the Atlantic. At the time, Koeth and his partner in the quest, then-graduate student Miriam “Mimi” Hiebert, had tracked down ten such cubes around the US. For instance, the Smithsonian Institute had a German uranium cube in storage. They tracked a third cube to Harvard University, where it was regularly passed around to students in introductory physics classes as a curiosity.

Underpinning the Manhattan Project in the US was the fear that German scientists under Adolf Hitler’s Nazi regime would beat the Allies to a nuclear bomb. The Germans had a two-year head start, but according to Koeth, “fierce competition over finite resources, bitter interpersonal rivalries, and ineffectual scientific management” resulted in significant delays in their progress toward achieving a sustained nuclear reaction. German nuclear scientists were separated into three isolated groups based in Berlin (B), Gottow (G), and Leipzig (L).

Heisenberg headed the Berlin group, and as the Allied forces advanced in the winter of 1944, Heisenberg moved his team to a cave under a castle in a small town called Haigerloch—now the site of the Atomkeller Museum. That’s where the group built the B8 reactor. It resembled an “ominous chandelier,” per Koeth, because it was composed of 664 uranium cubes strung together with aircraft cable and then submerged in a tank of heavy water shielded by graphite to prevent radiation exposure.

As the German scientists were racing against time, Manhattan Project lead Lieutenant General Leslie Groves kicked off a covert mission dubbed “Alsos,” with the express purpose of gathering information and materials related to Germany’s scientific research. When Allied forces closed in, Heisenberg took apart the B8 experiment and buried the uranium cubes in a field, ferreting away key documentation in a latrine. (Pity Samuel Goudsmit, the poor physicist who had to dig those out.) Heisenberg escaped by bicycle, carrying a few cubes in a backpack.

As Heisenberg reported, the German scientists’ final experiment failed because the amount of uranium in the cubes was insufficient to trigger a sustained nuclear reaction. But Heisenberg was confident that “a slight increase in its size would have been sufficient to start off the process of energy production.” A model described in a 2009 paper supported that, showing the group would have needed only 50 percent more uranium cubes to make the design work.

Koeth and Hiebert uncovered a box of declassified documents about German uranium in the National Archives and found about 400 other uranium cubes from a separate reactor experiment by the Gottow group. They concluded that the combined inventory would have been more than enough to achieve criticality in the B8 reactor, suggesting that Germany’s secretive, isolationist approach hampered their nuclear program because the two groups weren’t sharing information or resources.

This latest paper builds on that earlier work but reaches a different conclusion. Koeth, Hiebert, and their co-authors analyzed two of the surviving uranium cubes, along with combing through historical records, technical reports, correspondence, and similar archival sources to learn more about the uranium, heavy water, and graphite blocks used for the B8 reactor experiment.

Among their findings: The B8 reactor would have needed between 1,292 and 1,775 uranium cubes to achieve criticality, and twice the heavy water produced by the Norsk Hydro plant in Vemork, Norway—the only source at the time—over the course of the entire war. In short, “Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required,” the authors wrote.

The authors also cited prior work noting key differences in American and German graphite production. The former used petroleum coke. While some of this supply reached Germany, the British naval blockade in January 1940 deprived the Germans of that vital resource. The German nuclear physicists had to use metallurgical coke from the Ruhr, which had twice as many impurities as American coke. (The US scientists had similar issues until they located an unusually low-boron coke in Pennsylvania.) Thus, “no natural uranium, graphite-moderated pile could have gone critical” with the materials available to the German scientists at the time.

Greenland's parliament ratifies Trump's US security agreement

The US is set to expand its military footprint on the Arctic island, part of the Kingdom of Denmark. It follows pressure from Donald Trump earlier in the year which opened a major rift in the transatlantic alliance.

Greenland's parliament on Tuesday signed off on a trilateral security and military-access agreement with the United States and Denmark that paves the way for an expanded US' military presence on the strategically located and mineral-rich island.

The deal has alleviated pressure coming from US President Donald Trump, who earlier this year had repeatedly threatened to take control of the self-governing Danish territory, citing US security interests.

After months of negotiation the agreement was signed in September by Trump, Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen and Greenlandic Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly in New York.

The agreement allows for two new US military bases and Trump's "Golden Dome" missile defence system. It also keeps Greenland under Danish control, while reaffirming Greenlanders' right to self determination.

On Tuesday, shortly before the vote in the capital, Nuuk, Greenlandic Foreign Minister Mute Egede welcomed the agreement's ratification.

"We must accept that peace and stability are no longer something we can take for granted," Egede said.

In a first debate last month, a majority of Greenlandic lawmakers had already signalled support for the agreement.

Pele Broberg, chair of the pro-independence Naleraq party, questioned the deal's value for Greenland.

"We are looking for a better agreement that we can benefit from when we become an independent state. But no economic benefit has been gained from the agreement," Broberg said.

The agreement will come into effect after ratification by the Danish parliament, which is due to read the bill over two sessions in November. A vote is scheduled for November 19 in Copenhagen.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image People watch the ICJ hearing at a restaurant in a Rohingya refugee camp in Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh, on January 23, 2020. © 2020 Allison Joyce/Getty Images

(The Hague) – The International Court of Justice has announced that it will deliver its judgment on November 19, 2026, in the case brought by Gambia against Myanmar for alleged violations of the Genocide Convention, Human Rights Watch said today in releasing a question-and-answer document on key issues in the case.

In August 2017, Myanmar’s security forces began a sweeping campaign of massacres, rape, and arson against ethnic Rohingya in northern Rakhine State that forced more than 700,000 people to flee to neighboring Bangladesh. In November 2019, Gambia filed a case before the International Court of Justice alleging that Myanmar’s atrocities against the Rohingya constitute genocide and violate the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide. This is not a criminal case against individuals, but a request for a legal determination of Myanmar’s state responsibility for genocide. 

“The International Court of Justice’s Myanmar judgment will mark a crucial moment in the Rohingya’s quest for justice,” said Maria Elena Vignoli, senior international justice counsel at Human Rights Watch. “The ruling will also be a stark reminder that the oppression of the Rohingya is ongoing, and that rights-respecting countries need to act decisively to support them, promote justice for atrocities, and ensure their safe and voluntary return.”

The judgment, which is final with no possibility to appeal, will conclude the proceedings in this case before the court.

Casio announced a new addition to its Casiotone line of portable electronic keyboards with a feature that could help aspiring musicians master skills like rhythm and timing without the frustrations or discouragement of hitting the wrong note.

The Casiotone CT-FS1 is available now for $399.99 in a monochromatic black or white version where even the 61 touch-responsive keys are all the same color. It can run on AA batteries or a power adapter but at just under 10 pounds in weight it’s definitely not the smallest of portable keyboards. There’s built-in stereo speakers and Bluetooth connectivity if you’re playing for others, as well as a physical headphone jack.

The keyboard’s most compelling feature is what Casio calls its Feel Sync technology that works alongside a new Feel Sync mobile app available for iOS and Android devices. Through the app you can import a song file which is analyzed by an algorithm before playing through the keyboard. As you play along with the song the keyboard will only produce notes that match the song’s harmony, no matter what keys you press. You’re still responsible for timing and matching the song’s rhythm, but the keyboard takes care of everything else so you can work on developing your creative expression without being self-conscious about it sounding awful.

The CT-FS1 can be played like a standard keyboard without the Feel Sync assistance, but because it’s long enough to accommodate two musicians, you can also split the keyboard into two zones. One side works like a traditional keyboard for an experienced musician to play, while the other benefits from the real time Feel Sync corrections.

How Berlin's Arab queers are building their community

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

As the co-founder of the ADIRA Drag Festival, Lebanese performance artist, drag performer, DJ and cultural organizer Hassandra has become an essential part of Berlin's queer artistic landscape.

"Adira" is an Arabic slang term used in Levantine queer culture to describe someone with a strong presence — someone powerful and accomplished.

It is also the name of a Berlin-based collective that curates events and club nights celebrating the diversity of queer Arab* culture. The asterisk signals the inclusion of non-Arab ethnic minorities and people with diverse identities and roots in Southwest Asia and North Africa.

Among the collective's events is the ADIRA Drag Festival, which celebrated its third edition in Berlin in late October.

This year's theme, "Who's Your Diva?!," brought together drag performers paying tribute to legendary figures from across the Eastern Mediterranean who have become queer icons — from Egyptian music legend Umm Kulthum to Turkish star Bülent Ersoy and Lebanese pop icon Haifa Wehbe.

Hassandra, co-founder of ADIRA and the ADIRA Drag Festival, was among the performers, taking on the persona of Lebanese pop star Elissa.

"It is very important for me to bring my Lebanese Arab identity and culture into what I do, because I think it's a very beautiful culture. And there's a lot of campy, glamorous, beautiful divas," Hassandra said.

Elissa's "whole discography is very romantic, raw, soft, kind of like very Adele vibes," Hassandra told DW ahead of the October 30 show. "However, her personality is known to be the extreme opposite. She's very feisty. Very blunt, very unfiltered. Not soft at all as a person." The star is also known for speaking out on political issues in Lebanon. She has publicly voiced support for LGBTQ+ communities in the Middle East, while also frequently sparking controversy across the Arab world with her outspoken positions on other political issues.

Hassandra's ADIRA Drag Festival performance addresses this complexity, ultimately "tapping into her sort of controversial political opinions," the drag artist explained.

This reflects one of the ideas Hassandra hopes to promote with the theme of this year's festival. "As queer people, we tend to idealize a lot of pop divas. But these pop divas are very imperfect in their nature," Hassandra said. The show provides an opportunity to critically revisit these divas' legacy through a more critical lens. "And this sort of critical lens can be taken into the outside world, as in: never follow anyone in life blindly. No one should follow any politician, any political party, any pop diva, any activist blindly."

Born in Beirut in 1994, Hassandra studied theater and stage acting at the Lebanese University before moving to Berlin in 2017. "I would say it was it was just a very sort of impulsive decision at the age of 22. I mean, there's no doubt that the economic situation was really bad in Lebanon and continued to become even worse," the artist said.

In Berlin, Hassandra became increasingly involved in the city's independent art scene and queer nightlife. Drag emerged as another form of self-expression for the actor.

Through performances that combine theatrical intensity, political humor, grotesque aesthetics and moments of tenderness, Hassandra challenges conventional understandings of gender and identity.

Their work explores themes of home, nostalgia, displacement and queer Arab culture. These ideas are also central to ADIRA, which Hassandra founded in 2023 with Syrian-German DJ and cultural producer Zuher Jazmati, also known as xanax_attax.

Through panel discussions, performances and music events, ADIRA fosters collective participation and community-building.

As Islamophobia and homophobia rise alongside the far right, spaces like ADIRA offer a sense of belonging and support for Arab queer communities.

That sense of community has become even more important following the deadly Islamist terrorist attack during Berlin's Christopher Street Day pride parade in July.
