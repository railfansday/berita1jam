The New Zealand head coach Dave Rennie took plenty from his three years at the Glasgow Warriors but it is the chuckles that he remembers most fondly. "The Scottish are funny people and I haven't laughed as much in any environment as much as I have in my time here," Rennie explained back in 2020 after embarking on what proved an ill-fated stint with Australia. "I've loved the people and I've loved our time here. It's been special, we'll miss it."

Dave Rennie spent three years at Glasgow (Getty)

Perhaps it is no surprise, then, that there has been such a strong Scottish influence at the heart of his project to restore the All Blacks' spirit. Mike Blair won 85 caps at scrum half and captained Scotland 14 times; until deciding he rather preferred life overseeing attack, it appeared he was being groomed as Gregor Townsend's successor in the highest coaching office in the land. Instead, he and Rennie return to Murrayfield with a New Zealand side in a phase of reinvention or rediscovery – re-finding the tenets that made them great and forging perhaps the world's most outstanding attack.

It has proved a natural fit. Blair has admitted to feeling a degree of impostor syndrome when he first came into All Blacks camp earlier this year, but has found the squad highly receptive to his methods. He has done excellent work, too, getting a tune out of a series of playmakers and associated parts through a demanding itinerary. Unbeaten so far in the Nations Championship having overcome France, Italy and Ireland in July, they carry confidence from two professional performances against the Wallabies last month.

"We're in a good place, I think," Blair said this week. "We're pleased with what we did against Australia but we're also aware that playing Scotland at Murrayfield is a really big challenge. It's another good opportunity to show the growth in our game."

Scotland struggled to get close to New Zealand during the playing days of Mike Blair (far left) (Getty)

The former scrum half made a self-deprecating crack that he'd already contributed to a few All Blacks wins over Scotland this week but his own playing days do serve as a reminder of how this challenge has changed for the tourists. Back when their attack coach wore the thistle, the result was a foregone conclusion; in four meetings with the All Blacks as a player, Blair only got within 20 points once. "When I played, New Zealand played their second-string team. They gave guys opportunity against us," he admitted. "Times have changed from that." The last four meetings have been decided by eight points or fewer.

Scotland will rue missed opportunities in at least two of those, including last year – having hauled the game back to level pegging from 17-0 down, Townsend's side could not sustain their effort. One could feel echoes of that game during a not dissimilar defeat to South Africa during the first phase of the Nations Championship in July – this tournament may have its critics but in ensuring a regularity of fixture against the very top teams, it is of great benefit to Scotland.

Will that translate into the next step of finally beating New Zealand? The fear of some is that last year represented a significant missed opportunity against a team in flux. While consistency and coherence in selection puts Scotland in a good place, they will be at a disadvantage in starting a campaign against an international team that has been together virtually since July. No doubt, though, that Townsend will want his team to try to match their expressive visitors stride-for-stride.

Confidence will be taken from the strong start enjoyed in the United Rugby Championship by both Scottish sides, with Edinburgh joining Glasgow among the early pace-setters. 8 Episodes Of Netflix’s Greatest Sci-Fi Hit Deliver Peak Lovecraftian Horror

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Reading Lovecraftian horror feels like reading an ancient forbidden text. There is a timelessness to H.P. Lovecraft's display of the "fear of the unknown" that transcends the page and immerses one into complete existential dread. Yet, despite feeling ancient and timeless, Lovecraftian horror rarely translates well to the screen.

There are not even a handful of worthy direct adaptations of H.P. Lovecraft's stories. Quite a few cosmic horror movies and shows, however, have succeeded at loosely drawing from his works. One of them is Netflix's greatest sci-fi hit, Stranger Things. To clarify, Stranger Things, as a whole, is not a Lovecraftian horror show. It is arguably not even horror.

However, in its first 8 episodes, Stranger Things draws heavily from Lovecraft's chilling Eldritch lore and his ambiguous descriptions of monsters and ancient gods. Lovecraftian horror is certainly one of the many literary influences that ultimately shaped Stranger Things. However, the Netflix sci-fi show's heavy inclination towards cosmic horror in season 1 just cannot be ignored.

The origins of cosmic terrors in every Lovecraftian story stem from humanity's hubris. For instance, in H.P. Lovecraft's seminal short story From Beyond, an ambitious scientist makes an electronic device that allows humans to perceive what lies beyond our scope of accepted reality. With this, he unknowingly allows interdimensional creatures from alternate dimensions to perceive and manipulate humans.

Something very similar unfolds in Stranger Things season 1.

Dr. Martin Brenner and Hawkins National Laboratory open "The Gate" in an attempt to "expand" human consciousness and weaponize it to spy on the Soviets. Little do they realize that they have exposed humanity to an unknown dimension. Just like the terrifying co-existing but hidden dimensions in Lovecraftian horror, Stranger Things' "Upside Down" is not merely hell ruled by the devil.

Instead, it is more of an ancient and toxic "non-Euclidean" plane that mirrors Hawkins. Just like the sunken city of R'lyeh from The Call of Cthulhu, even the "Upside Down" is cold, dark, endless, and incomprehensible.

Almost throughout its run, Stranger Things season 1 also avoids revealing the true nature or form of the Demogorgon. It is packed with horrifying ambiguous imagery where Will attempts to communicate with his family from the "other side" while struggling to escape the monster's grasp.

Similar to Lovecraftian protagonists, even Stranger Things characters like Joyce feel so helpless against the cosmic indifference of their world that they start losing their minds.

In its final arc, Stranger Things season 1 also gives viewers and its characters an illusion of control. It establishes that the power of friendship seemingly saves the day and Will and his family live happily ever after. But just before Stranger Things season 1 ends, Will realizes that control and autonomy were mere illusions. With this, Stranger Things subtly mocks human authority just like Lovecraftian stories.

The Duffer Brothers have previously cited H.P. Lovecraft's stories as an influence on Stranger Things (via EW).

Going into season 2, Stranger Things manages to retain some of its cosmic horror aspects. However, at the same time, it makes its terrors and scares a little too "tangible." This marks the show's departure from cosmic horror. As it progresses, Stranger Things bends genres and even covers coming-of-age themes in latter seasons.

Towards the end, the Netflix sci-fi show feels more like a conventional supernatural thriller than a groundbreaking take on Lovecraftian horror tropes. Perhaps, this explains why, in hindsight, most viewers are kinder towards Stranger Things' earlier chapters and more harsh when it comes to its divisive season 5.

Stranger Things is a Netflix series set in the 1980s, where the mysterious disappearance of a young boy leads a small town to uncover secret experiments and supernatural forces.

Walter Koenig Had A Hilarious Explanation For Star Trek’s Infamous Khan Plot Hole

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan has one of the franchise's most infamous plot holes, but Walter Koenig hilariously explained it away.

Walter Koenig passed away at 90 on October 25, 2026. Koenig's finest dramatic performance in Star Trek was in director Nicholas Meyer's Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan in 1982, when the supervillain Khan (Ricardo Montalban) tortured and took control of Commander Pavel Chekov's mind. There was just one problem the Star Trek fans have pointed out for 44 years: Khan claimed he never forgot Chekov's face, but he never met the Russian Starfleet Officer in Star Trek: The Original Series.

The Nacelle Company released their full 90-minute interview with Walter Koenig that they conducted for The Center Seat II: 60 Years of Star Trek. Regarding Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan's plot hole, Koenig shared his tongue-in-cheek explanation that Ensign Chekov was in Star Trek: The Original Series' "Space Seed" and encountered Khan off-screen. Read Walter's quote below:

Walter Koenig: “So I made up a story. I made up a story how Chekov was in the bathroom, and he was so sick with malapropski's malady that he couldn’t get off the john. And Khan was on the other side banging pitifully on the door because he needed to relieve his bladder. Finally, when I came out, he looked at me and said, ‘Your face I will never forget.’”

Khan's Star Trek: The Original Series episode happened in season 1, while Ensign Chekov didn't join the show until season 2. Khan was stranded on Ceti Alpha V for nearly 20 years, suffering through the destruction of the neighboring Ceti Alpha V, which reduced his world to a barren wasteland and killed Khan's wife, Lt. Marla McGivers (Madlyn Rhue).

The full story of what happened on Ceti Alpha V was revealed in the 2025 podcast, Star Trek: Khan.

Director Nicholas Meyer's explanation for why Khan recognized Chekov is simpler than Koenig's: the director didn't know. When he accepted the job as Star Trek II's director, Meyer wasn't a Star Trek fan, to begin with. Nicholas gave himself a crash course on Star Trek as he wrote the script for The Wrath of Khan (which he originally titled Star Trek II: The Undiscovered Country), and the Chekov/Khan detail slipped past him.

Koenig said he raised the plot hole about Chekov and Khan during Star Trek II's production, but they went ahead and filmed the moment where Khan said he recognized Chekov regardless. It may not be canonically accurate, but the moment has undeniable dramatic power, as Khan used Chekov as a surrogate for his master plan of vengeance against Admiral James T. Kirk (William Shatner).

Walter Koenig was a talented writer and nimble, witty storyteller who penned an episode of Star Trek: The Animated Series, so he has naturally concocted his 'Khan did meet Chekov' story that he has told often over the years, changing details occasionally, like "Chekov was trapped in the boiler room" when Khan took over the Starship Enterprise in "Space Seed." Koenig also reviewed Star Trek II on his podcast, The 7th Rule.

Rewatching Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan is extra fun when one weaves in Walter Koenig's explanation for how Khan recognized Mr. Chekov on Ceti Alpha V as head canon.

'Yellowstone' Killed Off One of Its Best Characters Too Early

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Writer and director Taylor Sheridan has, simply put, taken over our TV screens. The Jeremy Renner-led Mayor of Kingstown is gearing up for its fifth and final season, Lioness is still going strong, and Tulsa King, starring Sylvester Stallone, is currently airing its fourth installment with a spin-off on the way. None of these shows, however, would likely be in existence if it weren't for the massive success of Taylor Sheridan's Yellowstone. While the Western series, led by Kevin Costner, came to a close with Season 5, the Dutton dynasty is still being represented on television through CBS's Marshals and Paramount+'s Dutton Ranch.

While it can be hard for a multi-season series to remember every plotline from its early years — especially given how long Yellowstone's episodes can run — one forgotten character stands above the rest, awaiting real justice for his absence. With spin-off prequels such as 1883 and 1923 introducing so many other Duttons to add to the growing family tree, it's easy to forget about Dave Annable's Lee entirely. After all, the character isn't in the show all that much, and his siblings, Kayce (Luke Grimes), Beth (Kelly Reilly), and adoptive brother Jamie (Wes Bentley), often fought (usually each other) for Yellowstone's center stage. A humble cowboy like Lee wouldn't really fit into the cutthroat world Sheridan created, hence why he didn't stick around. But what happened to Lee, and why did Annable leave the show so abruptly?

If you forgot that Costner's John Dutton had a third son (well, fourth if you include Cole Hauser's Rip Wheeler, John's surrogate son), then you're not alone. Annable only played the firstborn Dutton in three episodes of Yellowstone, and in that time, his character never exactly made a real impact on anyone involved. First appearing in the extended pilot episode "Daybreak," Lee is the first in line for the Dutton throne. More than that, he's the only one of John's children who genuinely cares about the land and cattle, not just the power it buys. But Lee's tenure on the series wasn't meant to last, and neither was his memory.

Near the end of "Daybreak," Lee, himself a Livestock Agent, is murdered by Robert Long (Jeremiah Bitsui) — who just so happens to be Monica Dutton's (Kelsey Asbille) brother — during a cattle dispute on the border of the Dutton Ranch and the Broken Rock Indian Reservation. As a result, Kayce (Luke Grimes) retaliates by killing his brother-in-law in self-defense, which ends up causing more issues than Lee's death did. In many ways, Lee's demise serves as the catalyst for the events of the entire series, as the Duttons go to war with all the encroaching forces on their ancestral land. However, they sort of forget about him in the process.

After his death, Annable was understandably written off the series. For years, his only reappearance was via a photo that cameoed in the Season 2 episode ironically titled "Resurrection Day." However, Lee did return via flashbacks to his sibling's childhood in the '90s (back when Costner's John Dutton looked more like Josh Lucas), where he was played by Kip Denton — who, aside from the episode "Blood the Boy," often went uncredited.

While Lee's occasional flashback appearances kept the character alive after his Season 1 demise (his pure moment with a young Kayce at the Dutton family Christmas of 1996 was a nice surprise), they're scarce and never focus on the character himself. In the present storylines, Lee's death is forgotten by the end of the first season, and rather than fight for the Dutton Ranch in honor of his son's memory, John Dutton does so out of some mysterious promise to his father that isn't revealed until later. By some miracle, Lee's the only Dutton child who's been murdered thus far, in spite of previous attempts on other family members' lives.

No doubt part of the reason that Lee is forgotten is that the death of a son and a brother is a hard thing to meditate on. No wonder John would rather put all his effort into preserving his land rather than grieve. But what starts as something understandable becomes something less so as time continues on. As Yellowstone continues to flashback to the Duttons' childhood, Lee is forgotten about entirely. Additionally, there are entire Dutton family photos that don't include their firstborn son at all, which we'd assume was a point of contention among the siblings if we didn't know it was clearly unintentional. More than anything, it just seems like Taylor Sheridan forgot about Lee's existence completely.

Hong Kong: Joshua Wong Gets Nearly 10 More Years in Prison

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Joshua Wong speaks to protesters near the Legislative Council in Hong Kong, June 17, 2019.  © 2019 Kin Cheung/AP Photo 

(Tokyo) – The Hong Kong High Court on November 5, 2026, sentenced Joshua Wong, a well-known democracy activist, to 10 years in prison on baseless national security charges, Human Rights Watch said today. Two months of the sentence will run concurrently with his current national security sentence. Wong has been in custody since November 2020, when he was 24, and faces imprisonment until late 2036, when he will be 40.

The Hong Kong government should immediately and unconditionally release Wong. Foreign governments should denounce the sentences against him and other wrongfully detained pro-democracy activists and impose targeted sanctions on officials responsible for Hong Kong’s growing repression.

“The harsh sentence on baseless charges against Joshua Wong is cruel and unjust,” said Elaine Pearson, Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “Young people promoting social change are an asset for any vibrant society, but the Hong Kong government puts them behind bars.”

To keep Hong Kong society subdued, and to reduce the chance of an international outcry, the Hong Kong government appears to have adopted the tactic of stacking charges against influential pro-democracy leaders to ensure their prolonged detention, Human Rights Watch said. Other notable Hong Kong figures who have faced similar tactics include Jimmy Lai, founder of Apple Daily, and veteran democracy activists Lee Cheuk-yan and Albert Ho.

The court sentenced Wong, 30, under the National Security Law for “conspiring to collude with foreign forces.” The authorities accused him of “conspiring” with the activist Nathan Law and other “unknown” individuals between 2016 and November 2020 to urge foreign governments to sanction Hong Kong and Chinese government officials as they advocated for Hong Kong’s “democratic self-determination.” 

The prosecution relied heavily on the international lobbying and media advocacy that Wong and Demosisto, the political party Wong co-founded in 2016, carried out before the National Security Law took effect. Wong initiated online petitions, spoke to international media, and met with foreign officials to draw attention to human rights abuses in Hong Kong. He urged governments to sanction Chinese and Hong Kong officials responsible for abuses and to stop selling weapons to Hong Kong police that they use to carry out rights violations.

Wong had mostly stopped these activities before the National Security Law became effective. Demosisto disbanded on June 30, 2020, and Wong resigned from the group. However, the government quoted social media posts by Wong as ostensible evidence that his alleged “conspiracy” with Law to commit “foreign collusion” continued after the law took effect. In these posts, Wong said he “would not change his views” and that he would “continue to seek support from the international community” in his personal capacity, while Law and others abroad would continue their international advocacy.

The government also cited Wong’s subsequent opinion pieces and media interviews, though none directly called for sanctions, which the National Security Law specifically prohibits. It also cited his reposts of Law’s posts broadly calling for sanctions against Chinese officials and Hong Kong police.

The court ruled that Wong’s offense was of a “grave nature.” The National Security Law sets prison terms of 3 to 10 years for “foreign collusion,” or 10 years to life for offenses deemed to be of a “grave nature.” Wong formally pleaded guilty on September 2. A guilty plea normally results in a one-third sentence reduction. But a sentence reduction cannot bring the term below the statutory minimum.

Because of reporting restrictions on pretrial committal proceedings, when a magistrate determines whether to send a case to a higher court for trial, the public did not know the details of the accusations against Wong until the formal plea. The government enacted a law in 2024 to enable the court, on national security grounds, to deny the defendant’s right to ask the court to lift restrictions on reporting about the case.

By restricting media reporting on the pretrial proceedings in national security cases, the government can control the narrative and undermine the fundamental legal principle of open justice.

Since Beijing imposed the National Security Law on Hong Kong in June 2020, Hong Kong authorities have used the new legal regime—along with existing laws—to rapidly dismantle civil society and detain, imprison, or force into exile hundreds of activists and government critics.

Wong has been in custody since November 2020, when he pleaded guilty to two charges of organizing and inciting an unauthorized assembly linked to the 2019 citywide protest movement. After that, he pleaded guilty to five more charges on separate occasions, including for violating an anti-mask law, participating in an unauthorized vigil for the 1989 Tiananmen Massacre, and breaching a court ban to disclose a police officer’s personal information.

While serving these sentences, Wong was charged with two national security offenses, which carry far harsher penalties. In January 2021, Wong was charged with “conspiring to commit subversion” for participating in an unofficial democratic primary in 2020 and sentenced to another four years and eight months in prison. 

Before Wong could complete these sentences, the authorities brought the “foreign collusion” charge against him in June 2025, ensuring that he would remain in prison.

Wong became one of Hong Kong’s best-known democracy activists as a teenager, after leading a massive 2012 protest against a government educational curriculum widely seen as political indoctrination. He became the global face of Hong Kong’s democracy movement during the 2014 Umbrella Movement and co-founded Demosisto with Law and others in 2016. The party disbanded on June 30, 2020, hours before the National Security Law came into effect.

The National Security Law’s sweeping provisions have been used to punish peaceful protest and expression. Its use against Wong and others violates the right to freedom of expression under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which is incorporated into Hong Kong law through the city’s de facto constitution, the Basic Law, and the Bill of Rights Ordinance.

“Beijing has paid no real price for its increasing repression in Hong Kong,” Pearson said. “Concerned governments should press for the release of Wong and other activists, impose targeted sanctions against implicated officials, and review their relationship with China more broadly in light of the deepening abuses.”

making room for nature: the urban landscapes of kongjian yu

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

what happens when urban landscapes are designed around ecological processes rather than human convenience alone? from restored waterways to constructed wetlands, these projects raise questions about biodiversity, interdependence, and whether cities can become shared habitats for multiple forms of life.

In Landscapes for Adaptation: Evidence from China, architect and Harvard professor Charles Waldheim examines 60 built experiments realized by Chinese landscape architect Kongjian Yu and his practice Turenscape over the past two decades. Analyzing their environmental performance, the book documents how landscape architecture can respond to flooding, climate volatility, and ecological change through implemented projects. These built experiments offer a starting point for exploring a broader question: can cities be designed as shared habitats for humans and other forms of life?

Yu, founder of Turenscape and a leading advocate of the sponge city concept, has spent decades arguing that urban development should work with natural processes. His approach uses landscape as infrastructure, allowing water, vegetation, and existing ecological systems to play an active role in addressing flooding, pollution, and habitat loss.

In Shanghai, Houtan Park demonstrates how landscape architecture can make ecological processes part of the urban fabric. Designed by Turenscape and completed in 2010 for the Shanghai World Expo, the 14-hectare park occupies a former industrial site along the Huangpu River. Once used by a steel factory and shipyard, the land was heavily degraded, while the adjacent river was severely polluted.

The design builds on the industrial past of the site, transforming its existing landscape into a new ecological and public space. A constructed wetland runs through the park, using a sequence of terraces, cascades, and wetland plants to treat polluted river water. The system was designed to treat approximately 2,400 cubic meters of water per day, improving its quality from Lower Grade V to Grade III for non-potable uses. The wetland also forms part of the flood-management strategy of the park, while its planted river edge creates conditions for aquatic and bird habitats. Reclaimed industrial materials, agricultural terraces, and a network of paths connect these ecological functions with the public experience of the park.

Water treatment, flood protection, habitat creation, and public access operate within the same landscape. Houtan Park makes infrastructure visible, allowing visitors to encounter environmental processes that are usually hidden behind concrete walls and underground networks.

A related approach appears in Yichang Yunhe Park in Hubei province. Designed by Turenscape and completed in 2013, the 12-hectare project transforms twelve polluted, abandoned fish ponds into what the practice describes as a ‘green sponge’. The design uses the existing water bodies as the foundation for a system that cleanses polluted canal water and remediates contaminated stormwater. The project relies on regenerative design and minimal intervention. The former ponds become part of a wider landscape of vegetation and water, while paths and elevated walkways allow people to move through the site. Its significance lies partly in the decision to work with an existing, damaged landscape.

In Ningbo, the Yinzhou Central River Transformation explores the potential of restoring an urban waterway. Completed in 2010, the project introduces wetland planting along the riverbanks, alongside walkways and areas for public use. The intervention softens the engineered edges of the river, retaining its role as part of the city’s water infrastructure.

These are some examples of Turenscape’s projects that suggest that ecological urbanism does not necessarily depend on introducing untouched nature into the city, but it can also involve reconsidering infrastructure that already exists: drainage channels, abandoned ponds, industrial waterfronts, and flood-control systems. Such sites become opportunities to restore ecological functions, changing how people encounter water and landscape. However, creating landscapes that manage water and support vegetation is not necessarily the same as designing cities around the needs of other forms of life. In Yu’s projects, wetlands and planted riverbanks help reduce flooding, improve water quality, and provide habitats. Their value also depends on whether other species can thrive there, how ecological systems develop over time, and how well these landscapes are maintained.

In her 2016 book Staying with the Trouble: Making Kin in the Chthulucene, feminist theorist Donna Haraway challenges the idea that humans exist separately from the rest of the living world, describing life as a network of interdependencies in which organisms and environments shape one another. Her concept of sympoiesis, or making-with, offers a way to understand the world as something produced collectively rather than controlled by a single species.

Seen through Haraway’s ideas, Yu’s projects can be understood as more than infrastructure for managing water and flooding. The wetlands and river corridors also create environments where plants, insects, birds, microorganisms, and aquatic species interact. This shifts the focus from what these landscapes provide for people to the wider ecological relationships they support and how these relationships shape urban life.

This does not mean that ecological infrastructure automatically produces a more-than-human city. A landscape can support biodiversity while remaining primarily designed around human priorities. Its ecological value depends on factors such as long-term maintenance, water quality, habitat connectivity, and whether different species can establish and survive. The presence of vegetation or the appearance of a natural landscape is not, on its own, evidence of a functioning ecosystem. There are also social questions to consider. Green infrastructure can improve urban environments, but it does not necessarily address unequal access to public space, the displacement of existing communities, or the uneven distribution of environmental benefits.

Ecological restoration can become part of wider redevelopment processes, sometimes increasing land values and changing who can afford to live nearby. Assessing these projects therefore requires looking beyond their environmental performance to consider how they affect both local communities and other forms of life.

The projects documented in Waldheim’s Landscapes for Adaptation: Evidence from China demonstrate how landscape architecture can contribute to climate adaptation through built, functioning systems. Their significance extends beyond the technical integration of water management and public space. They suggest that cities can be understood as environments shaped by relationships between human activity and ecological processes.

Yu’s work offers a practical foundation for this shift, while Haraway’s thinking invites us to take its implications further. If cities are understood as shared environments, their success cannot be judged solely by how efficiently they protect people from environmental change. It must also be considered in terms of the relationships they allow to develop between different forms of life. The more-than-human city is not necessarily a finished ecological utopia, but an ongoing process of learning how to live alongside the many species and systems that already make urban life possible.

Forests planted to fight climate change may be fueling wildfires

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A global study reveals that planting forests can increase wildfire risk, threatening carbon storage and challenging climate strategies.

There’s something darkly ironic about planting a forest to store carbon and watching it go up in flames.

But new research suggests that planting trees can sometimes increase wildfire risk, threatening the very carbon those forests were meant to store.

A new global study reveals just how much that risk varies across different landscapes.

An international team of researchers from Spain, Chile, and Finland analyzed satellite records spanning more than two decades to track what happens when non-forested land gets converted into planted forests.

Their dataset was enormous: 67,248 patches across the world’s temperate regions, monitored from 2001 to 2023 using MODIS land-cover and fire records.

After accounting for climate, accessibility, and patch size, afforestation was associated with a 26% increase in fire occurrence per hectare per year and a 23% increase in expected burned area when fires did ignite.

“Forest plantations change the fuel conditions in which fires start and spread,” said lead author Irene Repeto-Deudero from the University of Cádiz and the University of Eastern Finland.

“We wanted to understand how these changes differ across landscapes, rather than assuming that tree planting has the same consequences everywhere.”

Those global averages, though, hide enormous variation. The researchers compared fire patterns across four major types of temperate landscapes.

Temperate conifer forests took the worst hit. Fire occurrence there surged 2.7-fold, and the expected burned area roughly doubled.

These landscapes already contain large amounts of vegetation and face increasingly extreme fire weather.

Adding dense plantations can create more continuous fuel, allowing fires to spread more easily.

Mediterranean forests, woodlands, and scrub saw a 38% jump in fire occurrence and a 9% rise in burned area. In absolute terms, the probability of fire per 100 hectares over a decade climbed from 7.8% to 11.0%.

These regions already contend with seasonal drought, flammable vegetation, and frequent human-caused ignitions.

Piling more biomass onto that tinderbox gives extreme fire weather more fuel to work with.

Temperate broadleaf and mixed forests told a stranger story. Fire occurrence actually dropped by 23% after afforestation – but expected burned area grew by 20%.

Fewer fires, in other words, but the ones that did ignite burned larger. That trade-off matters more than it might seem.

Nearly half of the world’s planted forests sit in this biome. Even modest shifts in fire behavior could carry substantial consequences.

Temperate grasslands, savannas, and shrublands were the one bright spot. There, fire occurrence fell 18% with no significant increase in burned area.

“These contrasting responses show why local ecological conditions must guide afforestation decisions,” explained study co-author Alejandro Miranda from the University of La Frontera.

“The same intervention can have very different consequences depending on the vegetation, climate, and existing fire regime.”

Geographically, the increases in wildfire activity clustered in regions that are already under fire stress.

These include western North America, central Chile, southern Europe, southwestern Australia, and parts of South Africa.

In these regions, expanding plantations and increasingly dangerous fire weather could combine to intensify wildfire risks.

The study also highlights a data point that deserves more attention. Plantations are roughly twice as likely to burn as native temperate forests.

And monoculture plantations – single-species stands planted in uniform rows – have been linked to particularly extreme fire behavior.

All of this creates a serious accounting problem for climate policy.

Afforestation has become a flagship mitigation strategy, with temperate regions identified as high-priority planting zones.

But wildfires don’t just destroy trees. They also release stored carbon back into the atmosphere, potentially reducing or even reversing the climate benefits of tree planting.

“These results therefore require that tree planting initiatives consider their exposure to fire,” said Susana Gómez-González from the University of Cádiz.

“As the climate warms, increased wildfire risk may impact the capacity of forest plantations to store carbon in the long term.”

Planted forests provide wood, protect soil, and deliver a range of ecosystem services. The point isn’t to stop – it’s to think harder about where to plant them.

“Plantations are necessary in many cases to produce wood and other ecosystem services, including soil protection,” said Blas Mola-Yudego from the University of Eastern Finland.

“However, their location must be carefully planned to avoid unintended consequences on local fire regimes.”

“Fire risk needs to be considered from the outset, alongside production goals and environmental benefits.”

The findings challenge the assumption that planting more trees automatically delivers greater climate benefits.

In some landscapes, the additional wildfire risk could undermine the carbon gains that plantations are meant to provide.

For policymakers counting on planted forests to help combat climate change, the message is clear: where trees are planted may matter just as much as how many are planted.

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

A young grizzly in British Columbia (Photo: Colleen Gara / Getty Images)

Two hikers are dead after a suspected bear attack on Indigenous lands in British Columbia, provincial wildlife officials say. The incident comes just a year after a bear encounter in the same area left four people seriously injured.

Rescuers discovered one body on the afternoon of Nov. 2 near the Snooka West Trail in Bella Coola, leading to a search for a second, missing victim.

That search concluded late Tuesday night when searchers found the second hiker dead. Officials with the B.C. Conservation Officer Service (BCCOS) believe both were attacked by a grizzly bear, and efforts are ongoing to locate and trap any bears in the area for assessment.

Authorities and family members have since identified the pair as 48-year-old Jennifer Balcarras and her daughter, 20-year-old Selah Reed.

“It is not yet clear whether more than one bear was involved,” BCCOS Investigator Jeff Tyre told Backpacker. “Any captured bear will be assessed. If it is determined to have been involved in the attack, it will be dispatched in accordance with provincial human-wildlife conflict response guidelines to protect public safety.”

Family members first reported Balcarras and Reed overdue on Sunday, Nov. 1. Police and SAR operators found the first human remains on Monday afternoon, and called BCCOS due to evidence that suggested an animal was involved.

The victims’ remains have since been returned to their families, and the Snooka West Trail remains closed.

In the wake of the deaths, tribal authorities of the Nuxalk Nation, the First Nations people who live in the Bella Coola valley, expressed their support. Samuel Schooner, elected Chief of the Nuxalk Nation, said in a statement that the tribe understands firsthand the trauma this incident will inflict on the victims’ family members and on the survivors of last year’s attack.

That incident occurred near Highway 20 on Nov. 20, 2025, when a grizzly attacked a local school group taking a lunch break. Adults were able to repel the animal with bear spray and a bear banger, but not before it severely injured four people, including three children. The four individuals were rushed to the hospital in varying conditions, and seven others required care at the scene.

No one died, and investigators continued to search for grizzlies in the area, eventually determining three bears could have been involved. Over the following two weeks, officers captured eight adult and juvenile grizzlies for forensic testing. All eight animals were eventually collared and relocated after they could not be forensically connected with the attack.

A total of 24 officers were involved in the response to that attack. Some reportedly drove 14 hours overnight to hand-deliver samples to the Alberta Wildlife Forensic Laboratory. BCCOS described both the response and the incident as extraordinary.

“In my 34 years of experience, I have not seen an attack like this with a large group of people,” Kevin Van Damme, with BCCOS, told Global News at the time. “So this is extremely rare and I think the public needs to understand that part.”

As 2025 field operations concluded, the Conservation Office reported decreasing bear sightings and pointed out that grizzlies in the area typically transition from hyperphagia to denning in or around December.

It’s unclear how long the current search will continue. A spokesperson for BCCOS said investigators were unavailable for comment due to heightened field activity.

“Our thoughts are with the family of the victims during this tragic time,” the agency said. “We are in contact with the family to offer any support we can as we continue to investigate this incident.”

Uranium cubes reveal why the German nuclear project failed

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required.”

During World War II, physicists on the Manhattan Project worried that the Nazi German nuclear program might achieve the first nuclear bomb before the US and its allies. That’s not what transpired, but historians have debated just how close the Germans came to achieving criticality in their last attempt at building a reactor: the B8 pile. Werner Heisenberg, who led that effort, claimed that they were close and had only needed a little more uranium. But according to a paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences Nexus, that was not the case: the project simply didn’t have sufficient uranium and heavy water to build a working bomb, given the design they were using.

This particular research started in 2013, when University of Maryland physicist Timothy Koeth—a co-author on the new paper—received the gift of a mysterious heavy metal cube from a friend. He instantly recognized it as one of the uranium cubes used by German scientists in their unsuccessful attempt to build a working nuclear reactor. Only 14 such cubes are known to exist today.

In 2019, we reported on Koeth’s quest to track down the cube’s origins, as well as several other similar cubes that had somehow found their way across the Atlantic. At the time, Koeth and his partner in the quest, then-graduate student Miriam “Mimi” Hiebert, had tracked down ten such cubes around the US. For instance, the Smithsonian Institute had a German uranium cube in storage. They tracked a third cube to Harvard University, where it was regularly passed around to students in introductory physics classes as a curiosity.

Underpinning the Manhattan Project in the US was the fear that German scientists under Adolf Hitler’s Nazi regime would beat the Allies to a nuclear bomb. The Germans had a two-year head start, but according to Koeth, “fierce competition over finite resources, bitter interpersonal rivalries, and ineffectual scientific management” resulted in significant delays in their progress toward achieving a sustained nuclear reaction. German nuclear scientists were separated into three isolated groups based in Berlin (B), Gottow (G), and Leipzig (L).

Heisenberg headed the Berlin group, and as the Allied forces advanced in the winter of 1944, Heisenberg moved his team to a cave under a castle in a small town called Haigerloch—now the site of the Atomkeller Museum. That’s where the group built the B8 reactor. It resembled an “ominous chandelier,” per Koeth, because it was composed of 664 uranium cubes strung together with aircraft cable and then submerged in a tank of heavy water shielded by graphite to prevent radiation exposure.

As the German scientists were racing against time, Manhattan Project lead Lieutenant General Leslie Groves kicked off a covert mission dubbed “Alsos,” with the express purpose of gathering information and materials related to Germany’s scientific research. When Allied forces closed in, Heisenberg took apart the B8 experiment and buried the uranium cubes in a field, ferreting away key documentation in a latrine. (Pity Samuel Goudsmit, the poor physicist who had to dig those out.) Heisenberg escaped by bicycle, carrying a few cubes in a backpack.

As Heisenberg reported, the German scientists’ final experiment failed because the amount of uranium in the cubes was insufficient to trigger a sustained nuclear reaction. But Heisenberg was confident that “a slight increase in its size would have been sufficient to start off the process of energy production.” A model described in a 2009 paper supported that, showing the group would have needed only 50 percent more uranium cubes to make the design work.

Koeth and Hiebert uncovered a box of declassified documents about German uranium in the National Archives and found about 400 other uranium cubes from a separate reactor experiment by the Gottow group. They concluded that the combined inventory would have been more than enough to achieve criticality in the B8 reactor, suggesting that Germany’s secretive, isolationist approach hampered their nuclear program because the two groups weren’t sharing information or resources.

This latest paper builds on that earlier work but reaches a different conclusion. Koeth, Hiebert, and their co-authors analyzed two of the surviving uranium cubes, along with combing through historical records, technical reports, correspondence, and similar archival sources to learn more about the uranium, heavy water, and graphite blocks used for the B8 reactor experiment.

Among their findings: The B8 reactor would have needed between 1,292 and 1,775 uranium cubes to achieve criticality, and twice the heavy water produced by the Norsk Hydro plant in Vemork, Norway—the only source at the time—over the course of the entire war. In short, “Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required,” the authors wrote.

The authors also cited prior work noting key differences in American and German graphite production. The former used petroleum coke. While some of this supply reached Germany, the British naval blockade in January 1940 deprived the Germans of that vital resource. The German nuclear physicists had to use metallurgical coke from the Ruhr, which had twice as many impurities as American coke. (The US scientists had similar issues until they located an unusually low-boron coke in Pennsylvania.) Thus, “no natural uranium, graphite-moderated pile could have gone critical” with the materials available to the German scientists at the time.

Divers Spent 7 Years Hunting a Ghost Ship Linked to a Nazi Spy Plot. They Just Found It.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A citizen-led search found the SS Cornwallis—and revived a forgotten Nazi spy story in Maine.

For seven years, a group of volunteer divers scoured historical records and the coast of Maine in search of one of New England’s largest undiscovered World War II shipwrecks. Now the SS Cornwallis has been found. The Nomad Exploration Team located the Canadian merchant vessel, which a German U-boat sank off Mount Desert Rock in 1944. The discovery also helps tell the story of the 43 people aboard the boat who lost their lives in the attack and revives interest in the German spies linked to the sinking.

The team used side-scan sonar to map the ocean floor where they believed the Cornwallis might rest, and they nearly overlooked the 400-foot, 5,458-ton vessel. Some of the group’s most harrowing dives later confirmed that the sonar anomaly really was the lost ship. “It was exhilaration,” said Danny Allan, a founding team member, according to Seacoastonline.

The nine-member, New Hampshire-based team began the search in 2019 with a rough location about 10 miles southwest of Mount Desert Rock. By drawing on historical records, the ship’s blueprints, and local fishermen’s observations, they narrowed the search to three likely sites. Even so, the work wasn’t going to be easy.

“There are big tides up off the Mount Desert area,” team member Jeff Goodreau told the Portland Press Herald. “A lot of weather, a lot of fog … Any way you look at it, the challenge is just getting there, whether it’s a five-hour run from Portsmouth or trailering a boat up there.”

In fact, the team nearly missed the wreck twice. On a first pass within 300 feet of it, they didn’t recognize the sonar image. Later, while they were reprogramming the GPS, they almost skipped past it again when it showed up just 100 feet away. “We would’ve written off that whole section as ‘we already looked there’ and wouldn’t have found it,” Allan said.

Reaching the wreck, nearly 400 feet down, was just as tricky. Team member Don Ferrara called it the scariest dive he’d ever done, and Goodreau described the site as deep, cold, and full of current. Still, “We were over the moon,” Ferrara said of the find.

“You’re the first person to see and touch this wreck literally in 80 years, so you’re ecstatic,” Allan said. “But it’s also extremely sobering because this is a grave. 43 souls lost their lives on this wreck.”

The tragedy happened in the Gulf of Maine as the Canadian ship was heading home to Saint John, New Brunswick, after loading sugar and molasses in Barbados. At 6 a.m. on December 3, 1944, the German submarine U-1230 fired a single torpedo that struck the forward cargo hold, and the Cornwallis sank in less than 10 minutes, according to the team. Only five of the 48 people aboard, most of them Canadian, survived. Fishing boats picked up the survivors, and a U.S. Navy vessel then carried them to Rockland, Maine.

The Cornwallis wasn’t a target the U-boat had been hunting. “The U-boat wasn’t looking for them,” Ferrara said. “They were just there.”

The sinking alerted the U.S. to the U-boat’s presence and prompted an investigation into its mission. By interviewing witnesses in Hancock, the FBI learned about Germany’s Operation Elster (Operation Magpie in English). Just days before the attack, the same submarine had landed two German spies, Erich Gimpel and William Colepaugh, near Hancock Point. Their mission was to gather intelligence in the United States. The pair took a taxi to Bangor, a train to Portland, and then made their way to New York City. In the end, the plot fell apart from within: Colepaugh lost his nerve, confided in a friend, and turned himself in, which led to both men’s capture.

This wasn’t the first time the Cornwallis had been torpedoed. The ship was built in Vancouver, British Columbia, in 1921. In 1942, a torpedo struck it while it was anchored at Barbados, and it partially sank in shallow water. After repairs, it returned to service.

“For the team, finding the wreck was the culmination of years of persistence,” Allan said in a statement, according to MassLive. “For the families and communities connected to the ship, it brings renewed attention to a largely forgotten tragedy.”

The Cornwallis is the latest find for the team, which had already located the USS Eagle 56 and the SS William H. Machen. “I love history,” Allan said. “My father was a history teacher. Other people like to see pretty fish and reefs. For me, I love to go see wrecks.”

The president can veto legislation passed by Congress. Congress can override that veto with a two-thirds majority in both chambers. The legislature also handles impeachment proceedings. In addition, the president can only appoint Cabinet members, US ambassadors and federal judges from a list of nominees the Senate approves.

Trump’s Republicans currently hold majorities in both chambers of Congress, making it easier for the president to govern. Gossamer Gear The Free Tent Review: A Freestanding Double-Wall Tent Under 2 Pounds?

The Gossamer Gear The Free is a one-person, double-wall, freestanding ultralight backpacking tent. Unlike the brand’s traditional trekking-pole shelters, The Free uses a DAC aluminum hub-and-pole system to create a freestanding structure that can be easily repositioned at camp.

Constructed with Gossamer Gear’s exceptionally tear-resistant, PFAS-free nylon fabric, The Free aims to deliver durability and weather protection without unnecessary weight. At just 29.8 ounces, this tent joins the Durston X-Dome 1 and the Nemo Hornet Osmo 1 as one of the lightest freestanding, double-wall tents in the world — and it’s the most affordable of the three, to boot.

But does its minimalist design strike the right balance between weight savings, comfort, and practicality on the trail?

After taking The Free on a section hike of the Continental Divide Trail in northern New Mexico, I came away impressed by its weather-resistant construction and sturdy design. However, its narrow dimensions and some setup quirks left me wishing Gossamer Gear had not sacrificed quite so much interior space in the pursuit of ultralight performance.

I tested The Free during a section hike of the Continental Divide Trail in northern New Mexico, during which the weather was frequently miserable and wet. The conditions gave me an excellent opportunity to evaluate the tent’s weather protection, condensation management, and overall durability.

One of the most noticeable differences between The Free and my previous shelters was its double-wall design. I appreciated being able to avoid the persistent dampness that comes from brushing against a single-wall tent’s interior in wet conditions. In addition, a few nights were particularly windy, and I was impressed by how solidly The Free held up.

However, my overall experience was mixed. I felt that The Free lacked the interior roominess I’ve grown accustomed to in The One. Despite being a relatively small user, I often felt cramped and occasionally claustrophobic.

The Free’s DAC aluminum hub-and-pole system distinguishes it from Gossamer Gear’s traditional trekking-pole shelters. The tent can stand without stakes when pitched with its inner mesh body, making it possible to move the shelter to a different spot before securing it.

Like most freestanding tents, the fly requires staking out the vestible for proper tension, and additional stakes for guylines in less-than-ideal conditions. While the freestanding design sounds wonderfully convenient, I found the initial setup more complicated than expected.

Gossamer Gear developed a custom ultralight shelter fabric called ClearSkies. It combines 15D recycled Robic fabric with a 20D dope-dyed ripstop silicone/polyurethane coating. The company designed this material to improve strength and waterproofing while avoiding PFAS.

The fabric has a reported hydrostatic head rating of 4,923 mm — waterproof enough to withstand heavy downpours and wind-driven rain. The seams are factory-taped.

The fabric is manufactured using a dope-dye process, which incorporates color into the polymer before the fibers are formed. This can improve colorfastness compared with conventional surface dyeing and reduce water and energy consumption during manufacturing.

In the field, the fabric felt reassuringly robust for such a lightweight shelter. During wet and windy conditions, I appreciated the protection it provided without the bulk or weight of a heavier tent.

Although the double-wall construction kept me from directly touching the wet fly, moisture accumulated on its underside, particularly when I kept the vestibule closed to retain warmth overnight. By morning, the fly could be soaked and noticeably heavier.

The fly also sits close to the inner mesh body, helping maximize the available interior space. Unfortunately, that limited separation meant that contact between the inner tent and the wet fly could transfer moisture inside. The Free offers the advantages of a double-wall shelter, but its compact geometry makes condensation management especially important.

The Free’s inner mesh body must be pitched first. Two aluminum poles connect through a central pivoting hub that attaches to the inner tent’s ceiling, creating the tent’s basic structure.

This inner-first setup became a drawback in rainy weather. While I worked to get the fly in place, the mesh interior was exposed to the elements. Since the fly was often still damp from the previous night, assembling the shelter without adding more moisture wasn’t always easy.

The color-coded poles and corresponding corner attachments help guide assembly, but I struggled to keep everything in place while inserting the poles into their designated corners (often with comical results). In breezy conditions, the lightweight tent threatened to slither away before I could finish setting it up. I initially tried holding the corners down with my feet, but eventually resorted to staking them down. That rather defeated the convenience I had expected from a freestanding tent.

The Free is reasonably straightforward once you become familiar with its pole arrangement, but I wouldn’t describe the setup as effortless, especially when the weather is uncooperative.

As I mentioned above, The Free felt more cramped to me than Gossamer Gear’s The One, which I have used extensively. Although the two tents have similar floor dimensions (The Free is even a few inches longer and wider), The Free has a significantly lower peak height and the angle of the side walls feels more restrictive.

The Free has a tapered floor measuring 34 inches wide at the head and 21.5 inches at the foot. At 86 inches long, it’s long enough for most solo backpackers, but the narrowing floor is a significant consideration when choosing a sleeping pad.

My pad fit comfortably, with a little room to spare. However, anyone using a wide sleeping pad should check the dimensions carefully. Per Gossamer Gear’s website, “while The Free’s floor dimensions don’t technically support a traditional 25″ wide pad, it does fit — it’ll just push the side walls a bit. For the best fit, we recommend going with a wide tapered pad instead.”

At 5 foot 7, I could sit upright and even stretch my legs inside. Still, I frequently brushed against the sides or top of the tent. When the fly was wet, those accidental touches could bring moisture into the living space. The compact dimensions are understandable in a shelter designed for minimal weight, but the trade-off was more noticeable than I expected. I often felt confined, particularly when spending extended periods inside during bad weather.

Gossamer Gear lists a maximum user height of 6 feet 2 inches, but height alone doesn’t tell the whole story. Sleeping-pad width, sleeping position, and how much room you want to sit up or move around all influence how comfortable this tent will feel.

The D-shaped doors open into a modest vestibule with enough room for my backpack and a few smaller items. This was useful for keeping gear protected from the weather without cluttering the sleeping area.

My main complaint was the vestibule door’s toggle system. When rolled back and secured, the door tended to hang into the tent rather than staying neatly out of the way. One morning, I cinched it closed with the toggle, only to have accumulated ice fall directly onto my sleeping bag. Magnets or a more effective door-retention system would have made this detail less frustrating.

The Free includes two interior pockets: one near the ceiling and another beside the door. While I appreciated having somewhere to stash small essentials, I found the pockets less useful than expected. Items tended to slide out, making it difficult to keep things securely organized. For an ultralight tent, the storage options are welcome, but I would have preferred pockets that hold their contents more reliably.

The Free impressed me with its sturdy pole structure and durable-feeling fabric. It handled windy conditions well, and the double-wall design provided a welcome barrier between me and the wet fly.

However, the fly’s tendency to collect condensation and its close proximity to the inner mesh deserve consideration. Proper ventilation and careful pitching are important, especially in cold, damp conditions.

This was the tent’s biggest weakness for me. Although I could sit up and move around to a limited extent, the tapered floor and close-fitting walls made the interior feel cramped. Taller users and anyone carrying a wide sleeping pad should pay particular attention to the dimensions.

The color-coded pole system makes the design reasonably intuitive, but pitching the inner tent first can be inconvenient in rain or wind. I also found myself staking the corners during setup to keep the lightweight body under control.

The freestanding structure is useful, but it doesn’t eliminate every need for stakes or make the pitching process as simple as I had hoped.

At 29.8 ounces, The Free is impressively light for a double-wall, freestanding shelter. Its compact packed dimensions make it an appealing option for backpackers who want to keep their pack weight down without switching to a trekking-pole shelter.

The ClearSkies fabric feels like a thoughtful balance between low weight and ruggedness. Despite its delicate-sounding 15-denier thickness, the material is designed for improved tear resistance, and I was impressed by the tent’s overall robustness during my hike.

The use of recycled Robic fabric and PFAS-free materials is a welcome feature. The dope-dyed fabric construction is also designed to improve colorfastness. These choices make The Free appealing to backpackers who want a lightweight shelter without overlooking material considerations.

The Free comes with six aluminum stakes, four guylines, a pole-repair section, a stuff sack, and a pole bag.

The Gossamer Gear The Free makes a compelling case for an ultralight, freestanding backpacking tent. And while it isn’t the featherweight champion when compared to minimalist trekking pole shelters, The Free is still one of the lightest tents in the freestanding double-wall category. At $400, it wins heavily on its value-to-weight ratio. However, its impressive weight savings come with noticeable compromises in livability and convenience.

If minimizing pack weight is your top priority, it deserves a look. If comfort and easy setup matter just as much, I would consider a roomier alternative.

A new book looks at how Marcel Duchamp’s ‘impossible’ love for the sculptor Maria Martins changed his work

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Marcel Duchamp’s final work, Étant donnés (1946-66), an assemblage created in collaboration with Maria Martins © Artists Rights Society/ADAGP/Association Marcel Duchamp

Glacial, calculated, distant, sly: these are among the adjectives used to describe the “godfather of conceptual art” Marcel Duchamp (1887-1968). In the opening chapter of Impossible, a monograph by the prominent Duchamp scholar Francis M. Naumann, the artist appears as a (mostly) reticent introvert. But over the course of 192 richly illustrated pages, that familiar, spectral figure becomes flesh and blood: tender, wistful, even romantic. Duchamp falls in love.

Naumann’s account of the French-American artist’s relationship with the Brazilian sculptor Maria Martins (1894-1973) unfolds through a series of speculative scenes that draw, at times uneasily, on the conventions of the romance novel, interwoven with formal analysis that demonstrates reciprocal artistic influence. The author’s nearly five decades of writing on Duchamp, along with the rigorous notes and chronology included at the back of the volume, help temper any initial scepticism about the balance between projection and documentation. From Duchamp and Martins’s first meeting in March 1943 until their respective deaths five years apart, Naumann argues that the two sustained a profound emotional connection, even if the most intense—and presumably physical—phase of their relationship lasted less than a decade. Despite Duchamp’s evident longing, Martins never left her three children or her husband, a Brazilian ambassador to the US.

What first attracted the two artists intellectually to one another is not fully known. But from early on, Duchamp was no longer indifferent, at least where Martins was concerned. Asked to design an announcement for an exhibition at Julien Levy Gallery in December 1943, he placed a cupid figure atop a chessboard depicting an endgame problem with no way to win. “There is no solution,” the artist had famously said, “because there is no problem”. Now, however, there was a problem. Despite a previous marriage and a long-term relationship with the American artist Mary Reynolds, Duchamp had never been in love before. At this point, 26 years had passed since he made Fountain and 20 since he had declared The Bride Stripped Bare by Her Bachelors, Even (The Large Glass) definitively unfinished. It was the problem of Martins, Naumann suggests, that reanimated Duchamp’s interest in making—shifting the affair from a biographical register into a lens through which to view Duchamp’s late work.

The Impossible, III (1946) by Maria Martins is a comment on her relationship with Duchamp © MoMA, New York

As the relationship develops, Naumann focuses on the lovers’ sculptures, exchanged or produced in parallel, as a metonym for the physical encounters he is otherwise obliged to omit. There is Paysage fautif (faulty landscape), an intimate piece made with his own seminal fluid on plastic and backed with black satin, that Duchamp gave to Martins in 1946. And then her cast bronze The Impossible, III (at the Museum of Modern Art in New York) from the same year, in which two creatures, their heads ringed in tendrils, reach toward one another in a gesture that is at once an embrace and a devouring. Martins was, Naumann writes, “unquestionably referring to the impossibility of her relationship with Duchamp”. The goddess figures borrowed from Brazilian folklore that populated her second show at the Valentine Gallery in New York became increasingly abstract and psychologically charged as her career progressed, perhaps reflecting Duchamp’s own preference for the cerebral over the real, the concept over the image.

There is then no shortage of confusion surrounding Duchamp’s final work, Étant donnés: 1º La chute d’eau, 2º Le gaz d’éclairage (Given: 1. the waterfall, 2. the illuminating gas, 1946-66). Why, after decades of apparent withdrawal, would the celebrated Modernist devote 20 years to a highly illusionistic installation of a nude woman posed in a landscape that recalls Gustave Courbet’s The Origin of the World (1866)—the very tradition of “retinal art” he had long disavowed? Naumann’s retelling, which frames the work as the culmination of their coupling, is in this sense instructive. Martins emerges not merely as muse or model but as collaborator. She lends her body to be cast, but also choreographs its position, advises on process and technique. There is no precedent in Duchamp’s oeuvre for the imagery, yet echoes of Martins’s prints and sculptures persist in both the figure and the vegetal setting. Even after she returned to Brazil and their contact waned, Duchamp retained, through work on the diorama, a means of beholding her body and sustaining their artistic tête-à-tête.

Duchamp’s notebook page inscribed with “That is for Maria”

Interspersed passages from Duchamp’s letters to Martins reveal the force of his affections, rendering it difficult to regard Étant donnés as anything other than an act of devotion, however strange or disconcerting it may appear from the outside. The florid sentiments strain against the claim that it is just another one of his jokes. “At heart, my little one, I am profoundly sad, for I can see all our days passing rapidly and fading away,” he writes. And elsewhere: “I feel totally lost now that we are cut off from each other… This isolation hurts.” Reproduced in one of the final chapters is a notebook page with the words “Please do not throw this on the floor” typed above a hand-drawn heart, filled in with red crayon, and scrawled below it: “that is for Maria”.

Although Martins’s letters to Duchamp are not available to the public, it is said that she kept a photograph of him in her studio, and another on her charm bracelet, alongside a lock of his hair—observations that complicate Naumann’s repeated casting of her as a “femme fatale” who “did whatever she needed to get a man to fall in love with her”. The absence of her subjectivity on the page creates an asymmetry that the text acknowledges yet cannot quite overcome. What she truly made of Duchamp—of his work, their relationship—remains largely unknown.

After her husband’s death in 1965, Martins returned to New York and visited Duchamp, who was by then married to Alexina “Teeny” Duchamp. As Naumann notes, it is possible that he signed and dated Étant donnés then and there, unable to complete it until finally, at long last, they were together again.

Something Was Hiding in This Ancient Roman Capital. It Took a Drought to Finally Reveal It.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Drones caught the ghostly outlines of a 2,000-year-old city—before returning greenery erased them again.

A heatwave wreaked havoc upon all facets of life in Europe to some degree this past summer, but it also provided archaeologists in Switzerland with an interesting opportunity: the chance to view ancient Roman history in an entirely fresh way. With vegetation stripped back and parched, a team researching Aventicum, a once-thriving Roman city, took to the air via drone for a bird’s-eye view of the entire site. From the new vantage point, they discovered things they simply hadn’t ever known existed.

The site in Switzerland’s state of Vaud, which is now known as Avenches, looked different in the unusually hot and dry months of July and August, allowing archaeologists to “observe remains usually invisible underground,” according to a statement from those working at the Aventicum archaeological site. The airborne exploration revealed previously unseen buried structures, redrawing the map of what was once the capital of Roman Helvetica.

“These are the richest discoveries made there in the last 50 years, at least over the last two generations of archaeologists,” said Jordan Anastassov, archaeologist for Vaud and the University of Geneva, according to Reuters.

The archaeologist said less vigorous plant growth occurs atop buried structures. Combine that with a drought-stricken region and wilting vegetation this past summer, and the archaeologists were able to make a number of “observations that complement the data from the excavations in an exceptional way and show the full potential of surface prospecting methods.”

Aerial images not only showed the locations of houses (making up a full neighborhood), expansive villas, and roads, but also highlighted thermal baths and a potential rectangular temple site running nearly 100 feet in length that was lost to history. Some of the buildings appear in sections of the historic city that haven’t been excavated, and the large building sits on what was once thought to be the town’s outskirts.

Founded originally after Julius Caesar conquered local tribes 2,000 years ago, Aventicum had a successful run under Roman rule, eventually succumbing to Germanic invasions, starting in the third century. “We are now asking really interesting questions we wouldn’t otherwise be asking,” said Hugo Amoroso, an archaeologist on the project.

The team hopes on-the-ground exploration of the newly discovered areas will provide fuller detail and a richer history.

The summer heat proved critical in exposing the areas. By September, as temperatures returned to normal and vegetation started to flourish again, many of the newly seen sites slipped from view, although the pools discovered at the bath location were still visible in a struggling alfalfa field. “We aren’t going to wish for droughts,” said Pierre Blanc, director of the site’s excavations, “but we’re going to make the most of this and keep looking.”

The use of droughts to reveal lost sites isn’t a new concept. Going aerial simply helps cover more ground quickly. A post from the tourism website About Switzerland that Anastassov shared on his LinkedIn page noted that a 1976 drought played a role in the discovery of a Roman villa in the nearby municipality of Orbe.

With droughts increasing throughout the world, archaeologists are turning to aerial monitoring as a planned tactic, scheduling surveys systematically to better understand potential excavation sites and more accurately redraw ancient maps, just like at Aventicum.

A college student searching rocky glaciers in Yellowstone finally discovered one of the rarest insects in the world

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A University of Wyoming student discovered three rare ice crawlers in Yellowstone, marking the first known sightings of these cold-loving insects in Wyoming.

In summer 2026, a college student named Will Anderson found three rare bugs called ice crawlers on rocky glaciers in Yellowstone National Park.

As a student at the University of Wyoming, Anderson had spent the whole previous year looking for them without any luck. Ice crawlers are some of the rarest insects on Earth.

Ice crawlers belong to Grylloblattodea, one of the rarest orders of insects in the world. Only 35 species have been described, and many are known only from single localities.

Anderson is a biology major from Casper and a junior in the Wyoming Research Scholars Program. He works with Audrey Lindsteadt, a third-year Ph.D. student in the university’s Program in Ecology and Evolution.

“I am interested in this insect because there is so much not known about them, with their life history, habitat, and distribution all not being fully understood,” Anderson said.

“I also am passionate about and interested in their alpine habitats, which are understudied,” he added.

Starting in late July 2025, the pair surveyed 14 rock glaciers in the Red Mountains next to Heart Lake, in southwestern Yellowstone. They found no ice crawlers, even though Anderson also checked snowfields and set overnight traps.

Most snowfields had already melted by then. The area also had fewer glaciers than ideal, and most of them were small or inactive.

“Despite the close proximity to their known distribution and the plentiful suitable habitat, Gryllos have never been found in Wyoming,” Lindsteadt explained. “Therefore, Will hypothesized the rock glaciers and nearby snowfields that we are studying could have Gryllos.”

On a cold, snowy six-day backpacking trip in late June and early July 2026, Anderson found two ice crawlers near Antler Peak in northwest Yellowstone. Both turned up under rocks he flipped on a large rock glacier.

In mid-July, a mother grizzly and her two-year-old cub bluff-charged the pair and blocked their planned route.

They scrambled up Grey Peak instead and slid down to its two rock glaciers, where Anderson caught a third one. In August, he searched several alpine glaciers in Grand Teton National Park but came up empty.

Over the summer of 2026, he and Lindsteadt walked about 170 miles (274 kilometers).

“The specimens I collected in Wyoming are so far identified to the species Grylloblatta campodeiformis, which is the most widespread North American species, having populations in Montana, British Columbia, and Alberta,” Anderson noted.

He briefly kept two of the insects alive. He could not tell whether they were feeding on the dead insects and plant matter he gave them.

Ice crawlers are wingless insects that stay active in cold conditions, according to North Carolina State University entomologists. Most species are active below freezing and usually die above 50°F (10°C).

They develop through incomplete metamorphosis, from egg to nymph to adult, and their growth is slow. Some may need up to seven years to complete a single generation.

The University of Wyoming describes them as extremophiles that live at temperatures as low as 21°F (-6°C) in humid alpine settings.

By day, they hide under cool rocks near snow and ice. At night, they scavenge, eating mostly other invertebrates, including insects blown onto the snow, along with some plant material.

Little is known of their diet when they live more permanently under the snow, or in the soil through summer and winter.

The order lives in northeastern Asia, Siberia, the western U.S. and Canada. In North America, small, isolated populations occupy mountains and caves in several western states, British Columbia and Alberta.

Rock glaciers resemble alpine glaciers, but a layer of debris covers their ice, insulating and protecting it.

Lindsteadt suspects that ice crawlers prefer rock glaciers to non-glacial talus fields or snowfields because rock glaciers are colder, more humid and more permanent.

“Obviously, with just three species detections, we cannot confidently identify their habitat preferences,” Lindsteadt said. “But they at least point us in a promising direction.”

Yellowstone holds 58 known rock glaciers, and none had been visited or studied before this project. Next summer, the team plans to survey 17 rock glaciers in eastern Yellowstone and revisit others.

Ice crawlers are generally restricted to high-elevation glaciers or low-elevation caves with permanent ice, according to Purdue University entomologists.

The same entomologists warn that many of these habitats are at risk as glaciers and ice fields recede with climate change.

In Anderson’s view, the insects probably play a small part in these ecosystems because of their rarity, but they still belong to the food chains and nutrient cycles of their extreme habitats.

“More importantly, we can use ice crawlers to better understand how these ecosystems work and be more effective managers of them,” he argued.

He is also working on a paper he hopes to publish in a leading alpine or entomology journal.

Anderson will present the findings at the Entomological Society of America’s 74th annual meeting in Columbus, Ohio, on November 8 to 11.

The Bambu Lab H3C is a large-format 3D printer with nine printheads, and it's available now.

Multicolor 3D printing has been around for many years. Like all technologies, manufacturers are constantly looking for ways to push it further than ever before. Bambu Lab announced today the H3C, a large-format 3D printer that uses nine separate nozzles to print not only different colors or types, but also different-sized filaments. It’s designed to allow you to mix and match as you see fit, expanding your creative options beyond the standard.

The Bambu Lab H3C starts at $2,500 and comes with the nine-filament system and Automated Material System. I’ve spent the last few weeks using it, and while I haven’t finished my review yet, I already have quite a few thoughts.

This is not a small machine by any measure. I originally had it on my workbench, but the H3C sits so tall that opening the lid and loading the filament was arduous, even for a 6-foot-1 person like myself. Bambu Lab also sent me a metal rolling cart, sold separately, that lowers the machine to a usable height. That said, it still stands 63 inches tall on the cart, so yeah, it’s a big’un.

Despite its size and complex design, setting it up was simple. I would recommend having a second person around to do it, as it’s cumbersome — but not difficult to plug in all the parts. The AMS 3 that sits atop the machine is different from most of the best 3D printers because the H3C requires it operate. It’s not a separate add-on but an integral part of the machine.

You can tell from every metric that the H3C is designed to be a Bambu Lab flagship. It has all the top-of-the-line hardware, including a heatbed that can reach 120C and an optional but very cool bird’s-eye camera to monitor prints from above. It’s fast, too, with a top toolhead speed of 1,400 mm/s. Of course, that isn’t the print speed, but it does show you the type of speed it could reach if you pushed it to the max.

Printing at high speed does have some disadvantages, though. I have had several print failures on tall prints where the supports or purge tower have fallen due to accidental touches or vibration. The H3C moves fast, prints fast and fails fast if you aren’t careful. Most of those failures occurred at 125% speed, but that’s the standard setting I use for large prints on other machines, and they handle it just fine.

Bambu calls its nine-nozzle system, which heats using conductive heating, the Swaptek system. This isn’t to be confused with another nine-nozzle system you may have heard of: INDX, which uses conductive heating. INDX and Swaptek are extremely similar in execution but differ in a few areas. INDX is purely mechanical, whereas Bambu says its Swaptek system supplies data and power to each tool, allowing the nozzles to start heating faster than those in a standard INDX system — in around 6 seconds per nozzle.

In practice, there isn’t a huge difference between what I’m seeing on the H3C and the Prusa Core One + INDX in terms of speed. The Core One has the advantage that it’s calibrated to avoid a purge tower, and it doesn’t purge as much waste as the Swaptek system. The H3C has its own neat tricks, though: Not only can it print with different nozzle sizes and materials, but it can also print with materials of different diameters.

One of the biggest roadblocks with printing TPU — the very soft, rubbery material that 3D-printed shoes are made from — through an AMS is that it’s too soft to push through the AMS feeder. To combat this, you have to use a harder TPU that isn’t nearly as flexible as you would need for shoes or things that bounce. Adding toolheads capable of printing 2.85mm-diameter materials lets Bambu create TPU that’s more flexible after melting but thicker and easier to feed in the AMS. It works really well.

And it doesn’t stop at TPU, either. You can use 2.85mm PLA with a 0.8mm nozzle to print the infill of a model, while using a 0.2mm nozzle and 1.7mm PLA to print the detailed outer walls. It gives you so many options. The AMS even has two material slots with active heating chambers, so you can print materials that need to be warm and dry, along with other, more shelf-stable materials.

Given the $2,500 price and the H3C’s flagship status, it would be shocking if the print quality were bad. All of the models that didn’t fail due to purge tower collapse look great, and the nine separate nozzles make light work of just about any color project. I love watching this machine print as it swaps across the nozzles at blistering speeds.

Some of them did fail, though, and the H3C was good at catching the errors, pausing the print and letting me know. I chose to keep one of them going despite the failed tower, and it turned out fine, though a little damaged. I had some fun 3D printing my scanned head at life size, using silver for my greying beard — and I love the way it came out.

The CNET test print wasn’t nearly as good as the one the H2C produced, which pretty much maxed out every test, but it wasn’t the worst either. Accuracy was good, and despite the printer’s speed, ringing was kept to a minimum. It struggled on the 0.2mm tolerance test, but it did well on bridging and overhangs, showing that its temperature control is excellent. I still have more of these to do before I’m ready to give a complete score, but so far, I’m happy with the result.

In video games, there’s a concept called endgame content: the harder challenges you tackle after completing most of the story. It’s not for the faint of heart. The H3C is endgame content. It isn’t hard to use, but it’s designed for someone who has gone as far as possible with machines such as the P2S and A1 combos of the world. If you’re looking to go beyond what you thought you could do with a 3D printer, the H3C is the machine to get you there.

Swaptek, INDX and multi-toolhead printers are here to stay. And, like all 3D printers, they can come in many sizes and budgets. The Core One INDX is smaller but cheaper, but the H3C has a lot of options right out of the box to make some incredibly complex models. We live in interesting times and the landscape can only get better.
