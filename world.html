There’s something undeniably chic about a head of long, luscious hair. Think glossy, waist-skimming, bouncy, voluminous lengths that make extensions an option rather than a long-term commitment. What’s more, long hair provides the ultimate canvas for experimentation, with styling possibilities that feel limitless.

As hair matures, however, maintaining the same level of shine, strength, and vitality can require more attention. As texture changes and dryness increases, the key is to adapt your routine to give your longer lengths the care they deserve. “Long hair can look beautiful at any age, but keeping mature hair glossy and healthy-looking is all about replenishing moisture and maintaining shine,” says celebrity hairstylist Naomi Brooks. “I always recommend prioritising hydrating products that help smooth the hair cuticle, improve softness, and enhance shine, alongside regular trims to keep the lengths and ends looking healthy.”

If you’re considering making the transition and letting your bob grow out, look to a host of celebrities for inspiration. From Demi Moore and Naomi Campbell to Andie MacDowell and Claudia Schiffer, countless iterations are proving just how effortless long hair can look on women over 50. As Brooks explains: “Demi Moore is a fantastic example of how beautiful long hair can look at any age. Her hair is consistently glossy, healthy-looking, and full of movement.”

While genetics certainly play a role, Moore’s glossy lengths are also the result of great care and strategic products. Another expert-approved tip is to keep heat styling to a minimum where possible. If you do rely on hot tools, applying heat protectant can help shield strands from damage while preserving moisture, strength, and shine.

Finally, contrary to popular belief, long hair thrives on regular maintenance. Beer With a Painter: Ouattara Watts

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Ivorian-born painter works only at night, when the veil with the spiritual realm is thinnest and the outside voices of the physical world are quietest.

Last month, just a couple of weeks before Ouattara Watts’s solo show opened at Karma, I was directed to knock on the cracked glass door of his Bushwick studio. We met in the late afternoon, which is when his studio time begins. The Ivorian-American artist has coffee and a light evening meal, and works all night, before returning home to sleep during the day. To learn why Watts works only at night, one need only look at his paintings. He views art as a portal to the divine; night is when the veil with the spiritual realm is thinnest, and the outside voices of the physical world are quietest.

Although a gallery representative had told me Watts would be putting “finishing touches” on the paintings for his exhibition at the time of our meeting, I came to realize that he thinks about the work, and his process, without a beginning or end. The centrifugal energy of his rhythmic compositions does not obey edges. Totemic forms continue beyond the bounds of the canvases; arrows point in varying directions; seed pods are split open to reveal concentric circles. His compositions are stained and dripped fields populated by a lexicon of imagery and symbols, developed over the past four decades. They include coffee beans, sacred books, musical notes, serpents, bursting petals encased in atoms, diagrammatic cubes, and mathematical codes.

South Africa seized 5 ancient baobabs bound for Georgia, where 8 Kenyan trees died in a billionaire’s park

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

For more than three months, five baobab trees estimated to be 200 to 300 years old lay on their sides in a Durban warehouse parking lot, roots and branches hacked back, waiting to be shipped to the Black Sea nation of Georgia. South Africa’s Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment (DFFE) intercepted the trees on July 17 after their exporter, Pretoria-based Divine Landscapes, could not produce licenses to remove, transport, or export them.

Officials have not named the buyer. But reporting by Daily Maverick traced the trail to the Shekvetili Dendrological Park, a private arboretum on Georgia’s coast founded by billionaire Bidzina Ivanishvili, the country’s former prime minister and the founder of its ruling Georgian Dream party. Ivanishvili has not commented, and Daily Maverick said requests to his lawyer and to Georgia’s embassy in Pretoria went unanswered.

The case tests how far South Africa will go to keep a protected species from becoming a collectible for wealthy buyers abroad. It also left authorities with a practical problem: keeping living evidence alive while a case moves through the courts.

Environmental journalist Tony Carnie got an anonymous tip about the trees, photographed them through a fence at the Africa Global Logistics warehouse in Durban’s Mobeni industrial area, and reported the seizure in Daily Maverick in October. AGL told the publication it only provided logistics services and neither owned nor exported the cargo.

Every baobab in South Africa is a specially protected species under the National Forests Act, which bars anyone from cutting, disturbing, removing, transporting, or exporting one without a license. The DFFE says the five trees were dug up in the Lephalale area of Limpopo province without a provincial permit. It refused Divine Landscapes’ late license application at the end of July and dismissed the company’s appeal on Oct. 27.

The company denies wrongdoing. Its attorneys told Daily Maverick the trees were “obtained from private gardens with the full consent of the respective owners” and said it is seeking judicial review of the appeal decision. The company’s owner appeared in Durban Magistrates’ Court in September, and the case was postponed to January pending further investigation.

Ivanishvili, whose party critics accuse of tilting Georgia toward Moscow, has spent years moving giant trees to Shekvetili. Eight baobabs uprooted on Kenya’s Kilifi coast were bound for the park when Kenyan President William Ruto halted their export and ordered an investigation. The Kenya Forest Service later authorized the shipment, and the trees reached Georgia in 2023.

All eight died. In August 2024, the park confirmed the deaths and blamed delays it attributed to Ivanishvili’s political opponents.

“We also did not want to see a repeat of what happened in Kenya,” Dr. Sarah Venter, an ecologist and founder of the Baobab Foundation, told Daily Maverick.

The seizure comes as South Africa presses for tougher international enforcement. On Oct. 23, parties to the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime, meeting in Vienna, voted 83 to 12, with 27 abstentions, to begin negotiating a new protocol on crimes that affect the environment, including trafficking in wild flora and fauna. South Africa co-sponsored the resolution.

“Environmental crime is organized crime,” Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment Minister David Maynier said in a DFFE statement. Once negotiated, the protocol could give countries shared tools for cross-border investigations, extradition, and recovering criminal proceeds. Trafficking networks are already expanding their reach, as a U.N. report on the global illicit economy found this year, and wealthy collectors’ appetite for rare species is under scrutiny elsewhere, including in India’s trade in trafficked orangutans.

Seized cargo can usually wait in storage. These trees could not. “They’ve been lying on their sides. Their branches and their roots have been trimmed, well hacked, so they’re not in a very vertical position. They’re not in soil. They’re in full sun. There’s no water,” Carnie said in a Daily Maverick video interview.

“The really critical thing is to get them back into the soil as soon as possible to allow them to recover and regenerate, because they are using up stored stem water to survive,” Venter said. A healthy baobab can be up to 75% water.

The rescue was privately funded. Timbavati Private Nature Reserve, which straddles the Mpumalanga and Limpopo provinces, offered to cover loading, transport, and replanting, and Mark Read of the Everard Read Group found an unnamed private funder within 10 minutes of Venter’s call, Daily Maverick reported. On Nov. 11, the trees, each weighing an estimated 17 to 30 tons, began leaving Durban on abnormal-load trucks for the roughly 900-kilometer trip to the reserve.

All five had sprouted leaves and flower buds. “I think we got here just in time and I’m quite positive about their future,” Venter said.

T-19A Kestrel: Meet The US Air Force Academy's New Unlimited Aerobatic Aircraft For Cadets

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

On November 12, four new composite planes swept low over the Cadet Wing assembled at the US Air Force Academy in Colorado. The flight marked the official arrival of the T-19A Kestrel light trainer at Davis Airfield in Colorado Springs. Instead of wading through decades of defense bureaucracy and drawn-out congressional appropriations, USAFA secured its next-generation aerobatic aircraft in just 10 months, cutting through red tape with the help of a private donor group led by Andrew McKenna.

The T-19A is a derivative of the commercial GB1 GameBird. By skipping the traditional procurement, leadership bypassed the budget overruns and timeline slips that have paralyzed programs like the Boeing T-7A Red Hawk. The lightweight plane is purpose-built for agility thanks to its designer, German aerobatic champion Philipp Steinbach. He is a co-founder of Game Aerospace Composites in Bentonville, Arkansas, which produces the off-the-shelf civilian variant certified to pull a staggering load of ten G-forces.

The Kestrel went from concept to delivery in 10 months using $3 million in private donations, whereas the Red Hawk has spent over a decade in development using billions in taxpayer funds and still faces multi-year delays. The T-19A will fill a major gap in modern USAFA flight training by giving cadets early exposure to high-G, unlimited aerobatics and complex energy management before they ever step into Undergraduate Pilot Training.

The T-19A, or GB1 GameBird, is the only American-built two-seat aircraft approved for Unlimited-category competition aerobatics. Its all-carbon monocoque airframe lacks traditional mechanical wing-attach joints, relying on a continuous composite spar cured inside an autoclave. This gives the plane an operating empty weight of roughly 1,290 pounds (585 kg) while enabling symmetric flight loads of up to 10-G at its aerobatic maximum limit.

Under the hood, a 583-cubic-inch Lycoming AEIO-580-B1A flat-six piston engine delivers 303 horsepower (226 kW) to a four-blade composite MT-Propeller system. An inverted oil and fuel system keeps the engine running under sustained negative-g flight and zero-g push-overs. Cadets command the airframe from tandem seats equipped with Garmin G3X Touch glass displays, combining edge-of-envelope stick-and-rudder forces with modern electronic flight screens before stepping into standard turboprops.

To make the transition, USAFA pulled six active-duty instructor pilots from the 306th Flying Training Group to stand up the first training cadre. These officers will shape a new syllabus to train cadets and establish a demonstration and competition team. The initiative comes as the wider military struggles with a persistent shortfall of roughly 2,000 operational pilots, forcing leadership to seek shortcuts to build stick-and-rudder skills earlier in pilot development.

The Academy's existing fleet jumps from unpowered TG-16A gliders and basic T-53A/T-41D piston trainers straight to the UPT's T-6A Texan II turboprops after graduation. USAF Chief of Staff General Ken Wilsbach and Academy Superintendent Lt. Gen. Paul Moga secured the four airframes thanks to a $3 million donation coordinated by philanthropist Andrew McKenna. Meanwhile, the USAFA paid $400,000 in operational funds for initial tooling, spares, and flight gear, along with a projected annual operating budget of $1 million to maintain the fleet.

The Kestrel will act as an intermediate high-performance step, teaching cadets how to manage high power-to-weight ratios, rapid throttle response, and aggressive energy management. Standard trainers are built for stability and prohibit extreme maneuvers. Because the Kestrel is certified for unlimited aerobatics, instructors can deliberately put cadets into inverted spins, vertical stalls, and disorienting attitudes, teaching them instinctive recovery skills that directly prevent real-world loss-of-control accidents.

The Air Force is facing a persistent pilot deficit and needs to push students through UPT faster without lowering standards. By giving top cadets advanced stick-and-rudder proficiency during their junior and senior years at the Academy, they arrive at UPT needing fewer remedial rides and moving through the syllabus faster. Additionally, building an aerobatic competition team around the Kestrel will push the selected cadets to fly under public scrutiny with the precision and discipline they will further refine in operational flying squadrons.

Operating the Kestrel at the USAFA in Colorado Springs comes with real performance trade-offs. The Academy's Davis Airfield sits at an elevation of 6,572 feet (2,003 meters), and summer heat often makes the air feel like it is over 9,000 feet (2,743 meters). That means the T-19A's non-turbocharged engine loses roughly 22% to 26% of its power. That thinner mountain air requires longer takeoff runs and leaves pilots with less excess engine power during demanding maneuvers.

Weight limits create another hurdle for daily operations, as the Kestrel can weigh up to 2,200 pounds (998 kg) for standard point-to-point flights. Its maximum takeoff rating for extreme 10-G aerobatics only applies at 1,940 pounds (880 kg) or less. Subtracting the plane's 1,290-pound (585 kg) empty weight leaves just 650 pounds (295 kg) for people, gear, and fuel. Once an instructor and a cadet strap in, only enough weight allowance remains for 25 to 30 gallons (95 to 114 liters) of fuel.

That limits intense flights to roughly 45 minutes, requiring ground crews to refuel and turn the planes around quickly between sorties. Moving cadets from unpowered gliders into a 200-knot (370 km/h) airplane is also a major operational adjustment. Because rapid spins and heavy G-forces can easily cause motion sickness or make new pilots lose track of which way is up, instructors must design a step-by-step program that gradually introduces cadets to the plane's capabilities.

Built entirely from carbon fiber, the T-19A brings modern composite design to a general aviation field long dominated by traditional steel-tube frames. Even compared to the newer carbon-fiber Extra NG, the Kestrel weighs about 110 pounds (50 kg) less at an empty weight of 1,290 pounds (585 kg). Its wing and fuselage tanks also hold 81 gallons (307 liters) of fuel, giving it a cross-country range of over 1,000 nautical miles (1,852 kilometers).

In the air, the Kestrel reacts far faster than almost all other military trainers. The USAF's primary turboprop trainer, the Beechcraft T-6A Texan II, rolls at 180 degrees per second and handles forces between positive 7.0-G and negative 3.5-G. The Kestrel more than doubles that agility, rolling at 400 degrees per second while withstanding up to positive and negative 10-G.

Unlike older aerobatic planes, the Kestrel avoids the sluggish handling caused by a heavy nose-mounted radial engine. 5 Legendary Rock Bands With Only One Perfect Album, Ranked

It can take years for a rock band to finally get radio play, but for a lucky few exceptionally talented acts, all it takes is one album to make a lasting impression. It's not every day that a band comes together, puts out a single project, and then leaves it all behind. Whether it's a side project, an inability to maintain the same chemistry beyond the debut, or simply a matter of unfortunate circumstances, there's something fascinating about bands with only one album to their name.

Arguably, a one-album discography can represent some of the most honest work in a band's career, seizing its artistry at its rawest. There's also the pressure of making that one record count, leaving listeners with something truly memorable. Without further ado, here are five of the greatest rock bands with only one perfect album, ranked.

Following the massive MTV success of Enema of the State and Take Off Your Pants and Jacket, it was only a matter of time before fans (and their record label) expected Blink-182 to follow up with another hit-making album built around their tried-and-true three-power-chord formula. But for members Tom DeLonge and Travis Barker, they needed an outlet to explore heavier sounds and darker subject matter—something that contrasted with Blink-182's rambunctious energy, immature antics, and crass humor. Enter their side project, Box Car Racer. Originally conceived as a creative outlet during a break from touring, the short-lived project gave DeLonge and Barker room to branch out musically and experiment with a different sound, something they would continue exploring in later Blink-182 releases and with DeLonge’s future band, Angels & Airwaves.

Inspired by DeLonge's interest in post-hardcore bands like Fugazi, Refused, and Quicksand, Box Car Racer ditches the fast, chord-driven riffs he was accustomed to playing in favor of heavier guitar textures, distorted riffs, and drop-D tuning—all channeled into their self-titled album. While traces of his pop-punk roots remain unmistakable, the band isn't chasing catchy hooks designed to dominate radio airplay. Instead, explores darker themes ranging from apocalyptic destruction to Freemasonry—definitely a far cry from singing about teenage break ups and personal insecurities.

The Los Angeles hardcore punk scene owes a lot to The Germs. Their original run may have lasted only four years in the late '70s, but the band helped lay the groundwork for the scene, leaving an influence that would carry on for decades. The brainchild of lead vocalist Darby Crash and guitarist Pat Smear (who would later play with Nirvana and the Foo Fighters), The Germs drew inspiration from a surprisingly wide range of artists, including Iggy Pop, The Ramones, and The Sex Pistols. Their only studio album, GI, is regarded as one of the earliest full-length hardcore punk records and is notably produced by Joan Jett of The Runaways.

GI proves that hardcore's unrestrained chaos doesn't have to come at the expense of structure or artistic expression. Rather than reducing the genre to theatrical antics and one-minute bursts of noise with barely intelligible lyrics, The Germs showed that hardcore could be just as expressive and musically thoughtful as any other genre. Hardcore punk may have been at the heart of their sound, but it was their frenetic aggression, both on record and during notoriously chaotic live performances that sometimes got them kicked offstage, that cemented their status as legends of the first generation of Los Angeles punk bands.

Their time in the industry might be short-lived, but The La's got the world singing in karaoke thanks to their hit track "There She Goes," which has become an anthem for many romantic comedies. For a bunch of Brit lads making jolly, Merseybeat sing-along tunes à la The Kinks, The Who, and The Rolling Stones, The La's were surprisingly perfectionistic. This not only became a huge obstacle during the production of their debut album but also put a strain on the band's relationships, eventually contributing to their breakup. It's quite a contrast for a band whose repertoire sounds so raw and organic to have gone through a recording process filled with rejected takes, endless re-recordings, and rotating producers.

While The La's weren't happy with their self-titled debut album (which took a total of three years to record), its sound became a blueprint for Britpop, thanks to its emphasis on guitar-driven music and 1960s British rock influences. The album's legacy can still be heard in the music of Oasis, Arctic Monkeys, and Fontaines D.C. While it's a shame The La's never released another studio album, they at least had the chance to create something that would stand the test of time. The recording process may have been a pain, but the fruits of their labor are unmistakable.

Them Crooked Vultures takes the term "supergroup" to another level, bringing together a trio that's a match made in rock heaven: Josh Homme of Queens of the Stone Age, John Paul Jones of Led Zeppelin, and Dave Grohl of Nirvana and the Foo Fighters. Their sound feels like a more laid-back take on Homme's work with his own band, but with a catchier, more modern rock edge. The project took years to come together, with the trio making its debut in 2009. All three musicians bring their own playing styles to the table, but somehow, everything clicks.

Their self-titled album is a real treat for rock fans, especially when you consider the heavyweight talent packed into this tight trio. There's no shortage of heavy guitar riffs, but the album isn't all about loud guitars and flashy musicianship. It also throws in funk influences, piano arrangements, and plenty of catchy vocal hooks from Homme. There's a little bit of everything here, and that's what makes the record such a familiar yet fresh listen. Rather than sounding like three rock legends simply showing off what they can do, Them Crooked Vultures brings their individual strengths together without the need to overpower each other.

Britain's biggest troublemakers have long sparked debate over whether they're true punk pioneers or just cynical posers who were more interested in the lifestyle than the music itself. It doesn't help that bassist Sid Vicious reportedly could barely play his own instrument, with Steve Jones having to step in and re-record the bass tracks. Still, whether you buy into their authenticity or not, there's no denying that the Sex Pistols left a lasting mark on anti-establishment culture. Their rebellious attitude was a sharp contrast to Britain's prim and proper image, particularly the kind of regal etiquette expected in a society still shaped by its monarchy.

Their only studio album, Never Mind the Bollocks, Here's the Sex Pistols, is pretty much the epitome of their bad-boy attitude. Dropped by record labels, plastered across tabloids, and met with concert cancellations, the album attracted plenty of public scrutiny for its outrageous content. Few songs were as controversial as "God Save the Queen" and "Anarchy in the U.K.," both of which captured the spirit of punk at its most rebellious. The album was packed with political undertones, and at a time when censorship was still rife in an uptight British society, the Sex Pistols came in swinging with a sound that refused to follow the rules and lyrics that could give the royal family a headache.

Plants mix different breeding strategies to survive in a world with changing climates and fewer bees

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Simulations show plants that reject their own pollen and plants that self-fertilize can coexist for ages, swapping dominance without settling.

Apple trees have a strict biological rule for reproduction. They reject their own pollen. That means they need a completely different type of apple tree nearby to produce fruit.

While many wild plants rely on this exact same partner system, plenty of other plant species have evolved to drop the rule entirely, allowing them to successfully fertilize themselves.

A population holding both kinds was supposed to be passing through on its way to choosing one rule or the other.

In computer simulations of evolving plants, the mixed populations didn’t get there. With nothing in the environment changing, the two kinds kept trading dominance for as long as the simulations ran. Neither kind took over.

The researchers call it a volatile mixed mode. A mixed population in the wild that looks unstable, they wrote, may be in a lasting state of its own.

Tamar Friedlander, a senior lecturer at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem’s Robert H. Smith Institute of Plant Sciences and Genetics in Agriculture, led the work with Amit Jangid.

Ohad Noy Feldheim of the University’s Einstein Institute of Mathematics is the third author. Everything here happened inside a model.

In an interview with Earth.com, Friedlander said the result broke a habit of thought. Biologists expect that when a population has several options, some are better than others, she said.

Over the long run, the population is supposed to reach the better one and stay there. Earlier models had the mix fading away or holding steady at fixed proportions.

“Thus, it was very surprising that we didn’t get either of these under a large parameter regime,” she said.

The populations, in her words, “not only did not settle to either of the ‘pure’ strategies (only self-fertilization or only outcrossing) but also did not settle at fixed proportions of these strategies.”

The rejection system works like a lock and a set of keys. The female side of a flower makes a toxin that stops a pollen tube from growing. Each pollen grain carries proteins that disarm the toxins of other mating types but not its own.

About 40 percent of plant families use some version of this, including wild tomatoes, potatoes, apples, pears, citrus, and coffee. Washington State University‘s tree fruit site has a primer on why orchards need two varieties.

The system can break. A mutation that silences the toxin, or adds a key that fits the plant’s own lock, produces a plant that can fertilize itself.

That plant gains a sure route to seeds when pollen is scarce, but it passes on two sets of genes to each seed instead of one.

It pays for that with inbreeding. Seeds from self-fertilization carry more harmful mutations, and more of them fail.

The plants that reject their own pollen pay a different price. Their pollen is useless on every plant of their own mating type, so a type that grows common finds fewer partners.

Friedlander’s team simulated populations of 500 plants for 100,000 generations, discarding the first 50,000 as a warm-up.

Each plant carried two copies of the gene set, and each copy held one toxin and nine keys. Every protein was written as a string of 18 amino acids that could mutate.

Two dials set the outcome. One was how badly self-fertilized seeds suffer from inbreeding. The other was how loosely the keys fit the locks, which decides how many mating types a population can hold.

With heavy inbreeding costs and loose-fitting keys, every plant ended up rejecting its own pollen. With light costs, every plant ended up self-fertilizing.

In between lay the third state, where the share of self-rejecting plants rose and fell for the rest of the run and the number of mating types swung with it.

Types tended to vanish several at a time, in what the authors describe as avalanches. What triggers those collapses, they wrote, is still obscure.

A shortage of pollen was expected to push populations toward self-fertilization, since a plant that can pollinate itself doesn’t need a visitor. The model found the opposite.

When cross-pollen was scarce, the range of settings in which self-rejection won grew at the expense of the mixed state. Friedlander said the explanation lies in how the two sexes compete.

When pollen is plentiful, every female is sure of a partner and only the males compete, so female proteins evolve mainly to avoid matching their own pollen.

When pollen is scarce, females must compete too, which makes them easier to match and raises the number of mating types a population can support.

Researchers often treat self-fertilization as a plant’s backup plan for when cross-pollination fails, she told Earth.com. Self-fertilizing plants do turn up at the edges of a species’ range, where partners are few.

The move from rejecting self-pollen to accepting it has long been treated as a one-way street. A plant that gives up the rule usually sheds the flower traits that attract insects and purges some of its harmful mutations.

Across much of the model’s settings, though, the end state didn’t depend on the start, and a population that began self-fertilizing could evolve the rejection system from scratch.

The exception was the loosest-fitting keys, where the mutation needed for the reverse trip was so rare that the wait became impossibly long.

The model also held the cost of inbreeding fixed, so it describes the short term, before prolonged self-fertilization purges enough mutations to change the odds.

Mixed populations exist in the wild. Long-term studies that track one population’s proportions over time are lacking, the authors wrote, and those are what would test the prediction.

The model also predicts that mixed populations carry fewer and shorter-lived mating types than purely self-rejecting ones, and more inbreeding written into their genomes. Both could be checked in plant genomes.

Asked which population she would most like to watch, Friedlander pointed to wild tomato species growing in South America.

She also said the model doesn’t apply directly to orchards, where trees are genetically identical and do not evolve. Self-rejecting crops do depend on pollinators, she added, and a decline in them could cut yields.

A warming climate and fewer pollinating insects both change the pressures on how a plant reproduces, the authors wrote.

Whether real populations respond by picking a side, or by swinging between the two for generations, is what a long field study of those wild tomatoes could begin to answer.

The full study was published in the journal PRX Life.

The Nature Photographer of the Year 2026 winners have been unveiled, showcasing the world’s best nature, wildlife, and landscape photography.

This year’s competition received 27,108 entries from photographers in nearly 100 countries, a record-breaking number for the longtime photo contest.

Finnish photographer Hannu Ahonen bested stiff competition to earn the title of Nature Photographer of the Year. Ahonen’s award-winning photo, “The One that Left and the One that Stayed,” is an extraordinary image of a water lily leaf positioned alongside its icy duplicate.

“It is not often that a photograph of a plant is crowned the overall winner of a major photography competition, but the jury could not overlook this fascinating image by Hannu Ahonen,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom. “First and foremost, the photograph captures a unique situation: a water lily leaf with an almost perfect black-and-white replica of it lying next to it in the water.”

Ahonen’s winning photo tells a compelling and unusual story. The living water lily leaf is positioned on the left, while the ice that formed on top of it and slipped off sits delicately to the side, blown away slightly by the breeze. Even the veins from the leaf are visible in the icy shell.

Nature Photographer of the Year adds that the photo reveals the “marvelous beauty of nature” while embodying its transient character. The sheet of ice will soon melt away, joining the water that keeps the lily alive in the first place. It’s a symbolic photo at a time when climate change is impacting all life on Earth, especially its ice.

“The winter ice had arrived, but it remained snowless for a long time. Some of the pond’s lily pad leaves began to crumble on top of the ice. The wind carried a few of them away, revealing delicate, frosty ornaments left behind. I eagerly captured this natural phenomenon, something I had never witnessed before,” Ahonen explains.

Ahonen has an inspiring story. He has ADHD and lives in a wilderness home in Finland with no carbon footprint. Rarely venturing more than a couple of miles from his home, he spends much of his time behind the camera in his yard, using photography as a spiritual escape and creative outlet.

The Enterprise visits colony world Omicron Ceti III, home to a plant species that secretes spores that take root in humanoid hosts. Watching "This Side of Paradise" now, it might remind you of the Apple TV series "Pluribus," where all of humanity is turned into a blissful hive mind (collectively "The Others"). In "This Side of Paradise," though, there's no identity theft. The spores let Spock be his innermost self. Nimoy described other "Star Trek" episodes as favorites such as "Devil in the Dark," where the Enterprise crew visit a mining colony to find a creature killing the inhabitants. Nimoy praised that one as a "wonderful episode about the fear of the unknown," and "Devil in the Dark" is known to be a favorite of William Shatner's, too.

"Devil in the Dark" and "This Side of Paradise" are very different episodes, but both ones anchored by Spock. Little surprise that Leonard Nimoy loved them.

rune bosse puts living trees on life support in a vision of ecological collapse

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

equipped with respirators and artificial lighting, rune bosse's 'tree lungs' imagines a future where nature itself may need life support.

Danish artist Rune Bosse places living trees on artificial life support in Lung Trees, an installation presented at the Danish Architecture Center in Copenhagen as part of its Age of Nature exhibition, which ran through September 2026. Each tree stands inside a transparent, inflated plastic enclosure, and is surrounded by mechanical equipment and artificial lighting. With their branches reaching toward overhead lamps, each tree seems to have been removed from the forest and placed under intensive care.

The installation imagines a future in which trees might depend on technology to survive, even as humans continue to depend on the ecological systems that trees help sustain. It reverses a relationship we tend to take for granted. Forests have long been described as the lungs of the planet, absorbing carbon dioxide through photosynthesis and releasing oxygen. Here, Bosse gives those lungs a mechanical counterpart, and suggests a world in which the natural systems supporting human life might themselves require artificial support.

The installation brings together living vegetation and an exposed network of machines. Transparent plastic sheeting stretches over metal frames, enclosing the trees while allowing visitors to see the soil and branches inside. Artificial lights illuminate the foliage, while equipment supplies air to the enclosed environment. The temporary structures have the practical appearance of makeshift greenhouses, although their association with medical equipment gives them a more unsettling character.

In a related 2025 presentation at Berlin’s Neue Nationalgalerie, titled Lungetræ / Lungenbaum, Bosse made the breathing movement particularly visible. A living tree and a section of forest floor were enclosed within a flexible PVC membrane attached to a metal framework. At regular intervals, a respirator pushed air into the structure, causing the plastic to expand and slowly collapse around the tree. The movement resembles the inflation of a giant lung, giving physical form to the invisible exchange of gases between a tree and its surroundings.

Bosse’s interest in trees extends well beyond the gallery. Alongside his training at the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts, the artist studied forestry and nature management, developing a practice that follows the growth of living plants over time. At Oremandsgaard, an estate in southern Denmark, he has established an outdoor forest laboratory where he experiments with shaping trees as they grow. Some are bent into unusual forms, while others are guided to grow together. Their final shapes emerge over years, influenced by the conditions of the surrounding landscape.

This work grew partly from his frustration with bringing natural materials into museums, where plants often have to be cut, dried or preserved before they can be exhibited. His outdoor experiments allow trees to remain alive and continue changing long after the artist’s initial intervention. Lung Trees approaches the same relationship from a different direction. Inside the gallery, keeping the trees alive becomes part of the artwork itself, exposing the equipment and artificial conditions that make their temporary existence possible.

The installation touches on an uncomfortable contradiction in the way humans approach ecological preservation. By enclosing the trees and supplying them with artificial conditions, Bosse creates an image of protection that also suggests dependency. The machinery appears to care for the living organisms, yet the entire arrangement places their survival within a system designed and operated by humans. The trees remain living participants in the work, although their relationship with the surrounding environment has been fundamentally altered.

Bosse first developed Lungetræer in 2019, and its more recent presentations give the work a continuing relevance amid growing concerns about biodiversity loss and the deterioration of ecosystems. The installation offers a speculative glimpse of what technological preservation might look like when ecological conditions can no longer support life on their own. Its most unsettling quality comes from the simplicity of the scene.

A living tree stands inside a plastic chamber while a machine pushes air around it. The visitor watches the artificial lung expand and contract, confronted with the possibility that the relationship between humans and forests could one day become this fragile.

A Menorca Farmhouse Finds Its Easy Elegance

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Isabel López-Quesada honored the historical essence of a 19th-century Menorcan retreat while introducing vibrant colors and an elevated ease.

Above: In the living room, López-Quesada replicated the original ceiling beams and finished the walls in traditional Menorcan lime plaster. A sofa of her design is grouped with a 1960s Tito Agnoli rattan daybed, a Mexican palm leaf stool, and a custom cocktail table upholstered with a Moroccan rug.

For Spanish designer Isabel López-Quesada, summers tend to revolve around Biarritz, in southwestern France, where she has a home. Still, she always carves out time for Menorca, regarded as the most laid-back of Spain’s Balearic Islands. That vibe suits the ELLE Decor A-list designer, whose signature style is luxurious but unfussy, harmonizing old and new with breezy refinement.

Typically, López-Quesada goes there to relax and spend time with friends, but around five years ago she received a call from a Bilbao family who asked her to refresh their vacation home outside Mahón, Menorca’s capital. An old farm property, or finca, the five-bedroom residence required extensive updates. López-Quesada was steadfast in preserving such historical elements as a well and donkey stables, which she transformed into an indoor-outdoor bathroom with a patio and marble tub. “It was important to respect the style of Menorca,” she says. “We were very careful with the materials, so it all goes together and conveys the sentiment I want the house to have.”

López-Quesada founded her namesake studio as a precocious 20-year-old Madrileña 40 years ago. Initially inspired by her mother’s yen for decorating and the work of celebrated Spanish designers such as Paco Muñoz and Fernando Benjumea, López-Quesada garnered wide attention in the early 2000s, when she converted an old Madrid wax factory into an atelier and a luxurious home for her family, filled with antiques and contemporary pieces. Soon she had interest from clients all over the world. Today López-Quesada’s 12-person team includes one of her three adult children, daughter Isabel Llanza. Five years ago the pair co-founded the lifestyle brand Isita Home, selling products they make themselves or discover on their travels.

The Menorca project began by gutting the house’s interior and rebuilding it “in an old style,” López-Quesada says. Central to it all was adding custom windows and doors to each room so the island’s golden light could flood the spaces. Vaulted and beamed ceilings were left intact or were recreated. Reconfiguring the floor plan, López-Quesada eschewed a proper dining room in favor of an eat-in open kitchen. “Most of the time you are eating outside,” she says.

With Menorca’s unrelenting summer sun, bougainvillea-draped pergolas were a must for leisurely alfresco lunches and dinners. Swaths of white lantana and lily of the Nile border the terraces, while “the pool reflects the sky like a mirror and provides a refreshing feel,” López-Quesada says.

Her collaborator on the gardens was Spanish landscape architect Luis González-Camino. “My vision was to design a water-efficient Mediterranean garden, divided into distinct yet connected ‘garden rooms’ for different seasons and times of day,” he says. González-Camino preserved the property as a natural Mediterranean woodland, with wild olive trees providing cover.

Indoors, López-Quesada installed French terra-cotta floor tiles chosen for their aged look. The three fireplaces were all handcrafted in traditional Menorcan style—a little imperfect, painted white—and decorated with vintage tiles the designer keeps in her Madrid warehouse.

She offset the white ceilings and lime plaster walls with vibrant textiles and tilework in blues and greens inspired by Menorca’s native palette. An upstairs bedroom is anchored by an iron four-poster bed with a blue-and-white-striped canopy; its bath has a shower lined with lapis-colored zellige tiles. Doors, which across Menorca are customarily painted in bold shades, here are finished in verdant hues.

Custom cabinets are designed with whimsically patterned cutouts for airflow, since humidity is a major factor. “On the islands, if you close the cupboards completely, mushrooms will grow inside,” López-Quesada says.

Step Inside This Renovated Menorca Retreat

She arrayed the spaces with a lively mix of vintage and new furnishings, emphasizing organic materials: rattan chairs, bamboo shades, Moroccan rugs, lots of linen and cotton fabrics in spirited florals or stripes. “If I use a heavy piece, then the rest is lighter,” she says. The tableware maintains the blue and green theme, including pieces from Isita Home and hand-blown glassware from neighboring Mallorca.

In the end, the owners asked López-Quesada to redo their home in Spain’s Basque region as well. It’s one of several projects she currently has across the country, including reimagining a hotel in Barcelona, and she’s also designing homes in Paris, Shanghai, and the Dominican Republic. Many of her projects are with repeat clients. “That’s how it works,” the designer says. “It’s like a chain.”

Last year, chronic flight delays and cancellations at Newark Liberty International Airport revealed a litany of problems with antiquated air traffic control systems. Today, those problems persist and are even more illuminated thanks to the FAA’s nationwide ground stop on Friday.

Even Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy admitted last year that some of the decades-old computer equipment air traffic controllers use looks like it came off the set of Apollo 13, and compared it to a 1967 Volkswagen Beetle.

Delta CEO Ed Bastian also revealed last year that, due to aging air traffic control systems, it actually takes longer today to fly from Atlanta to LaGuardia than it did in the 1950s when the airline opened that route.

“That’s the air traffic control system. It’s very slow. It’s congested,” he told TODAY in May 2025. “If you modernize the skies, you can kind of bring greater efficiency.”

By modernizing the skies, the airline chief means using satellite technology, the latest state-of-the-art equipment, and GPS. But currently, air traffic controllers have to use a radar point-and-shoot system from the 1960s.

“The screens look like something out of the 1960s and ‘70s,” Bastian said. “Our controllers and our operators need the best in order to keep us safe and make sure it maintains its safety record.”

Despite a barrage of emergency landings, machinery failure, a collision, and other air traffic control failures in the airline industry reported this year, he insists it’s still safe to fly.

“It is absolutely safe. It’s the safest form of transportation in the world,” Bastian said. “The most skilled aviation professionals in the world work and operate the U.S. airspace. So it is absolutely safe.”

But just because it’s safe doesn’t mean it’s acceptable for the U.S. to have such antiquated technology operating its airspace, Bastian said.

Although there is bipartisan support to modernize America’s air traffic control system, it’s a process that requires a lot of time and money. On May 8, 2025, Duffy and President Donald Trump announced a plan to build a brand-new air traffic control system “that will be the envy of the world.”

The plan includes replacing outdated infrastructure with wireless and satellite technologies at more than 4,600 air traffic control sites, building six new air traffic coordination centers, and replacing towers and terminal radar approach control (TRACONs).

“Our antiquated air traffic control system is affecting our workforce,” the Federal Aviation Administration said in a statement. “As Secretary Duffy has said, we must get the best safety technology in the hands of controllers as soon as possible.” Reuters reported in late March the FAA was short about 3,500 air traffic controllers.

On Friday, Duffy called on Congress to appropriate $50 billion for modernization. The House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee, though, estimates it could cost $12.5 billion. The Modern Skies Coalition, an umbrella group for aviation industry groups, estimated it would cost $31 billion or more.

But in the short term, the airline industry is making adjustments to improve flight delays and cancellations that result from air traffic control problems. Many airlines are pulling down their operations at Newark, Bastian said, which reduces some of the congestion at the airport.

“Now, that’s not good in the long run. It’s not good for consumers,” Bastian said. “It’s not good for the airport itself, but it’s the only thing that we have in the meantime.”

This Is How Fast The B‑2 Spirit Bomber Can Fly

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is an aircraft that still looks unusual, even today, decades after it first entered service. Its smooth, triangular shape looks more like a shadow than a conventional airplane, setting it apart from the familiar outlines of wings, tails, and vertical fins.

That distinctive appearance is the most visible sign that the B-2 was designed to operate in a very different way from earlier bombers. Understanding the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber means looking at the reasoning behind its design rather than just its specifications.

Nothing about the B-2 Spirit Bomber is accidental, from its overall form to the systems hidden beneath its surface. Instead of relying on speed, the aircraft combines efficiency, stealth, and long-range capability, demonstrating how deliberate design choices can fundamentally change how a strategic bomber is used.

At its core, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is a pure flying wing. There is no traditional ​​​​​​fuselage, no tail, and no vertical stabilizers, meaning that the entire aircraft is essentially one continuous lifting surface. Flying wing concepts had been explored for decades before the B-2 Spirit Bomber, including earlier Northrop designs.

However, none had ever reached operational service at this scale or level of sophistication. This configuration offers major aerodynamic benefits. With fewer protrusions and less structural clutter, drag is reduced, and lift efficiency improves. The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber can stay airborne for long periods while carrying heavy payloads, which is exactly what a strategic bomber needs to do.

However, the trade-off is stability. Flying wings are naturally less stable than conventional aircraft, but the B-2 Spirit Bomber solves this problem with advanced flight control computers, making thousands of adjustments every second to keep the aircraft flying smoothly and predictably.

The B-2 is famous for its stealth, but how much punch does it pack? Let’s find out.

Stealth is the defining characteristic of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber, and it starts with the aircraft’s shape. The smooth, continuous curves of the flying wing reflect radar energy away from the source rather than back toward it, while sharp angles and right-angle intersections, which are common radar reflectors, are carefully avoided wherever possible.

The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber uses radar-absorbent materials across much of its surface, turning incoming radar energy into heat rather than reflected signals. Even the engine intakes and exhausts are designed to reduce radar and infrared signatures.

Air enters through serpentine ducts that hide the engine faces, while exhaust is spread and cooled to make the aircraft harder to detect with heat-seeking sensors, resulting in an aircraft that can penetrate heavily defended airspace in ways that would be unthinkable for older bombers.

This brings us on to the key question: how fast can the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber fly? It is important to note that the B-2 Spirit Bomber is not a speed-focused aircraft, but it is far from slow. Its maximum speed is generally cited at around 628 mph (1,010 km/h), with a typical cruise speed closer to 560 mph (901 km/h).

This puts it just under the speed of sound, operating at high subsonic velocities that balance efficiency, long range, and survivability. These figures place the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber in an interesting position among bomber aircraft. It is slower than the supersonic Rockwell B-1B Lancer, which can exceed 900 mph (1,448 km/h).

However, it is broadly comparable to the venerable Boeing B-52, which has a top speed of around 650 mph (1,046 km/h). Compared to stealth-focused contemporaries like the Lockheed F-117 Nighthawk, the B-2 Spirit Bomber offers similar subsonic performance but with vastly greater range and payload. In practice, the B-2 Spirit Bomber relies on stealth and planning rather than raw speed to survive, making its top speed more than adequate for what the aircraft is designed to do.

One of the most impressive aspects of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is how far it can fly. With a published range of around 6,900 miles (11,104 km), the B-2 Spirit Bomber was designed from the start for intercontinental services, and when paired with aerial refueling, its endurance becomes extraordinary, allowing it to stay airborne for well over 40 hours.

This endurance has real operational consequences, and Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber crews have flown combat missions directly from the continental US to targets halfway around the world and back again. Long endurance reduces reliance on forward bases, which may be politically sensitive or vulnerable in a conflict, and it also allows planners to approach targets from unexpected directions, further enhancing the aircraft’s survivability and strategic flexibility.

Despite its sleek appearance, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber can carry a substantial weapons load, and internally, it can accommodate up to 40,000 lbs of ordnance, all carried inside weapon bays to preserve stealth. This includes a mix of conventional and nuclear weapons, making the B-2 Spirit Bomber a key component of strategic deterrence as well as conventional strike operations.

The emphasis is on precision rather than volume, and the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is capable of deploying GPS-guided bombs, allowing a small number of aircraft to strike multiple high-value targets with remarkable accuracy. This precision reduces the need for large formations and minimizes collateral damage.

The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is flown by a crew of just two (a pilot and a mission commander), which is a striking contrast to older bombers that required crews of four or more. Advanced avionics and automation make this possible, handling navigation, flight management, and many defensive functions that once demanded dedicated crew members.

Inside the cockpit, the emphasis is on situational awareness and workload management. Long missions mean crew endurance is just as important as aircraft performance, so the cockpit includes provisions for rest and careful ergonomics. The aircraft’s systems are designed to be highly reliable, but also flexible.

This allows crews to adapt to changing mission requirements while airborne. This means that flying the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is less about aggressive maneuvering and more about precision, patience, and trust in the systems that quietly keep the B-2 Spirit Bomber invisible.

The operational history of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is unusually straightforward, largely because only one country has ever flown it. Unlike many combat aircraft that are exported and adapted by multiple air forces, the B-2 Spirit Bomber has remained exclusively in the hands of the USAF since it entered service in the late 1990s.

That exclusivity reflects both its cost and its strategic importance. The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber first saw combat during the 1999 Kosovo conflict, when it flew missions directly from the US to strike targets in the former Yugoslavia. These demonstrated its ability to deliver precision-guided weapons over intercontinental distances while bypassing sophisticated air defenses.

A small number of B-2 Spirit Bombers were able to hit a significant portion of high-value targets early in the campaign, highlighting the efficiency of stealth-based operations. In the years that followed, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber was used by the US in Afghanistan and Iraq, again flying long-range missions supported by aerial refueling.

Its ability to carry large numbers of precision weapons made it particularly useful in the opening phases of these conflicts, when defended targets needed to be neutralized quickly. The B-2 Spirit Bomber also took part in strikes in Libya in 2011, where it was used to attack aircraft shelters.

While no other country has operated the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber, its influence has been global, and the aircraft has shaped how other nations approach long-range strike and stealth technology, even if none have fielded a direct equivalent. In practice, the B-2 Spirit Bomber has been used sparingly, but when it has been deployed, it has typically been at moments where its unique capabilities mattered most.

The aircraft might be the Air Force's deadliest weapon.

When comparing the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber to other aircraft in a similar role, it quickly becomes clear that it was never meant to compete on the same terms. Rather than focusing on speed or payload alone, the B-2 Spirit Bomber was designed around survivability in heavily defended airspace, and that choice sets it apart from both earlier and contemporary bombers.

Against legacy platforms like the Boeing B-52, the contrast is especially sharp. The B-52 offers greater payload capacity and has proven remarkably adaptable over decades of service, but it relies on escort support to operate safely in modern threat environments. The B-2 Spirit Bomber, by comparison, is built to fly directly through advanced air defenses, using stealth rather than distance as its primary layer of protection.

While the two aircraft share similar subsonic performance, their operational philosophies are very different. The Rockwell B-1 Lancer provides another useful comparison: with its variable-sweep wings and supersonic capability, the B-1 emphasizes speed and flexibility. However, its radar signature is significantly larger than that of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber, limiting where it can safely operate without support.

In practice, the B-2 Spirit Bomber trades outright speed for the ability to reach targets that would be inaccessible to faster but more detectable aircraft. Compared to stealth-focused designs like the Lockheed F-117 Nighthawk, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber operates on a much larger scale.

While both rely on low observability, the B-2 Spirit Bomber offers intercontinental range and a vastly greater payload, allowing it to carry out strategic missions rather than precision strikes on a limited number of targets. In this sense, the B-2 Spirit Bomber occupies a unique space, blending stealth with global reach in a way no other operational bomber has fully matched.

Cozy Bed to Selden Pass — John Muir Trail Day 15

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

After last night’s fabulous dinner, I expected to sleep like a baby.  And I did.  For a while.

Then a very disturbing event occurred, relating to my left wrist injury.  I woke up in the middle of the night and my thumb, index finger, third finger and fourth finger were completely and totally numb on my left hand, and the pinky was partially numb.

I hoped (fervently!) that this only meant I had slept on my injured wrist in a bad way.  The alternative would seem to be nerve damage of some kind.  Of the kind that says, “Get off the trail right away and see a doctor!!”

Gradually I managed to get feeling back.  As it slowly returned, I was cautiously relieved.  But I recognized that if the extreme numbness came back, then it was not a random misfortune, but something I really needed to worry about.  Aside from possible long-term damage, hiking with no feeling in my left hand was out of the question.

The other change overnight was temperature.  When I turned in, it was hot, even stuffy in my little cabin home, so I went to sleep with just my shirt on, with bare feet and bare legs.  There was no top sheet on the bed, just a sheet on the mattress and a blanket.  So, I slept between the sheet and the blanket.

Over the course of the night, it started to get ever chillier.  As the night went on, I added my little knit cap; then I added socks.  As it cooled further, I got out my sleeping bag liner and hopped in.  Finally, I got my puffy jacket and wrapped it around my legs and eventually that seemed to do the trick.  I was extremely resistant to pulling out my sleeping bag, I did not want to do that if I could help it.  Somehow that would have made the overnight feel less like a true break from the trail, I guess.

When I got up in the morning, my fingers all still had sensation, they were moving.  My wrist was still injured, but my fear of nerve damage abated.

I went up to breakfast and oh what a spread!  This is a place that serves hikers. They had scrapple, bacon, pancakes, eggs, fruit, yogurt, and coffee, of course.  The same crew from the night before all sat around and we stuffed our faces, understandably, as hungry hikers are prone to do.

Smiles showed me a picture of the inside of her cabin from the night before and it was hilarious!

With a great fear of SPIDERS, Smiles had set up and slept in her tent INSIDE her MTR cabin!

This picture gives a good sense of how she obtained her trail name!

So that’s what retired New York oncologists look like!

Lauralyn was there too, and she said, ”You know, you said you were going to recite that poem, how about it?”

Lauralyn encouraging me to recite The Raven

I warned them all it would take more than ten minutes and they said, “That’s okay, go ahead.”

As everyone was getting served, and kitchen staff were moving through the room, I recited The Raven.  They said they really enjoyed it, and I think they did.  With the distractions in the room, it was probably not my best performance, but I did all right.

It was time to get going, so I got my resupply bucket that was stashed in the kitchen under lock and key for bear protection.  Shlepping it to my cabin it felt very heavy.  Then I opened it and there was so much stuff I didn’t need.  I began sorting – everything I wanted went on the bed for packing, everything I did not went back in the bucket.

Smiles dropped by and told me, “I extended, I’m gonna stay here another day.”

“Okay, fine,” I replied, adding, “you know, I’m so tempted to go up to the dining area and fill one of my water bottles with their lemonade!”

She said, “Come on, I’m going that way anyway, let’s go.”

Together we went up.  Maybe my plan was perfectly acceptable, but it helped to have a partner in crime.  I filled up on lemonade, and it proved so delicious on the trail, I enjoyed it so much.

While near the dining room I used the flush toilet, the only one on their whole property.  It didn’t seem like a big deal, but I wanted to get my money’s worth.  (It was $375 a night, remember?)

Unfortunately, I never went in the hot spring, and the people who believe in those things were just raving about it.  The day before, when I might have done it, they had girls cleaning it with toothbrushes and I just didn’t feel like it any other time.

Back in the cabin, I finished packing, surprised that almost all my food fit in the bear canister.  It seemed I was getting a bit more efficient at that task.  My charger block almost got fully charged, finally, so that was good news too.

Before leaving Muir Trail Ranch, I dropped off my plastic five-gallon  bucket full of not-needed items (mostly food) at the reception location.   Just at that moment a German couple arrived and said, “Oh we forgot to send food, we have no food.”

I replied, “Well this is your lucky day, there’s a ton of food in here.  It’s good, expensive food too.”  It was, because a lot of it was the pricey freeze-dried stuff.  Somehow, I had badly miscalculated.  Whatever the reason, that couple made out like bandits.  The adage, “the trail provides” was certainly true for them that day!

I was facing a long uphill, over 3,000 feet to cross Selden Pass, and the very first stretch was one of the steepest.  After a break, when you first start walking, especially uphill, and when your pack is heavy from resupply, your legs can feel like lead.  Mine felt that way, for sure, but the good news was, it didn’t last long.

Pretty soon I was going up at a good pace, but I was stuck behind a horse being ridden by next-door-cabin Jay, who was behind a horse ridden by his guide.  Oddly, my pace was pretty close to that of the horses, but I didn’t want to get in front and hold them up.  At the same time, walking behind I was breathing the dust they were kicking up, so I kept having to drop back to get clear of that.

Finally, there was some separation between us.  The ascent included a lot of long switchbacks, so that the “crow fly” distance to MTR was not that great.  I could even still see it, just far, far beneath me now.  My blood was really pumping from all the uphill!

I was pleased at how I handled it.  True, it was at a lower elevation, but I thought at last I might be getting my trail legs.  Soon I crossed over some forests and meadows and was out of sight of the resort.  I spotted some wildflowers by the trail.

Perhaps it was the season, but I did not see the profusion of wildflowers on the JMT I have seen on other trails

In one of the meadows I was puzzled.  There was a path straight across, but someone had blocked it off.  As I skirted the glade, following the detour, I came across the team building the trail on more sustainable ground, to preserve the fragile meadow.   These folks do such an important job, making the trail better for all of us, and getting paid rather small salaries for doing it.  As always I stopped and thanked them profusely.  I wanted them to know that they and their work were deeply appreciated.

I came to the twin Sallie Keyes lakes.  As I arrived, I crossed some steppingstones over the exit creek and saw a beautiful snake swimming in the water; a garter snake, I think.  I even got its picture.

Right after this shot I watched the snake enter the water and swim away

I thought, “That seems very appropriate. I will go to Heart Lake and have my lunch.”  At Muir Trail Ranch breakfasts they also lay out the fixings for making and packing a lunch.  I had prepared a sandwich and packed it into my Nalgene soaking container.  I also brought a fresh apple.

When I got to Heart Lake, I took off my shoes and socks, dunked my bare feet into the lake, and ate half of my sandwich.  Under the dry surface of the moss I was sitting on, the lower portion was very wet, meaning my shorts were quickly soaked.

The moss is where I sat and the water is where I swam

As I realized how wet my seat was, I also realized the thought of a swim was very attractive.  So, I took off my clothes, put on my water shoes, and walked into Heart Lake.  I plunged my whole body in, swam out my usual 10 yards, then swam back.  It refreshed me so much.  And yes, I was skinny dipping, except for my water shoes.

But nobody came by while I was swimming.  Afterwards I dried off a little bit and put my damp shorts back on.  Then I ate the rest of my sandwich and my apple and finished off the MTR lemonade.  It was so good.  I’d been drinking it all day, climbing and climbing.  Now it was gone.

A little hard to see, but those are the two upper lobes of the “heart” that names Heart Lake

On Selden Pass I was alone, and it was nice to be there by myself.  Then I started down, realizing it was now around 3.30 p.m., which meant my progress was faster than I expected.  That morning I envisioned arriving at closer to 5:00 p.m.  It seemed I was in better shape than I thought.

Twin estrangement is a really important issue that is often ignored because twin mythology suggests that all twins should get along, as their closeness creates a perfect interpersonal relationship. Raising twins is hard, and estrangement is very predictable when there is not enough focus on individual development. Believing that all twins should and will be able to get along causes shame and humiliation for twins and their parents when they don't. This is an unfortunate problem.

For most of my adult life, I have been writing about twins or gifted children. In general, my focus is on the social and emotional problems related to identity development that twins or other types of multiple births naturally experience because they share their caregivers and home environment. Twin development, in general, is quite different and more complicated than the developmental identity challenges of non-twins. The most common problems that twins have when they are young are questions and concerns that revolve around the sharing of their parents, day-to-day experiences, friendships, and education. Twins also have concerns about their twin's opinion, and their need for their twin to be present in stressful situations and understand immediately what is wrong.

The most common and unfortunate disagreements occur when twins continue to be estranged during the holiday season and cannot get the support they need from their twin. Twin estrangement is often seen when one twin is favored over the other by their parents or other caregiving adults.

Social development, including making relationships with non-twins, is also unique and very complicated as twins develop and mature. I can say with great certainty that fitting into the non-twin world is enormously difficult for most twins, who feel ashamed of themselves for fighting with their twin sister or brother and at the same time lonely for their twin sister or brother’s companionship and advice. Outsiders directly question why a twin cannot get along with their truest companion. These questions about closeness frustrate and annoy twins who are trying to get along and falling short of doing so.

Estrangement can grow out of frustration when twins disagree. When differences can be reconciled, the harmony between twins can be reestablished, and estrangement will be reduced. Twin estrangement is both very serious and hard to control. Do not minimize or ignore estrangement. Parenting for individuality helps twins get over estrangement more easily than twins who are encouraged to compete with each other and stay alike.

My interest in twins and their unique development is related to my own identity as a twin and why my twin and I could not resolve our love-hate relationship. As a USC graduate student in psychology and education, I learned a great deal about how twins were and are still used in psychological research to understand the relative importance of nature, nurture, and parenting in development. When genetic determinants are important, twins are often used to figure out what is inherited and what is a function of proper environmental care. There is no agreement about what is more important, genes or environmental care, for identity development, but there is a lot of research on this issue. In my opinion, all of these influences are extremely critical to twin identity development. In particular, parental attentiveness is extremely valuable.

Taking care of twins is extremely time-consuming and difficult physically, emotionally, and personally. Finding help and paying the bills for all of the extras is a challenge. Here are some of my suggestions for reducing your parenting stress levels.

1. Make sure that you get to know each of your twins' likes and dislikes. Know your twins as individuals with unique interests and feelings.

2. Teach twins how to get along with each other, even if this seems impossible when they are fighting intensely or arguing with you or each other.

3. Make sure that your twins know that some toys, food, attention, friends, and interests cannot be shared. This point of view will promote individuality and reduce anger, fighting, and estrangement.

4. Help your twins understand that friendships should not be shared.

5. Find twin families that want to be your friends and arrange family or twin play dates.

It is hard to be a twin, and it is hard to raise twins. Try to get the best help you can. Educate yourself on what twins need to thrive.

But the Tarmac is so seductive that he would just shrug and say “he’s got a point.” And in a moment of weakness, I would drop the hammer and head into the red, just because the SL9 makes that so rewarding.

The Tarmac SL9 is Specialized’s aero road bike, with the S-Level sitting just below the S-Works in Specialized’s top tier of carbon, aero road bikes, where it replaced the Tarmac SL8 Pro. After it got an update this year, I spent several weeks riding it, putting nearly 1,000 miles on it

Specialized offers the Tarmac SL9 in four models, all available with SRAM and Shimano drivetrains (Ars tested the SRAM version). Notably, you don’t need to buy the top-of-the-line bike to get the top-of-the-line groupset. Here’s the breakdown of the Tarmac SL9 lineup (note that the Shimano versions of the Expert is 20 g lighter and Comp is 70 grams heavier than the SRAM versions):

The $10,500 S-Level offers the top-of-the-line SRAM Red groupset and integrated cockpit, just like the $14,000 S-Works. But the frameset, wheelset, and saddle differ. The major difference is the frame: the S-Level gets the same carbon-fiber FACT 10r frame as the Expert and Comp. At 822 g for a 56 cm frame, the FACT 10r is 135 g heavier than the FACT 12r used on the S-Works. Despite the weight difference, Specialized says the FACT 10r offers the same stiffness and comfort characteristics as its lighter sibling. Along with the frame, different wheelsets and saddles make the S-Level 500 g heavier than the S-Works. With bottle cages, a Garmin mount, and my preferred Shimano XTR XC PD-M9200 MTB pedals, the S-Level I tested weighed in at 7.98 kg. By way of comparison, my own road bike—a Gen 8 Trek Madone—weighs in at 7.71 kg.

I rode the S-Level all around the Chicago suburbs as well as in central Illinois. Unlike the Diverge 4 Pro, I wasn’t able to test its climbing ability in the Rocky Mountains. All we’ve got in most of Illinois are short, punchy climbs … and a lot of long flats. I rode The S-Level on everything from freshly laid asphalt to freshly laid chipseal, including a couple of 60-mile-plus rides on the country roads downstate.

Visually, the S-Level is striking. The blue-on-white with gray striping is one of Specialized’s better-looking colorways. And it looks aerodynamic. Indeed, Specialized says the Tarmac SL9 is the most aerodynamic road bike it has ever made. Notable changes from the previous-generation Tarmac SL8 to SL9 involved tweaking the head tube, rerouting the rear hydraulic brake hose to accommodate the narrower head tube, and moving to a dropped-down tube for improved aerodynamics. But the most visually striking change is the Win Fin. As the seat tube rises from the bottom bracket, it deviates from the vertical to follow the curve of the wheel as it approaches the seat stays. When riding with just a single water bottle on the downtube—common in pro races where a full bottle is just a team car away—Specialized claims Win Fin saves riders half a watt.

The S-Level I tested came with Specialized’s 30 mm Specialized Cotton TLR tires—used by Red Bull-BORA-hansgrohe on the UCI World Tour—in a tubeless setup. According to Bicycle Rolling Resistance, the Cotton TLR outperforms the much-loved Continental GP 5000 S TR tires when it comes to rolling resistance (at 28 mm). Using Silca’s tire pressure calculator, I rode with the tires inflated to 73 psi in the front and 75 psi in the rear, a departure from the 62/64 setting on my 32 mm Contis—and there is a slight, but noticeable difference in ride comfort between the S-Level and my own road bike. Mounted on the Roval Rapide CL III carbon wheels with 21 mm internal width, the Cotton TLR tires measured 30.2 mm wide (my 32 mm Contis are a hair over 33 mm).

On every surface shy of brand-new chipseal, the Tarmac felt comfortable and balanced, with the bike absorbing some of the punishment meted out by uneven pavement. Fresh chipseal is another matter entirely, as it feels more like gravel, which felt jarring on the SL9 S-Level. What was particularly impressive about the SL9 is how great it feels on long rides. Some manufacturers are skilled at building aero bikes that feel great on hours-long rides, and the Tarmac is just as good as the Trek Madone SLR 9 AXS Gen 8 I reviewed in this regard.

When you’re ready to drop the hammer, the S-Level is your willing accomplice. It’s twitchy in all of the good ways, with instantaneous response to the rider’s effort. The SRAM Red AXS groupset makes shifting buttery smooth (and the bonus buttons on the brake hoods allow the rider to change screens on the bike computer without releasing the brake hoods), and the drivetrain (50t/37t chainrings and a 10t-36t cassette) offers gear ratios for every type of terrain.

The first few seconds of a climb on the Tarmac SL9 S-Level feel like they come for free. Whether I stayed seated or cranked out watts in an upright position, there was no lag or flex as the bike snapped forward. Descents were amazing, and cornering on the S-Level is sublime—I felt balanced and in control at all times.

There are a few nits to pick. The reach to the brakes in the drops felt excessive on my first couple of rides, and I had to adjust them slightly to brake easily on descents. Also, I would prefer shorter cranks on the Tarmac. The trend among professional riders has been toward shorter cranks because it makes holding an aero position easier and reduces hip and knee flexion. To its credit, Specialized reduced the crank length from 172.5 mm on the 56 cm SL8 to 170 mm on the same-sized SL9, but I run 165 mm cranks on my road bikes and noticed the difference on the S-Level. One demographic that buys expensive road bikes is middle-aged dudes with empty nests and disposable incomes, and they are the ones most likely to appreciate shorter cranks.

The 3D-printed Power Pro Mirror is a fine saddle, but there’s a reason I ride with a Specialized S-Works Power with Mirror on my Trek—it’s more comfortable and offers noticeably more padding around the cutout. And if I owned the S-Level, I’d move to 32 mm tires as soon as I wore out the 30s for better compliance.

Specialized brags that the Tarmac SL9 S-Works is the fastest bike ever made, and it claims the S-Level is the second-fastest bike ever made. I can’t speak to that claim, but the SL9 S-Level checks all of the boxes for an aerodynamic road bike. It’s lightweight, it’s fast, it’s responsive, and it’s great on rollers and punchy climbs. And it’s nice to get a SRAM Red groupset without springing for the absolute top-of-the-line model. $10,500 is a hefty sum for a road bike, but I’d be a lot more willing to forego the weight savings from the lighter components (frame, wheels, seatpost, bottom bracket, and bearings) of the S-Works—and spend $3,500 less. The real selling point, though, is how often it made me want to go harder just for the fun of it. That little imp has a point: Zone 2 can wait until tomorrow.

Posted November 12, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

Co-authored by Mark Shelvock and Joelle Adams

It’s an ordinary Monday; you’re buttering toast, answering emails, and negotiating with the dog about when it’s time to go for a walk. Underneath all of it, a question keeps surfacing in your psyche: What happened to me?

Maybe three weeks ago, on a psilocybin retreat, you dissolved into the universe and haven’t entirely come back. Perhaps during surgery, you watched the medical team from somewhere near the ceiling and had a near-death experience. When you finally tell someone, their reaction in those first few seconds may shape how you carry this for years.

If this sounds familiar, you’re in larger company than you might think. Meditation teacher Jack Kornfield named this predicament decades ago; after the ecstasy comes the laundry.1 The experience is over, and the work of figuring out what to do with it has just begun.

The good news is that people have been mapping this terrain for a long time.

Spiritual teachers have identified various pathways that experiencers take following psychedelic and other events such as near-death, out-of-body, spontaneous spiritual awakening, kundalini, alien or UFO, and similar exceptional experiences that alter consciousness and challenge concepts of reality.

Carl Jung, who described his own near-death experience after a heart attack in 1944, saw such moments as encounters with the deeper layers of the psyche.2 In his view, what surfaces in these states, including the shadow and archetypal images shared across cultures, needs to be gradually integrated into conscious life. He called this lifelong process individuation, which means becoming more fully and authentically oneself.

Rhea White and Suzanne Brown mapped five stages experiencers tend to move through following exceptional experiences.3 First, the initiating event itself, during which the experiencers’ ordinary worlds are disrupted by an experience that leaves them questioning the nature of reality. Then the search for reconciliation, during which experiencers try to make sense of what happened to them by seeking out others who have had similar experiences and looking for frameworks that explain the event. In the between two worlds stage, people begin bringing their new understanding of reality into daily life but often feel pulled in two directions: unable to fully embrace this new reality, yet unable to return to the beliefs they held before.

In the experiential paradigm, as the experiencer moves to the next stage, they begin to accept what happened during the initiating event and fully integrate their new perspectives into their daily lives. Finally, the experiencer finds a new way of being in the world, characterized by alignment to one’s purpose and a desire to be of service. Once a psycho-spiritually transformative experience has been woven into this new way of living, White and Brown call it an "exceptional human experience." Rather than a ladder to climb, these stages are more like landmarks on a winding path; people may begin anywhere, double back, or revisit a stage more than once.

Early findings from a recent preprint study point to more nuanced pathways people may take after a psychedelic or other exceptional experience. One of us (Joelle Adams), along with colleagues Pascal Michael and David Luke, found that experiencers tend to follow one of four pathways: challenging, what goes up, slow flow, or deep surrender.4

On the challenging pathway, people struggle to make sense of what happened and may experience depersonalization, derealization, depression and anxiety, sleep disturbances, and other symptoms that undermine their well-being. Those on the what goes up pathway report an initial "high" following their experience, a period of positive mood, energy, clarity, and alignment with the universe. This is followed by a longer "low" when they face psychological, physical, relational, and spiritual challenges. They may chase the initial high, seeking repeat experiences to recapture those early feelings. Eventually, with professional support and meaningful changes in their lives, they can find a new equilibrium, much like White and Brown’s new way of being in the world stage.

Adams, Michael, and Luke also found that some experiencers follow an iterative pathway: the slow flow. Following the initial experience, they take their time to process what happened and integrate new perspectives and insights into their lives, coming to a place of equilibrium. Only then do they seek out or initiate new events or altered states of consciousness. Finally, those on the deep surrender pathway report integrating their experiences with relative ease, accepting and surrendering to new insights and concepts of reality. They often change their life circumstances, such as jobs and relationships, quite quickly, and report few challenges in returning to homeostasis.

For those who find an experience difficult to integrate, the company of others who have walked this path can be invaluable. Sharing the experience with a trusted friend, or with a therapist who is open to non-ordinary states and approaches them without pathologizing, can provide the steadiness needed to make meaning of a reality-altering event. Reading, podcasts, and workshops can offer both frameworks for understanding and the quiet reassurance that others have travelled similar terrain, though some people find this material overwhelming and benefit from taking it in gradually. Solitude, time in nature, journaling, and creative expression are also frequently described as essential companions to integration.

Discernment matters too. Confiding in someone who dismisses or misunderstands the experience can deepen the isolation it so often brings. Likewise, rushing back into the demands of daily life without pausing to reflect can leave the experience unprocessed and, at times, psychologically destabilizing. Chasing a return to the original high, or clinging rigidly to any single framework for explaining what happened, can also keep people stuck. Meditation and other spiritual practices can sometimes aid integration, though for some they may reactivate difficult emotional states; it’s often wise to proceed gradually and with compassionate support.

In the end, integration doesn't fully happen on the idealized spiritual mountaintop. More commonly, people learn to let their insights shape how they live, work, and love, becoming, in Jung’s sense, more fully themselves. With support, time, patience, and conscious intentionality, these experiences can lead to greater well-being, deeper empathy, and a quieter fear of death. The toast still needs buttering and the dog still needs walking, but the difference is who is doing it.
