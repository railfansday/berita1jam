All the Award-Winning Photos From Nature Photographer of the Year 2026

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

All the Award-Winning Photos From Nature Photographer of the Year 2026

The Nature Photographer of the Year 2026 winners have been unveiled, showcasing the world’s best nature, wildlife, and landscape photography.

This year’s competition received 27,108 entries from photographers in nearly 100 countries, a record-breaking number for the longtime photo contest.

Finnish photographer Hannu Ahonen bested stiff competition to earn the title of Nature Photographer of the Year. Ahonen’s award-winning photo, “The One that Left and the One that Stayed,” is an extraordinary image of a water lily leaf positioned alongside its icy duplicate.

“It is not often that a photograph of a plant is crowned the overall winner of a major photography competition, but the jury could not overlook this fascinating image by Hannu Ahonen,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom. “First and foremost, the photograph captures a unique situation: a water lily leaf with an almost perfect black-and-white replica of it lying next to it in the water.”

Ahonen’s winning photo tells a compelling and unusual story. The living water lily leaf is positioned on the left, while the ice that formed on top of it and slipped off sits delicately to the side, blown away slightly by the breeze. Even the veins from the leaf are visible in the icy shell.

Nature Photographer of the Year adds that the photo reveals the “marvelous beauty of nature” while embodying its transient character. The sheet of ice will soon melt away, joining the water that keeps the lily alive in the first place. It’s a symbolic photo at a time when climate change is impacting all life on Earth, especially its ice.

“The winter ice had arrived, but it remained snowless for a long time. Some of the pond’s lily pad leaves began to crumble on top of the ice. The wind carried a few of them away, revealing delicate, frosty ornaments left behind. I eagerly captured this natural phenomenon, something I had never witnessed before,” Ahonen explains.

Ahonen has an inspiring story. He has ADHD and lives in a wilderness home in Finland with no carbon footprint. Rarely venturing more than a couple of miles from his home, he spends much of his time behind the camera in his yard, using photography as a spiritual escape and creative outlet.

Alongside the overall winner, Ahonen, the competition’s jury also selected winners across nearly a dozen categories. There are also special prizes for youth photographers and an accomplished portfolio. Ahonen took the top spot in the Plants and Fungi category, so that category is not included below.

French photographer Lucas Mugnier-Lavorel won this year’s Birds category for his environmental portrait of a Magellanic woodpecker in Patagonia.

“Environmental portraits of wildlife can make us feel closer to the animals despite being farther away from them, when done right. This image of a Magellanic woodpecker in its gorgeous habitat was a perfect example. It stood out from thousands of submissions because of its understated sense of place, beauty, simplicity, and timing,” says judge Steffen Foerster.

“Overall, it was a very successful reminder of how the portrayal of nature in its literal state can still be the most powerful of photographic expressions,” Foerster concludes.

British photographer Paul Goldstein’s foggy photo of a giraffe earned the top prize in the Mammals category.

“Quiet images can be easy to overlook in the fiercely competitive mammal category, one of the largest in NPOTY. This ethereal image of a giraffe peeking out of the mist revealed its strengths gradually. Initially a little disorienting, it offered just enough of a visual puzzle to keep the viewer engaged until the image resolved,” Foerster explains.

Judges tapped Marcus Kuan’s macro photo of a surprisingly rich and diverse little ecosystem alongside a pitcher plant in the Other Animals category. The Singaporean photographer impressed the panel with his composition and lighting.

“The lighting, colors, and unexpected gathering of species immediately caught our attention. Kuan beautifully captured a world where crustaceans, amphibians, and carnivorous plants cross paths,” explains jury member Louis Guillot.

“As judges, we were drawn to the possibilities unfolding within this single frame. Could the pitchers be digesting invertebrates? Might the frog be exploring a potential egg-laying site, while the crab continues its daily search for food among the plants?” Guillot continues.

Photographer J Fritz Rumpf from the United States won this year’s Landscape category with a silhouetted photo of Iguazú Falls in Argentina.

“What immediately caught our attention was the incredible light and atmosphere in this photo. The longer we looked, the more fascinating it became. There is so much to discover: the last light of day illuminating the mist rising from Iguazú Falls, the silhouettes of trees and rocks, the birds circling above, the sunlight streaming through an opening in the rock at the center, and the subtle waterfall on the left,” says jury member Ilse Koks.

Another American photographer, Matt Pérez, won the Underwater category with a striking photo of a turtle beneath a collection of newt eggs.

“Pérez’s photograph immediately drew us in. As a jury, we wanted to linger over it, exploring the details and wondering what we were seeing,” remarks Guillot. “Newt egg masses decorate the submerged branches like baubles on a Christmas tree, framing the scene and guiding our eyes towards the pond turtle with its piercing yellow eyes.”

Guillot adds that the scene looks “otherworldly,” which is a very apt description.

Chinese photographer Xuzhong Liu’s abstract drone shot of a winding landscape ticks all the right boxes. It has interesting patterns, striking colors, and a very pleasing composition.

Liu captured the image in a wetland area in China and says the scene strongly reminded him of peach blossoms he had previously photographed—hence the title, “Peach Blossoms in the Snow.”

“This photograph has everything that can make a drone shot interesting and fascinating. First and foremost, there are beautiful shapes and subtle colours, but there is also a sense of alienation. What am I actually looking at, and what is the scale of the landscape in the photograph?” asks the chair of the judging panel, Theo Bosboom.

Czech photographer Petr Bambousek’s winning photo depicts a sobering scene of a Malayan sun bear resting inside a garbage incinerator.

“The winning image stood out for its striking depiction of the impact human activity has on local wildlife,” explains jury member Lana Tannir. “The sleeping Malayan sun bear inside a garbage incinerator creates a powerful visual contrast, showing how habitat loss and human waste increasingly force wild animals into close contact with human environments.”

Tannir says Bambousek’s photo is “unexpected and unsettling.”

Hungarian photographer László Tóth won the Black and White category for his striking, high-contrast, abstract photo.

“This photo really stood out to us. At first, we were not quite sure what we were looking at. It sparked some wonderful discussions among the jury and made us look even closer,” says Ilse Koks.

As for what the photo actually depicts, it’s a thin layer of snow on top of a frozen lake, shaped by the wind.

This photo might be familiar to PetaPixel readers. Norwegian photographer Audun Rikardsen recently won an award for a similar underwater portrait of a polar bear peering down through a hole in the ice.

“Just when we thought we had seen about every kind of polar bear image there is, along comes this shot taken from inside a hole in the ice looking up at the bear,” says Steffen Foerster. “Once you realize what is happening, you start thinking about how this shot could possibly have been taken. Then it dawns on you that it seems almost impossible, no matter how.”

This special category, “De Lage Landen” (which translates to “The Low Countries”), celebrates photos captured in the low-lying region of northwestern Europe, including the Netherlands, where Nature Photographer of the Year is based. This year’s winning photo is by Hungarian photographer Enikő Kálovics.

“The winning image stood out for its ability to transform a familiar landscape into something dreamlike and unexpected,” says Lana Tannir. “The combination of the tiny lacewings, the autumn forest, and the purplish night sky creates a delicate, atmospheric interpretation of the nature of ‘De Lage Landen.'”

American photographer Parham Pourahmad, who was also recently celebrated in the 2027 North American Nature Photography Association (NANPA) Showcase, won this year’s Youth category with a lovely monochromatic photo of a coyote beneath a large tree.

“In ‘Tree of Life’, Pourahmad captured a striking scene that could comfortably hold its own in the adult categories,” says Guillot. “Black and white was an excellent choice, defining the coyote’s silhouette and revealing the majestic oak’s intricate network of branches. Even within this wider scene, the coyote’s downward gaze is clear. Pourahmad explained that it was searching for rodent prey, a small detail that brings the photograph to life.”

Pourahmad has also been named Youth Photographer of the Year.

The Fred Hazelhoff Award celebrates a portfolio of nature photos, rather than a single image. This year’s portfolio winner is German photographer Simone Baumeister.

“In the portfolio category, the bar is set considerably higher for participating photographers than in the individual categories. Whilst a single fantastic photograph is sufficient there, in the portfolio category you need a whole series of outstanding images that not only complement one another but also fit together well in terms of style and theme. Simone Baumeister has succeeded brilliantly in this with her portfolio on eagle owls in the city,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom.

Baumeister has been fascinated by owls for as long as she has been interested in nature photography. The eagle owl’s story is a positive one, in contrast to the plight of many species across the globe. The species, once on the brink of extinction in Germany, has made an incredible recovery. Its resurgence is thanks largely to major conservation efforts and extensive reintroduction programs.

“It was perched in twilight on a church, right below the street lamps, moving cars, and a string of people passing by, with music blaring from a pub right on the church square. Not a soul took notice of the impressive bird with a wingspan of 1.7 meters flying up the steeple, sitting enthroned right within everybody’s view,” the photographer says of the first time she saw an eagle owl in a city.

Ever since then, she has been consistently excited by eagle owls and keen to photograph them in many different places. Observing and photographing them in various cities inevitably morphed into a dedicated project.

Alongside the category and special award winners above, the judges also selected runners-up and highly commended photos, all of which can be seen on the Nature Photographer of the Year website and below.

Rockstar's Grand Theft Auto 6 launches on November 19, and it's expected to help boost console sales for PS5 and Xbox Series X|S. But the uptick in hardware sales might not be as dramatic as you might think--and there is a good reason why.

According to Circana's Mat Piscatella, there will be "probably" be a boost to PS5 and Xbox Series X|S sales when GTA 6 comes out, but the uptick could be muted due to the recent PS5 and Xbox Series X|S price hikes. Historical prices

When GTA 5 launched in 2013, the average price paid for a new PS3 was $252 and the Xbox 360 was $232. Adjusted for inflation, that would be $363 for the PS3 today and $334 for the Xbox 360. Those prices are significantly below the average price of a new PS5 ($646) and Xbox Series X|S ($663) today.

"Not a good thing"

Earlier this year, Take-Two boss Strauss Zelnick, who oversees the company launching GTA 6, said, "The rising cost of hardware is not a good thing." At the same time, Zelnick isn't worried about the impact on GTA 6, in part because he believes the game is so good that people will find a way to pay for a new console.

 "However, a lower price point for hardware would be a good thing because there’d be more hardware in people’s hands," he said. "Probably the most relevant thing is the world continues to move to open systems, and that will continue.”

Capcom Reveals Fanmade Robot Master for Mega Man: Dual Override

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Daniel is a lifelong gamer with a love for various genres and gameplay styles who enjoys exploring games and playing from various angles.

The most iconic characters in the Mega Man franchise, second only to ol’ Rock himself, are the Robot Masters, the powerful, highly-specialized robots built by Dr. Light, Dr. Wily, or whoever else who are programmed to wreak havoc upon the world, usually in batches of eight. Naturally, the newest upcoming entry in the mainline series, Mega Man: Dual Override, will have a new swath of Robot Masters to do battle with, several of which like Jet Man, Cook Man, and Twinkle Girl have already been revealed.

However, in addition to these new Capcom-made Robot Masters, Dual Override is slated to bring back a long-dormant tradition of the Mega Man series: fan-designed Robot Masters. Not long after the game’s initial reveal, Capcom launched a character design contest in which fans were encouraged to design their own original Robot Master and submit it for a chance to be included in the game.

Capcom showed off a new Mega Man: Dual Override demo at Tokyo Game Show this year, proving the veteran franchise has still got it.

Many months later, Capcom has officially announced the winner of this contest and revealed the fanmade Robot Master who will be joining the rest of the new Wily Numbers. Strap on your scouring pads and turn your spray nozzles to “ON,” because our latest menace is Cleanser Man.

As director Shinya Ikuta and producer Hiroyuki Minamitani announced in a video revealed during its TwitchCon 2026 panel and posted to the official Mega Man YouTube channel, Cleanser Man was selected out of around 10,000 entrants into the contest from around the world, which then had to be meticulously narrowed down to 20 proper participants, followed by just 6 finalists. These 6 finalists included Sweeper Woman, Recycle Man, Juggle Man, Valve Man, Cactus Man, and of course, Cleanser Man.

“In the end, we chose Cleanser Man because of the unique way the concept was brought together and the originality of the color design,” Ikuta explained.

These 6 finalists included Sweeper Woman, Recycle Man, Juggle Man, Valve Man, Cactus Man, and of course, Cleanser Man.

According to an official character summary sent by Capcom to Game Informer, Cleanser Man is a cleaning robot with an obsessive love of all things sparkling and pristine who will go to absurd lengths to scrub out any perceived imperfections, even if he ends up breaking things in the process. His stage will be set in a trash processing plant that’s flooding the world with garbage so people better appreciate his dedication to cleanliness.

The Tokyo Game Show 2026 Presentation revealed new details regarding both the second protagonist and the game's core mechanics.

Cleanser Man comes to us from a Brazilian artist who goes by “miya” on their X, formerly Twitter account. Upon the reveal that their design was chosen, they posted a screencap of Cleanser Man’s official render side-by-side with their concept art, adding the caption, “The attention to detail almost made me cry. It truly means a lot.”

Not only was the character-design contest a fun way to get people hyped up for Mega Man: Dual Override, not to mention showcase some really cool character-design work, it’s also an important revival of a long-forgotten Mega Man tradition.

Following the release of the original Mega Man game and prior to the release of Mega Man 2, Capcom held a similar contest for its Japanese players to decide the designs of that game’s eight Robot Master. This contest format would be repeated for every subsequent mainline Mega Man game up to and including Mega Man 8, resulting in many of the most notoriously-difficult Robot Masters, before being discontinued from Mega Man & Bass through Mega Man 11. While there was still some fan design encouragement, it was mostly relegated to spin-off series like Battle Network and Star Force.

While it’s only for a single Robot Master rather than a full set of eight, Cleanser Man is still the first fanmade Robot Master to make it into a mainline Mega Man title since 1996, with the added accolade that he’s the first Robot Master to come from a designer in South America. Nimoy, who played Spock for almost 50 years, couldn't be expected to give one consistent answer for that long.

But there are a few episodes that Nimoy consistently named among his favorites, including in his 1975 autobiography "I Am Not Spock." One of them was "This Side of Paradise," co-written by D. C. Fontana and Nathan Butler and one of the best Spock episodes of "Star Trek." The Enterprise visits colony world Omicron Ceti III, home to a plant species that secretes spores that take root in humanoid hosts. Once they do, the hosts remain in a state of perfect bliss, but they also lose all their ambition. (The story is a likely reference to the Lotus-Eaters in "The Odyssey.")

Spock is one of the infected. His stone face cracks into rubble as he becomes joyful, happily expressive, and even rekindles a romance with local botanist Leila Kalomi (Jill Ireland). Nimoy wrote in "I Am Not Spock" that he enjoyed the episode for showing Spock in a mood we almost never saw him — not coldly logical, sarcastic, or struggling to control his emotions, but genuinely carefree.

"This is a very special episode for me for obvious reasons and particularly because, where Spock was so totally adult in most of his attitudes and reactions throughout the series, this episode provided a chance to expose the complete child within. We saw him loving, picnicking, romping, and literally swinging playfully in the trees. It was a total exposure of internal Spock that so many people have always suspected was there."

Watching "This Side of Paradise" now, it might remind you of the Apple TV series "Pluribus," where all of humanity is turned into a blissful hive mind (collectively "The Others"). In "This Side of Paradise," strong negative emotions literally destroy the spores, which is also a weakness of the Others in "Pluribus."

In "This Side of Paradise," though, there's no identity theft. The spores let Spock be his innermost self. Compare other times we see Spock's emotions come out, like in "The Naked Time" where he cries over having never told his mother he loved her, or "Journey to Babel" where said mother, Amanda (Jane Wyatt), recounts how Spock used to cry because other Vulcan children bullied him. As only a half-Vulcan, Spock is especially self-conscious about living up to his people's teachings about logic.

When the spores hit him, those anxieties melt away. Note how Leila says to him he has "no need to hide [his] inner face anymore" and Spock says "I can love you," as if finally allowing himself something he'd previously denied.

Nimoy described other "Star Trek" episodes as favorites such as "Devil in the Dark," where the Enterprise crew visit a mining colony to find a creature killing the inhabitants. The culprit, a Horta (resembling a pile of living molten lava), is sentient and Spock telepathically communicates with it, discovering it was only acting to protect its offspring. Nimoy praised that one as a "wonderful episode about the fear of the unknown," and "Devil in the Dark" is known to be a favorite of William Shatner's, too.

"Devil in the Dark" and "This Side of Paradise" are very different episodes, but both ones anchored by Spock. Little surprise that Leonard Nimoy loved them.

rune bosse puts living trees on life support in a vision of ecological collapse

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

equipped with respirators and artificial lighting, rune bosse's 'tree lungs' imagines a future where nature itself may need life support.

Danish artist Rune Bosse places living trees on artificial life support in Lung Trees, an installation presented at the Danish Architecture Center in Copenhagen as part of its Age of Nature exhibition, which ran through September 2026. Each tree stands inside a transparent, inflated plastic enclosure, and is surrounded by mechanical equipment and artificial lighting. With their branches reaching toward overhead lamps, each tree seems to have been removed from the forest and placed under intensive care.

The installation imagines a future in which trees might depend on technology to survive, even as humans continue to depend on the ecological systems that trees help sustain. It reverses a relationship we tend to take for granted. Forests have long been described as the lungs of the planet, absorbing carbon dioxide through photosynthesis and releasing oxygen. Here, Bosse gives those lungs a mechanical counterpart, and suggests a world in which the natural systems supporting human life might themselves require artificial support.

The installation brings together living vegetation and an exposed network of machines. Transparent plastic sheeting stretches over metal frames, enclosing the trees while allowing visitors to see the soil and branches inside. Artificial lights illuminate the foliage, while equipment supplies air to the enclosed environment. The temporary structures have the practical appearance of makeshift greenhouses, although their association with medical equipment gives them a more unsettling character.

In a related 2025 presentation at Berlin’s Neue Nationalgalerie, titled Lungetræ / Lungenbaum, Bosse made the breathing movement particularly visible. A living tree and a section of forest floor were enclosed within a flexible PVC membrane attached to a metal framework. At regular intervals, a respirator pushed air into the structure, causing the plastic to expand and slowly collapse around the tree. The movement resembles the inflation of a giant lung, giving physical form to the invisible exchange of gases between a tree and its surroundings.

Bosse’s interest in trees extends well beyond the gallery. Alongside his training at the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts, the artist studied forestry and nature management, developing a practice that follows the growth of living plants over time. At Oremandsgaard, an estate in southern Denmark, he has established an outdoor forest laboratory where he experiments with shaping trees as they grow. Some are bent into unusual forms, while others are guided to grow together. Their final shapes emerge over years, influenced by the conditions of the surrounding landscape.

This work grew partly from his frustration with bringing natural materials into museums, where plants often have to be cut, dried or preserved before they can be exhibited. His outdoor experiments allow trees to remain alive and continue changing long after the artist’s initial intervention. Lung Trees approaches the same relationship from a different direction. Inside the gallery, keeping the trees alive becomes part of the artwork itself, exposing the equipment and artificial conditions that make their temporary existence possible.

The installation touches on an uncomfortable contradiction in the way humans approach ecological preservation. By enclosing the trees and supplying them with artificial conditions, Bosse creates an image of protection that also suggests dependency. The machinery appears to care for the living organisms, yet the entire arrangement places their survival within a system designed and operated by humans. The trees remain living participants in the work, although their relationship with the surrounding environment has been fundamentally altered.

Bosse first developed Lungetræer in 2019, and its more recent presentations give the work a continuing relevance amid growing concerns about biodiversity loss and the deterioration of ecosystems. The installation offers a speculative glimpse of what technological preservation might look like when ecological conditions can no longer support life on their own. Its most unsettling quality comes from the simplicity of the scene.

A living tree stands inside a plastic chamber while a machine pushes air around it. The visitor watches the artificial lung expand and contract, confronted with the possibility that the relationship between humans and forests could one day become this fragile.

Last year, chronic flight delays and cancellations at Newark Liberty International Airport revealed a litany of problems with antiquated air traffic control systems. Today, those problems persist and are even more illuminated thanks to the FAA’s nationwide ground stop on Friday.

Even Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy admitted last year that some of the decades-old computer equipment air traffic controllers use looks like it came off the set of Apollo 13, and compared it to a 1967 Volkswagen Beetle.

Delta CEO Ed Bastian also revealed last year that, due to aging air traffic control systems, it actually takes longer today to fly from Atlanta to LaGuardia than it did in the 1950s when the airline opened that route.

“That’s the air traffic control system. It’s very slow. It’s congested,” he told TODAY in May 2025. “If you modernize the skies, you can kind of bring greater efficiency.”

By modernizing the skies, the airline chief means using satellite technology, the latest state-of-the-art equipment, and GPS. But currently, air traffic controllers have to use a radar point-and-shoot system from the 1960s.

“The screens look like something out of the 1960s and ‘70s,” Bastian said. “Our controllers and our operators need the best in order to keep us safe and make sure it maintains its safety record.”

Despite a barrage of emergency landings, machinery failure, a collision, and other air traffic control failures in the airline industry reported this year, he insists it’s still safe to fly.

“Our antiquated air traffic control system is affecting our workforce,” the Federal Aviation Administration said in a statement. “As Secretary Duffy has said, we must get the best safety technology in the hands of controllers as soon as possible.” Reuters reported in late March the FAA was short about 3,500 air traffic controllers.

On Friday, Duffy called on Congress to appropriate $50 billion for modernization. The House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee, though, estimates it could cost $12.5 billion. The Modern Skies Coalition, an umbrella group for aviation industry groups, estimated it would cost $31 billion or more.

But in the short term, the airline industry is making adjustments to improve flight delays and cancellations that result from air traffic control problems. Many airlines are pulling down their operations at Newark, Bastian said, which reduces some of the congestion at the airport.

“Now, that’s not good in the long run. It’s not good for consumers,” Bastian said. “It’s not good for the airport itself, but it’s the only thing that we have in the meantime.”

The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is an aircraft that still looks unusual, even today, decades after it first entered service. Its smooth, triangular shape looks more like a shadow than a conventional airplane, setting it apart from the familiar outlines of wings, tails, and vertical fins.

That distinctive appearance is the most visible sign that the B-2 was designed to operate in a very different way from earlier bombers. Understanding the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber means looking at the reasoning behind its design rather than just its specifications.

Nothing about the B-2 Spirit Bomber is accidental, from its overall form to the systems hidden beneath its surface. Instead of relying on speed, the aircraft combines efficiency, stealth, and long-range capability, demonstrating how deliberate design choices can fundamentally change how a strategic bomber is used.

At its core, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is a pure flying wing. There is no traditional ​​​​​​fuselage, no tail, and no vertical stabilizers, meaning that the entire aircraft is essentially one continuous lifting surface. Flying wing concepts had been explored for decades before the B-2 Spirit Bomber, including earlier Northrop designs.

However, none had ever reached operational service at this scale or level of sophistication. This configuration offers major aerodynamic benefits. With fewer protrusions and less structural clutter, drag is reduced, and lift efficiency improves. The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber can stay airborne for long periods while carrying heavy payloads, which is exactly what a strategic bomber needs to do.

However, the trade-off is stability. Flying wings are naturally less stable than conventional aircraft, but the B-2 Spirit Bomber solves this problem with advanced flight control computers, making thousands of adjustments every second to keep the aircraft flying smoothly and predictably.

The B-2 is famous for its stealth, but how much punch does it pack? Let’s find out.

Stealth is the defining characteristic of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber, and it starts with the aircraft’s shape. The smooth, continuous curves of the flying wing reflect radar energy away from the source rather than back toward it, while sharp angles and right-angle intersections, which are common radar reflectors, are carefully avoided wherever possible.

The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber uses radar-absorbent materials across much of its surface, turning incoming radar energy into heat rather than reflected signals. Even the engine intakes and exhausts are designed to reduce radar and infrared signatures.

Air enters through serpentine ducts that hide the engine faces, while exhaust is spread and cooled to make the aircraft harder to detect with heat-seeking sensors, resulting in an aircraft that can penetrate heavily defended airspace in ways that would be unthinkable for older bombers.

This brings us on to the key question: how fast can the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber fly? It is important to note that the B-2 Spirit Bomber is not a speed-focused aircraft, but it is far from slow. Its maximum speed is generally cited at around 628 mph (1,010 km/h), with a typical cruise speed closer to 560 mph (901 km/h).

This puts it just under the speed of sound, operating at high subsonic velocities that balance efficiency, long range, and survivability. These figures place the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber in an interesting position among bomber aircraft. It is slower than the supersonic Rockwell B-1B Lancer, which can exceed 900 mph (1,448 km/h).

However, it is broadly comparable to the venerable Boeing B-52, which has a top speed of around 650 mph (1,046 km/h). Compared to stealth-focused contemporaries like the Lockheed F-117 Nighthawk, the B-2 Spirit Bomber offers similar subsonic performance but with vastly greater range and payload. In practice, the B-2 Spirit Bomber relies on stealth and planning rather than raw speed to survive, making its top speed more than adequate for what the aircraft is designed to do.

One of the most impressive aspects of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is how far it can fly. With a published range of around 6,900 miles (11,104 km), the B-2 Spirit Bomber was designed from the start for intercontinental services, and when paired with aerial refueling, its endurance becomes extraordinary, allowing it to stay airborne for well over 40 hours.

This endurance has real operational consequences, and Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber crews have flown combat missions directly from the continental US to targets halfway around the world and back again. Long endurance reduces reliance on forward bases, which may be politically sensitive or vulnerable in a conflict, and it also allows planners to approach targets from unexpected directions, further enhancing the aircraft’s survivability and strategic flexibility.

Despite its sleek appearance, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber can carry a substantial weapons load, and internally, it can accommodate up to 40,000 lbs of ordnance, all carried inside weapon bays to preserve stealth. This includes a mix of conventional and nuclear weapons, making the B-2 Spirit Bomber a key component of strategic deterrence as well as conventional strike operations.

The emphasis is on precision rather than volume, and the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is capable of deploying GPS-guided bombs, allowing a small number of aircraft to strike multiple high-value targets with remarkable accuracy. This precision reduces the need for large formations and minimizes collateral damage.

The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is flown by a crew of just two (a pilot and a mission commander), which is a striking contrast to older bombers that required crews of four or more. Advanced avionics and automation make this possible, handling navigation, flight management, and many defensive functions that once demanded dedicated crew members.

Inside the cockpit, the emphasis is on situational awareness and workload management. Long missions mean crew endurance is just as important as aircraft performance, so the cockpit includes provisions for rest and careful ergonomics. The aircraft’s systems are designed to be highly reliable, but also flexible.

This allows crews to adapt to changing mission requirements while airborne. This means that flying the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is less about aggressive maneuvering and more about precision, patience, and trust in the systems that quietly keep the B-2 Spirit Bomber invisible.

The operational history of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is unusually straightforward, largely because only one country has ever flown it. Unlike many combat aircraft that are exported and adapted by multiple air forces, the B-2 Spirit Bomber has remained exclusively in the hands of the USAF since it entered service in the late 1990s.

That exclusivity reflects both its cost and its strategic importance. The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber first saw combat during the 1999 Kosovo conflict, when it flew missions directly from the US to strike targets in the former Yugoslavia. These demonstrated its ability to deliver precision-guided weapons over intercontinental distances while bypassing sophisticated air defenses.

A small number of B-2 Spirit Bombers were able to hit a significant portion of high-value targets early in the campaign, highlighting the efficiency of stealth-based operations. In the years that followed, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber was used by the US in Afghanistan and Iraq, again flying long-range missions supported by aerial refueling.

Its ability to carry large numbers of precision weapons made it particularly useful in the opening phases of these conflicts, when defended targets needed to be neutralized quickly. The B-2 Spirit Bomber also took part in strikes in Libya in 2011, where it was used to attack aircraft shelters.

While no other country has operated the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber, its influence has been global, and the aircraft has shaped how other nations approach long-range strike and stealth technology, even if none have fielded a direct equivalent. In practice, the B-2 Spirit Bomber has been used sparingly, but when it has been deployed, it has typically been at moments where its unique capabilities mattered most.

Rather than focusing on speed or payload alone, the B-2 Spirit Bomber was designed around survivability in heavily defended airspace, and that choice sets it apart from both earlier and contemporary bombers.

Against legacy platforms like the Boeing B-52, the contrast is especially sharp. The B-52 offers greater payload capacity and has proven remarkably adaptable over decades of service, but it relies on escort support to operate safely in modern threat environments. The B-2 Spirit Bomber, by comparison, is built to fly directly through advanced air defenses, using stealth rather than distance as its primary layer of protection.

While the two aircraft share similar subsonic performance, their operational philosophies are very different. The Rockwell B-1 Lancer provides another useful comparison: with its variable-sweep wings and supersonic capability, the B-1 emphasizes speed and flexibility. However, its radar signature is significantly larger than that of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber, limiting where it can safely operate without support.

In practice, the B-2 Spirit Bomber trades outright speed for the ability to reach targets that would be inaccessible to faster but more detectable aircraft. Compared to stealth-focused designs like the Lockheed F-117 Nighthawk, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber operates on a much larger scale.

While both rely on low observability, the B-2 Spirit Bomber offers intercontinental range and a vastly greater payload, allowing it to carry out strategic missions rather than precision strikes on a limited number of targets. In this sense, the B-2 Spirit Bomber occupies a unique space, blending stealth with global reach in a way no other operational bomber has fully matched.

Specialized’s second-fastest bike may be the smart buy.

I love a good Zone 2 ride. There’s nothing like getting on the bike, settling into a groove, and letting the miles (and hours) flow by. But sometimes when I hopped on the Specialized Tarmac SL9 S-Level, Zone 2 would turn into a tempo ride. It was like having a little imp—the stereotype dressed in red with the pitchfork—on my left shoulder whispering into my ear: “Go fast. This bike was built to go fast. Speed is good. You can do a Zone 2 ride tomorrow.” Normally, I’d count on the good imp—the dude with the harp and wings—on my right shoulder to restrain these urges. But the Tarmac is so seductive that he would just shrug and say “he’s got a point.” And in a moment of weakness, I would drop the hammer and head into the red, just because the SL9 makes that so rewarding.

The Tarmac SL9 is Specialized’s aero road bike, with the S-Level sitting just below the S-Works in Specialized’s top tier of carbon, aero road bikes, where it replaced the Tarmac SL8 Pro. After it got an update this year, I spent several weeks riding it, putting nearly 1,000 miles on it

Specialized offers the Tarmac SL9 in four models, all available with SRAM and Shimano drivetrains (Ars tested the SRAM version). Notably, you don’t need to buy the top-of-the-line bike to get the top-of-the-line groupset. Here’s the breakdown of the Tarmac SL9 lineup (note that the Shimano versions of the Expert is 20 g lighter and Comp is 70 grams heavier than the SRAM versions):

The $10,500 S-Level offers the top-of-the-line SRAM Red groupset and integrated cockpit, just like the $14,000 S-Works. But the frameset, wheelset, and saddle differ. The major difference is the frame: the S-Level gets the same carbon-fiber FACT 10r frame as the Expert and Comp. At 822 g for a 56 cm frame, the FACT 10r is 135 g heavier than the FACT 12r used on the S-Works. Despite the weight difference, Specialized says the FACT 10r offers the same stiffness and comfort characteristics as its lighter sibling. Along with the frame, different wheelsets and saddles make the S-Level 500 g heavier than the S-Works. With bottle cages, a Garmin mount, and my preferred Shimano XTR XC PD-M9200 MTB pedals, the S-Level I tested weighed in at 7.98 kg. By way of comparison, my own road bike—a Gen 8 Trek Madone—weighs in at 7.71 kg.

I rode the S-Level all around the Chicago suburbs as well as in central Illinois. Unlike the Diverge 4 Pro, I wasn’t able to test its climbing ability in the Rocky Mountains. All we’ve got in most of Illinois are short, punchy climbs … and a lot of long flats. I rode The S-Level on everything from freshly laid asphalt to freshly laid chipseal, including a couple of 60-mile-plus rides on the country roads downstate.

Visually, the S-Level is striking. The blue-on-white with gray striping is one of Specialized’s better-looking colorways. And it looks aerodynamic. Indeed, Specialized says the Tarmac SL9 is the most aerodynamic road bike it has ever made. Notable changes from the previous-generation Tarmac SL8 to SL9 involved tweaking the head tube, rerouting the rear hydraulic brake hose to accommodate the narrower head tube, and moving to a dropped-down tube for improved aerodynamics. But the most visually striking change is the Win Fin. As the seat tube rises from the bottom bracket, it deviates from the vertical to follow the curve of the wheel as it approaches the seat stays. When riding with just a single water bottle on the downtube—common in pro races where a full bottle is just a team car away—Specialized claims Win Fin saves riders half a watt.

The S-Level I tested came with Specialized’s 30 mm Specialized Cotton TLR tires—used by Red Bull-BORA-hansgrohe on the UCI World Tour—in a tubeless setup. According to Bicycle Rolling Resistance, the Cotton TLR outperforms the much-loved Continental GP 5000 S TR tires when it comes to rolling resistance (at 28 mm). Using Silca’s tire pressure calculator, I rode with the tires inflated to 73 psi in the front and 75 psi in the rear, a departure from the 62/64 setting on my 32 mm Contis—and there is a slight, but noticeable difference in ride comfort between the S-Level and my own road bike. Mounted on the Roval Rapide CL III carbon wheels with 21 mm internal width, the Cotton TLR tires measured 30.2 mm wide (my 32 mm Contis are a hair over 33 mm).

On every surface shy of brand-new chipseal, the Tarmac felt comfortable and balanced, with the bike absorbing some of the punishment meted out by uneven pavement. Fresh chipseal is another matter entirely, as it feels more like gravel, which felt jarring on the SL9 S-Level. What was particularly impressive about the SL9 is how great it feels on long rides. Some manufacturers are skilled at building aero bikes that feel great on hours-long rides, and the Tarmac is just as good as the Trek Madone SLR 9 AXS Gen 8 I reviewed in this regard.

When you’re ready to drop the hammer, the S-Level is your willing accomplice. It’s twitchy in all of the good ways, with instantaneous response to the rider’s effort. The SRAM Red AXS groupset makes shifting buttery smooth (and the bonus buttons on the brake hoods allow the rider to change screens on the bike computer without releasing the brake hoods), and the drivetrain (50t/37t chainrings and a 10t-36t cassette) offers gear ratios for every type of terrain.

The first few seconds of a climb on the Tarmac SL9 S-Level feel like they come for free. Whether I stayed seated or cranked out watts in an upright position, there was no lag or flex as the bike snapped forward. Descents were amazing, and cornering on the S-Level is sublime—I felt balanced and in control at all times.

There are a few nits to pick. The reach to the brakes in the drops felt excessive on my first couple of rides, and I had to adjust them slightly to brake easily on descents. Also, I would prefer shorter cranks on the Tarmac. The trend among professional riders has been toward shorter cranks because it makes holding an aero position easier and reduces hip and knee flexion. To its credit, Specialized reduced the crank length from 172.5 mm on the 56 cm SL8 to 170 mm on the same-sized SL9, but I run 165 mm cranks on my road bikes and noticed the difference on the S-Level. One demographic that buys expensive road bikes is middle-aged dudes with empty nests and disposable incomes, and they are the ones most likely to appreciate shorter cranks.

The 3D-printed Power Pro Mirror is a fine saddle, but there’s a reason I ride with a Specialized S-Works Power with Mirror on my Trek—it’s more comfortable and offers noticeably more padding around the cutout. And if I owned the S-Level, I’d move to 32 mm tires as soon as I wore out the 30s for better compliance.

Specialized brags that the Tarmac SL9 S-Works is the fastest bike ever made, and it claims the S-Level is the second-fastest bike ever made. I can’t speak to that claim, but the SL9 S-Level checks all of the boxes for an aerodynamic road bike. It’s lightweight, it’s fast, it’s responsive, and it’s great on rollers and punchy climbs. And it’s nice to get a SRAM Red groupset without springing for the absolute top-of-the-line model. $10,500 is a hefty sum for a road bike, but I’d be a lot more willing to forego the weight savings from the lighter components (frame, wheels, seatpost, bottom bracket, and bearings) of the S-Works—and spend $3,500 less. The real selling point, though, is how often it made me want to go harder just for the fun of it. That little imp has a point: Zone 2 can wait until tomorrow.

Posted November 12, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

Co-authored by Mark Shelvock and Joelle Adams

It’s an ordinary Monday; you’re buttering toast, answering emails, and negotiating with the dog about when it’s time to go for a walk. Underneath all of it, a question keeps surfacing in your psyche: What happened to me?

Maybe three weeks ago, on a psilocybin retreat, you dissolved into the universe and haven’t entirely come back. Perhaps during surgery, you watched the medical team from somewhere near the ceiling and had a near-death experience. When you finally tell someone, their reaction in those first few seconds may shape how you carry this for years.

If this sounds familiar, you’re in larger company than you might think. Meditation teacher Jack Kornfield named this predicament decades ago; after the ecstasy comes the laundry.1 The experience is over, and the work of figuring out what to do with it has just begun.

The good news is that people have been mapping this terrain for a long time.

Spiritual teachers have identified various pathways that experiencers take following psychedelic and other events such as near-death, out-of-body, spontaneous spiritual awakening, kundalini, alien or UFO, and similar exceptional experiences that alter consciousness and challenge concepts of reality.

Carl Jung, who described his own near-death experience after a heart attack in 1944, saw such moments as encounters with the deeper layers of the psyche.2 In his view, what surfaces in these states, including the shadow and archetypal images shared across cultures, needs to be gradually integrated into conscious life. He called this lifelong process individuation, which means becoming more fully and authentically oneself.

Rhea White and Suzanne Brown mapped five stages experiencers tend to move through following exceptional experiences.3 First, the initiating event itself, during which the experiencers’ ordinary worlds are disrupted by an experience that leaves them questioning the nature of reality. Then the search for reconciliation, during which experiencers try to make sense of what happened to them by seeking out others who have had similar experiences and looking for frameworks that explain the event. In the between two worlds stage, people begin bringing their new understanding of reality into daily life but often feel pulled in two directions: unable to fully embrace this new reality, yet unable to return to the beliefs they held before.

In the experiential paradigm, as the experiencer moves to the next stage, they begin to accept what happened during the initiating event and fully integrate their new perspectives into their daily lives. Finally, the experiencer finds a new way of being in the world, characterized by alignment to one’s purpose and a desire to be of service. Once a psycho-spiritually transformative experience has been woven into this new way of living, White and Brown call it an "exceptional human experience." Rather than a ladder to climb, these stages are more like landmarks on a winding path; people may begin anywhere, double back, or revisit a stage more than once.

Early findings from a recent preprint study point to more nuanced pathways people may take after a psychedelic or other exceptional experience. One of us (Joelle Adams), along with colleagues Pascal Michael and David Luke, found that experiencers tend to follow one of four pathways: challenging, what goes up, slow flow, or deep surrender.4

On the challenging pathway, people struggle to make sense of what happened and may experience depersonalization, derealization, depression and anxiety, sleep disturbances, and other symptoms that undermine their well-being. Those on the what goes up pathway report an initial "high" following their experience, a period of positive mood, energy, clarity, and alignment with the universe. This is followed by a longer "low" when they face psychological, physical, relational, and spiritual challenges. They may chase the initial high, seeking repeat experiences to recapture those early feelings. Eventually, with professional support and meaningful changes in their lives, they can find a new equilibrium, much like White and Brown’s new way of being in the world stage.

Adams, Michael, and Luke also found that some experiencers follow an iterative pathway: the slow flow. Following the initial experience, they take their time to process what happened and integrate new perspectives and insights into their lives, coming to a place of equilibrium. Only then do they seek out or initiate new events or altered states of consciousness. Finally, those on the deep surrender pathway report integrating their experiences with relative ease, accepting and surrendering to new insights and concepts of reality. They often change their life circumstances, such as jobs and relationships, quite quickly, and report few challenges in returning to homeostasis.

For those who find an experience difficult to integrate, the company of others who have walked this path can be invaluable. Sharing the experience with a trusted friend, or with a therapist who is open to non-ordinary states and approaches them without pathologizing, can provide the steadiness needed to make meaning of a reality-altering event. Reading, podcasts, and workshops can offer both frameworks for understanding and the quiet reassurance that others have travelled similar terrain, though some people find this material overwhelming and benefit from taking it in gradually. Solitude, time in nature, journaling, and creative expression are also frequently described as essential companions to integration.

Discernment matters too. Confiding in someone who dismisses or misunderstands the experience can deepen the isolation it so often brings. Likewise, rushing back into the demands of daily life without pausing to reflect can leave the experience unprocessed and, at times, psychologically destabilizing. Chasing a return to the original high, or clinging rigidly to any single framework for explaining what happened, can also keep people stuck. Meditation and other spiritual practices can sometimes aid integration, though for some they may reactivate difficult emotional states; it’s often wise to proceed gradually and with compassionate support.

In the end, integration doesn't fully happen on the idealized spiritual mountaintop. More commonly, people learn to let their insights shape how they live, work, and love, becoming, in Jung’s sense, more fully themselves. With support, time, patience, and conscious intentionality, these experiences can lead to greater well-being, deeper empathy, and a quieter fear of death. The toast still needs buttering and the dog still needs walking, but the difference is who is doing it.

Photography by Kaia Wang, Yumeng Zhu, Tian Fangfang, Chen Hao

The Snøhetta-designed Shanghai Grand Opera House has introduced a paid, ticketing system for visitors wanting to access the signature spiral staircase and rooftop viewing platform at the recently opened building in Shanghai.

When Dezeen visited the site, we observed a temporary barricade that had been set up to separate the free-to-access, ground-level public from the spiral staircase leading up to the roof. Access to the staircase and roof now costs 50 yuan (£5.60) per person.

Since opening, access to the architectural feature has been restricted with visitors now required to pre-book tickets online, with the staircase open between 10:30am to 2:30pm.  According to the booking site, people with tickets to see a performance inside the opera house are still required to purchase a ticket and the fee includes accident insurance.

Asked about the apparent change in access requirements, Snøhetta said that demand meant that “visitor management is necessary” and that “access arrangements will continue to develop”.

“As with any major cultural institution, operational procedures continue to evolve during the opening period as the Opera House team gains experience with visitor patterns, capacity needs, and the building’s day-to-day use,” the studio told Dezeen.

“Given the exceptional public interest and the limited capacity of the roof and public stairs, visitor management is necessary to ensure a safe and enjoyable experience for everyone,” it continued.

“Access arrangements will continue to develop with operational experience, but the intention remains the same: to create a building that can be widely enjoyed by the public while balancing accessibility, safety, and the visitor experience.”

Following news that a fee had been introduced, the phrase “is it worth spending 50 yuan to visit Shanghai Grand Opera House” trended on Chinese social media.

Some commenters questioned whether charing to visit the attraction was justifiable, while others sympathised with the need for crowd control. Others shared tips on how to take the best photos of the building without paying the entrance fee.

Visitors Dezeen spoke to at the site were concerned about both the fee and the temporary barriers.

“I don’t think it’s worth the money,” Mr Qiu, who paid to climb the stairs, told Dezeen. “Basically you pay to climb a giant stairs. If you want good photos of the stairs, best to take from afar, which you can do for free,”

Ms Ye, who didn’t pay the entry fee, told Dezeen that she was really disappointed that the barricade was in her photos.

“I don’t know what the point of the barricade is – to separate the paid and unpaid? Or for better crowd circulation? But at least they need to put some effort in designing it to match the beauty of the building,” she said.

“It really is an eye-sore for the photos.”

The building, and its accessible roof, are often compared to Oslo Opera House, which was also designed by Snøhetta and is topped with a walkable roof. According to the studio, both projects were designed to “reduce thresholds wherever possible”.

“Buildings like opera houses inevitably create thresholds through walls, doors, and ticketed programs,” said Snøhetta co-founder Kjetil Thorsen at the time of the Shanghai Grand Opera House opening.

“We see it as our responsibility to challenge those thresholds and make the building as much a civic destination as a performance venue. People should be able to enjoy the building without attending a performance. It should become part of everyday life, a place to spend time, meet others, and experience the city,” he continued.

“A successful landmark is not only recognised by visitors, it is also embraced by the people who live around it and make it part of their everyday lives.”

Several other cultural projects in China have been built with publicly accessible roofs. However, the accessibility presented in concepts has often been restricted when the buildings open.

Also in Shanghai, the Start Museum, designed by French architect Jean Nouvel and opened in 2022, features a public roof that can be used for displaying large-scale art work. However, the space has rarely been used.

The Amateur Architecture Studio-designed Fuchun Cultural Museum Complex is defined by a continuous undulating roof to resemble the experience of climbing the local Fuchun Mountain. However, the roof didn’t open to the public for four years and is now only open to public visits for one hour a day, three days a week.

For seventy-six years its annual exhibition took place in Riverhead, where growers competed for premiums over pumpkins, potatoes, cauliflower, cattle, and poultry. Movement abilities in Metroidvanias are a fundamental part of their appeal, determining both the pace of exploration and our ability to interact with the surrounding world.

The genre is built on understanding spaces, their composition, and how to traverse them, making movement a crucial variable regardless of the game's perspective, mechanics, or philosophy.

As a result, the possibilities a game enables for movement along the Cartesian plane largely determine how satisfying, tense, or rewarding a campaign will be, depending on its intended purpose.

Therefore, in order to pay homage to those video games that make the best possible use of the philosophy of moving from one place to another, here are the ten most creative traversal abilities in Metroidvanias.

One of the main problems games in this genre face when creating spectacular movement abilities lies in their limitations, as they have to be balanced so that they aren't too powerful.

For this reason, Silk Soar from Hollow Knight: Silksong is a masterpiece of design. Among all the powers in Metroidvanias that let you fly upwards, it's part of the few that properly contextualize the limits of its capabilities.

Not only do you acquire it in the game's last third, unexpectedly opening paths out of nowhere, but its use is also tied to your needle, requiring the presence of a ceiling to be used, so you can't just break the map's traversal in its entirety.

In terms of creativity, it's on the spectrum's simpler side, yet it solves a major verticality imbalance by granting you a great deal of ease in exploration without making you overpowered, which is a tremendous feat.

Going in the opposite direction from the previous entry, Pipistrello and the Cursed Yoyo is a constant display of reinterpreting Metroidvanias' most common forms of movement, with Walk the Dog being the crown jewel.

The game plays with yo-yo tricks to establish its mechanics, adapting them with thematic coherence to make you feel that, more than just having powers within a video game, you're learning new maneuvers to show off.

In this particular instance, it's as simple as being able to move orthogonally across the map at a greater speed, but this is maximized thanks to the bounce pads distributed throughout all areas that change your direction and make the ability the key to numerous puzzles.

Pipistrello and the Cursed Yoyo is brimming with ideas magnificently adapted to its setting, making it by far one of the most inventive Metroidvanias to come out this decade.

Well Dweller breaks one of the genre's cardinal rules by not including a double jump at any point in its story, though it compensates with a Slingshot mechanic that is simply mesmerizing.

The title allows you to throw your weapon and embed it in any surface to use it as a springboard and jump higher than usual, encouraging inquisitive and creative exploration in a truly compelling way.

Most of the game's movement is based on the biomes you find yourself in, whether in the form of bubbles, air currents, or elevators, and it works precisely because this direct yet versatile ability adapts to any situation.

Of course, it leaves you without any means of defense, but, like Kratos's Leviathan Axe, Well Dweller's matchstick flies back to you when you need it, which also leads to spectacular and almost cinematic moments when transitioning from platforming to combat.

In terms of inventiveness, Animal Well is a couple of notches above the rest of the genre, with all its progression tools being as unique as they're rewarding.

Since the title is more puzzle-based than platforming-focused, most of its abilities are proportional to said distribution, but the Disc is a versatile component that you can use both to solve environmental puzzles and to traverse in seemingly impossible ways.

Throwing the frisbee and timing its trajectory to hop on and soar through the air never gets old, both because of how mechanically satisfying it is and because of all the paths mastering this skill opens up.

Animal Well is a sandbox where most of its castles are built through iteration and experimentation, but fortunately, you have the Disc on your side to make the journey faster, smoother, and more enjoyable.

Inside the First Competition Between Historic, Vintage Timepieces

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Want more insider watch coverage? Get Box + Papers, GQ's newsletter devoted to the watch world, sent to your inbox every Friday. Sign up here to get it free.

In the automotive world, high-end cars have gone head-to-head since the early 1920s. Judges didn’t evaluate them based on their speed, though—in a Concours d’Élégance, the prize goes to the best-looking vehicles. The tradition continues today in picturesque sites around the world where collectors submit their often very high-end vintage, classic cars. Surprisingly, there has never been an event for vintage timepieces.

Aurel Bacs, the famous auctioneer whose longstanding association with Phillips has shaped the watch collecting world, endeavored to change that. “It began with a meeting where I said, ‘I have a dream,’” he said in a press release. For the very first iteration of this competition, Bacs and Phillips partnered with Swiss heavyweight Vacheron Constantin. They expected to receive maybe 100 to 200 submissions, but the jury was forced to whittle down 550 of them to 34 finalist watches, whose owners were flown out from across the world for the Concours held at the Hôtel Président in Geneva.

“It was the most beautiful evening in the field ever,” Bacs said. At the Hôtel Président, around 200 attendees gathered, along with a jury of 14 watch-industry experts including Bacs himself, Ben Clymer of Hodinkee, writer and historian Nick Foulkes, longtime watch journalist Roberta Naas, and others. Watches could only be submitted for consideration in one category (Chronographs, Métiers d’Art, Chronomètre Royal, Astronomical Complications, Multiple Complications, Design, and Chiming Mechanisms) and were judged based on nine criteria: authenticity, elegance, rarity, impact on the history of Vacheron Constantin and/or on watchmaking in general, provenance, technicality, métiers d’art (artistic crafts), state of preservation, and emotional dimension.

The jurors deliberated over several hours until the winners of the seven categories and a Best of Show were finally selected. Following a cocktail reception, everyone gathered on the lower floor of the Hôtel Président to see the results from the watch world’s first Concours d'Élégance.

The first category featured a smattering of some of the maison’s most important wristwatches, including a highly rare ref. 4072 in stainless steel from 1940. However, it was a yellow gold pocket-watch chronograph from 1929 that won the day. The result was such a surprise that the audience, intimately familiar with the historical importance of pocket watches, audibly gasped inside the room. It made me wonder if these old-fashioned timepieces could regain their lost position as the pinnacle of watch collecting. This piece certainly warranted the love it received: in excellent condition and featuring a white enamel dial with dual registers in an “up-down” configuration, it’s a beautiful timepiece.

This category celebrates handcrafts such as enameling and gem-setting, and it featured a smattering of truly spectacular pieces—mostly sub-40mm wristwatches in precious metals with wild cloisonné or Grand Feu champlevé enamel dials depicting landmasses or animals.

However, a stunning pocket watch set with blue sapphires throughout the bezel and Roman indices won out.

When an older American gentleman stepped on stage to collect his prize, he explained to a stunned crowd that not only had his great-grandfather, Dr. Otto von Frangeš, been given the watch by His Majesty Alexander I of Yugoslavia, its original owner, but that he wore it while sitting in the motorcade during the king’s assassination in France in 1934. Otto’s middle son, Nikola, eventually smuggled the watch out of Yugoslavia by sewing it into the lining of his wife’s jacket as they fled the Iron Curtain in the late 1940s.

In 1907, Vacheron launched the Chronomètre Royal sub-brand, specifically built to endure a life sailing the seas. The model’s parts were anti-magnetic and resisted rusting. The dials were made out of enamel, which could also stand up to the moist atmosphere without rusting. Because they were critical to maritime navigation, timing precision was of the utmost importance, and models from this line frequently won observatory trials (which tested for a watch’s accuracy) that ran from the 1800s to the mid-1900s. Given all that information, the winner is a bit of a shocker: a ref. 2215 from 1976 that exemplifies the qualities of the line but modernizes them in many ways. It doesn’t even feature a full set of numerals, trading them out for a few diamond-set indices. In his acceptance speech, the owner said he wears it on the weekends with jeans.

The Astronomical Complications category is dear to the heart of anyone who appreciates a good moonphase display and also includes timepieces equipped with a triple-calendar, a complete calendar, or a perpetual calendar. Though more modern pieces, such as a skeletonized QP wristwatch from 1987, made it to the final round, it was a wildly neat (and unique) platinum-cased “Cioccolatone” from 1957 that won out. Housed in a 34mm x 43mm rectangular case and equipped with a complete calendar, it’s owned by a dapper Italian collector, who beamed as he collected the prize with his grown son. This would not be this pair’s first time at the podium…

This is a loaded category for Vacheron, which famously makes the most complicated pocket watch and wristwatch in the world, with 57 and 41 complications, respectively. To be eligible in this category, watches were required to have at least two complications—child’s play for VC.

Spanning everything from an enamel-dialed, minute-repeating, split-seconds chronograph pocket watch from 1901 to several Patrimony watches from the 1990s, the winner was the “Don Pancho.” Delivered in 1940, this diminutive yellow-gold timepiece with minute repeater, day, and retrograde date indications was once the world’s most complicated wristwatch. Double-signed by Brooking in Madrid, it sold for roughly $740,000 at auction back in 2019.

A more subjective category, perhaps, than the others, Design included watches “featuring Vacheron Constantin’s signature design, either in the case or through the time display.” One entrant, for example—a wild time-only ref. 4695 dress watch—featured incredibly intricate lugs and a Maltese Cross-type dial. But the winner was nothing short of revelatory. Once again owned by the aforementioned tall Italian gentleman, the possibly unique ref. 6213 is an automatic world timer from 1957 in yellow gold. Based upon Louis Cottier’s famous system, it is so rare that even Clymer admitted he had never heard of it before the competition. The owner’s son described his father returning home with it one day when he was a boy, excited beyond belief that he had finally tracked it down after years of searching.

This category included pocket watches or wristwatches fitted with a minute repeater, quarter repeater, half-quarter repeater, or grand sonnerie—a mechanism that automatically strikes the hours and quarter-hours every 15 minutes. Incredibly, among the entrants was a red gold quarter-repeater pocket watch from the early 1820s with a stunning guilloché dial. However, the winner was another pocket watch dating to 1926 and cased in platinum. Its ultra-thin design, black Breguet numerals, Breguet blued steel handset, and simple silver dial belied the complex mechanism within. The visibly emotional American gentleman who owns it recalled how, as a boy, he would open a chest of drawers, remove his grandfather’s pocket watch, and listen to its minute repeater.

A bonus category, Best of Show went to a truly spectacular wristwatch: a ref. 6448 in platinum with diamond-set indices. Seemingly a simple time-only dress watch, this tremendous piece—which was produced in just three examples in the early 1960s—boasts a hand-wound, 13-ligne minute-repeater movement. Housed in a brushed-finish case with incredible teardrop lugs, it’s the only known example in this metal, and it was notably the last minute-repeating reference produced by the brand for nearly 30 years.

Nominally, the crowd at the awards ceremony was there to judge the merits of a watch, but I left emotionally moved by the stories each watch owner shared about their timepieces. As the very first concours for watches, the format will no doubt be repeated in the future—either by Vacheron, adopted by other brands, or done as a more traditional multi-brand event like the Concours d’Élégance at Pebble Beach.

Of course, the Holy Spirit is often compared to fire in both the Old and New Testaments, notably during Pentecost, when "tongues of fire" appeared on Jesus Christ's disciples when baptized by the Spirit in Acts 2:3-4, while Hebrews 12:29 describes God metaphorically as a "consuming fire." Just as Gandalf considers himself a "servant" of the Flame Imperishable, so too do the Faithful in Númenor. As The Rings of Power Season 3 continues to expand to the far reaches of Tolkien's world, the advent of the Secret Fire honors the author who first brought Middle-earth to life.

It's a tad unfortunate that it took a second go for it to dawn on me, but don't they say that love is sweeter the second time around?

You're responsible for your rose planet The first and likely most obvious difference between this and the aforementioned Nintendo property is the fact that the entire world is, well, a world - albeit on a (seemingly) much smaller scale. You're a Planet Tender, responsible for tending to your very own mini-planet across a charming, spherical space. I feel like this ties perfectly well with the more emotional aspect of your smaller home - giving it all kinds of TLC never feels like a burden, because you're never pressured to compete or perform, never hounded to optimise numbers, never pushed or overwhelmed by the to-do list that's practically breathing down your neck. It's a gentler, kinder, and more considerate experience, which brings me to my next point. Interview: Petit Planet dev team talks life vs management, and the ultimate introvert's dream

It was initially made as a flat space, but it wasn't in line with the team's core design.

"As a life simulation game, we decided to use some rendering techniques to present a feeling of that sphere. That indeed brought a lot of challenges technically and visually." One of those challenges was actually how to make it so that players don't feel any kind of motion sickness across this spherical world.

And it's certainly a very different style when compared to other HoYoverse games. "It's very important for HoYoverse to explore different genres, and with a life simulation game, it's the kind of genre where we can build an emotional connection with players and the community. And from HoYoverse's past experiences, we've accumulated a lot of knowledge in terms of technology and the ability to build out narratives and characters. We believe that those capabilities developed from our past games can bring brand-new experiences for players of this genre."

"We always try to provide high-quality games to our players, and that is also what Petit Planet wants to do. And in Petit Planet, we've also tried to explore something new - the multiplayer gaming experience and also a social system - and that is something unique to this game."

Still, from what I've played, it did feel like the single-player aspect is robust enough as it is, which is interesting for a life sim like this. "For us, it's very important to provide a good gaming experience for both types of players. For those who prefer to play solo, there are always settings that will allow them to turn off those multiplayer experiences, but once they feel comfortable or they want to explore the social part of the game, they can always go to the Galactic Bazaar and also use the Epistolarian's Desk. But the core gameplay will be largely the same for both types of players," they explained.

"We have adopted certain technical measures to make sure the multiplayer experience will go as smoothly as possible. For example, when a player gets to the Galactic Bazaar, the multiplayer settings will adjust dynamically based on the number of players. There's just something so heartbreaking and tragic - yet still so inherently beautiful about it.

I told the team that the system felt very considerate, as you're always asking for permission if people are allowed to reply or if you're going to accept their reply. I just wanted to know the inspiration behind the idea, because I don't think I've seen that anywhere else before.

"It's a kind of asynchronous design on purpose, because that kind of system won't bring our players, especially introverts, too much pressure. Once you become friends online in a game, you will need to invest a lot of energy and time to sustain the friendship, so we want to avoid that in Petit Planet."

"We'd like to use the fishing system as an example. So, in some other games, you might develop skills and levels for your fishing skills. So there's no growth curve; we want to make it simulate real life, so the fish you get will vary depending on the season or the weather. Our purpose is to provide a little surprise for players every time they log in to the game, so that they will have the incentive to log in and enjoy the experience."

The narrative goes hand in hand here as well. "Unlike other HoYoverse games, there's no such thing as a 'main plot'. The whole narrative will be more fragmented - for example, if you look at the descriptions for the items, there will be a lot of content or stories behind them. I know that developers said they designed some of the controls to be "less automatic" - for lack of a better term - to force players to pay closer attention to what they're doing, which is something I also observed while playing.

For instance, I noticed that you really can't simply jump across ledges, as you really need to go around to find the door or the right pathway should you need to. So I asked them how they're able to balance those "less automatic" controls with making sure things aren't frustrating for players when the controls start to get wonkier than usual.

"In terms of controls and cameras, we did a lot of research and considered different solutions, from the most limited ones to allowing players to freely explore the landscape and environment. We thought of different degrees of freedom for players while also keeping the imperfections for the life simulation. The team debated different solutions for, say, climbing. Initially, internally, we discussed how many layers a player can climb, but there will be different creatures on the lower level and higher level, so if players can jump multiple layers at once, that will remove the joy of exploring and the sense of surprise. So there are deliberate considerations behind the control of players in Petit Planet."

With that, we wrapped things up - and I do have to say that so far, Petit Planet is shaping up to be a dream come true. I've never really connected with life sims and management sims before, as I've always found the fetch quests and social aspects a tad too grindy for me. Thankfully, Petit Planet seems to be offering a gentler way for us all to enjoy a virtual world without the demands that usually come with it, and with its launch on December 4th, we'll get to see if that is indeed the case.

For now, if you're curious about how it plays, I did a quick preview of Petit Planet when it was in its beta phase earlier this year, and I'll have a proper review ready in time for its release soon too. I was pretty sure I could identify the 10,000-foot contour.  In the Sierras, open fires are not allowed above 10,000 feet.  For this reason, it seemed every time we crossed that elevation there would be a sign flagging the change in fire status.  Most hikers would thereby know whether they were above or below 10,000 feet.

As I ascended slowly, I eventually reached the turnoff to Bishop Pass, an indicator that I had gone ten miles already.  I was feeling pretty strong.   Without resting, I drank some water to prevent dehydration and pressed on.  It was a boost of confidence to reach the point I intended to stop, and to realize I still had energy for several miles more.

At a bridge over the stream I looked down and saw three little fishes swimming in an eddy of the stream.  I began to sing “Three little fishies in an itty bitty pool…”  You know that one?  First recorded in 1939, so maybe not.  At that moment a hiking couple arrived and no doubt thought I was completely insane singing to the fishes!

Two of the three little fishies… the third was camera-shy.  I am fairly confident they are Golden Trout!

I continued climbing and climbing through woods that changed as I went higher up.  For a while a number of thorny bushes lined the trail.  In my shorts this was uncomfortable; though they didn’t actually break the skin, they left many surface scratches.

I stopped to fill up my water bottle again at an ideal camp site.  While tempted to stay there, I decided it was still too early to pitch my tent.  Just as I was leaving, a guy came and asked if I’d take his picture on his phone.  I looked across the river and saw a stunning mountain.  “How cool,”  I thought, “I’ll crouch really low, aiming upward, and it will make a great picture with this giant crag looming behind him!”

That was not his intention at all.  It turned out I had not realized it, but I stopped at an iconic trail location.   The man moved toward a giant boulder, and then I saw it.  It was a face!  A face naturally created  by the geology of the place, but with a huge gaping ogre’s mouth.  In that yawning maw, people had placed a row of small triangular stones to represent teeth.  They added a couple on top of his head as silly little ears.

The man entered the “mouth” and lay down between the “teeth.”  He said his girlfriend back in France had taken her picture there several years before, so now I took his picture to send back to her.   Of course, having seen “The Thing” I had to ask him to reciprocate with a picture of me!  How funny that I almost left the site without even noticing it…

The forest was reduced to occasional scraggly trees as I hiked higher, passing more waterfalls as the slope became steeper.

Waterfall catching the sunlight, with the wildflowers in front? Lovely!

Shortly after passing the symbolic 10,000-foot sign (“No Fires!”) I came to a lush meadow.  It looked like a perfect camping area, and I found what seemed like an ideal spot off the trail.

I dropped my pack and turned off my AllTrails app and prepared to settle in.  Then I realized the meadow was flat and green and there were no logs or rocks for me to sit on.  I thought, “Let me wander around here a little nearby.  I’m sure I can find a nice big stone to carry over by my tent to sit on.”  Indeed, I found just such a stone.  But when I picked it up, I found under the stone was… toilet paper!  Ugh!

I put it back down in the same spot quickly and reassessed my decision.  Suddenly that place felt like bad karma, so I turned AllTrails back on again, shouldered my pack, and continued looking for a place to spend the night.

It was late afternoon already, and I worried that the camp sites higher up might all be full.  I decided to poll the descending southbound hikers passing me to find out what I might expect.  My question was simple and uniform:  “How many people did you encounter higher up who had already made camp there?”

The first person who came by answered, “One” and I was relieved.  But the second person who came along answered, “Six.”  I grew concerned.  Finally, the third downhill hikers were two women; they looked like mother and daughter.  To my question, the mother answered, “Fifteen, I think.”   Fifteen!  Then, to my relief, the daughter chimed in, “Oh no, I don’t think so, maybe five?”

This was a quasi-scientific experiment proving how totally unreliable we humans are at reporting anything.  Yet it left me with the same dilemma.  Then the daughter continued, “there’s a pretty site just up ahead and there still seemed to be room there.”

I looked on the AllTrails app, and it did show a spot nearby with four tent sites.   I figured, “Well, even if there are a couple of people there, I don’t mind sharing  and it will give me a little extra elevation and a little extra distance that I won’t have to take on tomorrow.”

Upward I trudged.  Soon, there was the spot with three tents already pitched, but space for a fourth about twenty yards apart from them.  The owners of the tents seemed to be friends, or maybe a family, as they had camp chairs and were all playing cards together.

Late afternoon arrival, with the sky still a deep blue!

I took my spot and pitched my tent, feeling very tired.  As I took off my trail runners and socks, I realized my feet were well beyond filthy.  I wanted a bed and a shower; not necessarily right away, but soon.

That was when I broke down and texted my wife on my Garmin.  It was something like:  “If you can get me a cabin or a room at the John Muir Ranch for day-after-tomorrow, I will be very grateful.  It is probably all booked up, but just in case, please give it a try.”

It was deep twilight by the time I soaked my dinner and dug my cathole.  I wolfed down my repast and called it a night.  It felt like it might prove to be a cold one.
