Blackburn Rovers vs West Ham Preview & Lineups – Five Changes For Parker?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Blackburn Rovers v West Ham United EFL Championship Ewood Park Saturday 7 November 2026, 3pm GMT

Scott Parker had less than 24 hours to prepare West Ham United for Wednesday night’s extraordinary 3-3 draw at Cardiff City, but the second-half transformation should heavily influence the team he selects against Blackburn Rovers.

The Hammers were unrecognisable after the interval, recovering from three goals down through Divine Mukasa, Arne Engels and Max Kilman. Parker, Mark Noble and Jarrod Bowen clearly delivered a rousing half-time message, while the tactical and personnel changes completely altered the match.

West Ham remain eighth and four points behind second-placed Wolverhampton Wanderers, making victory at Ewood Park extremely important. Parker will have the international break to properly implement his methods, but first he must use the lessons from Cardiff to select a side capable of taking three points into it.

I have already covered the Cardiff match extensively, so there is little value in going over every detail again. What matters for this preview is the enormous contrast between West Ham’s two performances.

I very much detach the three goals conceded in the first half from what happened after the break. The opening 45 minutes looked like a continuation of everything we had witnessed under Nuno Espírito Santo, while the second offered the first glimpse of Parker’s influence.

Parker introduced Ezra Mayers, Mohamadou Kanté and Joël Piroe at half-time before later replacing Manor Solomon with Edvin Austbø. Those decisions gave West Ham greater energy, aggression and bite.. in short, the changes worked an absolute treat.

The substitutions worked, but so did the tactical changes. West Ham pressed higher, attacked space behind Cardiff’s defence and played with an urgency which had been completely absent before the break. As I discussed in my reaction to Parker’s first post-match interview, the new manager was particularly enthusiastic about the more aggressive, front-footed approach.

That should influence Parker’s selection at Blackburn far more than anything he can achieve during two brief training sessions.

Engels & Piroe can both expect to start against Blackburn

I think it is time to give Mads Hermansen a rest and select Alphonse Areola in goal. Hermansen has endured a difficult period, conceding nine times across the past three matches, and temporarily removing him from the firing line may benefit everybody.

Parker already knows Areola extremely well from their season together at Fulham. The goalkeeper was brought to Craven Cottage during Parker’s time as manager and performed impressively despite their eventual relegation.

That existing relationship could make this an obvious early change. Parker knows Areola’s strengths and temperament, while the goalkeeper will understand what the new head coach expects from him.

Replacing Kyle Walker-Peters is straightforward if he remains unavailable. Joël Veltman was sensational during the second half at Cardiff and should continue at right-back.

Veltman provided leadership, composure and attacking support for Bowen. At a time when West Ham’s defence has looked incredibly nervous, his experience is particularly valuable.

Mavropanos returned from his knee problem on Wednesday and should remain alongside Kilman. The partnership still needs work, but Parker has very little time to experiment before the international break.

The significant change should come at left-back. Ezra Mayers impressed again after replacing Morato at half-time and deserves an opportunity from the beginning.

Mayers offered more natural movement down the flank and was willing to attack the penalty area. He almost completed the comeback with a late volley and looked considerably more comfortable in the position than a centre-back being asked to operate as a full-back.

I do not see how Tomáš Souček can force his way into the starting XI. He nearly scored the winner at Cardiff with a late header, but both Engels and Mukasa deserve to retain their places.

Engels improved enormously after half-time and took his goal like a striker, timing his run perfectly before producing a confident finish. Mukasa also deserves to continue in his preferred attacking-midfield role after scoring West Ham’s first and helping change the momentum of the game.

Kanté has absolutely forced himself into the team. His energy, ball-carrying and aggression transformed the midfield, giving West Ham the ability to press Cardiff and move possession forward quickly.

The most sensible balance is therefore Engels and Kanté as the two deeper midfielders, with Mukasa ahead of them. Souček remains an excellent option from the bench, particularly when West Ham need a goal or greater aerial presence late in a game.

Joël Piroe must start in attack. His clever flick created Engels’ goal, but his overall movement and willingness to drop deeper also helped connect West Ham’s midfield with the forward line.

Piroe is the only recognised striker in the squad with a proven Championship goalscoring record, yet he has not received a consistent run of starts. Parker should now give him the opportunity to establish himself.

Austbø also deserves to replace Solomon on the left. The Norwegian was direct and positive after coming on, forcing a late save and immediately looking more dangerous than the player he replaced.

Bowen will naturally continue on the right, although Parker should allow him to drift into central areas. The captain ran the second half at Cardiff, creating all three West Ham goals and dragging the team back into a match which had appeared completely lost.

I expect Parker to retain the 4-2-3-1 formation but reward the players who changed the game at Cardiff. Areola, Mayers, Kanté, Austbø and Piroe should all come into the starting XI.

Blackburn have lost consecutive matches against Swansea City and Stoke City, but their victory over current leaders Middlesbrough demonstrates that West Ham cannot take them lightly.

Tony Mowbray’s team have won five Championship matches this season and will be determined to enter the international break with a response of their own. Ewood Park has not been a regular destination for the Hammers, with this our first league visit since the 2010/11 Premier League season.

Nevertheless, West Ham should travel north with genuine confidence following the Cardiff comeback. Parker now has evidence of which players responded to his instructions and which combinations gave the team greater balance.

This is his final match before receiving two uninterrupted weeks on the training pitch. A victory would reduce the gap to the automatic-promotion positions and give him valuable momentum before beginning the more substantial work of reshaping the team.

Blackburn’s recent defeats suggest this is an excellent opportunity for Parker to claim his first victory as West Ham head coach. The Hammers must still defend considerably better, but the second-half attacking performance at Cardiff showed what this squad can produce.

If Parker selects the players who drove that comeback and retains the more aggressive approach, I believe West Ham will have too much quality for Blackburn. There may still be another defensive mistake in this team, but I expect the Hammers to win convincingly enough.

Prediction: Blackburn Rovers 1-3 West Ham United

Audio Chateau Records, a music and creator company founded by Jingle Punks’ Jared Gutstadt, secured up to $9 million in Series A financing to support its next phase of growth. According to a press release, Audio Chateau merges “the creative resources of a record label with the commercial capabilities of an agency and content studio,” recognizing “that artists are multifaceted creators and aims to help established and emerging talent grow their careers, brands and businesses.”

Investors in the funding round include Jonathan Schulman, Ben Lurie and Julie Gauthier of CW Ventures.

“I’ve seen the shift to creator culture firsthand, and I believe the next generation of successful artists will be the ones who build something bigger than their music,” said Gutstadt in a statement. “That’s what we’re building at Audio Chateau. We want to give artists the creative resources, relationships and opportunities to turn their talent into lasting businesses. This investment allows us to bring more talent into that model, strengthen what we’re building and create new avenues for how artists are discovered and developed.”

Oak View Group (OVG) partnered with Independent Artist Group (IAG) in a deal that will provide sponsorship and brand partnership opportunities to IAG’s roster. Through the agreement, touring and music assets from IAG artists will be made available to OVG brand partners through OVG’s network of sponsorship sellers in North America. The deal will encompass title sponsorships, artist presales, artist and brand engagements, fan activations, meet-and-greets and more.

Adobe announced it’s working with Universal Music Group (UMG) and Warner Music Group (WMG) to bring fully-licensed music clips directly to Adobe Premiere mobile. The deals will soon allow Adobe Premiere users to integrate up to 60-second music clips from UMG and WMG into their videos. The licenses will cover personal, non-commercial use only; users working on projects requiring commercially licensed music can continue to use Adobe Stock Audio. — Chris Eggertsen

U.K. indie ticketing platform Skiddle received its first external investment since its launch 25 years ago, with Soho Square Capital investing to “accelerate the company’s existing five-year growth plans,” according to a press release. Co-founders Ben Suborn and Richard Dyer will retain control of the business, with Soho Square taking a minority equity stake. Soho Square’s Joe Tebbutt will join Skiddle’s board.

The funding will help allow Skiddle to expand beyond music, festivals and nightlife; the company is looking to focus more on lifestyle events while also making additional investments in tech, marketing and business development. The Soho Capital investment will additionally allow Skiddle to expand into new European territories.

The deal with Soho Square is being touted as a greater partnership that will give Skiddle “access to additional strategic experience and a network of businesses that have previously navigated European expansion, supporting the company as it pursues its international plans with greater pace and scale,” according to the release.

Launched in 2001 by Suborn and Dyer, Skiddle generated more than 178 million pounds ($235 million) in face value ticket sales across 180,000 events in 2025, with revenue growing an average of more than 20% a year over the past two years, according to the company. — Chris Eggertsen

Music metadata infrastructure company Music Story signed a multi-year partnership with Sonos that will see the latter drawing on Music Story’s consistently updated music metadata — allowing Sonos devices to “better understand music: stronger discoverability, easy connection to external platforms, and fewer incorrect tracks played through the speakers,” according to a press release. — Chris Eggertsen

Sonilo, described in a press release as a “generative audio company building video-native music and sound models,” partnered with AI music attribution and rights management company Musical AI. According to the release, the companies “aim to demonstrate a model for generative music where licensing, attribution, and compensation are built into the process from the start.”

Sonilo “generates music and sound effects directly from video footage, text, or both, using models built around licensed source material,” the release adds. Through the deal, Musical AI’s technology will analyze Sonilo’s generated outputs to measure how licensed recording and compositions contributed to them. This will provide Sonilo’s licensing partners with the data they need to account for compensation for the songwriters, artists and other rights holders behind the original music.

Sonilo says it’s currently finalizing master recording and publishing agreements with major rights organizations as it readies the launch of its new music model.

Earth has caught itself a new mini-moon, and it is sticking around for Christmas.

The space rock, designated 2026 QQ8, was first observed in August 2026 after entering orbit around Earth in April 2026. The discovery was reported by the International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center on Thursday (Oct. 29).

The stay of QQ8 is a temporary one. The mini-moon will remain in the thrall of Earth's gravity through December before breaking away in 2027.

Scientists have recreated the orbital path of QQ8, finding that it takes 359 days, or about 0.98 of an Earth year, to orbit the sun. QQ8 closely approaches Earth around once every ten years, but this time the object came so close that Earth has been able to gravitationally seize it.

According to the Minor Planet Center, the closest QQ8 will come to our planet during this stay is around 353,000 miles (568,000 kilometers). That is around 110,000 miles (177,000 km) further out than the average distance between Earth and the moon. Even if QQ8 were to come close enough to collide with Earth, it would likely be completely destroyed in our planet's atmosphere before it reaches the surface, as it is only around 6 feet (2 meters) in diameter.

This is far from the first time Earth has snagged itself a mini-moon, more accurately called a temporary satellite. In fact, research last year suggested that our planet could be orbited by as many as six mini-moons at any given time, with many of these being fragments of our more permanent natural satellite, the moon.

While QQ8 is tiny, it is larger than the average mini-moon. That is according to modelling earlier this year that suggested the average size for a temporary satellite is 75 centimeters (30 inches) across.

The same modelling suggested the average stay for such an object would be around nine months.

So if QQ8 was captured in April 2026, that means it should move away in January 2027.

The researchers behind those findings suggested that, were Earth to gravitationally grip a space rock around 10 feet (3 meters) wide, it could serve as a mini-moon for 10 to 20 years.

Jordan Firstman on Club Slang, Directing His Friends, and the Perfect Date Night

Jordan Firstman’s directorial debut, Club Kid, was the talk of Cannes this year, earning a seven-minute standing ovation and starting a bidding war.  “I had a funny LA tarot card reader right before Cannes. I hadn’t sold the movie until Cannes and she was like, ‘This card is A24, and this card is Neon. If you go with A24, I think there’s a multi-picture deal happening,’” Jordan Firstman shared with Chloe on The Run-Through.

The film is out Friday and features Cara Delevigne, Saturn Risin9, and a cast of extras found on Grindr and in the local nightlife scene. But one of the standouts is newcomer Reggie Absoholm, who plays Arlo, the long-lost 10-year-old son that Peter (played by Jordan) finds out he has. “I do stuff to, like, cringe [Reggie] out ’cause I just like it. I do feel like a dad to him sometimes, and, like, it’s fun. I feel like that’s what made him comfortable on set was just being able to make fun of me.”

In his conversation with Chloe, Jordan shares why he chose to center New Yrok for a story about friendship and club culture, gives her a quick rundown on drug lingo, and talks about being newly in love.

Chloe Malle: Jordan, welcome to the podcast. Thank you so much for being here.

Jordan Firstman: Thank you for having me.

I’ve been getting a lot of press alerts about your outfits, so I feel like you’ve been really on a tour.

I’m really glad to hear that because I’ve had some looks I’m proud of.

What was the first fashion show you ever went to?

During the pandemic, I was the virtual host of a Thom Browne fashion show.

I played a newscaster, and that was my first fashion-related thing. I think the first show I went to was randomly Vetements. They sat me next to Edward Enninful and someone else fancy, and I was living. But it was exciting to be at a fashion show.

It is exciting because it’s like theater for people with ADD. You sit, you wait, you’re excited, and then 12 minutes later, it’s done.

And you’re like, “Oh, I witnessed this cultural experience, and I didn’t have to sit through two and a half hours and an intermission.”

It’s true. I didn’t have to watch a whole feature film. You’re so mad at me for making a movie. You’re like, “Why wasn’t it a short?”

I find it to be. I’m proud that, in the beginning screenings, I wasn’t telling people the runtime. I’d ask them afterward, and they’d say, “It felt like 1:40.”

I went with my 23-year-old club-kid colleague, and it was as if I were watching a foreign film. Edward was translating for me. We also discovered afterward that Edward had received an email asking him to be an extra.

Every gay in New York City did. It’s very much that Madison Beer meme: “I was supposed to be in the video.” Every gay guy in New York.

We had two amazing producers I brought on, Zach Stone and Ben Hubley, who were already in the party scene. I couldn’t have done it without them.

I went to college with Zach Stone, but anyway…

He’s great. He’s the best. They basically sent out mass texts and emails to everyone they’d ever partied with, and people showed up. It was actually a beautiful part of the process, seeing the community show up for creativity. It moved me to see everyone in one space together. They were there to have a good time and party.

I want to know what your relationship with club culture is. I think of you as an LA person, both because of I Love LA and before. Why did you set this in New York club culture rather than LA club culture?

To me, New York club culture has so much history. We’re standing on the backs of giants. From Studio 54 to the ’80s to the actual Club Kids days in the ’90s, it has such a lineage and such a vibe. LA has things going on, but it’s a bit less specific. I also think the idea of marathon partying has changed. I say in the movie, “It’s the Berlinification of New York, mama.” I think that’s happened, where marathon-style partying, starting on Friday and going until Sunday, is a new thing for New York.

Yeah. People really stay out all night. You end up at an afters, then go to another party. I don’t think it’s really meant to be in New York because New York isn’t Berlin. You have to hustle all week. You have to work really hard. I just heard a girl in the Vogue bathroom screaming at someone, “You need to keep your phone on!” So there’s a different vibe. She’s going to watch this and be like, “Oh, shit.”

But I think there’s a different grind in this city. When you put that next to the party scene, it creates a hamster wheel that I think a lot of gay men are living through, loving, but also having problems with. It was interesting for me to set the movie here. Also, New York and a kid are cinematic. There’s a lineage of movies like this that feel cozy, movies I wanted to replicate from the ’70s, like Kramer vs. Kramer.

Gloria, Kramer vs. Kramer, and obviously Big Daddy, which we’ve gotten a lot of comparisons to. There’s also a movie called One Fine Day. Do you remember that?

That was a visual reference. Uptown Girls, obviously. Such a classic. I wanted to be part of that canon, and I felt like a queer retelling of the story was deserved and made sense. We have this delayed coming of age as gay men. We don’t have the same pressures to have kids or settle down, so we keep partying. But eventually that becomes an issue, even for your body. It’s a hard burden to carry, continuing to party every weekend.

When I’ve taken writing classes, they always say that novelists write what they know in their first novel. They write something that feels true. What’s the germ of inspiration for this story? I don’t think you actually have a club kid, but where are you coming from?

It came from a very simple idea. I was at a dinner party with friends who had an eight-year-old son. I was saying inappropriate things in front of him and making him laugh, and the parents were laughing too. I thought there might be something funny in the dynamic between me and a kid.

I feel like a kid in a lot of ways. I think I have a childlike spirit, and I’ve always gotten along with kids. I really like them and relate to them. That was the germ of the idea. Then I had a boyfriend in Berlin, so I was there a lot, partying. It was really fun, but the comedowns were hard, and I think it took away from things.

2021–2024. I basically wrote the first draft before the breakup, and it got really good after the breakup. I’ve been calling it my Adele breakup album.

It is a breakup movie in a lot of ways. There’s a feeling of love in the movie, and I think that comes from the love I had and have for my ex, the times we spent together, and the parties we had together. I see it as a love letter to him. We haven’t spoken since, and I hear he’s doing well. I want him to do well. I felt like I had a lot of love to put on the page, and that’s when it got really good.

I read that you crashed real parties during filming and brought crew members with you. What did that look like? Paint the scene.

I didn’t crash. There were weeks of discussion with the party planners. My friend Noah Shakoor throws a party called Pit Stop, and he was like, “Come shoot the party.” Really, all you have to do is put up a sign saying you’re being filmed. There’s also a very famous afters in New York that goes until 3:00 p.m. the next day. It starts at 7:00 p.m. on Saturday and ends at 3:00 p.m. Sunday.

What do you do for fake bundle or kundle?

There’s actually a funny story here. There’s a scene where I’m supposed to be selling drugs at the party.

For listeners at home, bundle is cocaine.

Bundle is the original. Everything stems from bundle. Kundle is K, gundle is GHB, which I heard you didn’t know about.

You shouldn’t try GHB. I love you, but I don’t think you should try that drug.

I’ve been told I’d be even more insane. People have said I have no chill.

I think molly is good for you. I think you’re a molly girl.

Feel it out. Feel the music. Hug someone. That’s the vibe. Mundle, molly.

Now there are all the numbers—3-MMC, 4-MMC—so thrundle, frundle.

This is too much. You’re supposed to remember this when you’re high?

Everything is undle, so you just add the first initial.

What about ayahuasca? Is there ayawundle?

Oh, wow. I’m going to be using that all week.

I’m imagining the Woody Allen big sneeze scene. Tell me about people doing fake cocaine.

Here’s the story. I was selling fake drugs, and people knew there was a camera there, but they didn’t really know what was happening. They thought something else. They were high. I gave one girl a bump, and she said, “What the fuck? That’s not K.” She started getting really mad at me. I told her it was fake drugs, just powdered lactose. She said, “I’m lactose intolerant.” I actually ended up drugging someone with fake drugs by giving them that.

I had powdered vitamin D or C and powdered lactose. I found that the lactose looked the most like coke. The vitamin C looked more like K. It was a little saltier-looking. There’s a lot of trickery. Diego, our props guy, shout-out. What a fun job for the props department.

We talk a lot at Vogue about the different etiquette for different situations. What is club etiquette?

I think club etiquette is actually very boundaried and respectful. If you lose control at the club, it’s not a good look. Especially in Berlin. When I was new to Berlin, my friend was DJing behind the decks. I was trying to say hi and get his attention, and someone told me no. It’s a no-no to distract the DJ. There are definitely rules. You don’t want to be messy. Having a good attitude is important, too. You don’t want to be a bitch.

Anytime I’m in a group and people have an attitude, I’m like, “What are we doing here?” We’re here to have a good time. Be open to new people. Read the room. If someone doesn’t want to talk to you, move on. Those are my rules at the club.

Those are good rules. You also cast a lot of people who are club habitués. How did you find these individuals? What was it like working with people who aren’t actors? This was also your first directing experience.

I did everything they say you shouldn’t do on your first film. I worked with a kid, worked with non-actors, shot on film, shot at real parties, and shot in New York. There were days when I thought, Why did I do this to myself? But as far as the non-actors go, they just came. We had so much history together by that point. We were friends, and I’d been partying with Saturn for years. I’d also partied with Alaska [Riley].

She always felt like such a maternal figure in the LA party scene, but also had this power and confidence while being warm. I always wanted that for the character. The movie is about the different types of families we can create. Saffron is a mother figure to Arlo in a lot of ways.

She shockingly convinced me, as someone with a four- and six-year-old, that it was a good idea for him to DJ.

That’s the trick of the movie. You see it and you’re like, “Sure, we’re making this argument.” Then the second time, you’re like, “This is a terrible plan.” That was intentional. I wanted you to feel like you’re watching this kid learn and express himself creatively, and you’re like, “Yes, yes, yes.” Then you’re like, “No, no, no.”

It’s interesting to think about because there’s obviously a way to nurture creativity, even in a DJ setting, without actually bringing a kid to the club. There are so many merits to having these queer ideas in a kid’s life, while also being realistic and practical. That’s the whole thing with Peter. He’s doing the baseline of parenting, which is love. He’s also a pushover to everyone, especially Arlo. He just wants Arlo to have everything he wants.

I love him. This kid is such a star. I hear he also teased and bullied you.

I gave him permission. He would say things like, “You’re being really cringe right now,” or, “That’s such a dad joke.” I became the embarrassing dad to him. Now I do things to make him cringe because I enjoy it. I feel like a dad to him sometimes, and it’s fun. I think that’s what made him comfortable on set: being able to make fun of me.

It’s hard to work with a young person in that way, and also to get to know them.

That’s one thing I hope people take away from the movie: having a more honest relationship with kids. It’s something I wish I’d had when I was younger. I didn’t know my parents were people. That never occurred to me. I don’t think they really saw me as a person, either. They saw me as their kid. I wish I’d known more about their struggles or what they were feeling inside. I think it would’ve taught me more about what life was going to be like. We have these barriers between parents and kids that I think we could soften a little.

I want to know whether Reggie is actually into music. I was impressed with how seamless it was.

Yes, majorly. But his music taste has completely changed. He’s at that age where he’s impressionable. He came in loving Slipknot and Metallica. He was like, “I like the hard stuff.” Now he loves Cocteau Twins and Massive Attack. His whole playlist has changed. We have a shared playlist now.

Wow. Did you start that during the movie?

We started it a bit after the movie, but obviously the movie inspired it. It’s really sweet to watch him change through this experience. Watching him start doing press and interviews, he’s become so mature, grown-up, and funny. I feel like a proud parent. I understand why parents cry watching the school play. You’re like, “Oh my God, this is my kid. He’s up there doing it, and he’s so good.”

He’s so good. I hear you guys have been DJing together.

He DJs, I’m the hype man. I’m in the back. I don’t know all the knobs and stuff. Saturn taught him the basics. He knows his highs, lows, and mids.

Absolutely. I also heard that you brought the straight film crew to Basement and were teaching them some lingo. What was that Duolingo lesson like?

Duolingo, cundle edition. We need to get the Duolingo owl.

That’s an A24 idea. Are you here? That’s a really good idea. Pay her some money. You have a good job; you don’t need the extra funds. I’ll take the Duolingo license.

The question was about bringing your straight crew to Basement and teaching everyone.

They just had the best time. It’s not like they’d never partied before, but exposing them to the exact type of queer people the movie was about was important. I wanted everyone to deeply understand the details. The movie comes alive with the details, even the types of furniture a New York gay guy has in his apartment. We have Butt magazine, and we have our own specific things.

A lot of chrome right now. There’s one kind of chain-link table that a lot of gay guys have. It’s a lot of chrome and black. Black leather couch. Chic, but also a little grimy.

I want to know if you ever considered having someone else play Peter.

Keanu Reeves. No, this was always my goal and my dream. I thought of people like Lena Dunham, Donald Glover, Phoebe Waller-Bridge. I knew I had a voice, and I knew I could act a bit, so it was always the plan.

I’m always impressed with people who can do all three. You wrote it, directed it, and acted in it. How did you move between those three roles?

I tried to think of it like Pandora’s box. The movie was the box, and everything went into it. All my jobs were funneling into the project. I tried not to separate my actor brain from my writer brain or my director brain. If I did that, I’d start to freak out and lose control of the whole thing. Everything was in service of getting all the ideas, vision, and craft into one thing.

That’s such a good question. I think it’s both a mistake I made and something I don’t regret. You can tell in a club scene when people are faking dancing. I had a looped beat and thought we should dance to it to get the energy up. My sound guy said we really needed to do takes without sound because the edit would be a nightmare. The edit was a nightmare because you have to go beat by beat. But the crowd had more energy because the music was playing. It was a double-edged sword: harder in the edit, but better for the movie.

You’ve cast a lot of your friends in your projects. Do you ever find it difficult to work with friends? Is it harder to give direction?

I found that both the party host in me and the people-pleaser in me conflicted with directing. I wanted everyone to have a good time. Part of shooting is doing a lot of takes. It’s not comfortable and it’s annoying. Some of my friends didn’t realize that they’d do a scene full-out, and I’d say, “That’s the wide, and we’re not really going to use the wide.” By the time we got to their coverage, they didn’t have the same energy as they did in the wide.

Especially in those group scenes, where I had every cool gay guy in New York, excluding your friend who didn’t show up to work. I felt bad asking them to do 10 takes of dancing. They were sweaty and tired and they weren’t getting paid that much. That was the hardest part: getting what I needed as a director while keeping everyone happy.

I want to know about your crazy T-shirt collection. That was one of my favorite things. We’ve been talking a lot on the Vogue fashion team about how there’s a real slogan T-shirt renaissance right now.

What’s your favorite slogan T-shirt in the rotation right now?

I have some things I’m making. We made an “Innit Babes” one for the movie. We’re going to make a “Mama, Let’s Research” one.

I saw you made “I Boofed at Cannes and All I Got Was This Lousy Shirt.”

That was a good one. I’m going to go with “I Boofed at Cannes” because people didn’t understand what either of the two T-shirts I made for Cannes meant. There was “I Need a Doll Line and a Sickening Dose Stat” and “I Boofed at Cannes and All I Got Was This Lousy Shirt.” The beauty of them was that no one understood either of them.

I loved your character in I Love LA. You’re a stylist. What did you do to research for that role? Are there stylists you look to?

There’s one stylist whose aesthetic I’d been looking at. I won’t say who it is. In LA, you’re around a lot of creative people. Being friends with Charli, you see her whole glam team. Makeup people and hair people have their own personalities. Stylists have their own personalities.

I’ve been around a lot of stylists, so I was channeling some specific behaviors. But I was also thinking about the loneliness you can have as a gay stylist. You’re basically a paid best friend to these celebrities. You’re there when they need you, but then at a certain point you have to leave and go do more work for them. Sometimes they yell at you. Sometimes you mess up, and sometimes things aren’t perfect. It’s a hard job being a stylist, and you don’t get a lot of gratitude from the world or sometimes even the people you work with.

I Love LA has started production on season two, or not yet?

Ended. It comes out very soon. I don’t think I can say the date, but soon. Sooner than you could ever imagine.

What’s the vibe on set with that crew? Do you have group chats? How much are you guys in touch?

All the time. We’ve become family, and the set is so easy and fun. We improvise, help each other with our looks, and it’s definitely family vibes now.

I want to know what your cultural diet is right now. Have you seen the Elizabeth Holmes documentary?

I have. I was in the room at Telluride when they dropped it.

It was a pure, unadulterated gag. I can’t remember the last time I was squeezing someone’s hand like that. I couldn’t believe it. The “Who’s on First?” thing really got me.

Sorry. Never mind. It was a once-in-a-lifetime kind of thing. It only would have worked with those two people together. There aren’t two other people who could make that movie work. I think it’s a real accomplishment.

Remi Wolf. Her new album is so good. She has a song called “I Won’t Cry” that’s one of the best love songs I’ve ever heard. I’m also getting into the new Troye Sivan album. “Fucking Forever,” another good love song. I’m newly in love, maybe, so I’m into love songs right now.

What’s the dream date when you’re newly in love?

I actually like a party. I love a classic walk. If we’re in Paris, walking, eating, and sitting by the river is always romantic. New York, too. A little rain never hurt anyone. I love a first date with kissing. Sitting at a bar for a long time and just kissing is really hot to me.

For a gay guy, it’s kind of transgressive to only kiss on a first date. It’s sexy not to know what’s going on underneath the pants and just keep it to mouth stuff. I also like going to the club with someone I love, doing a little molly, and making out. That’s really sweet and fun to me.

Okay. Let’s do it. Then I have to run upstairs.

Who is the worst type of person to party with?

Who’s the best type of person to party with?

Strappy. We’ve seen it. Strappy and midriff. We’ve seen it.

I’m such a gay guy. I think a white tank top is hot and looks good. A black tank top, too. Shorts. Athletic shorts are so hot at the club.

3:00 to 4:00 a.m. I think it’s a really cool hour. Things get a little loosey-goosey, but not cracky.

That’s when you want loosey-goosey, not cracky.

Loosey-goosey, not cracky-wacky. You’re not only learning lingo, you’re inventing it. I’m helping gay culture. You’re really pushing the culture forward.

An espresso martini at the club is kind of a vibe.

That doesn’t conflict with the mundle or the trundle?

If you’re doing G, you can’t drink anything. If I’m being real, a crisp Diet Coke. Or a mate. In Berlin, they have Club-Mate. Mate and vodka is really nice.

You know what? I like a soup. Something nourishing, like a vegetable soup or pho. You want to go fried, but your body actually wants something nourishing.

Let’s go pho. You say to someone, “Let’s go pho. Let’s get out of here and go pho.”

‘The Wizard of Oz’ Was a Big Reference for Doc ‘A Want in Her.’ Now Its Creator Is Moving Into Fiction.

Irish artist-turned-filmmaker Myrid Carten, who won three BIFAs for her feature debut and made the U.K. BAFTA Breakthrough list, talks to THR about her journey so far – and what lies ahead.

About 11 months ago, Myrid Carten’s debut feature film, the documentary A Want in Her, an exploration of intergenerational trauma, mental health and addiction, was honored with three British Independent Film Awards (BIFAs), namely for best feature documentary, the best debut director – feature documentary, and The Raindance Maverick Award.

On Wednesday, the Irish artist-turned-filmmaker was revealed as a member of the 2026 U.K. BAFTA Breakthrough cohort, supported by Netflix.

A Want in Her was written and directed by Carten, who also appears in the doc and handled cinematography. It follows Carten and her mother, Nuala, who goes missing somewhere in Ireland. The creative’s search for her takes her into a family feud, a house dispute and a history that threatens to take everyone down, including the filmmaker herself. The doc had its world premiere at the 2024 International Documentary Film Festival Amsterdam (IDFA) in Amsterdam. On the occasion of her inclusion on the U.K. BAFTA Breakthrough list, Carten talked to THR about her journey behind A Want in Her, just how vulnerable she made herself in the process and what’s next for her.

What led you to take on such a challenging film project, for which you made yourself so vulnerable?

I was led very much by impulse, which comes from my artistic background. I studied fine art, and there, if you have an idea, follow it. Don’t think too much about outcomes! And then, as time went on and the film started to grow, I developed what I call my north star. I felt this adult-child story, the story of the children of people who suffer from mental illness and addiction, isn’t really told that often. So I felt compelled to do it for that particular cohort.

But even in the development process, everybody I met was like, “Oh, I have experience with that.” There’s a statistic that every addict touches 10 other people’s lives. I felt the addict story is often the one that’s told because it’s dramatic with a capital D, but this story felt really important.

On the vulnerability issue, I just really focused on making a film that worked. With myself as a character, it actually felt in some ways that the vulnerability served the film.

I was never thinking about BAFTA or BIFAs when I was in the middle of this old, derelict house and all this drama. My dream was just that the film would be seen in some cinemas. That was the height of my aspiration, and it’s gone beyond that. It’s done better than I thought it would do, especially because it’s quite an experimental film. So the fact that audiences have really taken to it has been a great boost of confidence. People are very smart. I think we often don’t acknowledge how much audiences get things, even if they’re not spelled out for them.

What is your hope for BAFTA Breakthrough?

BAFTA are the best of British and arguably world cinema. I was nominated for this outstanding debut category, and there tends to be one doc in there. But I really want to make a fiction film.

That’s always been my desire. I tried to use as many dramaturgy elements in A Want in Her as I could. I think it’s going to be an interesting move into fiction and blurring that space.

It is just amazing with that transition into fiction because I feel BAFTA is really a bed for lots of producers, directors, creative talent who are all drawn to BAFTA. So the idea is that I am going to create meetings.

What BAFTA Breakthrough does is it puts you in the role of a mentee, and you can ask people for meetings in more of a mentor capacity as opposed to meetings that are all about collaborating and business. I’m really interested in talking to people who have done debut fiction films that I think are extraordinary and chatting to them about how they did it, not just navigate the industry, but maintain artistic integrity.

I’m also really looking forward to having the BAFTA [View streaming] player and watching all the films. When you have a toddler, you can’t go to the cinema that much. So this is perfect for me.

Do you already have any ideas for a fiction film that you want to start developing?

I have one idea for a doc hybrid that I’ve put on the back shelf, and I’m developing two ideas for fiction films. I am trying to think about how to bring that sense of reality and rawness to this form that is artificial or has artifice in it. Doing screenplays is a whole new process. My story editor always says you learn about making a film by making a film.

Is there anything else you’d like to share?

I’d love to have a career like Jonathan Glazer, in that each of his films is so different. But the person I’d really love to meet through BAFTA Breakthrough would be someone like Lynne Ramsay.

But I have my own path, and actually, that’s what really worked in A Want in Her. I wasn’t trying to make a film like anybody else. I have to really hold onto that.

And the big reference for A Want in Her was The Wizard of Oz, which most people wouldn’t even know. Wait, what!? Now I am curious to hear more…

At the start, we called it No Place Like Home. That was the working title for years, and we would shoot the house as though it was Technicolor. It was this idea of this dream, this fantasy of the house and trying to return to that. So, [The Wizard of Oz] was one of the early references, but it’s so buried that people wouldn’t even recognize that. I like when things are kind of subliminal.
