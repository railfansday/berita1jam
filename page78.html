John Yoo Joins the Exodus from California; Departs for Texas

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

The University of California at Berkeley's law school loses its last right-leaning professor to teach in public law.

The Daily Californian reports that Professor John Yoo is leaving the University of California at Berkeley's law school. Beginning next year, he will be full-time at the University of Texas at Austin's School of Civic Leadership.

"Let the celebrations at my fan clubs in the City of Berkeley begin," Yoo said in an email. "Great as our university is, it would not allow a school to focus on restoring the central importance of western civilization to the education of all undergraduates." . . .

"I am sorry, however, that my departure will reduce the number of conservative professors in public law at Berkeley by 100 percent," Yoo said in the email. "I hope that the university will take this as an opportunity to consider that the notion of diversity should, most importantly, include ideology, not just the usual race, gender, and economic categories that my colleagues usually mean by diversity."

There had been one other right-leaning law professor at Berkeley, our co-blogger Orin Kerr, but he left Berkeley for Stanford in 2025.

According to Dean of Berkeley Law Erwin Chemerinsky, Yoo's decision to leave the faculty was entirely his own.

"John (Yoo) has been an important part of the faculty for many years," Chemerinsky said in an email. "This was entirely John's decision to retire. … He has indicated to me for a few years his thoughts about retiring. John is a very successful teacher and an eminent scholar." . . .

Yoo's move is also less drastic than it might appear, as he is moving from one blue state to another.  All of the other California transplants that have moved to Austin helped make sure of that (with a major assist from the Republican Party).

The Culture Club singer names the former collaborator who told him “Shame on you” and backs Robert Kraft’s decision to bar Macklemore from Gillette Stadium: “You can take your freedom of speech over there and do it on the street.”

Boy George wrote his new single, “We Will Dance Again,” after visiting the Nova Exhibition in London, an immersive installation that commemorates the Oct. 7, 2023, attacks on the Nova Music Festival that resulted in the deaths of 378 people and 44 people taken hostage.

He did it, he says, to send a message to his Jewish friends: “Not everybody hates you. You are loved.”

The Culture Club frontman, 65, was not nearly prepared for the massive backlash that ensued, which spilled outside of social media into friendships that went back years. Undeterred, he recently visited Israel and played two sold-out shows at Tel Aviv’s Menora Mivtachim Arena on Nov. 4 and 7. He also met with Israeli president Isaac Herzog and Nova survivors.

With his single now officially released and available on most streaming platforms, Boy George checked in with The Hollywood Reporter on a stop in Chicago.

He opened up about his Israel trip, explained why he used AI to make “We Will Dance Again” and revealed where he stands on the Ed Sheeran/Macklemore controversy that resulted in Sheeran’s entire band walking off of his world tour.

What’s a memory you cherish from the trip to Israel?

One of the sweetest things to me was people from London saying, “Oh, my aunt’s in Israel, can she come and see you? And will you say hello?” And I met a few friends of mine’s families that they haven’t seen for years. And just meeting the Nova survivors, that was probably the highlight of the whole trip, because you suddenly understand why this journey started in the first place.

I saw the Nova exhibition in L.A. It’s a very, very dark exhibit. When you played Israel, didn’t some of the survivors come onstage with you?

Yeah. There was about 13 survivors and also people that lost family. So it was a real mix of people. I think it was really beautiful to let people be seen. And obviously, as a performer, you are uncomfortable because you don’t want to hijack someone else’s experience. You want to show it respect, but at the same time, going to Israel was a massive statement, and I had to be bold about it.

Somebody that reviewed my show said I should have snuck in quietly. I was like, “That wasn’t an option.” And sneaking in quietly right now is not helpful because nobody else is being quiet. So I don’t think it’s the time to be quiet. I think it’s the time to be clear and to be loving. And I’ve made some mistakes in my communication online. There are people I shouldn’t have bothered reacting to.

You’ve said you’ve lost friends over this. Is there anyone you’d name?

Martyn Ware, who was in Human League and Heaven 17. I’ve worked with Martyn. I did a version of “I Wanna Be Your Dog” with him, and we sung onstage together. He came out quite early and said, “Shame on you,” and all of that. I just tweeted back, “You’ve lost that loving feeling. “

Most of them I don’t want to name because I don’t want them to have any more attention than they’ve already had from me.

You’ve said the music industry has been very one-sided on this. Do you find most people in the industry are anti-Israel?

I don’t think they’re anti-Israel. I just think they’re not necessarily informed about history, and it’s become quite trendy to shout certain things out. There’s a lot of pressure to agree with everybody else. Your thought, my thought, are not the deciding factor on what happens in the Middle East. We’ve got nothing to do with it. We’re not going to change it. So what we can do is, in our immediate surroundings, be more loving, be more open, be more kind, respectful, all of those things that you can do.

I was getting messages from friends of mine all over the world. Some people I didn’t even know were Jewish. And I’ve had letters from people. So there is a lot of support out there, but it is scared of itself.

When I saw you speaking up for your Jewish friends on Ireland’s Late Late Show in May, I knew you had something in your heart that needed expressing. I didn’t realize to what extent it would end up expressing itself.

When I decided to make this record, I was sending a message to my friends to say, “Not everybody hates you. You are loved.” And that’s the point I tried to make when I was in Ireland, is that growing up during the punk period, one of my biggest allies was a man called Philip Sallon.

One of the first things he ever said to me was, “You look Irish.” And I said, “I actually am.” And he said, “I’m a Jew and a homosexual.” And that was 1976. And when you’re young and impressionable, you don’t forget those moments. And Philip was a big reason why I got to know so many Jewish people because in London, this one knows that one, that one knows that one.

I can’t sit here and say every single Jewish person I’ve ever met I’ve got on with. Of course I haven’t. But Jewish people are characterful, they’re kind, they’re passionate, they’re funny, they’re bitchy. They’ve got all the qualities that I love.

You said you used AI to make “We Will Dance Again.” What aspect of the song uses AI?

The music I wrote with an AI tool. The melody I [wrote], the words I [wrote] And the reason I used AI was because I didn’t want somebody hanging over my shoulder saying, “Oh, you can’t say that. Oh, that’s too harsh.” And that would’ve happened because I’ve had 40 years of that. Forty years of “That’s too gay, that’s too this, that’s too that, that’s not commercial.”

I make a lot of music. I put out almost 200 tracks in the last two or three years. So I’m always writing. In respect to “We Will Dance Again,” that line, that’s a hook. You hear it straight away. As a writer, you go, “Got it. I got it. OK, there we go.”

I saw a video where you were performing a concert and the music cut out and you kept singing a cappella. I couldn’t tell whether it was intentional sabotage or a technical snafu. What happened?

I think it was a “technical snafu.” I love that word. I don’t think it was deliberate. There was a couple of moments when the mic went and I wasn’t sure whether I’d pulled out the wire. And also the guitar went out in a really pivotal moment, but I kept my cool. We were in the round, so there was nowhere to hide. When you’re in a proscenium situation, you can always dart off into a curtain for a minute, but in this instance, you couldn’t really leave.

What it reminded me of is Ed Sheeran, who went onstage after his band walked out on him and did his show a cappella.

In the moment, you always have to remember that people have paid to see you and that you’re there to perform. You can’t get lost in something, but it can really distract your whole evening. I think it’s really important to go, “No, no, no, I have to get through this.”

Do you have anything else to say about Ed Sheeran in terms of that whole controversy regarding Macklemore being dropped from his tour?

Obviously, the stadium owner, Robert Kraft, I know that he’s Jewish. He spent a long time fighting antisemitism, so he was never going to allow that to carry on where he worked, where he’s an owner.

You have a choice who comes and sits at your dinner table, whether you’re a billionaire or a housewife, you make a choice. And if you’ve cooked the meal and paid for the building, you do have a right as a person, as a Jewish person, to say, “No, you can take your freedom of speech over there and do it on the street.”

What Is a Thought Made Of? Scientists Reveal Why Human Consciousness May Be Beyond the Reach of AI

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A familiar song can pull you back decades in an instant, and the chemistry behind that rush of memory may hold clues to what separates human minds from AI.

How does the brain turn a collection of memories into thoughts, emotions, and the experience of being conscious? Scientists have spent decades studying how neurons communicate through electrical and chemical signals, yet the physical processes that allow us to recall experiences, attach feelings to them, and give them meaning remain incompletely understood.

A new scientific review suggests that part of the answer may lie in the complex chemistry surrounding brain cells. Rather than viewing memory primarily as a product of neuronal activity, the researchers propose that interactions among neurons, supporting brain cells, and molecules in their surrounding environment could play a fundamental role in shaping thought and conscious experience.

Dr. Gerard Marx of MX Biotech Ltd. and Prof. Chaim Gilon of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem’s Department of Chemistry present their theory in Consciousness, Memory, and Intelligence: A Mechanistic Perspective. Building on earlier research into how experiences might be physically stored in the brain, they examine whether the chemistry of memory could help explain the origins of thought and consciousness.

Their proposal also raises a question about artificial intelligence: If human memory and emotion depend on the chemistry of living cells, can computers ever develop consciousness simply by becoming more powerful?

Scientists have long studied how neurons communicate to form and retrieve memories. But Marx and Gilon suggest that important parts of this process may also occur in the molecular environment surrounding brain cells.

Their model, called the Tripartite Mechanism of Memory, involves three interacting components: brain cells, particularly neurons and astrocytes; the molecular material surrounding those cells; and chemicals such as metal ions, neurotransmitters, and gliotransmitters.

According to the theory, these components could allow the brain to chemically encode and retrieve information. Rather than serving only as a support structure, the environment surrounding brain cells may actively participate in memory storage.

Astrocytes, star-shaped cells that interact closely with neurons, play a central role in the proposed mechanism. The researchers suggest that coordinated activity among these cells, neurons, and surrounding molecules could contribute to how memories are formed and processed.

A familiar song can bring back memories from decades ago, complete with feelings that seemed long forgotten. Such experiences show that remembering involves more than retrieving information.

Marx and Gilon propose that the emotional content of memories may be linked to their chemical encoding. In their model, signaling molecules, including neurotransmitters and gliotransmitters, help connect stored information with the feelings associated with an experience.

The researchers also suggest that retrieving and interpreting these chemically stored memories may contribute to the emergence of thought. In this view, memory is not simply a record of past events but an active process through which the brain assigns meaning to experience.

Computers and brains both store and process information, but the underlying mechanisms differ. While computers rely on electronic hardware, biological memory involves living cells and complex chemical interactions.

Marx and Gilon argue that this distinction could matter for consciousness. AI systems can perform increasingly sophisticated intellectual tasks, but their ability to process information does not establish that they experience thoughts or emotions.

For example, an AI system can identify a familiar song and describe the memories or feelings it might evoke in a person. Whether such a system could actually experience nostalgia is a different question.

The researchers suggest that if consciousness depends partly on the biochemical processes involved in human memory, greater computational power alone may not be sufficient to reproduce it.

The proposed model offers a possible connection between memory, emotion, and thought, but it does not establish how physical processes in the brain produce conscious experience.

The authors acknowledge that consciousness remains poorly understood and do not claim to have demonstrated that AI cannot become conscious.

Instead, their work suggests that investigating the chemical mechanisms of memory could help explain how stored experiences acquire emotional meaning and contribute to thought. Whether those processes are essential for consciousness remains an open question.

Reference: “Consciousness, Memory and Intelligence: A Mechanistic Perspective” by Gerard Marx and Chaim Gilon, 4 August 2026, International Journal of Psychiatry Research. DOI: 10.33425/2641-4317.1251

How “American Gothic” Took On a Life of Its Own

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

After decades of collecting pop culture riffs on the iconic 1930 painting, Wanda M. Corn traces the work’s canonization and the life of its maker Grant Wood, whose story remains largely overlooked.

You already know the image by heart: Two stern figures stand before an old wooden house. To the left, a blonde, blue-eyed woman wears an apron and a somber expression. Beside her, an unsmiling man in glasses and overalls grips a pitchfork. Since it was first exhibited at the Art Institute of Chicago in 1930, Grant Wood’s painting has joined the ranks of art historical touchstones and shown up in countless ads, books, and memes. But what made this unassuming image so popular, and why does it continue to captivate us today?

In The Couple with the Pitchfork: American Gothic from Easel to Icon (2026), art historian and curator Wanda M. Corn provides some compelling answers. The author began writing about Wood’s painting in 1981, and she has spent decades amassing an archive of “American Gothic”-related imagery that tracks the farm couple across General Mills cereal ads, Gordon Parks’s 1942 portrait of African-American janitor Ella Watson during his fellowship with the Farm Security Administration, magazine covers, COVID-era cartoons, and other surprising riffs. Her book traces the painting’s history from the controversy behind its creation and the bitter fallout it brought to its hapless maker through to its transformation into a pop culture cornerstone and a symbol of Wood’s home state.

OpenAI says its AI found 14% gains in AMD’s flagship GPU design

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Some of the model's circuit changes are already in a chip AMD has sent for manufacturing.

OpenAI says its models improved power, performance and area by up to 14% on AMD’s flagship GPU designs, with some changes in a chip sent for manufacturing and single-digit gains on AMD’s next flagship CPU. It also detailed AI-made gains on its own Jalapeño chip and is training a GPT-Synopsys model with Synopsys.

OpenAI says its AI models have improved the design of AMD’s flagship graphics chips, with some changes already in a chip sent for manufacturing. In a blog post on Wednesday, it reported gains of up to 14% in power, performance and area, the three measures chip designers trade off, against AMD’s heavily optimised designs.

The model rewrote the code that describes a chip’s circuits, and used Synopsys software to check each change still worked. OpenAI says this was about a year’s worth of progress in under five months. AMD also confirmed single-digit gains in key parts of its next flagship CPU, according to OpenAI.

OpenAI used the same approach on Jalapeño, its first in-house chip, which went from design to manufacturing in nine months. Changes suggested by its models cut the size of two key parts of the chip by about 13% and 10%, and worked in the finished silicon. Startups are chasing the same idea: Architect Labs raised $24M in June to help companies design custom chips with AI.

OpenAI also tested five generations of its models on designing two small open-source processors. Its newest, GPT-6.2 Astra, scored highest both when improving existing designs and when building a processor from written requirements. OpenAI noted the second test was easier, because models could leave out features the originals had.

Next, OpenAI is training GPT-Synopsys with Synopsys, which is refocusing on AI design, to run the design tools engineers already use. Not everyone is fully convinced yet. OpenAI’s own chip chief, Richard Ho, said last month he still does not fully trust AI design changes, so every one is checked.

Why your fine-tuning job takes longer than the FLOPs say

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Predicting end-to-end fine-tuning time on shared GPU clusters, from 272 production runs.

A preview of our upcoming paper, Predicting End-to-End Fine-Tuning Time for Generative Language Models. The full version will be on arXiv soon.

GPUs are among the most expensive and scarcest resources in AI today. Anyone running a shared cluster has to decide, every day, which jobs go on which GPUs and in what order. That plan is only as good as the estimate of how long each job will take. When a job finishes hours early, GPUs sit idle until the next one is dispatched. When it runs hours late, everything queued behind it slips, experiments wait and delivery dates move.

Better duration estimates turn this into planning instead of guesswork: what runs, when, and on how many GPUs, with less idle capacity in between. Yet the estimate most teams use is a back-of-the-envelope FLOP calculation. It says nine hours, and the job finishes the next afternoon.

We wanted to know how far off that math really is, and whether we could do better before a job is even dispatched. So we took 272 real supervised fine-tuning (SFT) jobs from our production pool of NVIDIA H200 GPUs, about 7,382 GPU-hours in total, and compared analytical estimates against learned predictors.

The short version: the usual FLOP-based estimate explains little of what we observed (R² = 0.31, MAE 12.41 h). A CatBoost regressor that only sees information available before dispatch reaches R² = 0.98, with a mean absolute error of 1.71 h and a median error of 0.51 h in 5-fold cross-validation. The gap comes from two things a closed-form estimate cannot do. The regressor sees more training-specific signals, such as evaluation cadence, checkpoint policy, per-GPU load and fine-tuning regime, and it learns the nonlinear interactions between them from past runs.

For a dense Transformer, training costs roughly 6·P·D floating-point operations, where P is the parameter count and D the number of training tokens. Divide that by what your GPUs can deliver and you get a training-loop time:

Here N_GPU is the number of GPUs, FLOPS_peak is the peak throughput of one GPU from its spec sheet, and MFU (Model FLOPs Utilization) is the fraction of that peak the training actually achieves. Real jobs never run at peak because of memory traffic, communication and kernel inefficiencies, so MFU typically sits somewhere between 30% and 60%, and the right value for a given job is not known in advance.

This is a good description of the optimization loop. A job is more than its loop, though. Before the first step there is environment setup, model download and loading, and tokenization. During training there are periodic evaluations and checkpoint uploads. At the end, the final model goes to object storage. On a multi-tenant cluster, all of it competes with other jobs for network and storage I/O.

These overheads depend on configuration, and they do not scale neatly with workload or GPU count. Two jobs with the same nominal compute can finish hours apart.

To keep the comparison fair, the FLOP baseline we test counts the scheduled evaluation tokens as well as the training tokens, and we sweep MFU over a wide range.

We treat end-to-end duration as a supervised regression problem. Given a feature vector built from the job configuration and model metadata, predict the wall-clock time between the moment the job gets its GPUs and the moment it is done. Queue time is excluded.

We compared Linear Regression, Ridge, XGBoost and CatBoost. Feature selection combined correlation filtering, mutual information and permutation importance across 18 candidate feature sets, followed by a grid search with MAE as the criterion. The final CatBoost model uses 24 features.

Distribution of end-to-end duration across the 272 runs. Mean 23.43 h, median 15.29 h.

No single MFU value fixes the FLOP baseline. We swept MFU from 0.25 to 0.65. The best setting, 30%, still leaves a mean error above 12 hours on jobs that last 23 hours on average.

A more detailed analytical model does not close the gap. As a second baseline we took Calculon (Isaev et al., SC '23), an analytical performance model for LLM training from prior work. It builds an explicit layer graph and accounts for compute, memory traffic and inter-device communication. We extended the public implementation to cover LoRA, grouped-query attention, fused attention, bfloat16 and SwiGLU. Its estimates still correspond to a median of only 11% of the observed wall-clock time. That says nothing against Calculon as a step-level tool, which is what it was designed for. It shows how much of a production job happens outside the optimization step.

Linear models help, and then stall. Ridge brings MAE down to 7.23 h. The relationship between configuration and duration is not well captured by a purely linear model.

Gradient boosting does the heavy lifting. CatBoost and XGBoost land within one standard deviation of each other on every metric. We carry CatBoost forward because it has the lowest mean error across the board.

CatBoost's feature importance concentrates in a handful of features. Model and workload variables are there, as expected, but so are evaluation and checkpoint variables. num_eval_data, the total number of samples processed in evaluation over the life of the job, ranks first, followed by num_trainable_params, batch_eval_size_percent, num_all_params and num_gpus*batch_train_percent, with num_params_saved close behind. SHAP values tell the same story: high evaluation volume consistently pushes the predicted time up, while trainable parameter count has a nonlinear effect that only becomes large for the biggest models.

Mean feature importance of the CatBoost model across folds (PredictionValuesChange).

Feature importance is not a causal claim. Still, the pattern lines up with how a job is actually spent. How often you evaluate, how large the evaluation set is and how many checkpoints you persist all shape the wall-clock time, alongside model and dataset scale. These are choices people often make by habit when writing a training config.

One caveat on reading the plots: larger GPU allocations show positive contributions to predicted time. In our data, bigger allocations go with bigger jobs, so num_gpus acts partly as a proxy for job scale. It does not mean adding GPUs makes a job slower.

Looking at all 272 out-of-fold predictions, the median absolute error is 0.51 h. 80% of predictions are within 2.20 h and 90% are within 4.31 h. Broken down by job duration:

Up to 60 hours, the median error stays at about one hour or less. The long tail is where the model struggles, and with only 13 runs above 60 hours we read that as a data limitation.

Out-of-fold absolute error against real duration, colored by duration range. Errors stay low up to 60 h and widen in the sparse tail.

More data should still help. On a fixed 20% holdout, MAE drops from 1.63 h to 1.50 h when going from 85% to 100% of the training partition, with no clear plateau.

In practice, the predictor gives a scheduler a duration it can plan around before a job starts: which jobs to run together, when GPUs will free up, and whether a run will finish in time for its deadline.

What does transfer is the method: overhead-aware features, multicollinearity-aware selection and the comparison protocol. The full paper will report the feature formulas, the selection procedure, the search spaces and the final hyperparameters in full, so the pipeline can be rebuilt on any execution-log dataset with a similar structure.

Next on our list: hardware-specific descriptors and validation on other GPU types, transfer learning for configurations with few observations, other training objectives, and more data on long runs.

You have another chance to catch Project Hail Mary on the biggest screen possible

Sometimes a movie's spectacle is so great that it really does need to be experienced in a theater, preferably in the best possible format available to you. Project Hail Mary, this year's stunning Andy Weir adaptation starring Ryan Gosling, is one such movie. It may have been difficult to catch in IMAX, however, as IMAX screens are a hot commodity and the film has been out of theaters for a while. If you missed it the first time around, Amazon MGM Studios has an end-of-year treat for you.

As announced on the film's official X account, Project Hail Mary will return to theaters​​​​​​​ in all formats this December. That includes IMAX and 70mm​​​​​​​. The special event begins on Dec. 11, but it's not clear if it's a one-day-only event or if Project Hail Mary will have IMAX screens up until Dune: Part 3's release on Dec. 18.

The film adaptation of Andy Weir's 2021 novel​​​​​​​ of the same name follows Ryland Grace (Gosling) as he wakes up on a spaceship without his memory. Grace encounters the alien Rocky and the two embark on a shared mission to save their species, while Grace slowly begins to recall the events that led him to go on the mission in the first place.

Project Hail Mary became an instant critical and commercial success when it was released earlier this year. Much of that success was due to​​​​​​​ Gosling's heartwarming performance and the incredible puppetry behind Rocky. The film has already garnered some awards buzz, and its December theatrical re-release — right around when awards voters begin considering end-of-year-awards contenders — is a smart move.​​​​​​​ Of course, the movie is also currently streaming on Amazon Prime Video if you want to stay in the comfort of your own home.

After years of openly saying he wanted the role, Gosling's casting was finally revealed at San Diego Comic-Con​​​​​​​.
