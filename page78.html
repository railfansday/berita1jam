Two Brewers receive Silver Slugger awards for 2026

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Jackson Chourio and Jake Bauers of the Milwaukee Brewers both became first-time winners of the annual Silver Slugger awards, presented by Louisville Slugger to one player at each position in each league.

Chourio was one of three winners in the outfield, along with James Wood of the Washington Nationals and Pete Crow-Armstrong of the Chicago Cubs. Chourio, 22, adds one more trophy to the mantel along with a Gold Glove and first-team All-MLB nod. His third consecutive 20-20 season included a career-best .837 OPS, with 25 homers and 23 steals.

Bauers, who just signed a free-agent deal with the Boston Red Sox, won the award at utility. Though primarily a first baseman (119 games), Bauers did also play 46 games in the outfield, often in right field, with four appearances at designated hitter, as well. He hit a team-leading 26 home runs and posted an .874 OPS.

Both, of course, also authored signature moments during the run to the 2026 World Series title.

The winner at first base in the National League was Matt Olson of the Braves.

Brice Turang did not win at second base despite a third-place finish in the MVP voting; the second-base Silver Slugger went to Pittsburgh's Brandon Lowe.

The Cubs were named the NL Team of the Year after leading the Major Leagues in runs.

The Brewers last had a Silver Slugger winner in 2024, when William Contreras won at catcher for the second consecutive year. Before that, Christian Yelich won in the outfield in 2018 and 2019. It's the first time two Brewers players have won Silver Sluggers in the same year since Ryan Braun and Prince Fielder in 2011.

1980: Cecil Cooper (first base), Robin Yount (shortstop), Ben Oglivie (outfield)

2026: Jake Bauers (first base), Jackson Chourio (outfield)

10 Classic Thriller Books Worth Reading Again and Again

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

If you read books once, even great ones, that’s good. That’s better than not reading anything at all. It’s hard to find the time sometimes to read things fully, but it is rewarding once you get into the habit, because even if books get adaptations into other mediums, those adaptations aren’t always ideal (and often, even when they are done well, there’s still a lot you can only get from the source material).

So, if you’ve got even more time on your hands, or otherwise feel capable of re-reading a few books, these ones are probably worth tackling. Or, well, re-tackling. Reading again? Whatever sounds better. These are great thriller books that are rewarding to read again and again, often because they're overwhelming and heavy on details and/or they have some pretty great plot twists that might be even easier to admire once you’ve read the book one time and the shock of those revelations has worn off.

The Hunt for Red October came out when the Cold War was still ongoing, and so while it might not be as timely now, it’s an interesting snapshot of the times. But more importantly, it holds up as a thriller regardless of the broader conflict it’s set against, being the first of the Jack Ryan books, and one with a great premise, too: there’s a Soviet submarine captain who goes rogue while being equipped with some immense weaponry, and chaos ensues.

Well, people work to stop things from getting too chaotic, but The Hunt for Red October is certainly tense, and it’s written in a way where you can believe something bad will happen at any point (even if you know, reading post-1984, that Jack Ryan has a fair few more books/events to get wrapped up in). It’s a strong thriller/war novel, and a good starting point for Tom Clancy, since it was his first published novel and all.

If you like science fiction, you can’t go wrong with the better-known Michael Crichton novels, since he did very much like writing within this genre, and Jurassic Park is no exception… though this one might be a bit more thrilling than some of his earlier hard sci-fi books. Make no mistake: there’s still a lot of science in this science fiction, but the whole bringing dinosaurs back to life thing does ultimately give Jurassic Park a bit more in the excitement department.

As long as you're not expecting something as thrilling or broadly enjoyable as the film adaptation (which is tense for sure, but gentler overall), then the original Jurassic Park is well worth diving into. There’s a lot here, and so as with the case with all books overflowing with ideas, going back to them and reading it again is always helpful.

There was a famed movie adaptation of Rebecca just a couple of years after the book came out, and it’s notable for being the only Alfred Hitchcock-directed film to win Best Picture. On its own, though, and just looking at the book, it’s a classic with or without that noteworthy movie, since it’s one of the best gothic novels of all time, and one with a good deal of mystery, crime, and romance elements, alongside some other things.

You can call it a thriller, but perhaps more of a slow-burn one that rewards patience, and then it has enough by way of reveals that re-reading Rebecca will also prove rewarding. It’s about a young woman whose first name is never learned, and she becomes the second wife of a mysterious man who shows increasingly troubling signs that he’s haunted by the death of his first wife. Some patience is needed to go with it while it’s all unfolding, but it’s more than worth it and everything.

Living up to its title, The Running Man keeps things going at a very fast pace, and that’s extra appropriate when you learn that Stephen King (under his Richard Bachman pseudonym) wrote this in a shorter period of time than it might take you to read some of the author’s longest books. The similarly titled The Long Walk might be more horrifying, and ultimately more psychologically devastating (as well as better overall), but The Running Man has more thrills.

Stephen King (under his Richard Bachman pseudonym) wrote this in a shorter period of time than it might take you to read some of the author’s longest books.

It is unapologetically a thriller alongside being a work of dystopian science fiction, with the premise here involving one man’s struggles to survive a deadly competition that’s like extreme reality TV, and involves people trying to kill you the whole time. It’s a high-risk/high-reward show (well, on paper, there are rewards), and it’s ultimately such a wonderfully quick read, being the sort of thing that’s hard to put down once you start. So, the idea of returning to it doesn’t feel like too much of a leap, nor an especially big time commitment.

The Black Dahlia is the first book in the L.A. Quartet series, though the books generally have different characters at their centers, with references and thematic similarities, sure, but not much by way of continuity between books until you get to some shared narrative elements between books #3 and #4 (L.A. Confidential and White Jazz). All four books get progressively more complicated, too, meaning The Black Dahlia feels refreshingly straightforward in comparison, even if it’s quite dense and harrowing by most other standards.

It’s the most focused on a real-life crime out of any book in the quartet, with the titular case being infamously grisly and unsolved: that of the murder of Elizabeth Short, who became known as the Black Dahlia. There are fictional characters who get wrapped up in this real-life murder case, and it all makes for a great psychological thriller book dealing with obsession, and comparable to Rebecca in the sense that it feels like it’s a book about being haunted, too (though here, not in a horror/supernatural sense).

If you judge it as a postmodern book, House of Leaves is not one of the hardest to follow, so long as you get used to some of the strange stylistic and formatting decisions. By most other metrics, though, it’s pretty full-on and confounding, since it’s about a guy researching another guy who was researching a documentary film about a strange sort of haunted house.

But then different discoveries are made at different levels, and lots of things cross over and/or appear uncertain. The book itself does strange things that make you feel like you're going a bit mad as well, like all the characters, and so calling House of Leaves exceptionally immersive would be quite an understatement. Still, it’s also extremely entertaining and ambitious with all the genres and emotional beats it goes for, so diving into it all again at some point feels pretty much essential and undoubtedly rewarding.

The most legendary of the Hannibal Lecter books would have to be The Silence of the Lambs, and sure, that’s also something you can say about all the movies featuring the character. The Silence of the Lambs just has the most by way of stuff going on, with Lecter himself being imprisoned and used by an FBI trainee who is after another serial killer actually at large… or is she being used by him?

Or both? You know the drill. You’ve either read the book or seen the movie, or perhaps you’ve done both, because that’s always very much worth doing. There’s a great dynamic here between Lecter and Clarice Starling, as the two main characters of the book, and then the more outward/traditional villain, Buffalo Bill, is no slouch in the “gets-your-heart-racing-and-your-stomach-feeling-queasy” department. Sure, that department does need a catchier name, but you get the sentiment, right?

The Talented Mr. Ripley centers on a man named Tom Ripley, and he is talented in the sense that he definitely knows how to be a con artist, and he continues his conning ways in a whole series of books. But The Talented Mr. Ripley is the first, and probably the best, being about what happens when Tom becomes infatuated with another man named Dickie Greenleaf, aspiring to have just about everything that Dickie has.

If you're worried about spoilers for a 70-year-old book… uh, well, there’s something dramatic that happens, maybe more dramatic than in most books, and then there’s a whole series of consequences that follow that event. It’s textbook stuff maybe, but also executed so well, with Patricia Highsmith being one of the all-time greats when it came to crafting this kind of psychological thriller/crime story.

Stephen King knows his way around the horror genre, without a doubt, and Misery does see him tackling horror while also doing what’s probably his strongest effort as far as ventures into psychological thriller territory go. Here, things also feel pretty grounded, since Misery has a villain who’s famously terrifying without having any fantastical or otherworldly powers, and the protagonist is memorably at odds with her the whole time, since he’s an author she’s a fan of, and she’s kidnapped him.

From there, things get more intense and gruesome, and protagonist Paul Sheldon is indeed one of the Stephen King characters who’s gone through the most misery (ha), and that’s saying something. Overall, Misery is one of the very best Stephen King books, and so long as you’ve got the stomach for some of the grislier scenes, it’s probably one worth returning to sooner rather than later.

Agatha Christie is the quintessential murder-mystery writer, and so her books are very thrilling, and sure, some – like And Then There Were None – cross over a little genre-wise (there are some borderline-horror elements here, honestly). In any event, that level of praise is not really something that can be said casually, but she earns it, and this novel is perhaps her best. It’s got the greatest premise imaginable for such a book, since it’s about a bunch of people on an island getting murdered one by one.

The slowest it gets is right at the start, because you do need to have some introductions for the characters (or murder victims), and you care just enough before the murdering and dying starts happening. And Then There Were None is truly relentless once it gets going, and the re-readability of it all is obvious: like, it’s fun to be surprised the first time around, but then the second time, it’s neat to appreciate all the foreshadowing and better understand how Christie toys with you the first time around.

After Gilligan's Island, Jim Backus Appeared In This 1977 Disney Cult Classic

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

"Gilligan's Island" came to an end after three seasons in 1967, and while he reprised the role of Thurston Howell III in two animated "Gilligan's Island" spin-offs and three TV continuation movies, Jim Backus found time for plenty of other projects. That included two collaborations with director Don Chaffey.

The first of these saw Chaffey, of "Jason and the Argonauts" fame, direct Backus in the 1977 live action/animation hybrid "Pete's Dragon." /Film previously dubbed "Pete's Dragon" a fascinating and surprisingly mean Disney oddity, and that's largely true — though watching this movie as a kid I don't recall being particularly struck by the meanness of it. Looking back, however, there were certainly some surprisingly dark moments in the film, including the opening musical number in which a gang of hillbillies sing enthusiastically about recapturing their escaped child slave and "tying him screaming to a railroad track."

Disney was going through a transitional phase at the time, during which things just seemed to get a little more grim than usual. "Pete's Dragon" debuted the same year as another Disney movie with some dreary overtones: "The Rescuers." But the former was particularly odd for combining its songs about child slavery with a wholesome throwback musical vibe and a lovable cartoon dragon overlaid on live-action scenes. It resulted in a film that seemed simultaneously ahead of its time technically and yet felt like one big anachronism in a year that also gave us two of the greatest sci-fi movies of all time. Having Thurston Howell pop up just added to the disorientation, even if Backus was only in a few scenes.

"Pete's Dragon" stars Sean Marshall as young orphan Pete, who escapes from the aforementioned hillbillies, the Gogans, with the help of his invisible dragon friend, Elliott. Pete manages to avoid being tied screaming to the railroad tracks and makes it to the Maine fishing village of Passamaquoddy. There, he meets lighthouse keeper Lampie (Mickey Rooney), and his kind adult daughter Nora, (Helen Reddy), who take in Pete and become the loving family he's always needed.

Meanwhile, traveling snake oil salesman Doc Terminus (Jim Dale) and his assistant Hoagy (Red Buttons) arrive in Passamaquoddy and win over the townspeople with their grift. When Dale's villain discovers the existence of Elliott, however, he becomes obsessed with capturing the beast to concoct new tonics. Soon, the Gogans also arrive to claim Pete, before forming an alliance with Terminus to recapture both their former child slave and Elliott.

Jim Backus appears in a few brief scenes as the Mayor of Passamaquoddy, who twice finds himself the victim of an invisible Elliot's antics. Backus isn't in the film much, but does a decent job with the physical comedy of his scenes, recalling moments from his "Gilligan's Island" years. The actor previously lent his voice talents, honed over several decades of playing one of animation's most famous characters, Mr. Magoo, to the 1951 Disney animated short "Plutopia" and later played Timothy Forsythe in the 1972 live-action Disney comedy "Now You See Him, Now You Don't." The Mayor in "Pete's Dragon" was his final Disney role — though he was referenced in Disney's attempt to revive Mr. Magoo with a live action update that Roger Ebert called "transcendently bad." Happily, the same can't be said for "Pete's Dragon," even if it has gone mostly overlooked.

Originally conceived as an episode of "Walt Disney's Disneyland" back in the mid-'50s, "Pete's Dragon" was scrapped before eventually being revisited in the mid-'70s. By this time, pop culture looked a lot different than two decades prior. 1977 was, of course, the year a little movie titled "Star Wars" debuted, as well as Steven Spielberg's "Close Encounters of the Third Kind."

Released on November 3, 1977, "Pete's Dragon," a throwback musical set in early 1900s Maine and shot on the Disney backlot, found itself sandwiched between two of the best sci-fi movies of all time. "Star Wars" and "Close Encounters" were both symbolic of mass audiences' changing tastes as sci-fi came to rule the world. As such, "Pete's Dragon" went mostly overlooked.

That is, the film has been mostly overlooked since its release. In 1977 it actually made Disney a decent profit, earning $35.4 million upon its 1977 debut and a further $4.1million when it was re-released in 1984. On a $10 million budget, that ain't bad. The movie also earned two Oscar nominations, one for musical scoring and another for original song. Still, "Pete's Dragon" never became a Disney classic, which is a shame because I, and I'm sure others, were enchanted by it as a kid.

Meanwhile, Jim Backus went on to collaborate with Don Chaffey a second time in the 1979 sci-fi comedy "C.H.O.M.P.S. " It, too went mostly overlooked. Some 37 years later in 2016, we got a "Pete's Dragon" remake which was similarly underrated and is a must-watch for "How to Train your Dragon" fans. But the original is such a charming little oddity that it could never truly be outdone. If you fancy checking it out, the film is available to watch on Disney+.

Nvidia’s startup stakes ballooned to $65B, but Huang’s exit puts future deals in doubt

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia’s equity in private companies continues to grow: it revealed its stakes are now worth $65.5 billion on Tuesday, up from the $22 billion reported in January. The increase includes both new investments and significant markups of existing portfolio companies.

The company invested $16 billion into equities in the past quarter alone, it said, and $58 billion in the past nine months of its fiscal year. This amount includes buying shares in public and private companies, yet it shows how the chipmaker made massive bets over the course of the year, especially compared to last year; in November 2025, the company reported spending $5 billion buying equity in the first nine months of that fiscal year.

But it’s unclear if this growth will continue under the interim CEO and Jensen Huang’s successor.

Nvidia stated in late 2023 that the goal of its corporate investing is to expand the AI ecosystem by backing startups it considers to be “game changers and market makers.”  Over the nearly three years that followed, the chipmaker has invested in nearly 350 startups, according to PitchBook. The companies Nvidia backed include Anthropic, OpenAI; neolabs like Thinking Machines and Reflection; neoclouds, like Lambda, Nscale, and Crusoe; as well as other companies developing AI infrastructure, robotics startups, and applications.

It’s also become a big financier of the data center build out, reporting $118 billion in guarantees with $105 billion of that the backstop it supplied for OpenAI’s $500 billion data center in Ohio.

The company made those commitments by allocating a meaningful portion of its excess cash flows. In Tuesday’s earning call, Kress said Nvidia would continue providing financing assistance to strong customers and that “as the market matures and additional sources of capital become available,” the company would be “rigorously evaluating future investments, commitments, and guarantees.”

But the question for the VC ecosystem is whether the company will continue to back startups at anywhere near the same level without Huang at the helm.

Huang’s rampant dealmaking has reportedly been a source of concern for Kress. Investors have told TechCrunch that they anticipate that she will take a more conservative approach to startup investing, becoming more selective with new investments and writing smaller-sized checks into its existing portfolio companies.

“We intend to return all excess free cash flow net of strategic uses to shareholders during this last quarter and going forward,” Kress said on the earnings call on Tuesday. Just how strategic startups will be under her leadership, and under the future permanent CEO, remains to be seen. But for now, VCs are bracing for a meaningful pullback in capital.

Researchers train AI in simulation and unlock real robot skills

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers trained a 27-billion-parameter AI model in simulation to control an unfamiliar robot, succeeding in 60% of real-world trials.

Researchers behind PEAR, a system for training AI models to control robots, reported that a model completed 18 of 30 physical trials after learning its robot skills entirely in simulation.

The preprint submitted Nov. 13 describes robot post-training of Qwen3.8-27B, an open-weight reasoning model with 27 billion parameters. Researchers used reinforcement learning without demonstrations during that stage.

The result offers evidence that simulated practice can teach skills that carry over to different physical hardware. The final model had already practiced the two evaluated task types, pickup and block alignment, in simulation.

The physical test used a dual-arm YAM robot, which none of the compared models received training on in this study. It included 20 pickup attempts across ten objects and ten alignment attempts across five block layouts, with each object or layout tried twice.

The final model succeeded in 12 pickup attempts and six alignment attempts. A checkpoint trained only on the LIBERO simulation benchmark completed seven of the 30 trials.

The final model had continued training on the corresponding two tasks in RoboDojo using a different robot, according to the paper's physical-trial account.

At each turn, the model reads camera images and the robot's state, reasons in text, and specifies changes to the gripper's pose. Inverse kinematics translates the target pose into joint movements, which a controller executes.

The prompt supplies coordinate frames, gripper orientation, cameras, and task-completion conditions, and the authors acknowledge that performance depends on those descriptions.

All three models used the same high reasoning-effort setting, with trials having a 25-round budget and failing if a single round exceeded 32,000 reasoning tokens. The final model's successful attempts required 7 to 24 rounds.

The authors say reasoning before every move slows tasks. Their separate decoding demonstration reached 747 tokens per second on a GB300 using lower-precision NVFP4 weights and DFlash2, a decoding accelerator.

That suggests about 4.3 seconds to generate an average high-effort reasoning round. The reported evaluations used BF16 weights because quantization reduced accuracy, so the speed demonstration and physical success rate describe different operating conditions.

The public PEAR repository provides training infrastructure and deployment recipes. The result remains an author-reported experiment pending independent replication, and turning it into a broadly useful robot system will require wider task coverage and shorter reasoning delays.
