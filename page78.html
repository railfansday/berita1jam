researchers turn simulated moon dust into 3D-printed bricks for future lunar homes

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

a 3D printing method fuses simulated moon dust into bricks without adhesives, opening possibilities for construction with lunar materials.

Researchers at the University of Arkansas have developed a way to 3D print interlocking building blocks from simulated moon dust, using heat to fuse the material together without glue or cement. Led by mechanical engineering professor Wan Shou and researcher Cole McCallum, the experiment explores how future lunar settlements could be built using materials gathered directly from the Moon’s surface.

The idea could reduce the amount of construction material that needs to make the journey from Earth. Instead of transporting cement and other building supplies into space, astronauts could eventually manufacture structural components using the mineral powder already covering the lunar landscape.

The team has produced a series of small, LEGO-like modules that can be assembled into larger structures and rearranged as needed. Each block is made entirely from a material that mimics lunar regolith, the loose layer of crushed rock and mineral dust covering the Moon.

Lunar regolith might resemble ordinary sand or soil, but its physical properties are quite different. Over billions of years, meteorites and other space debris have struck the Moon’s surface, breaking down rock into increasingly fine particles. Without wind or flowing water to gradually wear away their edges, these fragments remain sharp and angular, with an abrasive texture that NASA has compared to tiny pieces of glass.

The dust also behaves differently under lunar conditions. Its particles can become electrically charged and cling to nearby surfaces, making them particularly troublesome for astronauts and sensitive equipment.

During the Apollo missions, lunar dust damaged spacesuit materials and clogged mechanical components. Engineers planning future missions are still working out how to keep the powder from entering machinery, where its jagged edges can wear down moving parts.

For their experiments, the Arkansas researchers used Lunar Highlands Regolith Simulant, known as LHS-1, a specialized powder developed by the University of Central Florida’s Exolith Lab and supplied by Space Resource Technologies.

The material is made from terrestrial minerals selected to reproduce the composition and granular texture of lunar highland soil, drawing on samples collected during the Apollo missions.

Creating convincing artificial moon dust has become a field of research in its own right. Specialized laboratories and companies manufacture different regolith simulants to help engineers test lunar excavation systems and study the effects of abrasive particles on spacecraft hardware. These mixtures reproduce selected properties of lunar soil, although the real material has characteristics that remain difficult to replicate on Earth.

To manufacture the blocks, the team turned to a 3D printing method called laser powder bed fusion. A thin layer of simulated regolith is spread across a platform, where a focused laser traces the intended shape and heats the mineral particles until they fuse together.

The platform then lowers, another layer of powder is added, and the process repeats until a solid component takes shape. The approach allows the researchers to work with the regolith alone, avoiding the polymer binders or cement mixtures used in some earlier experiments with lunar construction materials.

Instead, thermal energy bonds the grains directly to one another through a process known as sintering. ‘There’s nothing else it’s binding to. The regolith powder is binding to itself,‘ explains McCallum, who was an undergraduate at the university when he began the research. For future construction on the Moon, this could eliminate the need to transport large quantities of adhesives from Earth.

The researchers also considered how individual printed components might come together to form larger structures. Drawing on the principles of traditional mortise-and-tenon joinery, they developed two complementary brick geometries: one shaped like a cross and the other resembling the letter H.

The cross-shaped piece acts as a projecting connector, fitting into the recessed profile of the H-shaped module. Together, the pieces can interlock without additional fastening materials. While the researchers describe the system as LEGO-like, the comparison refers to its modular assembly instead of the familiar rectangular bricks with circular studs.

The team has demonstrated small wall configurations and shielding units assembled from its cross-shaped and H-shaped pieces. Printing individual components could also offer more flexibility than manufacturing an entire enclosure with a large-scale 3D printer. Blocks could be produced in batches and replaced individually, allowing structures to be rearranged as the needs of a lunar settlement change.

The researchers are also investigating the possibility of using concentrated sunlight to harden lunar regolith. In their published study, they consider how optical devices such as Fresnel lenses or parabolic mirrors could focus solar radiation onto the powder, producing temperatures high enough to fuse the mineral particles together.

Earlier solar-sintering experiments have already demonstrated the potential of concentrated light to harden simulated lunar soil, although the Arkansas team’s own prototypes were manufactured using a laser. The idea could eventually allow construction equipment to draw on two resources already present on the Moon: regolith and sunlight.

The current bricks remain experimental, with porous surfaces and connections that require further refinement. Their strength also needs improvement before they can support substantial structural loads. ‘These structures we’re making can be self-supported,’ says McCallum, while acknowledging that the technique still needs development for load-bearing construction.

The idea has already been explored in another lunar construction experiment. In 2023, the European Space Agency (ESA) tested a method for creating roads and landing pads from simulated moon dust, using a powerful laser to melt the powder into a solid, glasslike surface.

The project, called PAVER, aimed to prevent passing rovers and landing spacecraft from kicking up abrasive lunar dust that could clog machinery and damage equipment. Researchers even produced interlocking, triangular paving elements, and proposed using sunlight concentrated through a large Fresnel lens to replace the laser on the Moon.

Further experiments under lunar vacuum and gravity conditions will be essential before the team can begin translating its laboratory experiments into a practical construction system. For now, the printed blocks are only a few centimeters across, and the researchers are continuing to investigate how their mechanical properties can be improved.

Still, the research offers an early glimpse of how future lunar settlements might manufacture their own building materials. With mineral powder gathered from the surrounding landscape and heat supplied by lasers or potentially sunlight, the basic components of a building could eventually be produced directly on the Moon, one layer of regolith at a time.

Cillian Murphy's 28 Years Later: Part III Will Only Happen Under One Condition, Says Director Danny Boyle

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Are we going to see "28 Years Later: Part III?" Director Danny Boyle is certainly interested in doing it and Alex Garland has written a script. The problem? Money. This year's "28 Years Later: The Bone Temple" finally delivered the moment fans have been waiting for, bringing back Cillian Murphy as Jim. It was a small appearance teeing up something much larger in a presumed third installment. As Boyle recently explained, that movie is only going to happen if they can find the money.

Speaking recently with Deadline during an event for his new movie "Ink," Boyle's tabloid media origin story that will make you feel sick, the Oscar-winning director addressed the status of the third and presumably final entry in the "28" franchise. "There is a plan for a third part of '28 Years Later.' Alex has written a fantastic script," Boyle said. "We gotta raise the money to make it."

Sony Pictures produced both "28 Years Later" and "The Bone Temple." And while "28 Years Later" killed it at the box office, the sequel didn't. That's why Sony isn't eager to give it the green light. Speaking further, Boyle said that the third installment would bring the whole franchise to a close:

"[Cillian Murphy]'s up for it, and it's a fantastic part. Alex has written this fantastic part for him. Those of you who saw 'The Bone Temple,' the second part to '28 Years Later,' he's teased at the end of that. The third film does complete the story in a beautiful, mathematically perfect way, in which the circle is closed."

Creatively speaking, critics and audiences generally agreed that both of the recent "28" movies were damn good. But Hollywood is a business and money became an issue pretty quickly. "28 Years Later" made $151 million against a budget in the $60 million range. That was good enough. "28 Years Later: The Bone Temple" flopped at the box office though, bringing in just $58.5 million worldwide on a similar budget. Unfortunately, the math doesn't math for anyone who wants to make money in this business.

That's why "28 Years Later: Part III" probably won't happen. Admittedly, "probably" is doing some heavy lifting there. Cillian Murphy is an Oscar-winning actor for his work in "Oppenheimer," Danny Boyle is an Oscar-winning director, and Alex Garland is an Oscar-nominated screenwriter. It's a powerhouse team. That makes it unlikely but not impossible.

The most realistic path to getting it made would be partnering with a streaming service. Netflix is releasing Boyle's new movie "Ink" and the streamer also has a partnership with Sony. That would be the logical choice. But if Boyle and Garland want it as a more traditional theatrical release? That's where it gets more complicated and far less likely.

As for where it all went wrong? The bonkers ending in "28 Years Later" left audiences divided, for one. There was also a very short, roughly six-month gap between installments, which seemingly hurt more than it helped. But playing Monday morning quarterback doesn't help now. It's really about Boyle and co. getting creative to see if they can cobble together the money to bring this story to a close.

Economic Headwinds Drag Homebuilder Confidence to New Low

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Homebuilder confidence in November’s National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index (HMI) report slumped four points to 25, a new 14- year low amid low consumer sentiment and significant pressure on the housing sector.

Last month’s homebuilder confidence score, a measly 29 points, was already a 14-year low. This month’s new slump sends homebuilder confidence even further back in time to April 2012.

NAHB blamed a variety of causes, the most important of which were “high mortgage rates, ongoing inflation pressure, elevated energy and material costs, (and) rising labor shortages and hesitant buyers.”

“Builders are confronting multiple challenges in today’s economy,” said NAHB Chairman Bill Owens in a statement. “Stepped-up immigration enforcement is keeping legal workers off job sites, deepening labor shortages and driving up costs. Higher fuel prices are prompting subcontractors to raise their rates sharply, and with fewer buyers in the market, more builders are scaling back construction.”

Robert Dietz, NAHB chief economist, focused on the housing market’s supply-side challenges, homing in on “building material costs pushed higher by tariffs, an ongoing shortage of construction workers and excessive regulations that undermine housing affordability.”

Dietz also added that “(m)ortgage rates above 7%, softer consumer confidence and an uncertain economic outlook are holding buyers back.”

The NAHB/Wells Fargo HMI, an ongoing monthly survey of more than 40 years, asks builders to rate their perceptions of current single-family home sales, potential single-family home sales in the next six months and prospective buyer traffic. Builders’ perceptions on each of these three component indices is scaled onto a range of zero to 100, which is then used to calculate a general homebuilder confidence level.

The three component indices this month were, unsurprisingly, also down. Current sales conditions dropped six points to 28, while future sales conditions dropped two points to 30.

Prospective buyer traffic ticked down two points to 16, another new low for this report. The last time prospective buyer traffic reached these troughs was 15 years ago (excluding a brief period at the start of the pandemic in 2020).

The NAHB/Wells Fargo HMI this month also reported that 46% of builders reduced prices, up 3% from last month. This is the highest share of builders cutting prices in the post-Covid period.

Almost seven-in-10 (68%) builders offered incentives, also up 3%, representing a post-Covid high. November 2026 is now the 20th consecutive month in which at least 60% of builders utilized sales incentives.

Regional HMI scores were similarly gloomy. All four regions experienced declines, but the Northeast and the Midwest experienced the largest declines point-wise at four and five points, respectively. The Northeast now sits at 34 points, the Midwest at 35, and the South and West both tumbled three points to 27 and 23 points, respectively.
