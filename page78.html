Google Unveils Ask AdMob and AI Agent Tools for App Monetization

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google introduced Ask AdMob and a set of AI agent tools on November 16, 2026, targeting the work of configuring, troubleshooting and coding app advertising. The package combines a Gemini-built conversational assistant with developer playbooks and an upcoming server connection to AdMob accounts, each at a different stage of availability.

Naomi Ben-Ayoun, general manager of AdMob, presented the release as a way to reduce the manual work of managing ad setups, fixing integration errors and auditing brand safety. The features cover both account-level decisions about demand and blocking and the code required to implement those decisions.

Ask AdMob is a conversational agent built with Gemini that will reside in publishers’ AdMob accounts. Google describes it as working like an AdMob product expert, trained on monetization best practices, with a troubleshooting hub that will flag setup anomalies and software development kit (SDK) integration issues. Its visibility into how ads load is intended to support recommendations for problems affecting revenue.

For optimization, Google says the assistant will use peer benchmarks to recommend adding SDK bidders it identifies as high-value, with predicted revenue uplifts, and help optimize mediation groups. It will also check third-party network configurations for typos and floor-price discrepancies before they affect yield.

Publishers will additionally be able to configure custom brand-safety rules with natural-language prompts in the AdMob interface instead of manually managing blocklists. Google will begin testing Ask AdMob’s capabilities over the next few months and plans more advanced features that act on a user’s behalf later in 2027. Every recommendation will remain subject to user review and approval before it is applied.

Agent skills are available with the announcement for implementation work. They give a developer’s preferred AI agent pre-built, tested playbooks to build, edit and validate ad code, including migration to the Google Mobile Ads Next-Gen SDK, validation of an SDK implementation and implementation of new ad formats. Google is positioning that workflow as an alternative to clicking through dashboards, copying ad-unit IDs and maintaining ad configuration files outside the codebase.

Google’s developer documentation describes the skills as portable, self-contained modules containing SDK knowledge, instructions and workflows, intended to help AI assistants perform complex tasks more accurately. They work with assistants that support skills, including Antigravity, Claude Code, Codex and Cursor. Installation uses npx skills add google/skills/skills/ads, while npx skills update --all updates the installed skills.

The Android documentation lists six skills: initial SDK setup, banner ads, full-screen interstitial ads, rewarded ads and their reward callbacks, migration from the legacy Google Mobile Ads SDK, and integration validation. The validation skill covers pre-launch audits and checks for common implementation errors, while the setup skill includes setting the application ID and initializing the Next-Gen SDK.

The upcoming AdMob MCP server would connect developer environments directly to AdMob, expanding an agent’s role from code assistance to account operations. Google says agents will be able to read account data, create ad units and manage mediation settings, and that the server will expand to more developers in early 2027 after early testing. Developers will review and approve updates before pushing them to production.

Google also plans a broader wave of in-product and AI-agent capabilities throughout 2027 to handle more routine operational work. That makes the approval workflow central to the product strategy: as agents gain access to code and monetization settings, the open question is whether publishers can review suggested changes thoroughly without recreating the manual workload the tools are meant to remove.

OpenAI to Award 50,000 ChatGPT Plus Grants to Researchers

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On November 16, 2026, OpenAI announced that it will award 50,000 new ChatGPT for Academic Researchers grants, with applications opening November 23, 2026. Each grant provides free ChatGPT Plus access for a recipient and up to two collaborators, covering as many as 150,000 researchers this round.

Selection will occur on a first-come, first-served basis among eligible applications, replacing the pilot’s lottery. Qualified researchers already on the waitlist will receive activation instructions by email before applications open. OpenAI’s overall goal is to reach 200,000 researchers, twice the target set when the pilot launched in July 2026.

The pilot has enrolled 20,000 researchers across 33 countries. Pilot participants received ChatGPT Pro. OpenAI said its usage review found that most participants would have remained within Plus limits, and it attributed the switch to Plus for new grants to its aim of supporting more research teams.

Program participants can use OpenAI’s frontier models and receive training on using ChatGPT in research. Data shared through the program is not used to train OpenAI models by default.

Pilot projects illustrate different research uses. Shuqi Li of Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Harvard Medical School is exploring whether ChatGPT can connect laboratory and patient data to accelerate antibiotic development for tuberculosis. At the Florida Museum of Natural History, Arthur Porto and his team use AI tools to turn specimen photographs into searchable databases. They also created a 3D butterfly model whose wings change color as it is turned, like the real specimen.

A companion study from OpenAI’s Economic Research team, Science at the Frontier: How Scientists Use ChatGPT, analyzed 580,186 scientific messages from 1,819 scientists between August 20 and September 22, 2026, alongside a survey of 251 participants conducted from September 30 to October 7, 2026. To protect privacy, automated classifiers sorted messages by research activity; the research team saw the categories but did not read the underlying messages or ChatGPT’s responses. Activity shares were calculated for each scientist and then averaged, giving every scientist equal weight.

The study reports that reading and learning accounted for an average 28% of scientific messages and writing and communicating 27%, followed by analyzing evidence and theories at 17% and writing research software at 14%. Sixty-eight percent of scientists used ChatGPT to develop research ideas, defined as sending at least three ideation messages during the study. Among ideation messages, 36% involved a scientist bringing an idea for ChatGPT to refine, 35% began with a scientist’s specific question, and 23% asked ChatGPT to suggest a question.

The study also measured follow-up requests to clarify, check, or revise ChatGPT’s responses. Average follow-up rates were highest for writing and communicating at 38%, followed by writing research software and analyzing evidence and theories at 34% each. The metric captures a follow-up in the next scientific message in the same conversation within seven days.

Surveyed participants estimated that completing the same work at the same quality without AI would have taken 1.9 times as long. That figure is self-reported. Ninety-eight percent of the 250 scientists answering a separate discovery-timeline question expected AI to accelerate discovery in their field. Those responses describe expectations rather than observed discovery gains.

OpenAI said the findings do not establish causal effects and that determining whether these expectations translate into faster discovery will require more research. The study also identifies funding, staffing, and experimental capacity as constraints on testing ideas. The expansion therefore pairs broader access with an unresolved scientific question: the pilot documents how AI fits into research work, while the larger program’s impact depends on researchers’ ability to test AI-assisted ideas and validate any resulting findings.

Using AI for listings, where marketing ends and misrepresentation begins

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As AI speeds up listing copy and outreach, fewer checkpoints can increase compliance, disclosure, and fair housing risk. The column argues for training, tool controls and meaningful human review.  AI Summary

Over the years, I have seen too many six-figure lawsuits over the words ‘fully-renovated.’

In real estate, a single, seemingly innocuous word or phrase can carry significant legal ramifications and consequences. Descriptions with words including “fully renovated,” “brand new,” “resolved” and “permitted” can and too often do cause years of unexpected, costly litigation for buyers, sellers and even their respective agents more often than people may think. Each word can change a buyer’s understanding of a property and potentially expose a seller, agent, and/or brokerage to liability if the property description no longer matches the reality of the property’s true condition.

By modifying agent and seller property descriptions, AI has inadvertently and undeniably introduced this new layer of exposure and risk to the way agents and their clients do business.

According to the National Association of Realtors 2026 Technology Report, nearly half of Realtors surveyed use AI daily or weekly. Among those agents surveyed who use AI, 75% use it to write listing descriptions and more than half use it for social media posts, emails, or follow-up communications. Other industry research shows that AI adoption may be moving even faster. A 2026 Delta Media survey found that 82% of real estate agents asked use AI to write listing descriptions, which is up from 58% in 2024.

Creating consumer-facing content is the most common use of AI in our industry, helping to craft the words consumers see when considering one of the most important decisions of their lives. At the same time, the biggest challenges brought by AI appear to be growing far more rapidly than the policies and oversight needed to control them.

Surely, most sellers or agents are not deliberately leveraging AI-generated property descriptions to misrepresent properties. The more likely outcome is that an agent will provide accurate information into an AI tool to create a more compelling description, and in turn, the AI tool changes the description to one that is less accurate, thereby exposing an agent and seller to unexpected pitfalls.

For instance, rulings in lawsuits can depend on specific legal issues concerning whether a property is represented, whether by an agent or AI, as partially or fully renovated  or whether a defect was described as repaired or permanently resolved. Lawsuits pertaining to undisclosed permitting issues often concern whether work awaiting approval is described as being in the “final stages of permitting.” The aforementioned are only a few of examples of obstacles an agent or seller may encounter and such compliance issues are challenging companies across industries more often than ever before

The pitfalls of AI are not limited to the sale of real estate. AI has rapidly morphed from an experimental marketing tool to common resource for many areas of business, but especially marketing. Teams use it for everything from drafting copy to generating or cleaning up images to analyzing customer behavior. Work that used to take hours can be done in minutes, if not faster.

Marketing across industries relies on speed that the AI gods are now supercharging. Through their use of artificial intelligence, marketing teams can create dozens of variations of print or digital ads, internal or external emails, social media posts and listing descriptions. The speed at which content is being created naturally removes some of the checkpoints that were in place when it took longer to create or more people were involved in the process.

However, increased efficiency and speed often translate to increased exposure to risk and liability.

A listing description does not have to be completely fabricated to cause a problem. It can come down to the embellishment of one or two words.

Outside of real estate, AI-generated marketing can introduce unsupported claims, make definitive statements from what was originally nuanced, or alter the meaning of disclosures. Without the necessary checkpoints, it can lead to tremendous legal issues.

Accordingly, companies and their employees must take seriously the need for more extensive oversight to stay compliant.

The reason real estate is a great example of how AI can impact compliance is the industry’s significant regulatory requirements, including fair housing laws.

For example, if an agent uses AI to make a listing description more appealing to a certain buyer profile, the language used could potentially implicate protected characteristics or discourage certain groups from being interested in the property without the agent even realizing it.

Even if the agent did not intend to be discriminatory, it is still a compliance issue.

Fortunately, most real estate professionals recognize and consider the risks associated with AI use. A 2026 Realtors Property Resource survey found that 92% of agents surveyed were using or planning to use AI, while 63% identified accuracy of AI-generated content as their top concern.

Industries like financial services, insurance, healthcare, employment, and other highly regulated fields face their own requirements governing advertising, disclosures, accessibility, privacy, and discrimination.

A common answer to how to lower AI risk is to keep a “human in the loop.” If your strategy includes having someone simply click “approve” before AI-generated content goes out, that’s not enough. Reviewers need to know what they are looking for and the potential risks this content creates, such as unsupported claims, industry-specific advertising rules, and whether it misleads consumers. In addition, has confidential or proprietary information been entered into an AI platform to create the content?

The same principle applies when employees or consumers turn to AI for legal, financial, or other professional advice. I find myself repeating one simple phrase constantly: “AI is not an attorney.”

AI can certainly be useful to organize information, generate ideas, and streamline work processes. But it can never replace professional judgment.

A glaring problematic approach taken by many businesses these days appears to be that they are thinking of AI oversight backwards: providing employees free rein to use it and then creating a policy only after something goes wrong.

Instead, real estate professionals must be armed with tools, policies, and guidelines, and practical guardrails now, before AI continues to be utilized further.

At minimum, businesses should identify which AI tools employees are approved to use, what information can and cannot be entered into them, when AI-generated content requires additional review, and an approval and compliance review for any public-facing content.

The level of oversight must reflect the level of risk. Generating ideas for an article headline is a lot different than using AI to determine which consumers should receive a certain ad. Summarizing notes from an internal brainstorming meeting is not the same as synthesizing confidential client information to create a strategy.

Training is just as important as policy, and it is not enough to send out a document once via email announcing how employees are expected to use AI in the workplace.

The companies that manage AI most effectively will be those where legal and marketing work collaboratively.

Legal and compliance teams need to understand marketing workflows and how AI is being used so they can create guardrails. Marketing needs to involve the legal teams early enough in the process so it’s not an afterthought right before launch.

With the way AI is accelerating work, things are happening too quickly for either to operate in a silo.

We are not trying to slow innovation. We are working to create an environment where teams can move quickly because there are established boundaries and protections to keep risk as low as possible.

AI is going to continue to transform marketing, and businesses should be able to leverage this tool while remaining compliant. At the end of the day, the technology may be new, but accountability is not.

Spider-bear? Black cub wins the 2026 Cutest Climber Award.

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 16, 2026 12:15 PM EST

They may be small, but bear cubs have a pretty steep learning curve. In Alaska, brown bear (Ursus americanus) cubs have a roughly 50 percent survival rate within their first year, while the black bear (Ursus americanus) cub survival rate can range from 51 to 88 percent, depending on where they live. Cubs must learn how to fish, forage, find shelter, and climb trees to escape predators. Fortunately, biology helps out a bit with climbing. Bears have claws that curve and help them go up the tree and then back down safely.

One baby black bear who spent time at Anan Creek in southeastern Alaska’s Tongass National Forest showed off those climbing chops in style. The park’s rangers have officially awarded this bear with the 2026 Cutest Climber Award.

“This little cub slipped on his way down but caught himself and hustled right after mom,” the rangers write. “An adorable reminder that even when you lose your footing, you can recover and keep going. A tiny, furry lesson in resilience provided by the bears of Anan.”

Tongass National Forest’s rangers  are currently awarding the Anan Bear Awards to the 39 bears spotted at the Anan Wildlife Observatory in Alaska over the summer.

Tongass is the largest national forest in the United States, and covers most of southeastern Alaska, including the state’s capital city of Juneau. The area includes Mendanhall Glacier (a popular destination for tourists on Alaskan cruises), numerous hiking trails, eagles soaring above, and plenty of bears on the hunt for food. One of the hotspots is Anan Creek, home to one of the largest runs of pink salmon in Southeast Alaska. As such, the creek is a very popular dinner spot among black and brown bears.

According to Friends of Anan, 39 bears are known to feast on the salmon at Anan Creek. The population includes 31 black bears and eight brown bears. Similar to  their chunky counterparts farther to the north in Katmai National Park, the bears return each summer to feast on salmon and teach their cubs how to fish in order to survive. And also like the fat bears of Katmai, every bear here has their own habits and quirks.

The U.S. Forest Service tracks and documents every bear spotted at Anan, while explore.org’s cameras broadcast live streams of their antics.

FHA’s MMI Fund stays 5x above floor, fueling calls to cut insurance premiums

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

FHA’s 2026 Annual Report to Congress shows the Mutual Mortgage Insurance Fund’s capital ratio at 10.29%, down from 11.47% in 2025 but still far above the 2% statutory minimum for the 12th straight year. Economic net worth slipped to $182.6 billion while capital resources rose to $148.9 billion.   AI Summary

The Federal Housing Administration (FHA)’s Mutual Mortgage Insurance (MMI) Fund posted a strong financial performance in fiscal year 2026, even as its capital ratio declined from 2025 levels, renewing industry pressure to cut annual mortgage insurance premiums amid affordability concerns.

The MMI Fund had a capital reserve ratio of 10.29% as of Sept. 30, down from 11.47% a year earlier, according to FHA’s Annual Report to Congress released Monday. It’s the 12th straight year the fund has remained above its 2% statutory minimum.

“Over the past year, the MMI Fund remained strong despite a year-over-year decline,” the report states. “Both the Single Family forward and Home Equity Conversation Mortgage (HECM) programs have separately maintained capital levels above the 2% ratio for the last six years.”

The fund’s economic net worth ended FY 2026 at $182.6 billion, a $6.3 billion decrease from 2025. Total capital resources, however, increased by $9.3 billion to $148.9 billion, signaling continued strength in FHA’s insurance capacity even as modeled future performance softened.

Scott Turner, secretary of the Department of Housing and Urban Development, wrote in the report that under the Trump administration, HUD has continued “our work to streamline and deregulate FHA Single Family programs, while remaining vigilant stewards of taxpayer resources by eradicating fraud, waste, and abuse within our programs.”

Joseph Gormley, assistant secretary for housing and president of Ginnie Mae, wrote that the administration has eliminated roughly 150 “burdensome and unnecessary” policies, implemented alternative credit scoring models and updated FHA’s appraisal technology submission processes.

Gormley added that HUD has proposed a reform to the partial claim process through a Reinstatement Advance Payment, “which we intend to implement in Fiscal Year 2027.”

In a recent interview with HousingWire, Gormley said the administration is “comfortable” with the fund’s capital levels, signaling no immediate changes to FHA premiums or broader pricing. But the fund’s continued strength is fueling fresh calls from the mortgage industry to reduce FHA’s annual mortgage insurance premiums and ease monthly payments.

Bob Broeksmit, president and CEO of the Mortgage Bankers Association (MBA), said in a statement after the report’s release that the 2026 results create room for “targeted changes that improve affordability for borrowers.”

“FHA should modestly reduce annual mortgage insurance premiums, lowering monthly payments for moderate- and lower-income homebuyers,” Broeksmit said. “Given the slowdown in the housing market, any MIP reduction should be carefully calibrated and paired with additional safeguards to limit multiple layered risk factors on individual loans and protect the fund’s long-term health.”’

The Community Home Lenders of America (CHLA) also weighed in on the report, urging the FHA to end its life-of-loan policies on mortgage insurance.

“FHA Life of Loan premiums were intended as a stop-gap fee hike in 2013 to respond to financial concerns resulting from the 2008 Housing Crisis.” the group said in a statement. “With today’s MMIF report showing a long-term 12 year run of solid financial performance and bulging FHA reserve levels, it is long past time to stop overcharging homeowners for FHA loans, in order to help them in asset building.”

Within the overall fund, the single-family forward loan program reported a standalone capital ratio of 10.47% in FY 2026, compared with 10.95% in 2025. HUD attributed part of the decline to the growing share of loans with reduced annual MIPs following a premium cut in 2023, as well as slower home price appreciation and updated macroeconomic forecasts that increased projected losses.

The reverse mortgage book saw a much sharper move. The standalone capital ratio for the HECM program fell to 5.43% in 2026, down from 24.06% in 2025. HUD noted that HECMs, which account for less than 4% of the MMI Fund, are more sensitive to home price movements. Meanwhile, loan payoffs — viewed as the most favorable recovery outcome for HECMs — have become less common.

“Lower actual and forecast loan payoffs increased losses,” the report stated. “Finally, the HECM program spent substantial capital resources on assignment claims in FY 2026, reducing available cash.”

Overall, in FY 2026, FHA insured more than 935,000 single-family home mortgages, 83% of which were used by first-time homebuyers. FHA also endorsed more than 24,000 reverse mortgages through the HECM program.

As of Sept. 30, the agency managed active insurance on more than 8.4 million forward mortgages with $1.7 trillion in unpaid principal balance and $63.5 billion in outstanding HECM obligations.

The Permanente Medical Group announced on Monday the launch of a physician-led innovation company it said will strike technology partnerships to co-develop, incubate and launch new enterprise healthcare delivery technologies.

Permanentech—a wholly owned subsidiary of the 10,000-physician medical group, which is itself part of the massive integrated nonprofit Kaiser Permanente—looks to rapidly advance potential new healthcare offerings informed by its frontline physicians and the broader organization’s focus on value-based care.

It marked the launch by announcing its first portfolio company: Wrapt Health, which offers an automated tool that predicts likely missed medical appointments for early outreach and rescheduling. In line with Permanentech’s broader thesis, it was born from a TPMG physician’s experiences, validated within the medical group and so far scaled to 22 sites in Kaiser Permanente’s Northern California region.

“Our clinicians are continuously innovating and refining how we deliver care,” Kenneth Trauner, M.D., CEO of Permanentech and chief innovation officer at TPMG, said in a release. “Permanentech gives them a dedicated resource to rapidly turn their insights and creativity into scalable solutions that benefit both our patients and the wider health care community.”

Permanentech will be focused on technologies and AI-enabled tools addressing patient service, access and quality, TPMG said, with successful products also having the potential to improve “the economics of care delivery.” The business is structured “to move at the pace of the technology industry,” but has “a clear advantage” via its access to TPMG and Kaiser Permanente’s substantial footprint for deployment and validation, it said.

Those efforts will involve both home-grown tools and those developed by established or early-stage partners.

The former will champion physician-developed tools created inside TPMG’s ecosystem as an independent company launches. Starting from the concept phase, that support would include financing as well as clinical and operational validation, as was the case with Wrapt Health.

“Our physicians and care teams see every day where technology can make care better,” Kristine Lee, M.D., an associate executive director and chief transformation officer at TPMG, said. “Permanentech gives them a real hand in building tools that lift administrative burden and give time back to patients. When clinicians help build the tools, those tools fit the way they actually work.”

Strategic partnerships and joint ventures, meanwhile, will focus on co-development, clinical valuation and scaled deployment. Permanentech said the latter will begin with local deployments that are expanded to other TPMG sites when solutions “prove their value,” and could include additional strategic support as a partner company looks to advance their offering beyond TPMG.

Why database touch plans fail real estate agents

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Despite 91% of buyers saying they would reuse or recommend their agent, NAR data shows most repeat clients hire differently, with only 18% returning to a prior agent. The article proposes replacing high volume touches with intentional conversations across a 150 person network.  AI Summary

Agents are consistently trained to do their touches. “Have you done all your touches?” “Have you done your outreach?” “Are you touching people?” “Are you sending things of value?” That antiquated philosophy needs to stop and almost nobody in this industry cares to address it or examine why it fails. It is wildly ineffective, and I would argue that most of the time it hurts the agent’s brand rather than helping it.

If I walked up to your front door 32 times a year, rang the bell, and said, “Just a reminder that I’m in real estate,” how long would it take you to look into a restraining order? Nobody would call that a relationship. Yet the same schedule applies when it arrives as an email one week and a postcard the next.

Agents are constantly told they need to remind people that they are in real estate. That is an interaction about what the agent cares about, not what the other person cares about. Trust isn’t built by constantly telling someone how amazing I am, why they need me, or why I’m relevant or important. That is blatant narcissism, and yet somehow we call it great top-of-mind marketing.

The National Association of Realtors’ 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers shows the problem. Ninety-one percent of buyers said they would use their agent again or recommend them. But among repeat buyers, only 18% went back to an agent they had worked with before, and among sellers the figure was 29%. The touches are supposed to close that gap.

The industry explains that clients forget, so its answer has always been more reminders—the rest of the survey points somewhere else. 80% of sellers contacted only one agent before hiring, and 66% hired someone referred to them or someone they had used before. Sellers are not comparing the agents who emailed or mailed them the most. They call the one person they trust. Seeing your name in their inbox every month does not make you that person.

One of the agents I coach has phenomenal email automation, with reminders and alerts set up for everyone they have ever worked with. By their own count, 90% of their business comes from less than 5% of the people in their database. If 90% of your business comes from less than 5% of those people, why are you digitally harassing the other 95%?

In 2003, researchers Russell Hill and Robin Dunbar measured how many people we keep up with by using the most ordinary list there is, the one for Christmas cards. Participants logged every card they sent and everyone in the household it went to. Networks averaged about 150 people, and participants stayed in touch weekly with the people they felt closest to.

A later study by Sam Roberts and Dunbar followed people for 18 months. Friendships lost closeness when contact dropped off, while family relationships were far less affected. Contact matters, and the touch plans are right about that much. They go wrong by treating a Christmas card to the whole database as the same act as staying close to 150. A mailing treats everyone alike, so the people who matter most get the same thing as the strangers, and the relationships that produce business fade.

Pick your 150. Write down the people you would be glad to call and stop when you reach that number. They will be past clients and friends whose lives you know something about. Dunbar’s later research found an inner circle of about 15 inside the 150. Give those 15 their own rhythm, a conversation with each of them every month, because in my experience that inner circle is where most referrals come from. A database you can’t keep up with starts to feel like a weight. A list of 150 is small enough that you notice when one of them is about to move.

Reconnect at four months. A conversation creates what I call mind share: the trust that comes from talking with someone. Name recognition never builds it, and without the next conversation it fades. In my coaching, I see it run low about three to four months after the last conversation. Once a week, scroll back about four months in your text messages, pick three people, and send something like “Hey, was just thinking about you. Hope all is well; would love to catch up.” Leave real estate out of it. Anyone in your text messages already has a relationship with you that a mailing list never will, so the message reaches someone who trusts you at the point where that trust is lowest. Keep it to three. Send 30, have 10 people answer and your afternoon is gone, which is the fastest way to make sure you never do it again.

If you lead agents, ask how many conversations they had with their 150 this month, not how many touches they made. That number tells you whose relationships are fading before the referrals stop. We keep overcomplicating this business by pushing agents to do more and more, which only creates overwhelm and makes them more likely to do less. We owe them better.

The United States will likely lose its measles elimination status, a major public health achievement that was secured 26 years ago.

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), the World Health Organization’s (WHO) regional office for the Americas, determined that measles has spread continuously in the U.S. for more than 12 months. This marks the end of the country’s measles elimination status, which it has held since 2000.

Researchers familiar with the discussions told KFF Health News that the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) determined this week that measles is once again spreading through sustained transmission within the country. PAHO is expected to make an official announcement on November 17.

PAHO’s review examined measles spread starting January 20, 2025, when the first person became ill in a large West Texas outbreak, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

CDC data show 5,412 confirmed measles cases this year as of November 12. That’s the highest annual total since 1991 and more than double the 2025 count of 2,289 cases.

Andi Shane, MD, chief of pediatric infectious diseases at Children’s Healthcare of Atlanta, called the U.S. losing measles elimination status “a public health alarm that we are not being effective and we’re not controlling measles as well.”

The measles virus is highly contagious. It spreads through the air when an infected person coughs or sneezes. People can catch it in a room up to two hours after an infected person has left, according to the CDC.

“There’s really been very little measles in the United States over the past 25 years until January 2025,” William Moss, MD, a professor of epidemiology at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, told Healthline.

Losing the status itself doesn’t change much about daily life for most Americans, Moss said. The concern is the measles outbreaks, hospitalizations and deaths behind the loss, he said.

He called the loss “a big red flag” after “enormous efforts over the past 26 years” to keep the status.

A country loses its elimination status when there is “uninterrupted circulation of the same virus genotype and lineage for 12 months or more,” according to PAHO. Shane explained it this way: “For the past 12 months in the United States, we’ve had ongoing circulation of the measles virus and person-to-person transmission is occurring.”

Canada lost its elimination status in November 2025, and the U.K. and five European countries lost theirs earlier in 2026.

The U.S. can regain the status. “Other countries have done that,” Moss said. Brazil, which lost its status in 2019, regained it in November 2024. Venezuela, which lost its status in 2018, regained it in 2023, according to PAHO.

Before the measles vaccine, measles infected an estimated 3 million to 4 million Americans each year, according to the CDC. Among reported cases, about 400 to 500 people died each year, 48,000 needed hospital care, and 1,000 developed encephalitis, or brain swelling.

Moss said elimination efforts “really intensified” after a large outbreak from 1989 to 1992. Part of that push was “really promoting school entry vaccination checks,” he said.

Every state and Washington, D.C., require children to be vaccinated against measles to attend school, though exemptions vary by state, according to the nonprofit health policy organization KFF.

“So since we’ve been below that for many, many months, that is also contributing to the loss of our elimination status,” she said.

“It takes two things to have a measles outbreak,” Moss said. A community needs low vaccine coverage, and someone with measles has to enter it and “start the chain of transmission,” he said.

About 96% of this year’s cases were among people who were unvaccinated or had unknown vaccination status, up from 93% in 2025, according to the CDC.

Moss pointed to the federal government. “We know what it takes to achieve and sustain elimination. We have the expertise. We have the resources,” he said.

He cited staff cuts at the CDC and said messaging from Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and President Donald Trump has undermined vaccine confidence. He called federal messaging on vaccines “really mixed and weak.”

Moss also criticized the President’s August 10 executive order calling for the MMR vaccine to be split into separate shots for each disease.

Babies under 12 months are too young for the vaccine. “So they depend on the protection of the people around them,” Shane said.

OpenAI executives and researchers framed computer use as the next step in healthcare AI, moving beyond copilots that draft or summarize information to agents that can actually navigate EHRs, payer portals and other applications on a user's behalf.
