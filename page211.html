Marvel Officially Announces New 'Angela' Series

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Marvel has announced a new series starring Angela, the forgotten daughter of Odin and sister of Thor and Loki. Originally a character in the Spawn universe, Marvel acquired the rights to the character in 2013, introducing her as an Asgardian raised in the forgotten realm of 'Heven.'

The daughter of Odin and Freyja, Angela (born 'Aldrif') was kidnapped by Angels and spirited away to the Tenth Realm, which was locked off from the Nine Realms of classic Thor lore. Thor and Loki later went on a mission to free her, with the character starring in her own solo comics as well as appearing in Guardians of the Galaxy and Strikeforce.

Angela was partially adapted in the MCU's Thor: Ragnarok, with elements of her backstory given to classic villain Hela. Now, Angela is getting her own series as Marvel takes the character back to basics.

Marvel's new ongoing Angela series will see the character descend on New York as a demon hunter, working on a bounty from Heven itself. Coming February 2026 from Wes Craig and Pere Pérez, the new series will see Angela face off against the demon lord Abraxis, seemingly sidelining past lore to give fans a perfect jumping-on point for the character.

Angela was last seen as a supporting character in Doctor Strange, helping the sorcerer during his time in Asgard. Now, she has a brand new mission as Marvel reveals:

Demons are running amok in New York City, and a new demon hunter has been assigned by Heven to hunt and destroy them - Angela. Are things not what they seem? What does the demon lord Abraxis want with the Big Apple? Why is Heven so concerned with Earth? Angela doesn’t really care but plans to wet her blade with evil blood.

Marvel has also shared covers by Kaare Andrews and Pérez, showcasing Angela's golden armor and razor-like ribbons. Her enemy in this series is the demonic Abraxis, who shouldn't be confused for Abraxas - a multiversal predator held at bay by the existence of Galactus.

It's possible that Angela will spin out of the newly launched Avengers Volume 10, in which Mephisto's scheming will seemingly unleash demons on the city.

Despite multiple solo series and a dedicated fanbase, Angela has never truly been integrated into Marvel's Earth-616, rarely interacting with heroes who aren't directly linked to Asgard. The new Angela series seems set to change that, basing her in Marvel's hero capital of New York and giving her a unique role as a dedicated demon hunter.

Part of Marvel's decision in publishing a new Angela series seems to be based on her appearance in Marvel Rivals. The hero shooter game has increased Angela's profile, as she appears alongside icons like Captain America and Hulk, familiarizing casual fans with her attitude and abilities.

Speaking to Marvel, editor Nick Lowe appears to confirm that the series is intended to cater to fans who know Angela as a character, but haven't followed her stories thus far, saying:

Is Angela Thor’s sister? Yes. Was she a Guardian of the Galaxy? Yes. Does she care about either of those? Hel no! Angela’s focused on her new assignment from Heven - rid New York City of the epidemic of demons currently plaguing it and you get to read all about it in her new ongoing series!

Angela is relatively unique in being a character who began outside Marvel but was then given a very personal connection to it, introduced into sprawling Asgardian lore. Writer Wes Craig emphasizes Angela's nature as a loner character and the challenges that poses, saying:

Writing my first Marvel series has been very cool. It's such a fun world to play in. Angela's an old school warrior. Stoic, no-nonsense, she doesn't suffer fools, doesn't have time for BS, and she's tough as nails. I love that kind of character. But that also makes her an interesting challenge: What would make someone like that show us her heart? And is there anything that would make her break? Over the next year, we're gonna find out...

Artist Pere Pérez reveals a personal connection to Angela going back to her Spawn era, confirming there'll be plenty of classic fantasy combat:

I'm having a crazy amount of fun drawing this series. Wes and I are totally on the same page with the creative choices for Angela, and he is writing a very fun and layered story. One of the very first comics I bought with my own money was the original Angela miniseries from the ‘90s, so being able to add my own twists to her design has been very exciting. I love drawing monsters and demons, and I've been wanting to draw fight scenes involving weapons like... swords, axes, spears and medieval stuff, so expect some crazy action! This book is going to make you feel as if a heavy metal song is blasting in your brain!

Over the last decade, Angela has had some great stories in Marvel Comics, but has never felt like a true Marvel character. That's seemingly about to change as Angela begins a new mission in New York, hunting an army of demons and unavoidably crossing paths with some of Marvel's biggest heroes.

8 Neo-Western Shows That Define The Genre

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The western was one of the first TV genres to gain traction. Like cop shows and variety shows and classic situation comedies, the western is an enduring staple of the television landscape, and like those other genres, it never goes away; it just evolves. Back in the TV western’s heyday, old-school cowboy shows like Lancer and Wagon Train and The Virginian dominated the airwaves. But audiences eventually fell out of love with their tired tropes and rigid formulas and romanticized view of the ugliest period in American history, and the genre had to adapt with the times.

Now, the airwaves (or, rather, streaming-waves) are dominated by neo-westerns, subverting the conventions of those old classics. Neo-westerns can either bring the tropes of westerns into a modern-day setting, like Yellowstone and Justified, or they can do something else entirely, like the spacefaring adventures of Cowboy Bebop or the philosophical meditations of HBO’s Westworld.

Taylor Sheridan has singlehandedly revitalized the TV western with the Yellowstone saga. Yellowstone has proven to be so popular that it’s no longer just a hit show in its own right; it’s been expanded into a Marvel-sized cinematic universe of sequels, prequels, and spinoffs. We’ve seen different eras of the Dutton family, different corners of the Yellowstone universe, and different chapters of the overall timeline.

But the flagship show remains the most compelling. Yellowstone brings the land disputes of Shane and the complicated family dynamics of East of Eden and the horse-wrangling antics of The Cowboys into a modern setting. The show relaunched Kevin Costner’s career and made Sheridan the Shonda Rhimes of dad TV.

Walker, Texas Ranger ran for the better part of a decade, and its success had nothing to do with the subtlety of its storytelling or the profundity of its themes or the poignancy of its character development; it had everything to do with the irresistible star power of Chuck Norris. Norris was one of the greatest action heroes to take to the screen, and Walker, Texas Ranger was the perfect vehicle for his talents.

Viewers spent nine seasons tuning in to watch Norris’ U.S. Marine-turned-Texas Ranger/kung fu teacher Cordell Walker use his martial arts skills to bring bad guys to justice. Walker, Texas Ranger was the last classic TV western before Deadwood and Hell on Wheels would bring a gritty realism to the genre, and it ran for more than 200 episodes.

Kurt Sutter’s creation of Sons of Anarchy was primarily influenced by Hamlet. It reimagines all the archetypes from William Shakespeare’s seminal play — the grieving son, the usurping stepdad, the morally gray mother — and filtered them through the lens of a contemporary California biker gang. Sons of Anarchy is essentially The Sopranos on wheels. It has all the crime family dynamics of The Sopranos, contrasting relatable family situations with life-or-death stakes, but with the rubber-burning thrills of hitting the open road atop a Harley.

While you could accurately describe it as Hamlet meets The Sopranos on wheels, Sons of Anarchy’s setting is very western-coded. Jax Teller and his fellow bikers are modern-day cowboys — on a steel horse, they ride — and all the chases along dusty roads and armed standoffs in the desert feel very analogous to classic TV westerns.

Michael Crichton’s original Westworld film was a pretty straightforward chase movie. The theme park full of robotic cowboys was just a novel setting for a relentless cat-and-mouse pursuit between Yul Brynner’s unstoppable gunslinger and Richard Benjamin’s frightened tourist. But Jonathan Nolan and Lisa Joy took the bones of that old B-movie and turned it into a complex, thought-provoking philosophical examination of artificial intelligence for HBO.

Westworld was poised to be HBO’s sci-fi alternative to Game of Thrones. Where Thrones had branched outside fantasy fans to attract mainstream viewers, Westworld hoped to do the same. Ultimately, it was much too complicated to have that kind of impact. But even just as a western fan, it was fun to see such a grand, cinematic show deconstruct the genre in such an experimental and esoteric way on such a huge, ambitious scale.

Adapted from Tony Hillerman’s Leaphorn & Chee books, Dark Winds essentially brings a neo-western edge to an old-school cop show. It combines elements of westerns, police noirs, and psychological thrillers in a chilling tale of three Navajo Tribal Police officers patrolling the Four Corners area in the American Southwest, circa the ‘70s.

It mixes the frontier justice of a western like Gunslinger with the mystery and intrigue of a neo-noir like Chinatown. Zahn McClarnon shines in the lead role of grizzled cop Joe Leaphorn, and his uneasy alliance with a border patrol agent and an undercover federal officer provides a compelling dramatic anchor for the series.

Cowboy Bebop was the gateway anime that opened a lot of western viewers’ eyes to the joys of Japanese animation in the late ‘90s. This sci-fi western noir takes all the familiar tropes of an old cowboy show — bounty hunters chasing bad guys, drifting from town to town, fighting crime in a lawless wasteland — and launches them to the edge of the universe.

In a future where the Solar System has been colonized, and the human race is more divided than ever, the crew of the Bebop balance their fledgling bounty-hunting career with their heartbreaking personal struggles. Every episode is a dazzling intergalactic adventure, but it’s also a deeply touching human drama about fascinating, layered, sympathetic characters. Cowboy Bebop paved the way for every TV space western from Firefly to The Mandalorian.

After playing a hardware salesman-turned-reluctant sheriff in HBO’s Deadwood, Timothy Olyphant took his gun belt and his cowboy boots and swaggered 130-ish years into the future to play a very different kind of TV gunslinger in Justified. Although it’s set in the modern day, Justified is more of a traditional western than Deadwood was. Deadwood was brutal and bloody and painstakingly historically accurate, but Justified is a lighthearted action series about a U.S. Marshal chasing fugitives across the American frontier.

Justified was based on an Elmore Leonard short story, “Fire in the Hole,” and the dialogue has that Leonard-lite, Tarantinoan clip to it. Olyphant shares an endlessly riveting cat-and-mouse dynamic with his on-screen arch-nemesis Walton Goggins. In its first season, Justified started out as a traditional case-of-the-week show, but it became a more serialized drama starting in season 2, and then it was off to the races.

Vince Gilligan changed the way a story could be told on television with Breaking Bad. David Chase had introduced the notion of the TV antihero in The Sopranos, but Gilligan took it a step further and used the long-form medium of television to chronicle a character’s gradual turn from hero to villain. Tony Soprano is the same character in the pilot episode that he is in the series finale, but Walter White slowly transforms from a timid suburban family man into a ruthless drug lord with plenty of blood on his hands, and Bryan Cranston played every step of that dramatic journey pitch-perfectly.

Gilligan told that story of moral downfall through the lens of a contemporary western. He set Breaking Bad on the dusty streets and sun-scorched deserts of Albuquerque; he put an armed standoff in every other episode; he even had Walt and his cohorts rob a train in the final season.

What are the limitations of eGPUs vs onboard graphics cards for laptops?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

An eGPU can bring desktop-class graphics to a laptop, but cables, bandwidth limits and extra costs can eat into the advantage.

Connecting a desktop graphics card to your laptop seems like a neat solution to one of the oldest problems in portable computing. Use the thin machine for work and travel, and connect to a much more powerful GPU when you get home. An external GPU (eGPU) can do just that, but all that extra graphics power still has to travel through Thunderbolt or USB4 instead of the direct internal connection used by a built-in laptop GPU. That bandwidth bottleneck can eat into the desktop card's advantage, and the cable it's connected to holds sway as well.

In this comparison, "onboard" primarily refers to a dedicated GPU built into the laptop, not the integrated graphics in the laptop's processor. Even when a laptop GPU shares its name with a desktop GPU, the hardware can be vastly different. The RTX 5090 Laptop GPU features 10,496 CUDA cores, 24GB of GDDR7 memory, and a GPU subsystem power range of 95W to 150W. Meanwhile, you'll find 21,760 CUDA cores and 32GB of GDDR7 memory on NVIDIA's desktop RTX 5090. That's a significant gap before even considering bottlenecks, like Thunderbolt.

A modern eGPU typically communicates with the laptop via Thunderbolt or USB4, rather than directly on its PCIe bus, and that's where some of the desktop card's edge is lost. A built-in laptop GPU uses an internal PCIe connection instead.

Thunderbolt 4 has 40 Gbps of overall bandwidth and requires 32 Gbps of PCIe data throughput, while Thunderbolt 5 upgrades the base speed to 80 Gbps and doubles that PCIe requirement to 64 Gbps. Internal laptop GPUs can use wider multi-lane PCIe connections, depending on the laptop's design, giving them more room to move data without the Thunderbolt bottleneck.

That bottleneck is more noticeable when it comes to a powerful desktop card. NVIDIA's desktop RTX 5090 has 1,792GB/s of memory bandwidth and a 575W total graphics power rating, compared with 896GB/s and a 95W to 150W GPU subsystem power range for the RTX 5090 Laptop GPU. Those aren't PCIe bandwidth figures, but they show how much more raw hardware the desktop card brings before the external connection starts holding it back.

Thunderbolt 5 also has a 120 Gbps Bandwidth Boost mode, but that isn't 120 Gbps of PCIe bandwidth for the GPU. Intel says PCIe data throughput remains at 64 Gbps. While USB4 can also support PCIe traffic, a USB-C-shaped port alone doesn't guarantee eGPU compatibility. The host hardware needs the right support, and the enclosure, drivers, GPU and power requirements must all cooperate (because apparently one cable was getting too easy).

Using the laptop's built-in display can add another penalty because rendered frames may have to travel back through the same limited connection. This is why some brands suggest connecting an external monitor directly to the eGPU for better performance. There's no universal performance-loss percentage, since the GPU, laptop, connection, resolution and game all affect the result.

A laptop GPU begins with less raw hardware, but it doesn't have to deal with most of this plumbing. It operates within the laptop's cooling and power constraints, doesn't have to send rendered frames back over an external cable, and remains in the laptop when you pick it up. The portability benefit is more like a suggestion with a traditional desktop-card eGPU. You now have an enclosure, a desktop GPU, and maybe an ATX power supply on the desk, while a big card can add size and noise to the mix.

Let's not forget to talk about the cost associated with eGPUs. As an example, Razer's Thunderbolt 5 Core X V2 supports full-size NVIDIA and AMD desktop cards up to four slots wide. The enclosure also has its own 120mm cooling fan, but it doesn't come with a GPU or even the necessary ATX power supply to keep it running. Those would be added expenses on top of approximately $350 for the enclosure itself.

That math gets considerably friendlier if you have a suitable GPU sitting around after a desktop upgrade. At that point, the enclosure could significantly upgrade your laptop without buying another computer. It's more difficult to justify purchasing a high-end desktop card specifically for an eGPU, especially since you're paying for all that power and then sending it through a cable that might not allow you to use it all.

What A Road Sign With A Black 'Y' Means (And Other Letters, Too)

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

We have covered all manner of weird road signs on Jalopnik before, including some seriously goofy ones. While oddities from around the globe are fun to explore and explain, it is a little more relevant to touch on road signs that crop up on U.S. soil. Some of these will no doubt be familiar, but others may not, so there is good reason to delve into them and provide a quick explainer. The black "Y" sign was the one that initially piqued our interest, which, by the way, highlights that a Y-shaped intersection is to be found up ahead. That led us to look at other weird letter- and symbol-focused road signs drivers may come across.

The obvious candidates are "P" for parking areas and "H" that marks hospitals, which are universally understood, but throughout this article, we will also explore what those weird "T" or upturned "U" signs mean, in addition to winding "S" and plus-symbol signs. That includes where drivers are most likely to find them and how best to react when they do. Listed in no particular order, here are our picks of eight letter- or symbol-focused road signs that we thought are worth exploring in a bit more detail.

As already explained briefly above, a sign that depicts a capitalized "Y" is there to let drivers know of a Y-shaped three-way intersection or a forked road ahead. The sign will typically be a diamond shape with a yellow background, which means it's a simple warning sign alerting drivers that the road splits. The choices of where to go are obvious — either left or right — but what might be less obvious is how the right of way is determined in a three-way intersection that isn't controlled by any other signs.

The general rule is that drivers must yield to the car on the right. Then there's always the first-to-arrive rule, which works as you would intend — the first to arrive at the split goes first. Of course, some drivers will assume right of way when it is not deserved, or perhaps they just do not see the other car(s). In such instances, it's best to yield to the other vehicle even if you believe you should have the right of way to ensure everyone gets past the intersection without incident.

When a road splits like this, it pays to be especially cautious. Drivers may attempt to dart from one lane to the next when correcting a last-minute wrong decision, plus motorbikes or cyclists could be hiding in your blind spots, so try to ensure you have sufficient space around you if possible. The NHTSA highlights that roughly one third of all traffic fatalities occur at signposted intersections, so doubling down on cautiousness here is never a bad idea, no matter how simple it may first appear.

Surprise surprise, the letter "T" on a road sign warns of a T-shaped intersection — we would hope that you managed to work that one out. The road you are traveling on is about to end and merge with another. Once again, this is a classic warning sign finished in a yellow and black colorway, on a diamond-shaped sign that lets drivers know something's coming up and they will have to act accordingly.

In this instance, drivers must yield to traffic on the intersecting road — they don't have the right of way here — before heading either left or right. There will usually be a stop or yield sign prior to the intersection itself, and to avoid any frustrating bumps with other drivers making late decisions, it's best to get into the required lane early rather than last-minute. Trans Catalina Day 2: Views for Days

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Despite being a late sleeper at home, I always seem to wake up early on trail. Today, I wake up before 6 am, but I force myself to sleep until 6:30. My campsite is just under 2 miles from Airport in the Sky, Catalina’s small airport. It has a small shop and offers breakfast, but it doesn’t open until 8:30 am.

I take my time packing up, enjoying the cool morning temperatures, finally heading out around 8:15 am. I am the first person in camp to leave.

Almost immediately I encounter my first confirmed sighting of the Catalina Quail. Two walk down the road in front of me as I climb the road out of Black Jack. I see countless quails the rest of the hike, almost always in pairs. It seems it may be mating season!

The trail follows a road before heading sharply downhill on singletrack. There seems to be no one else out enjoying the cool morning hiking hours. It is beautiful, the sun glinting off the ocean and the dew.

The trail dives into a valley before climbing to the airport. The valley is lush and green, and I even encounter a small, stagnant stream at the bottom — remarkable given the dry reputation of the trail.

What goes down must go up, though. After reaching the bottom of the valley, the trail immediately twists up towards the airport. I could see the airport from the top of the valley before descending, and it looks barely closer. The sun has come out and is beating down, even this early. This side of the valley is more exposed, with no trees and minimal foliage. The descent actually had a few trees, but it’s clear this ascent is closer to what the rest of the day will look like.

Airport in the Sky taunting me as I climb toward it.

Breakfast “sandie” – eggs, bacon, cheddar, avocado on sourdough.

I sit on the porch and enjoy the chance cool down, rehydrate, and fuel up. More and more people arrive on planes and from trail as the morning continues. It seems less crowded if you arrive for breakfast than for lunch. The line is much longer when I leave an hour and half after arriving.

My only regret is that I never got to meet the airport’s resident black cat.

As I head out, the sun is blazing in the middle of the sky, no cloud in sight. Today’s hike is much shorter and easier than yesterday’s, but the temperature is much warmer. As I scope out my path ahead, I see few trees and lots of road walking. It’s going to be a warm afternoon.

The trail alternates between singletrack and dirt road. I spend a couple of miles cruising along the wide, shadeless road before diving back into the landscape, but the road walk is not unpleasant. The views are perpetual and the track is relatively quiet. I quickly realize, though, that with minimal tree coverage, bathroom breaks require creativity or a lack of modesty — or both. The hillside often steeply climbs away from the road, making it even more difficult.

A lovely blooming cactus flower from along the side of the trail.

As I leave the road for singletrack, I notice movement on the side of trail. A small lizard freezes as I approach. I’ve noticed two distinct types of small lizards, but this one is my favorite. It has rainbow coloring, appearing almost to shimmer in the sun. I believe it is the common side-blotched lizard. I have already seen quite a few of them, and will see many more before the end of my hike.

The side-blotched lizard, known for the male’s throat colors indicating their mating style.

I continue to dodge more lizards under foot as I climb up a crest, and immediately my eyes are drawn forward. The trail winds along the very top of ridge before beginning the descent into Little Harbor. It’s rare for me to experience a trail on a ridge like this, and it takes my breath away.

The ridge walk is lovely, but the constant sun starts to make me weary. The temperature keeps increasing, no clouds or shade breaks to be found. I stop to sip my electrolytes and re-apply sunblock. Just ahead, at the bottom of this hill, I see a semi-shaded bench at a trail junction. It becomes all I focus on, my entire motivation for the remaining ridge walk and descent. Get. To. Shade.

The palm trees come into view, making me both smile (camp is near!) and chuckle (palm trees are not native). Mostly, I am thrilled at the view of trees. Trees mean shade. Shade means relief.

Even so, I stop and take in the view. It really is a picturesque sight.

Once at the campground, I head for Campsite #10, which turns out to be at the very front — right by the beach. Talk about incredible luck. When I reserved it, I was not sure what I was getting. A giant palm tree provides much needed shade over a fire ring and picnic table. A stream inlet burbles past, while straight ahead is the harbor itself.

I spend the rest of the afternoon exploring the camp and the beach. There is a water spigot in the next camp over, just like last night — though this isn’t typical. A row of porta potties on the road just outside the campsites serve as the toilets. Not my favorite, but at least there is a hand washing station!

I run into some fellow hikers I met at the shaded bench earlier. With wide eyes, one asks me, “Did you see the bison as you walked into camp?” Surprised, I reply that I did not. Apparently, it was lounging under a tree next to the trail into camp and I walked right past it. It must have blended with the dirt, obscured in the shade.

Despite missing the bison sighting, I still see quite a few new creatures throughout the afternoon and evening. I encounter more more Catalina quail as I walk around – click here for the video: Bobbing Catalina Quail. I see several Brown Pelicans diving for food near the shore.

What I believe to be a brown pelican hunting in the slough next to my campsite.

The real gem of wildlife for the evening, though, is the Catalina Fox. I say hi to the Isabellas at their campsite, and as we chat, a fox scurries past just behind the campsite. It slows and looks longingly at the picnic table, clearly familiar with human food, before finally disappearing into the bushes.

The Catalina Fox, staring longingly at our food. It looks a little like a stray dog.

Little Harbor is absolutely lovely. After dinner, I wander onto the beach to enjoy the sunset. The temperatures have finally cooled down, thanks in part to the cool ocean breeze. I enjoy it while I can.

Tomorrow is the day I am most nervous about. The temperatures are supposed to be highest tomorrow, and the official trail has a brutally steep climb and descent with zero shade, which I will face in the full afternoon sun. Somehow, though, I don’t feel too daunted. I feel excited, rejuvenated by my lovely evening. I knew the trail was short, but tomorrow is my last full day on trail. I can’t believe how fast it has flown by.

I crawl into my tent early, ready to get an early start to beat the heat tomorrow. Perhaps tomorrow is the day for my bison sighting!

For those who enjoy watching hardened gunslingers shooting their way to revenge, there are plenty of worthwhile options that can be streamed for free. Tubi, a streaming service known for not requiring a paid subscription to watch its content, hosts a vast library of Western movies.

Vengeance is one of the most popular themes of Western films. It's the driving force of so many stories in the genre. Because of how common it can be, a long list of these Westerns proved to be rather forgettable, somewhat standard entries into the genre. Others, however, stood out from the crowd to join the ranks of the greatest Westerns of all time, a distinction earned by the likes of Unforgiven, Hang 'Em High, The Searchers, and For a Few Dollars More.

A few movies that belong in that same category can currently be streamed for free, thanks to Tubi. Beyond that, the streaming service also offers a number of lesser-known - but incredibly entertaining - Western movies that cast the spotlight on bitter gunslingers with a score to settle.

Released in 2003, Open Range stars Western icons Kevin Costner and Robert Duvall as aging ranchers who resist when a gang of thugs led by an unscrupulous cattle baron tries to bully them off the land in the Montana territory. Open Range starts out as a story of resistance against a powerful force in a Western town, but when the villains of the film ultimately take a step too far, it evolves into a brutal tale of revenge.

The mysterious past of Kevin Costner's character comes into play here, with his skills as a gunfighter being key to their efforts to fight back. Their determination to not only hold their own ground, but also get revenge for their fallen culminates in a strikingly gruesome grand finale. Unlike many of the Westerns of the previous era, Open Range pulls no punches when it comes to showing its characters dying in grisly fashion. But the violence is never gratitious; Open Range successfully presents itself as a dark but appropriately realistic take on life in the American Wild West.

With its gritty take on the Western formula and outstanding performances from its two lead actors, Open Range has a well-deserved reputation as one of the greatest Westerns of the 21st century thus far.

The Forgotten Pistolero is an underrated 1969 Spaghetti Western that places Leonard Mann in the role of Sebastian, a man who grew up with no knowledge of his true history. After getting dragged into a conflict with a pair of scheming criminals and their cronies, Sebastian learns the tragic details of his past, and the identies of those responsible. Teaming up with his friend, Rafael, Sebastian goes on a mission of revenge to get justice for his father and save his sister.

A big part of what makes The Forgotten Pistolero's revenge tale so interesting is the target of his crusade; Sebastian's own mother is one of the two main villains of the movie, setting up an emotional confrontation at the end of the story where the hero gets a chance to make her and her former lover pay for their crimes. If the plot sounds familiar, that's because The Forgotten Pistolero is essentially a loose adaptation of the Greek myth of Orestes, though done in the format of a Spaghetti Western. It's an unorthodox combination, but the concept makes for a compelling revenge film.

In 1971, Western heavyweight Burt Lancaster starred in Valdez is Coming. Lancaster plays a lawman named Valdez, who was deceived into killing someone in the movie's opening act. Shortly afterward, the man who perpetrated the deception used the power he holds in the town to have Valdez painfully humiliated and dragged out into the desert.

Unbeknownst to the villain, Burt Lancaster's Valdez is a seasoned sharpshooter, a background that is crucial to his plan of retaliation. Unwilling to forget what was done to him, Valdez meticulously hunts down his enemy's henchmen, one by one. Valdez's relentless killing spree continues until it builds into a dramatic showdown with his prime target.

Similar in some ways to Unforgiven, Valdez is Coming is a movie where the villains unwittingly invite disaster upon themselves by an old gunslinger they once deemed as a non-threat. Fear consumes them as Lancaster's character easily asserts control over the situation. He's on his own, but it's clearly the villains who have their backs against the wall.

For those who enjoy the one-man army aspect of both Western movies and modern-day revenge thrillers like John Wick, Valdez is Coming is a must-watch. And despite being in his late 50s at this point in his career, Burt Lancaster is still as imposing as ever, demonstrating his worth as one of the Western genre's best leading actors across its entire history in Hollywood.

Two years into the legendary run of the celebrated Western TV series Gunsmoke, the show's star - James Arness - played the lead role in Gun the Man Down, a significantly darker film set in the same genre. Rather than keeping the peace and maintaining law in order, Arness plays an outlaw with a serious grudge on his mind, and one whose vicious methods go a bit beyond the norm.

Gun the Man Down delivers a gritty take on the Western revenge trope that is likely to please fans of movies like Hang 'Em High and High Plains Drifter. In the movie, Arness' character is a criminal whose cohorts betrayed him and essentially left him holding the bag. But rather than simply exact revenge on them following his release from prison, he decides to drag out the process by terrorizing them emotionally, adding an interesting, psychological horror element to the Western.

In 1959's Ride Lonesome, Randolph Scott plays a bounty hunter named Ben Brigade, who captures a criminal (James Best) and sets out to deliver him to justice. But his efforts actually hide an ulterior motive; his real goal is to force out of hiding his prisoner's parter-in-crime, who is responsible for the death of Brigade's wife years earlier.

Ride Lonesome boasts a surprising amount of star power; in addition to having Randolph Scott in the lead role, the 1959 Western also includes bad guy roles for two of the Western genre's best villain actors in Lee Van Cleef and James Coburn, and naturally, neither disappoints. The sinister nature of Van Cleef's character only make Randolph Scott's hero even easier to root for.

Ride Lonesome is easily one of Randolph Scott's best Westerns, and that's saying a lot when taking into consideration that he was one of the most prolific and most popular actors associated with in the genre during the 1940s and 1950s.

Ewan McGregor’s ‘Jurassic Park’ Replacement Is Officially Taking Over Streaming

It's difficult to imagine a busier Hollywood star in 2026 than Anne Hathaway. Not only is she expecting her third child with her husband, Adam Shulman, and has been lined up to star alongside Tom Cruise in Day of Thunder 2, but she has also been part of an incredible five of the year's big-screen hits. 2026 began with Hathaway's quietest release, as she starred in the criminally underseen Mother Mary from A24, which has since become a streaming hit.

Then came one of her most anticipated performances of the year, as she rejoined Meryl Streep, Stanley Tucci, and Emily Blunt for The Devil Wears Prada 2, which was called "near-perfect" by Collider's own Taylor Gates in a glowing review. The $693 million global haul of The Devil Wears Prada 2 pales in comparison to Hathaway's next project, which ranks as her highest-grossing of all time. She starred as Penelope in Christopher Nolan's $1.8 billion-earning historical epic The Odyssey, stealing scenes and helping provide an emotional anchor for Matt Damon's King of Ithaca.

Any film released after The Odyssey was always likely to struggle under the shadow of its predecessor. This is what happened to Hathaway's next release of 2026, The End of Oak Street, as she joined Star Wars alum Ewan McGregor as their quiet suburban life is inexplicably transported into the jaws of prehistoric danger. Sadly, The End of Oak Street earned just $120 million against a reported budget of $85 million. However, since moving to streaming, it's been able to step out of The Odyssey's shadow. At the time of writing, The End of Oak Street ranks as one of the most-watched movies on Amazon in the world.

Following The End of Oak Street, Hathaway wrapped up a congested 2026 with the release of Verity, the latest in a quick run of Colleen Hoover adaptations, following It Ends with Us, Regretting You, and Reminders of Him. Although the film was accused of being "afraid to go too campy, creepy, or even overtly erotic with its visual styling and script choices," in a review for Collider by Carly Lane, Hoover fans supported it in the millions and turned Verity into a box office hit.
