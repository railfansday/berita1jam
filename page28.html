How One Designer Brought a Discerning Eye to Her Deeply Personal Manhattan Apartment

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ask an interior designer about their most difficult client, and there’s a good chance they’ll name themselves. Dani Dennis spent more than a decade at Mark Cunningham Inc. before founding her own New York–based practice, Danielette Dennis Interiors, translating other people’s tastes into rooms. “I’m such a decisive person,” she says of her approach to projects ranging from a traditional Brooklyn Heights townhouse to a sleek Park Avenue pied-à-terre. But when it came to decorating her own Gramercy Park apartment that she shares with her husband and two daughters, Dennis had no client brief to answer to—and no one to make the final call but herself.

When the family moved in three years ago, the apartment had plenty going for it: tall windows, handsome moldings, and an airy living and dining area. Dennis left the architecture alone, putting furniture, textiles, and her growing art collection to work instead. Benjamin Moore’s Steam keeps the walls a warm white, though the rooms are anything but hands-off. “I almost wanted it to feel like a gallery,” she says. “But, obviously, it’s lived in, and I have kids, so nothing is too precious.”

A portrait by Patrick Eugène, sourced via Mariane Ibrahim, anchors one end of the living room. Nearby are an Alfred Grenander chair upholstered in Toyine Sellers fabric, a black mushroom side table by Emma Donnersberg, and Christian Woo’s Noga Cube side table.

A portrait of a young girl by Jerrell Gibbs hangs near a Pierre Augustin Rose Cadillac ottoman upholstered in Rosemary Hallgarten’s alpaca bouclé. Vintage Saint-Louis crystal, sourced at the Paris flea market, sits atop the credenza.

The living room’s opposing sofas encourage conversation. The sofa (right), upholstered in Pierre Frey wool and mohair, is by Marked NYC. The ceiling fixture is a reedition of Pierre Chareau’s Quart de Rond lamp, and the rug is by Scott Group Studio.

Joseph-André Motte chairs surround the dining table, while a 1960s Italian burlwood credenza from Naga Antiques sits beneath an abstract painting by Carmen Neely. The dining rug is by Crosby Street Studios.

A first-generation American of Liberian heritage, Dennis has built a collection of art that includes contemporary Black artists from across the diaspora. A painting of girls playing in a circle takes her back to her school days in Côte d’Ivoire. Others show Black women in moments of rest and repose. “I want my kids and my family to be surrounded by reflections of us,” she says. Her clients’ collections tell their own stories; this one tells hers. Black joy runs through the collection, but Dennis doesn’t shop by theme; she listens to her gut. “I buy what I love,” she says. “I don’t buy for decoration.”

Dennis is equally at ease with an English-country chintz and a graphic geometric. In her own home, she borrows a little from everywhere, favoring clean lines and unexpected combinations. A custom sofa in Sanderson velvet faces a curved Marked NYC design in Pierre Frey wool and mohair. Camel-colored lounge chairs play against the sharp geometry of a Roger Sprunger coffee table; a 1906 Josef Hoffmann side table and an early-20th-century Alfred Grenander chair join the mix. “When the harmony comes from shape and proportion and feeling, I think that’s what makes spaces dynamic,” she says.

An expressive Ferrari Sheppard painting, acquired through Massimo De Carlo in Milan, hangs above a custom sofa upholstered in Sanderson’s indigo Quartz velvet. Camel-colored chairs wear Holland & Sherry fabric. A glass-and-metal coffee table by Roger Sprunger joins a modernist stool by Charles Vandenhove and JM Szymanski’s slender Table No. 3.

Dennis took a moodier approach in the library, where the family gathers. The walls and ceiling are painted the same dark hue, creating a cozy retreat for her two daughters, who prefer sprawling on the soft rug to sitting on the sofa. Artwork includes a floral painting by Alexandria Tarver and Hazel Mphande’s Stone or Lavender (2021). The striped pillows are in Fortuny fabric.

The dining table seats 10. Dinner guests, however, are still forthcoming. “I haven't had people over yet, because in my head it’s not done,” Dennis says. Joseph-André Motte chairs surround the table, while a 1960s Italian burlwood credenza and a Crosby Street Studios rug anchor the dining area. The living room sofas were positioned for entertaining too, although the family’s day-to-day use of the space is less formal. For Dennis’s daughters, 10 and seven, the mohair-and-wool sofa comes with ground rules: “You guys can sit on it, you can perch, but if we want to play, let’s do it elsewhere.”

The library, on the other hand, is fair game. The girls can sprawl out with a book or watch TV, though they rarely bother with the sofa. “They’re always lying on the rug,” Dennis says. She painted the walls a deep olive-green, carrying it across the ceiling for an enveloping effect. The art also takes a moodier turn, with a floral painting by Alexandria Tarver and a black-and-white self-portrait by Hazel Mphande. The primary bedroom, meanwhile, provides a brighter, quieter escape. “I always wanted to be in a cloud,” the designer says of the neutral palette. The upholstered bed, 1970s steel bench in alpaca wool, and leather-bound nightstands supply the texture.

Since the photo shoot, Dennis has switched out accessories and rearranged objects. When she recently swapped a lamp in the living room, even her husband took notice. “Another one?” he asked. At work, Dennis knows when to call a room finished. At home, she’s beginning to wonder why she should.

“I always wanted to be in a cloud,” Dennis says of the primary bedroom, where texture takes precedence over color. A vintage 1970s steel bench with alpaca upholstery sits at the foot of the bed, while leather-clad nightstands add another tactile element. The ceramic lamp is by Christiane Perrochon, and the understated artwork above the bed is by Carmen Neely.

Republican Senator Wants to Freeze AI Data Center Boom as Congress Pushes to Expand It

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Republican Senator Wants to Freeze AI Data Center Boom as Congress Pushes to Expand It

Republican Senator Josh Hawley wants Congress to hit the brakes on new data centers, just as lawmakers are moving forward with legislation intended to make building the infrastructure behind America's AI boom easier.

The Missouri senator called for a nationwide moratorium Wednesday, listing it among five priorities for Senate Republicans during the six weeks before Democrats take control of the chamber.

“Senate Republicans trying to figure out what to do in the next 6 weeks?” Hawley wrote on X. His answer included a moratorium on data centers, alongside defunding Planned Parenthood, suspending the gas tax and other Republican priorities.

A spokesperson told Politico that Hawley wants a federal pause until Congress passes a “bill of rights giving taxpayers guarantees of no rate increases.” Hawley has not presented detailed legislation specifying which projects would stop or how long the freeze would last.

Data centers are the warehouses packed with powerful computers that train and operate services such as ChatGPT. Building more AI systems means installing more servers, which requires additional electricity, cooling equipment and often upgrades to local power grids.

The scale helps explain the concern. U.S. data centers consumed an estimated 183 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, according to figures cited by Pew, and that could climb to 426 by 2030. In other words, their annual electricity demand could more than double in six years.

On Monday, the Senate voted 73-25 to advance debate on the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, a proposal backed by lawmakers from both parties and President Donald Trump's administration.

The bill would speed federal reviews for energy projects and transmission lines, helping bring new power supplies online. It would also require data center operators to pay the additional grid costs their projects create, rather than shifting those expenses onto ordinary electricity customers.

In practical terms, Congress is considering how to keep construction moving without making households subsidize it. Hawley is asking lawmakers to stop new construction until consumer protections are in place.

His position is not entirely new. In February, Hawley introduced the GRID Act with Democratic Senator Richard Blumenthal, seeking to make new data centers obtain electricity from their own sources rather than relying on existing grid supplies.

He now joins independent Senator Bernie Sanders and Democratic Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez in publicly supporting a national pause. Their moratorium proposal goes further, calling for rules addressing AI's effects on workers and public safety as well as the environment.

The politics have shifted quickly. In the November 3 elections, voters backed restrictions on data center development across the country, including local bans and measures requiring public approval before construction.

A Pew Research Center analysis published Wednesday found 60% of Americans uncomfortable with a new data center in their area, including 54% of Republicans and 68% of Democrats. Another survey found 58% disliked political leaders who support building data centers near them.

Opponents worry about rising bills, water consumption and noise. Cities have already imposed restrictions, while OpenAI recently promised to cover electricity and grid costs at future campuses it owns and operates.

Industry supporters see a bigger economic risk. BlackRock CEO Larry Fink warned last month that a construction moratorium could trigger a recession because so much investment now depends on AI infrastructure.

A federal construction freeze could delay the additional computing capacity that AI companies expect to bring online, along with the power infrastructure built to support it. Existing data centers would not necessarily shut down, but the scope of Hawley's proposed moratorium remains undefined.

Hawley's call does not freeze a single project, and he has not said whether he would support the Sanders bill. For now, the Senate is debating legislation to speed infrastructure approvals, not legislation to ban new data centers.

Zimmermann Brought the Aussie Party Spirit Back to New York

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

A Zimmermann night out out was long overdue in New York City. It’s been some six years since sisters Nicky and Simone put on a party for their label around these parts—and you could hear Kylie Minogue’s voice blasting from the speakers the moment you pulled up to Indochine on Lafayette Street.

“The Irish and the Australians, it’s a lethal mix,” said Eve Hewson with a mischievous laugh. The Disclosure Day actress, who has recently relocated to Los Angeles, was more than happy to accept the invite to fly into town for the night—particularly after attending her first Zimmermann runway outing during Paris Fashion Week last month (her seat mate from the show, Charlotte Lawrence, quickly ran up and grabbed her with glee upon arrival).

Speaking of showing up to support sisters, Hewson divulged that there could indeed be a season 3 of the cult-favorite Apple TV show Bad Sisters “if people kick up enough of a fuss” to bring it back for another round of murderous fun. In the meantime, after a year where she’s been booked and busy, she’s ready to “just sleep” once the holiday season rolls around.

Around the buzzing restaurant, well-dressed groups broke off into huddles. Naomi Watts and Tommy Dorfman linked hands as they tattled, loved-up models Vivienne Rohner and Henry Rank canoodled in a corner, and sisters Jemima and Quentin Jones showed off their synchronized sultry black frocks at the step-and-repeat. Watts’s photographer brother, Ben, reunited with his fellow Aussies, and their accents audibly cranked up a notch as they cracked jokes together. DJ Amrit Tietz, also flying the flag for Australia, had rushed back from her cool mom platform Spread the Jelly’s most recent “Jellymoon” brand trip upstate to man the decks. (Hence the Kylie.)

Uma Thurman, who’s gracing screens in the just-dropped season 2 of Dexter: Resurrection, arrived in an all-black ensemble from the Cruise 2027 collection. After a quip was made about the extreme height difference between the 5’11” actress and Nicky Zimmermann, Thurman discreetly slipped her high heels off—prompting peals of laughter from the designer. “I hate heels anyway—I only ever wear them for work,” Thurman said, smiling.

As guests found their seats at a long table dressed in dramatic bundles of burgundy anthuriums, magenta dahlias, and amaranthus, a lace-clad Lawrence surprised the room with a performance of her new music, as well as Fleetwood Mac’s “Dreams.” Once the chocolate truffles and mini fruit tartlets were served, everyone was well and truly warmed up for the dance floor as Tietz returned to the DJ booth. Safe to say, more than a few pairs of shoes were kicked off.

Harry Kane marks historic 126th England cap with a little reminder of his greatness

England 2-1 Croatia: Harry Kane celebrated his record-breaking night with a match-winner from the penalty spot to seal a place in the Nations League quarter-finals

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Harry Kane was asked this week to name the lowest moment of his 125 England caps, and his mind travelled back to that evening in Qatar when he sent a penalty over Hugo Lloris’s crossbar and up to the heavens. Kane being Kane, he would score the next 30 penalties of his career. “It made me improve,” he said. “A lot of the bad things have helped me get to where I am now.”

Perhaps it was apt, then, that on the night of his record-breaking 126th England cap, Kane scored his 92nd England goal from the penalty spot to settle this 2-1 win over Croatia and book a spot in the Nations League quarter-finals. It was a little display of his work ethic, a simple kick of the ball honed to a fine point through thousands of repetitions on the training ground.

Kane dinked his penalty down the middle and a diving Dominik Livakovic almost blocked the ball with his trail leg. The goalkeeper beat the ground in frustration as if to say, I knew you’d do that. But perhaps this is the point: since that World Cup miss against France, Kane has trained every penalty into his armoury – the stutter or the run, the blast or the dink, left or right or the space a goalkeeper leaves behind – and Livakovic couldn’t cover them all.

It came after a rare miss for Bayern Munich last month. “The next one is always important to bounce back,” Kane said. “I did my usual routine yesterday in training and looked at the goalkeeper. I haven’t gone down the middle with a penalty for maybe three or four years. With penalties, you have to keep things fresh, keep things moving. I thought it was a good time to do it, looking at his clips. Thankfully, it paid off today.”

What did we learn from this slightly underwhelming performance? England are better than Croatia, but then we saw that at the World Cup when England beat them 4-2, and more emphatically in Rijeka last month when England won 7-0. This was naturally a more cautious Croatia, against an altogether sloppier England who made a series of uncharacteristic errors all over the field.

Trent Alexander-Arnold gave the ball away on the edge of his own box, Lewis Hall played a fancy flick straight off the pitch and Jarell Quansah assisted Croatia’s equaliser with a deft, daft header inside his own six-yard box. Jude Bellingham showed frustration towards some of his teammates’ errors, yet his best game largely deserted him too.

The England fans who shivered through these 90 minutes might instead remember the few moments beforehand. The commemorations began with a giant Tifo behind one goal displaying England’s captain roaring into the night sky with the words “England’s finest” and “Kane 126” emblazoned top and bottom. Kane emerged from the tunnel with his four children as mascots, and then spent a couple of minutes waving around the Ballon d’Or before being presented with three gifts, like a scene from the nativity. The three wise men were played by Peter Shilton (gift one: a golden crest), Croatia captain Luka Modric (gift two: an old football), and Prince William (gift three: a framed shirt).

"It's been a crazy few weeks,” Kane said. “It takes a lot out of you emotionally, physically and mentally ... it’s a hard balance because you want to take it in. Breaking the record is a massive thing in the history of our game, seeing the tifo there, having my kids with me, and being presented by all the people was beyond dreams, so of course you have got to take it in. Once the whistle goes and you’re on the pitch, you just start focusing on what your job is.”

England started haphazardly but began to get a grip midway through the first half and it was Kane who unlocked Croatia’s defence with a raking pass to Bellingham arriving at the far post. Bellingham’s first-time cross found Morgan Rogers, who managed to impart just enough energy to the ball to bounce it home from three yards.

Kane had to wait. His first big chance came early in the second half when he received a cutback in the box from Declan Rice and a half-empty goal prepared to bulge, but Josip Stanisic wasn’t feeling sentimental and he lunged in front to block Kane’s goalbound shot.

Croatia drew level a few minutes later when Quansah took the extraordinary step of attempting a header back to Jordan Pickford inside a crowded penalty area. Pickford understandably wasn’t expecting the pass, and he was beaten to the ball by Petar Sucic who knocked it into the roof of England’s goal.

But Quansah went some way to making amends when his run drew some suspicious shirt pulling from Ivan Smolcic. Danish referee Morten Krogh missed it at first look but VAR intervened, and when Kane placed the ball down on the spot, the rest felt inevitable.

England now travel to Madrid to face Spain on Sunday, where victory would clinch top spot in the group. It would also serve as a sign of England’s standing in the game right now if they were to beat the world champions on their own turf, after a narrow but encouraging 3-2 defeat by Spain at Wembley in September.

Kane will earn cap number 127 and he is intent on gathering many more. Since winning the Ballon d’Or he has made his remaining career targets abundantly clear: scoring 100 England goals, winning the Champions League with Bayern Munich and winning a major trophy for England.

Whether the Nations League counts as “major” is debatable – Cristiano Ronaldo might say yes but just about everyone else says no – yet it presents an opportunity to end England’s 60-year wait for a men’s trophy. It would be fitting if Kane, now England’s greatest player by any measure, was the man to lift it.

Naz Reid landing spots: Four NBA contenders that make sense if Hornets look to move sharp-shooting big man

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

You could argue that before this season, there was not a single role player in the NBA more beloved by his own fanbase than Naz Reid. Simply uttering his name, devoid of context, became a popular meme among the Minnesota Timberwolves faithful in recent years, and for good reason. The former undrafted free agent grew into a Sixth Man of the Year winner and one of the best shooting bigs in basketball during his seven-year run with the Timberwolves. Leaving the only team he had ever known this offseason has proven difficult thus far.

Reid, 27, is posting big raw numbers in his first season as a full-time starter with the Charlotte Hornets, but there are bigger concerns under the surface. After shooting just under 57% on 2-pointers in Minnesota, he is at around 44% with the Hornets and struggling to score efficiently near the basket. His defense has raised eyebrows as well, and after he was benched in an early game against the Detroit Pistons, Hornets coach Charles Lee said that Reid "didn't meet our standards in the first half, and at halftime we made the decision that he wouldn't play in the second half."

Reid, along with an unprotected 2033 first-round pick, was one of the centerpieces of Charlotte's return in trading star guard LaMelo Ball to Minnesota. The 4-7 Hornets have struggled mightily without Ball, posting the NBA's worst offensive rating this season despite having the league's best offense from early December forward last season. Injuries have played a big part in those struggles, but at this stage, Reid's role as a foundational Hornets player moving forward is already coming into question. Timberwolves beat writer Jon Krawczynski of The Athletic even said on his podcast that the chances of Reid getting traded again are "very high."

"The Hornets now are clearly in a period of transition, and they decided that LaMelo Ball was not going to be a part of the team that they believe can eventually get to real contender status," Krawczynski explained. "They made a decision with that core group that they were not good enough as constructed and so they had to take a step back, amass some assets and go forward. And what that also means is that, A, the fans are going to be upset because they finally had a fun season last year and now it is going sideways again, but B, the possibilities of Naz Reid getting traded again are very high. This is a roster that I think is going to change over quite a bit over the next couple of years as the Hornets look for the right combination of people to really vault into what they hope is contention."

Krawczynski did not specify a timetable, and we've certainly seen frustrating starts turn into fruitful long-term partnerships, but all of this underlines just how difficult things have been for Reid and the Hornets so far this season. Charlotte knew what it was doing by trading Ball. The idea was to take a step back now to take a bigger step forward later. If that's still the idea here, there should be plenty of teams with real interest in Reid, so let's identify a few possible landing spots for the sweet-shooting big man.

The Warriors traded for and eventually re-signed Kristaps Porziņģis because they need big men who can shoot in a lineup featuring both Draymond Green and, presumably, Jimmy Butler eventually. Al Horford can cover some of those minutes, but he's 40 and can only be expected to play so much. Fortunately, Green's ability to defend across the positional spectrum means the Warriors don't necessarily need their center-sized shooter to actually defend centers. If he's big, he rebounds and he shoots, he'll fit.

This is Reid to a T. With Green on the team to actually guard the most dangerous centers and the combination of Butler and Yaxel Lendeborg providing size on the wing, the Warriors are fairly well-equipped to minimize Reid's weaknesses. His strengths are exactly what the Warriors need; the not-currently-getting-paid Porziņģis takes Golden State most of the way there when it comes to salary matching (with only a smaller salary like Gui Santos or De'Anthony Melton needed to close the hard cap gap), and the Warriors control almost all of their own future draft capital, so they have the ammunition they'd need to satisfy the Hornets.

The big question here -- especially with Stephen Curry now set to miss around a month with an oblique abdominal tear -- is how eager the Warriors are to invest in their present. Before the season, the answer should have been a resounding "not at all." But Curry was playing like an MVP candidate before he got hurt, and the emergence of Lendeborg as the Rookie of the Year favorite has arguably changed the equation. While the Warriors (8-4) may not be able to justify an all-in trade, giving up, say, one unprotected pick to nab the 27-year-old Reid, who can be a part of their post-Curry future as well as their present, might be a satisfactory compromise.

The Pistons have reportedly had an eye on Reid ever since his anticipated 2025 free agency, but he wound up re-signing with Minnesota before the opening bell even rang. The appeal is obvious. Two of Detroit's three best players, Ausar Thompson and Jalen Duren, are non-shooters. Having a big man who can shoot theoretically helps offset that deficit, particularly in lineups featuring just one of those two. Detroit tried that very strategy this offseason in acquiring John Collins. The results have been mixed, with the Pistons faring far better in the minutes Collins sits than the ones he plays. Reid is better and younger.

The Pistons are enormously flexible in how they could construct a deal. They control all of their own first-round picks, and they have a fair bit of breathing room beneath the luxury tax. That breathing room evaporates next season, though, when Thompson's rookie extension kicks in. This is where the decision calculus gets harder. Duren and Cade Cunningham are already making market rate. Thompson will be next season, and Duncan Robinson has simply proven too indispensable to move on from. Paying all four of those players plus Reid would leave precious little flexibility to add the second ball-handler Detroit badly needs.

Maybe the Pistons feel they're good enough as is to win without one, or they could be prepared to trade someone else out of their core down the line if they feel compelled to seek out that needed ball-handling addition. The Trajan Langdon-led front office is now in its third season at the helm, yet it has not traded a single first-round pick amid Detroit's ascent. The Pistons have been cautious with buy-now moves. Maybe Reid is the sort of player they'd splurge for, but considering how well the Pistons are playing, they will likely be cautious in shaking things up too much.

Cleveland's starting five simply hasn't worked this season. The Cavaliers are getting crushed with their five starters on the floor, and the Jarrett Allen-Evan Mobley duo, a frequent target of the rumor mill, is losing its minutes together by over five points per 100 possessions. That duo has never generated the elite rebounding that double-big lineups are supposed to create, and while they do a good job of defending shots at the rim, they give up so many of them that they net out as a fairly underwhelming rim defense. Peyton Watson is struggling to fit in as a small forward, but lineups featuring him and just one of Mobley or Allen are faring far better.

Swapping one of the bigs out for Reid, essentially trading defense for shooting, recalibrates the team in ways that theoretically create a friendlier environment for Watson. The Hornets, still presumably looking for a long-term starter at center, would likely have some degree of interest in either Mobley or Allen. The question is which makes more sense in a trade like this.

The answer is probably Allen. He's older. He's worse. He's more traditionally a center than Mobley, whom Cleveland has historically been reluctant to move up from power forward. But the on-off data is pretty clear this season: Cleveland is playing better with Allen on the floor than it is with Mobley. If the Cavaliers are going to go with a single big, it might behoove them to stick with the one they know can be a center. Mobley's 30% max contract is going to prove limiting down the line, especially with Donovan Mitchell starting a 35% supermax deal next season. Mobley would almost certainly net more in a trade than Allen would, and Charlotte has the pick surplus to make a pretty compelling offer if it wants to.

If Mobley is available in any way, shape or form by February, offers will come in from several teams. Not many have a package that makes more immediate sense than one built around Reid and Charlotte's picks, which Cleveland could either keep or redirect for other assets. If Cleveland and Charlotte do engage on Reid talks, though, Allen is the far likelier Cavaliers big man to become a Hornet.

We can't fully judge the Raptors until we see an extended stretch with a healthy Kawhi Leonard, but thus far, Toronto's shooting weakness elsewhere has been as severe as expected. The Raptors rank 24th in 3-point attempt rate and 27th in 3-point percentage as of this writing. Like Golden State, Toronto is perfectly equipped to cover for Reid's deficiencies as a rim protector. There isn't a better trio of defensive forwards in the NBA than Leonard, Scottie Barnes and Collin Murray-Boyles, and Murray-Boyles already plays a reasonable amount of center.

The money would presumably be RJ Barrett's expiring contract. Losing his shot-creation would sting, but with a healthy Leonard, they should be covered in that respect. The question is draft compensation. The Raptors already gave up unprotected picks in 2031 and 2033 for Leonard, leaving their 2029 selection as their only tradable, unencumbered pick. They could move their 2027 pick as well, though it has swap rights attached to the Oklahoma City Thunder, limiting its appeal. A swap or two would probably be needed here to seal the deal, but Toronto probably has the chips to pull this off if it's motivated enough.

Reid would eat up Toronto's room beneath the luxury tax line next season, but with all of their core players locked in, that shouldn't be much of an issue. The Raptors haven't started their tax clock yet, so a year or two above that line (and still well below the dreaded second apron) seems a small price to pay for a genuine championship contender.

Critics are very split on Ti West's 'A Christmas Carol' adaptation, calling Depp everything from a "soulless worst Scrooge ever" to "eminently redeemable."

Nearly every critic reviewing Ebenezer seems to be finding their way around the same question: How do we feel, exactly, about Johnny Depp starring in a major studio film once again?

Conveniently, Ti West‘s adaptation of A Christmas Carol provides plenty of metaphorical fodder given it’s a redemption tale, after all, with Depp’s Scrooge given lines like, “I am not the man I was!” and “I was once good, and I know I can be again!”

Still, many critics aren’t sold on the film — or Depp, for that matter, with the actor once again buried in makeup and prosthetics. The film currently has a 51 percent positive score on Rotten Tomatoes, and even the positive reviews tend to be a bit tempered (it will be interesting to see if the audience score for Ebenezer is significantly higher, as critics assigned to cover the film might very well feel more negatively towards Depp than fans). The movie’s supporting cast — including Rupert Grint, Ian McKellen, Andrea Riseborough, Daisy Ridley and Tramell Tillman — are largely drawing praise. Here is a sampling of reviews from major publications. Ebenezer opens Friday (watch the final trailer below).

The Hollywood Reporter: “Johnny Depp really needed a comeback vehicle, and he’s found an effective one in Ti West’s horror-tinged adaptation that allows the actor to deliver the sort of quirky theatrical performance for which he became famous. While Ebenezer doesn’t break any new ground, other than possibly being the first version of the story not really suitable for young children, it fulfills its ostensible goal of restoring the actor to A-list status … Depp … delivers a performance that no one would call subtle. In style, it most closely resembles a heavily made-up Albert Finney’s broad take on the character in the now largely forgotten 1970 musical Scrooge. But the actor’s charisma and star power eventually come through.”

Vulture: “Johnny Depp is the worst part of Ebenezer, but he’s also the reason it exists, so it’s futile to try and separate his presence from the rest of the movie, which is, inconveniently, pretty good. Depp is also doing a tiresome variation on the mugging that’s become his primary approach to acting ever since the role of Captain Jack Sparrow vaulted him into Disney-franchise levels of fame — a vaguely comic, scenery-chewing mode that doesn’t fit in with the rest of the movie…West’s interpretations of the spirits who visit the miser over the small hours of the night are imaginatively spooky, especially the Ghost of Christmas Past, played by Andrea Riseborough as a kind of corpse bride clad all in white with rheumy eyes.”

The New York Times: “It’s an attempt to, as much as one can in one role, play the hits. Whether consciously or simply out of a lack of creativity, the aging star is playing an agreeable version of himself, more than anything, aimed at providing a fond reminder of the Depp-in-drag iconography he crafted for himself over the last few decades … Ebenezer implicitly markets itself on the empirically shaky idea of a beloved Depp that was taken away. If you don’t squint too hard, his Scrooge might not only function as a return, but a memory wipe.”

The Times UK: “It’s just a shame, then, that the film around him, directed by the horror film-maker Ti West (Pearl, X), is hugely uninspired and has little to offer the already swollen canon of Christmas Carol adaptations. The Alastair Sim version from 1951, the Bill Murray comedy from 1988 and the Muppet musical reboot from 1992 are all vastly superior to this surprisingly vanilla take. … But really this is just a chance for audiences to see that Depp, like the character he is playing, is eminently redeemable and ready again, in the strongest possible terms, for his close-up.

Boston Globe: “Under all that makeup, Depp is easily the worst Scrooge I’ve ever seen. His performance is a soulless, hollow shell, never once convincing us of Scrooge’s miserly rage or his ultimate redemption. There isn’t an ounce of humanity in this performance; it may as well have been generated with AI.”

The Guardian: “As Scrooge himself, Depp is in fact rather good; theatrically bad-tempered and petulant and, as with his Jack Sparrow, I suspect Depp’s comedy timing and sonorous British accent might have been learned from his old Fast Show comrade Paul Whitehouse. He is better at the unredeemed Scrooge than the saved version at the end, where some of the abject pathos is missing. This is a decent enough new version, avoiding any big new revisionist takes which might have clunked.”

Slashfilm: “With the exception of a small moment here and there, Depp’s Scrooge is largely solid yet unremarkable. Which begs the question: why bother in the first place? It seems like the entirety of Ebenezer was arranged to allow Depp to chew scenery and showboat around, which the actor perversely refuses to do. It’s possible that Depp now considers such an approach something he’s no longer interested in, and it’s not like he hasn’t given grounded performances before, like his turn as John Dillinger in Public Enemies, for example. Depp’s performance as Scrooge simply doesn’t feel fully realized.”

IndieWire: “While Depp has never been the subtlest of actors, his post-Sparrow pivot away from artistic curiosity has distended his talents into such a grotesque caricature of their former charm that Ebenezer left me feeling like I may never be able to empathize with his screen presence again … Given the emphasis the director puts on the film’s genre spin, it’s ironic and surprising that Ebenezer is at its best when it focuses on the most basic human elements of its story, if only because the supporting characters offer such a welcome contrast to whatever Depp is doing.

The Daily Beast: “Ebenezer has enough commendable qualities to justify its existence, and Depp sturdily balances Scrooge’s nastiness, silliness, and, finally, kindness … Not fresh enough to become a classic, but with enough scariness to unnerve its younger viewers, West’s latest is a competent, accurate retelling fit for the Yuletide season—and, perhaps just as crucially, a Depp vehicle that suggests the beleaguered star isn’t ready to relinquish his A-list status just yet.”

Nel corso del consueto appuntamento con Maracanà, programma in onda sulle frequenze di TMW Radio, il procuratore Giocondo Martoretti ha fatto un po' il punto sui nomi più interessanti in vista del prossimo calciomercato invernale, soffermandosi anche su quello di Nicolò Tresoldi, finito nel mirino del Milan e di mezza Europa. Di seguito il suo pensiero.

Cardinale pare che voglia investire ancora per il Milan nella sessione del mercato invernale. Tresoldi dal Brugge potrebbe essere un colpo all'altezza? "L'impatto che ha avuto Malen a Roma lo scorso Gennaio fa capire quanto un attaccante bravo possa elevare tutto l'insieme di una squadra. La bravura della società e degli operatori sta nello scouting, è lì che non bisogna sbagliare. Certo, poi a Gennaio giocatori importanti si spostano difficilmente. Tante volte però si può sempre migliorare con le giuste operazioni. Tresoldi comunque si è presentato alla grande ai tifosi italiani, segnando tre gol all'Inter. Ha una potenzialità enorme e se la società è disposta a spendere molti milioni questa operazione può andare in porto. Lui e Endrick, che era stato accostato precedentemente, hanno caratteristiche molto diverse. Ora devi capire su che tipo di profilo puntare".
