Cashmere Walls, Baby Goats, and a Bruce Springsteen Sighting at Loro Piana’s Cashmere of the Year Award

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The invitation to Tuesday evening’s Loro Piana dinner arrived via courier. It was printed on heavy card stock, made out in gold ink calligraphy and sealed with red wax. It was no run-of-the-mill invite, which these days mostly arrive via email or DM, nor was it to announce the usual celebration, like a new shoe or bag. Instead, it celebrated farmers and their fibers—in particular, the farmers hailing from Mongolia who act as the conduit behind the brand’s beloved cashmere clothing and the fibers they reap from their flocks of goats in the mountains.

Now in its 11th year, the Loro Piana Cashmere Award spotlights the finest in cashmere fiber production, and that is meant in the most literal sense. At The Brant Foundation in the East Village, the first sight that greeted guests was three bundles of soft white fiber, illuminated with a dramatic spotlight. These were the winning lots selected for their superior quality, and cultivated by Mr. Siqingbilige and Mrs. Hasiqiqige, a husband-and-wife duo in first place, followed by Mr. Jiyatu and Mrs. Gegentuya in second place, and Mr. Lifuqiang and Mrs. Qiqi in third place. “The herdsmen bring us about 10 million kilos of sheared fiber, and this is the best 100 kilos, which we celebrate for their fineness and length of the fiber,” Pier Luigi Loro Piana, the son of the brand’s founder, Franco Loro Piana, told the room. “That denotes the quality.”

In addition to his role as the company’s deputy chairman, he is widely credited with creating the “baby goat cashmere” that thrust Loro Piana into the upper echelons of luxury when it was first revealed in 2008. “It took me many years to convince the herdsmen not to blend the 3% of baby cashmere fiber with the rest of the older goats,” he recalled. “It required extra work, but the difference was so clear, and it is this measure of quality that creates wealth for this population and attracts younger herdsmen to join them.”

In the room were many Loro Piana loyalists, including Huma Abedin, Gucci Westman, Jeff Koons, and Jessica Springsteen, the accomplished show jumper who serves as an ambassador for the brand’s equestrian line. She was joined by her father, the legendary rocker Bruce Springsteen, who was gleefully received by a room largely filled with Italian, French, and American guests. Evidently, the appeal of The Boss is universal.

Also on hand was Jeremy Strong, who has been outfitted by Loro Piana both on and off screen many, many times, though his first memory of the brand predates his career. “I have a memory of being in Italy and seeing the chicest man I’d ever seen in my life with two wolfhounds in a vintage Maserati convertible, and I saw the Loro Piana red mark on the back of his collar, so that image has forever emblazoned itself in the aspirational sky of my mind—just wanting to be that guy in that car,” he told Vogue.

For this occasion, Strong sported a monochromatic suit in a precise shade of Loro Piana brown-beige, and went on to tour the exhibition that had been built to highlight the farmers and their bounty. It included one room with walls of voluminous, fluffy strands of cashmere fiber which ballooned and wafted toward guests walking by. It quickly began clinging to the arms and shoulders and heads of many partygoers before an army of dashing men would offer to remove it with lint brushes with glossy tortoise handles. (In Loro Piana land, even the lint catchers are a considered detail.)

“I think what I was struck by upstairs walking through the exhibition was the way Loro Piana achieves such specificity by hand in this age of mechanical reproduction, this age where everything is computers and AI, and everything is mechanized and done by a machine,” Strong later added. “There is this care, and tactile beauty, and sort of sweat equity that goes into creating these garments. I find something very moving about that.”

The evening’s dinner—beef carpaccio, a plate of saffron risotto, and a fig tart—set the stage for further reminiscing about Loro Piana vintage finds and making plans to visit Mongolia to see the goats and the herdsmen firsthand, followed by an announcement.

After all these ceremonies celebrating the finest 100 kilos of cashmere fibers, something would finally be made using all of these fibers. “We have kept these winning fibers for the last 11 years, and we will be making a collection using them,” Frédéric Arnault, the company’s CEO, told the room. “It will be very small quantities.”

Most people will beat the game without upgrading their stick bag, but if you want to do everything there is to do, don't skip out on these bag upgrades.

Below, we explain where to find all the Deku-Stick Bag upgrades in the Ocarina of Time remake.

You can unlock these upgrades as child Link (which works out, since you can't use Deku Sticks as adult Link), but if you forgot to nab them as a kid and are feeling too lazy to swap back, you can also unlock these upgrades as adult Link.

You can find both of these upgrades in the Lost Woods, though you will need the Skull Mask to get the second one.

Originally, your Deku Stick Bag can hold up to 10 Deku Sticks, but these upgrades will let you carry 10 more each, for a total of 30 sticks.

For the first bag upgrade, head into the Lost Woods from Kokiri Forest and then immediately head to the left. This will put you in the room with the Skull Kid. Ignore them and take another left, which will send you down a wooded pathway.

At the end of the pathway, there's a Business Scrub. Defeat it by reflecting its nuts back at it, and it'll beg for your forgiveness and offer to sell you a Deku-Stick Bag upgrade for 40 rupees.

For the second upgrade, you'll need to follow another specific path when you enter the Lost Woods from Kokiri Forest: right, left, right, left, and then left again.

This takes you to a clearing with some greenery surrounding a hole in the ground. If you come here as young Link, there'll be two Business Scrubs. (There's also a soft soil mound nearby if you want to plant a bean or find a Skulltula.)

Drop into the hole, and you'll see a sign that says, "Stand on the stage and prepare your face for an evaluation." Put on your Skull Mask and then stand on the stump. The Deku Scrubs will be so excited to see you in that mask. After they swarm you for a little bit, a bigger Deku Scrub will pop out of the ground and give you another Deku-Stick Bag upgrade for free.

Johnson & Johnson Sees a Path to Double-Digit Growth by 2030. Is the Dividend King a Buy?

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The business has undergone major changes in recent years, and these might pay off over the medium term.

Johnson & Johnson (JNJ -0.35%) has made significant business changes over the past few years. Perhaps the most notable was the company's decision to spin off its consumer health division into a stand-alone, publicly traded company. The drugmaker made this move partly because consumer health had become a drag on sales growth, and it bet that eventually, top-line growth would improve as a result of this spinoff. Now, management thinks that Johnson & Johnson could achieve double-digit sales growth by 2030. Does this guidance make the stock a buy? Let's find out.

In the first nine months of the year, Johnson & Johnson reported $74.8 billion in sales, up 7.4% year over year. That's a strong performance for the pharmaceutical giant, and even more impressive given the biosimilar competition for Stelara. This immunology drug lost U.S. patent exclusivity in 2025. Stelara was a meaningful growth driver. In fact, even after losing exclusivity, it remains one of Johnson & Johnson's top-selling drugs.

Through Sept. 30, it generated $2.1 billion in sales, down 56.4% year over year. Excluding Stelara, Johnson & Johnson's total revenue through Sept. 30 grew by about 12.2% year over year. Looking forward, Johnson & Johnson could face more patent cliffs over the next five years. Darzalex, a cancer drug and the company's best-selling product, could lose U.S. patent exclusivity by the end of the decade.

To achieve its goal of double-digit sales growth, Johnson & Johnson will have to find new drugs to navigate upcoming patent expirations. Fortunately, the company has made progress along those lines. In recent years, Johnson & Johnson has launched several new products to diversify its pipeline, boost top-line growth, and overcome patent cliffs. The company's newest drugs include Icotyde, an oral pill for plaque psoriasis. Others include Inlexzo and Lazcluze, both of which are cancer drugs, and Imaavy, which is approved to treat myasthenia gravis, a chronic autoimmune condition.

Johnson & Johnson has estimated that all four of these products have peak sales potential exceeding $5 billion. These are projections, not results. But it's not hard to see the potential of these medicines. For instance, consider Icotyde, which belongs to the IL-23 pathway inhibitor category, a group of medicines that treat various diseases by blocking immune signals that cause excessive inflammation.

Most medicines in this group require injections. Icotyde became the first oral IL-23 pathway inhibitor to earn approval for plaque psoriasis. Since it offers a much more convenient option, it could carve out a niche in this large market, as it earns additional indications elsewhere. Multibillion-dollar peak sales don't seem outside this medicine's reach. Further, Johnson & Johnson has other highly promising candidates.

One of them is Milvexian, an investigational anticoagulant that could solve one problem today's anticoagulants face: it could reduce the significant bleeding risk they pose. Lastly, even within its medical device segment, Johnson & Johnson has made progress.

For instance, the company has earned U.S. clearance for the Ottava. This robotic-assisted surgery device will allow it to tap into the underpenetrated robotic surgery market -- Johnson & Johnson has estimated that fewer than 8% of eligible soft tissue surgeries are currently performed robotically.

Johnson & Johnson can see revenue growth rates increase as sales of newer products ramp up, even as it continues to face generic and biosimilar competition for older drugs.

Johnson & Johnson looks well-positioned to achieve its goal of double-digit sales growth by 2030, and there are several other reasons to buy the stock. Here are two of them. First, the company has taken significant steps toward resolving the thousands of talc-related lawsuits it has faced for years. A recent proposed settlement would have Johnson & Johnson pay $5.5 billion to plaintiffs, with the first payment of no more than $3 billion due in 2027 and no additional payment due before 2028.

For a company that could generate over $100 billion in annual sales this year, that's a fairly small price to pay to put thousands of lawsuits that have tarnished its public image in the rearview mirror. Second, Johnson & Johnson remains a fantastic income stock. The company's streak of 64 consecutive annual payout increases makes it a Dividend King, a status that requires at least 50 straight annual dividend increases. Dividend seekers should seriously consider adding this stock to their portfolios and holding it for the long term.
