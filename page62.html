Meet the 27-Year-Old Who’s Trying To Bring Housing Back to Rural Ohio

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When it comes to the hottest locations that attract young 20-somethings, Mansfield, OH, probably isn't the first place that comes to mind.

For years, Mansfield’s nickname was “The Fun Center of Ohio” because of its family-friendly attractions and its status in the 1970s as a mini-bike manufacturing hub.

More recently, the town of 47,000, located halfway between Columbus and Cleveland, earned the local nickname of “Danger City” after the Westinghouse electric plant left in 1990 and the town fell into deep economic decline.

The 27-year-old serial entrepreneur, who started a painting and landscaping business as a teenager, relocated to Mansfield from Seattle a year ago, hoping to revitalize rural housing through his vertically integrated housing development company, Western Dominion Land.

Vertical integration is a development model in which a single company controls the full life cycle of a housing project.

“In other countries, they’ve figured out that vertically integrating construction means you’re not paying a margin out to every single step," Goehner explains. "If you own the system—from a lumberyard, to producing panels or modular, to buying the land, to developing the land, to building the homes—you're not paying everyone’s markup along the way, and you can actually deliver that home at a reasonable price.”

Goehner is building a factory in Mansfield to produce structured panels that he says will cut single-family home construction from months to weeks and allow him to charge much less than other builders. The first panels are expected to roll off the production line in early 2027.

Currently, it takes 8.8 months to construct a home from start to finish, according to the National Association of Home Builders. Goehner says his process allows a home to be built and move-in-ready in 40 days.

Western Dominion has already built about 500 housing units across Ohio, starting at $200,000 for a single-family home.

Western Dominion focuses on what it calls "attainable housing"—homes priced below what the median household income in the area can afford but not income-limited, meaning they work for the vast majority of families and buyers.

In Mansfield, where the median listing price is $229,000, he says, "single-family homes at $215,000 sold instantly. At $260,000, they sat for months."

As he sees it, 25% of the homes on the market are priced for 60% to 70% of buyers, while the rest of what’s being built is at the top end of the market. Furthermore, only 8% of construction happens in rural areas, despite 20% of the population living there.

"The government is too focused on low-income housing" and low-income housing tax credits, he says. “And the last few housing bills they passed were only structured to deliver essentially more urban multifamily units and incentivize builders to build more low-income housing with tax credits."

In rural communities, he adds, “We have a terrible amount of aged housing inventory. Most of our homes are from before 1970, which would be fine if it didn't cost so much to remodel.

“From the '90s to the 2000s, we’re building a good amount of homes. As a country, we were doing about 1.6 million a year. And then in rural America, we were doing about 220,000 homes a year. And then 2008 hits, and that drops down to about 60,000 homes a year in rural America."

Along the way, smaller builders disappeared, and larger builders were disincentivized from building in rural areas because of the red tape involved.

"If you want to build in rural America and you go to a bank, unless you have all the cash, you have to show comps," he says. "You've got to be, like, ‘All right, we think these homes are going to sell for this much because these guys sold for this much.' And if no one's built for five to 50 years, which is the case in a lot of these communities, that can be difficult."

The added costs of building in rural areas, like having to pull utilities, "mean you're spending $25,000 to $50,000 per lot before you can even start putting a house on it or connecting your utilities to the lot."

Since the COVID-19 pandemic, home prices in rural communities have risen sharply. A Realtor.com® housing analysis of home prices from November 2019 to November 2025 found that median listing prices increased more than 70% in rural counties while in-metro counties saw home prices increase around 30% during the same time period.

"A lot of the politicians we talk to, they go, 'I didn't know that that county needed housing. I didn't know rural needed housing because the population is shrinking.' But then when you look at why the population is shrinking, it's because they haven't gotten any new homes for 20 to 40 years."

"You've got this huge demand. But on paper, on the surface, it looks like there's no demand because the population's declining," he continues. "And that's how you get cities like Holiday City."

Holiday City, near the Ohio border with Indiana and Michigan, has a population of just 48 people, but it is home to three large businesses—Chase Brass & Copper Co., 20/20 Custom Molded Products, and a Menards Distribution Center—which draw around 1,500 commuters from out of state.

"Let's put up, you know, 300 homes, and a portion of the workforce can actually stop commuting an hour or two every day," Goehner says.

Goehner hopes Ohio's new slate of elected officials will help advance rural building by offsetting some of the infrastructure-related costs of building in rural areas. In exchange, he would sell homes at his “attainable” housing rates.

"The government needs to stop spending thousands of dollars a month per unit to subsidize rents when they could simply spend a little bit upfront to make it viable to build at a reasonable price point again," he says.

Amorim discusses post-Genoa pride, the Ramos ‘team problem’ and losing being ‘end of the world’

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Ruben Amorim wants his AC Milan side to fear losing, while he believes Goncalo Ramos’ lack of goals is a team problem.

Milan got their seventh win in 11 Serie A games on Sunday afternoon as they overcame an in-form Genoa side 2-1 at the Marassi. Alphadjo Cisse and Christian Pulisic scored in the first half to put the Rossoneri in command, though Ellertsson’s late strike from range made it a nervy finish.

It was a result and a response that was needed after the dismal defeat to Ferencvaros on Thursday night, and it calms some of the turbulence before the international break. However, the search to become consistently good side is one that will continue in a fortnight.

Amorim spoke to DAZN not long after the final whistle to give his assessments on what was an important win for his side but one that they made hard work of in the end, with his words via MilanNews.

A response was expected after the Europa League, and it arrived. But it’s only the beginning. What positive things do you take away from this?

“I’m proud of my players. The most important thing was to be competitive, I said it before the match. If we approach every game like this, we’re one step closer to winning them all.

“There are times when you manage to play really well, but there are also times when we changed our approach and were strong on second balls and played a different kind of football. And that’s what great teams do: they play differently within the same game. I’m really pleased with my players today.”

“What I can say is that we can win every game, but I can’t guarantee we’ll reach that level of confidence by the end of the season. I don’t feel like we’ve got that kind of consistency yet.

“And I don’t want to say anything more than ‘we can win the next game’. Because that’s the feeling I get when I look at the team. But it depends on many things. If we’d had everyone available, we would definitely have been better in the Europa League, too.

“This run of matches has been tough for us with everything that’s happened. But I can say that if we’re competitive, then we can win every game in the league. Let’s focus on that and take it one day at a time.”

“It’s the mentality. When you’re a player, there are no off days. If you play for Milan, for these clubs, there are no off days. You can’t show up like you did on Thursday and then turn on again for Sunday. It’s not possible, it’s not something that will work.

“It’s not a physical condition, it’s a question of anxiety about defeat. If you ‘panic’ about defeat, it’s not that you’re afraid of losing, but that you know that losing is the end of the world. That’s the feeling I want my teams to have.”

How is Gonçalo Ramos’ development going? He got an assist today…

“I think it’s difficult for a striker when he doesn’t score goals. But I’ll repeat: we don’t spend much time in the final third of the pitch. We have possession, we’re improving our ball output, but we’re missing that. When we’re consistently in the final third, Gonçalo will score a lot of goals.

“It’s a team problem, we’re not helping Ramos, but he’s helping us a lot. It’s a process, a journey. And we’re not yet at the point of being so dominant that Ramos spends most of his time in the opponent’s penalty area. We’re still a long way off.”

Crystal Palace 1-3 Liverpool: Andoni Iraola’s side bounced back from two defeats on the spin but Palace, bottom of the table after 10 games, could now be forced to make a change in the dugout

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

For Andoni Iraola, at the end of a week of vexation following two avoidable one-goal defeats, this was just what the doctor ordered. On a cool November day, a comfortable margin of victory in second gear in South London halted Liverpool’s two-game losing run heading into the internationals and catapulted them back into the top-four, nine points off leaders Arsenal.

Even Florian Wirtz, Liverpool’s ultimate attacking fall guy over the last 15 months, netted a much-needed first Premier League goal of the season. Off a Bradley Barcola assist, too. Here at Selhurst, amid a forward quartet made up of just shy of £400m worth of transfer fees, there were more positive sprinklings of encouragement. And when Alexander Isak, who it should be said is second in the race for the golden boot with seven league goals, smashed the ball home on the cusp of 90 minutes, the three points were safe and heading back up to the north-west.

That said, this was all against a Crystal Palace side – and a manager in beleaguered Frenchman Pierre Sage – who by the end looked out of ideas and inspiration. No head coach has lost his job in the Premier League this season – yet – but the Eagles are now rooted to the bottom of the table after 10 games with just two wins to their name. Could a change be in the offing from Palace chairman Steve Parish, a man who does tend to afford managers time to put things right? We’ll find out soon enough.

Yet what will frustrate Sage the most on this occasion is that, until Liverpool’s second just after half-time, Palace were well in this match. But the team that has scored the second-fewest goals in the division (ten) were punished here for failing to take their chances in the first-half.

But a week on from failing to have a single shot on target against Arsenal, it took 20 seconds for Liverpool to find their range here as Mac Allister gave Dean Henderson an early test. The diminutive 5’9” midfielder, curiously effective from set pieces, went closer after 10 minutes, heading into the side netting from Dominik Szoboszlai’s free-kick. It was a warning and routine that Palace would not heed.

Yet after the first 15 minutes, Palace were the team on the ascendancy. Yeremy Pino’s neat swivel and shot crashed off the crossbar while Jogen Strand Larsen, in for the suspended Jean-Philippe Mateta, saw a dinked finish ruled out for offside, albeit correctly so.

And when Daichi Kamada gave Barcola a lesson in back-post volleys with a punchy effort kept out by Alisson, it was somewhat against the run of play when Mac Allister’s opener arrived.

Iraola had spoken in the build-up about his team perhaps being “too honest” as a statistic this week did the rounds: while Liverpool have conceded the most fouls in the Premier League, only three sides have won fewer fouls. But it pays to be shrewd. After Cody Gakpo won a questionably-given foul off Jefferson Lerma, Szoboszlai’s near-post free-kick was once again met by Mac Allister, who glanced sweetly home for his fifth goal of the season, equalling his tally from the last campaign.

His provocative celebration in front of the Holmesdale End only added to the home crowd's fury. “One-nil to the referee,” they sang at official Tony Harrington at the interval.

The Reds were, however, fortunate to be ahead at half-time. Their goal was under siege before the interval, with Quinten Timber and Adam Wharton forcing good saves out of Alisson. Palace’s momentum was stymied by the interval though and when Barcola’s square ball after a surge down the right found Wirtz, the German found the attribute he so often has been accused of lacking since his £105m arrival on these shores: composure. Stroking the ball beyond Henderson, it is just his sixth Premier League goal since arriving from Bayer Leverkusen last summer.

But even in the closing stages, Palace carved out decent opportunities. Mino somehow squandered a one-on-one opportunity with the goal gaping and substitute Eddie Nketiah poked over from close range. It was indicative of Palace’s fortunes: neat build-up, a large intake of breath in the box before failing to find the back of the net.

And when Isak put the icing on the cake, set up by Wirtz in the box and coolly cutting inside before lashing home past Henderson, Sage had the look of a man on the brink on the touchline. Even Milos Kerkez’s brainless foul on Dario Osorio in stoppage time, clattering the Palace substitute’s head in the box, which resulted in Nketiah’s consolation penalty and a needless ruining of Alisson’s clean sheet, could not brighten the Palace mood. Now, ahead of a two-week pause in action, the question for Parish is: will he stick or twist?

Fan injured after falling through hole in stand

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tromso's Romssa Arena, pictured before a Conference League game against Brighton in August, is 200 miles inside the Arctic Circle

A fan was taken to hospital after a number of Bodo/Glimt supporters fell through a hole in the away stand at Tromso.

The Norwegian top-flight match at Romssa Arena on Sunday was paused just before half-time after Bodo keeper Nikita Haikin called for the referee's attention.

Bodo/Glimt press officer Simen Pedersen told the Reuters news agency that "up to five people fell through the stands".

"We stood and bounced to a supporter song, then the floor gave way," Bodo supporter Andrine Moen told local newspaper Verdens Gang, as reported by Reuters.

"It cracked in two in the row in front of us. There were at least two people who fell through the floor, but we don't know how far they fell or how many fell in total."

The remaining Bodo supporters were moved into other parts of the ground before the match resumed.

The match at Tromso's stadium - the northernmost venue in Norway's Eliteserien - was played in heavy rain.

The score was 0-0 at the time of the incident, but Bodo forward Ola Brynhildsen scored twice after the game resumed to give the league leaders a 2-0 win.

Bodo are locked in a title battle with Viking and the Eliteserien season is set to finish in the first week of December.

Why Nvidia Stock Looks Like a Bargain Right Now

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia's shares have stalled, while its business hasn't. Here's the case for buying the dip.

Nvidia (NVDA +2.77%) has made investors a tremendous amount of money over the past several years. But lately, the artificial intelligence chipmaker has been heading in the wrong direction.

Shares have been essentially flat so far in 2026 and have fallen about 22% from their all-time high. That's quite a reversal for a company whose revenue more than doubled in its most recent quarter.

And I think investors are getting an opportunity with Nvidia stock.

Nvidia reports its fiscal third-quarter earnings on Nov. 17, and I'm expecting another blowout performance. The company's financial results and the extraordinary demand for AI computing suggest its growth story has plenty of room to run.

In its fiscal second quarter, which ended July 26, Nvidia generated $96.2 billion in revenue, up 106% from a year earlier. Data center revenue reached $89 billion, an increase of 117%.

Those are remarkable growth rates for a company operating at Nvidia's scale.

Management expects third-quarter revenue of approximately $108 billion, representing another 12% sequential increase at the midpoint. Nvidia also forecasts a non-GAAP gross margin of 74%, suggesting it expects to maintain substantial pricing power despite its enormous sales volume.

And there are good reasons to believe demand remains strong. SK Hynix, a major supplier of high bandwidth memory used in AI accelerators, has indicated that its advanced memory capacity is effectively sold out for years. That matters, because Nvidia's GPUs need that memory to handle increasingly demanding AI workloads.

Meanwhile, Nvidia's Vera Rubin platform is already in full production, with systems being deployed by major cloud providers and AI infrastructure companies.

Customers aren't waiting for AI computing demand to disappear. They're spending billions to secure more capacity.

The latest weakness in Nvidia stock reflects concerns about the enormous cost of AI infrastructure and the possibility that political opposition could slow data center construction.

Those aren't imaginary risks. If companies struggle to obtain electricity or construction permits, some GPU deployments could be delayed.

But there's a difference between a delay in building data centers and a collapse in demand for AI computing. Nvidia has already demonstrated that it can sell enormous quantities of increasingly sophisticated chips while maintaining exceptional margins. Its next earnings report will provide another opportunity to see whether that demand is holding up.

I'm expecting the results to exceed management's already aggressive outlook.

A 24% decline doesn't automatically make any stock cheap, and Nvidia still faces risks from customer spending cuts, competing chips, and restrictions on AI infrastructure development. But with revenue doubling and a new generation of products entering production, I see a stronger business than what the stock's recent performance suggests.
