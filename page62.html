How the surge in AI-discovered bugs is straining Microsoft security and threatening tech stability

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

by Angela Gunn on Nov 20, 2026 at 9:25 amNovember 20, 2026 at 9:25 am

It has come to the attention of the general public this month that adversaries successfully adopt technology a lot faster than defenders do. This has nothing to do with the willingness of defenders to try. It has everything to do with the ancient truth that to succeed, defenders have to get it right every time but adversaries only have to get it right occasionally.

Now multiply “every time” and “occasionally” by AI. Do you like that math? Does anyone?

The disastrous update that broke Teams, Outlook, SharePoint and other packages was not a direct attack by AI-wielding bad guys. Nor was the FAA outage. Nor were most of the outage events of the past few weeks, though who can say what’s up with those European Union refrigerators.

No. The true AI-driven mayhem has been predicated for several months now not on powerful new AI-honed attacks, but on an absolute deluge of bugs of relatively low severity that Microsoft and other defenders with a formal patch process aren’t built to process.

The question is whether traditional patch programs can adapt their processes to handle the deluge. Microsoft’s is MAPP (Microsoft Active Protections Program), to which the company has been committed for nearly two decades. If the industry-spanning events of the past few weeks are any indication, these programs can’t.

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella said Wednesday at the New York Times DealBook Summit that last week’s Microsoft 365 outage came from a latent bug and wasn’t directly tied to AI, but he warned that AI-driven patching could bring more instability.

He elaborated in a separate appearance on CNBC the same day, saying the flood of patches being applied to older infrastructure is making systems less stable.

AI has “allowed us to find these bugs and fix these bugs, but that means you’ve got to then patch them, and that creates instability,” Nadella said, when asked by Jim Cramer about a string of recent outages at United, Schwab, Chase and other organizations.

Some context: MAPP turned 18 this year. The program, run by the MSRC (Microsoft Security Response Center), handles outreach to partner companies that gain early access to information on the bugs in advance of each month’s Patch Tuesday release. In-house, it coordinates the patch release process, just as the larger MSRC organization helps coordinate the handling of bug reports from external finders.

MSRC also runs point on prioritizing patches of the worst bugs through its SSIRP (Software and Services Incident Response Plan) program, itself dating to 2004. In its time, it was an industry leader in how companies can efficiently get their arms around bad bugs.

MAPP in its own way is similarly remarkable. It has undergone a few traumas over the years, usually when a partner breaks confidence and leaks the advance details it receives. In 2025, after attacks on SharePoint raised suspicions of a leak from the program, Microsoft stopped giving Chinese companies early access. But MAPP continues to tick along in a way that would make any program manager proud. Other companies have their own version of MAPP / MSRC, as do community efforts such as those supporting Linux.

Full disclosure: I’m a former MSRC employee who learned about MAPP at the knee of the program’s principals. I subsequently tracked patch releases at a company partnered with MAPP at its highest (Validate) level, which means I was privy to the 14-day multi-stage run-up to each month’s release. I admire and respect the program… and I’m afraid that in the AI-finder era, it’s in big, big trouble.

Since January 2021, Microsoft has issued patches for an estimated 10,319 CVEs. (I use estimated numbers throughout this analysis because there are various ways of counting such things, including whether or not the patch accrues to an application such as Edge, which actually relies on the Chromium project for its fixes. Other analysts arrive at larger or smaller counts.)

Over half of those — 5,383 CVEs — are 2026 releases, 5,128 of which were addressed since April. In comparison, 2025’s full-year CVE total was 1,180.

What happened in April? As nearly as analysis can determine, the floodgates opened and finders (security researchers) started barraging the company with reports.

The first quarter of 2026 saw 255 CVEs addressed over three months. April had 163, May 132; in June the number jumped to 209. In July it was off to the races with 575 CVEs. In October, the monthly count topped 1,400.

Most of these bugs aren’t coming from the usual security stalwarts. They’re coming from new finders, they’re coming in with multiple simultaneous finders not believed to be working together, and they’re coming in relatively weak.

Though the overall volume has skyrocketed, the number per month of bugs under active exploit in the wild — the notorious “zero-days” — hasn’t increased proportionally.

Moreover, average bug severity has in fact decreased. Until 2026, the average CVSS severity score for all bugs tended to hover around 7.8 (out of 10) each month. In 2026, that’s dropped to approximately 7.4 as of September — not garbage finds, but the sort of thing that doesn’t scramble the jets.

No surge in zero-days and a drop in severity — that’s good news. The problem is the volume.

Once bugs are received by Microsoft, each goes through a MAPP-helmed, multi-team process of investigation and patch development.

The true AI-driven mayhem has been predicated not on powerful new AI-honed attacks, but on a deluge of bugs of relatively low severity that Microsoft and others aren’t built to process.

Since many products have several versions still in support, that often means that multiple teams within each product group are asked to ingest a bug report, replicate the problem, devise and test a fix, ensure the fix doesn’t trigger other problems or inadvertently point to a similar bug elsewhere in the product and ready it for mass consumption. Even AI slop has to be taken seriously, just in case.

Meanwhile, MAPP is the traffic cop for the entire release, working with each team to ascertain patches are ready, understand the nature of the bugs and what can be safely communicated to partners and the public, and ready the processes that make the second Tuesday of each month a festival of downloads and reboots. From where we stand right now, it’s all fairly… hand-crafted.

In theory the process is automatable to some extent, though the success of that effort so far in light of recent events is debatable. But high volume also leads to other problems, such as exposing information on incomplete patching.

For instance, Microsoft has recently patched Office bugs that apply to both Windows and Mac, but patches have initially only been available for Windows versions.

Microsoft’s release-day information about such bugs — patching Windows versions while asking Apple users to monitor Microsoft communications for news of their own patch — alerts the world, including eager adversaries, that the flaw exists. Apple customers just have to hope no adversaries exploit it while their patch is pending.

More broadly, many of the steps in the current patching process involve judgment calls, not processes that scale well or that can reliably be handled by existing AI-driven systems. In Microsoft’s case, MAPP is a remarkably small team of humans and, being security folk at Microsoft, they’re not always the loudest or most popular voice in Redmond.

Add the mercurial nature of finders, who may or may not provide full details on their bugs (ask any MSRC person how often they receive a crash dump and a demand for payment) and may or may not disclose their finds publicly if they don’t feel they’re getting sufficient response.

Do not add the kind of defender staffing needed to handle a massive increase in volume — either in MAPP or in the individual product groups building the actual patches — because in the AI era, it’s likely the companies won’t add it either.

The cracks are showing. Microsoft has been compelled to issue OOB (out-of-band) releases and servicing updates monthly since July, often within just a few days of the original release.

OOBs are important enough to merit release outside the usual patch cadence, often because there’s a security issue involved, but they’re tough on administrators already wading through a slew of regularly scheduled patching. With volumes high, patch testers may rush or elide certain steps, such as checking that a fix doesn’t break something deeper in the system.

Nadella told the DealBook audience this week that a patch for a critical bank system might once have been tested for a week. Now, he said, the patch “needs to go instantly from being available to being patched in production because it’s being exploited in the wild. So that’s going to create instability.”

Think of MAPP as Mariners catcher Dan Wilson in 2001: a pro handling a staff of skilled human pitchers, one pitch at a time. Now it’s 2026, and the pitchers have been swapped for a bank of mediocre pitching machines all slinging fastballs at … 2026-era Dan Wilson.

Here, again, AI can’t fix a system that requires defenders to catch every pitch. Secondary lines of defense that have worked in the past — for instance, the requirement by various regulatory programs that enterprises ascertain that patches are safe for their own systems — rely heavily on companies sending out the soundest possible patches.

Managed service providers might at least make sure that patches are flagged to customers as available, relevant and urgent. But they’re in no position to do much more than trust that what’s shipped is safe to deploy and react when things go wrong.

Even that gets harder when a bug kneecaps the security tools they depend on. Last week’s Microsoft 365 outage affected parts of Microsoft Defender that interact with Exchange Online.

It’s not just Microsoft, of course. The Linux community is seriously suffering, and both Apple and (especially) Google have struggled with patch-release quality in 2026 as evidenced by their own emergency updates and post-release device instability reports. Industry-wide, ecosystems built around reliable, transparent patch cadences and patch-development processes have never seemed more in danger of being overwhelmed.

What needs to happen? Sure, build those AI systems; the marketing people will be pleased. It’ll speed up some parts of the process. But a system capable of replicating, every time, the human judgment calls that go into the patch-release process isn’t going to magically create itself; people have to create it.

Is there some chance that the deluge will ebb — that researchers and their tools will at some point finally find all the bugs in all the world’s products?

It’s a pretty thought, and some relief may eventually come. When more and more unrelated finders turn up the same bug, it gives one hope that the set is finite. But no one knows when relief will come, and most folk in tech suspect that really, no, there is no bottom to the bug barrel. Nor, for the foreseeable future, any rest for the weary.

GTA 6: How to Escape Police & Clear Heat

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

If you've ever played a GTA game prior to Grand Theft Auto 6, you're probably already familiar with the concept of Heat: commit a crime, and get chased by the cops. The more heinous your crimes, the greater your Wanted level — and the more forceful the police will be in taking you down.

But Heat and police pursuit work a little differently in GTA 6. While previous games make it fairly easy to escape the cops — just stay out of sight until they give up — there's a lot more at play in the latest entry. The police will track certain details about you, and recognizing what they are and how to change them could save you a lot of trouble.

Firstly, you generally don't get a Wanted level in GTA 6 by committing a crime. There are only a select few cases where this is true: if you enter a high-security area, like a military base or steal a plane from an airport, people will know immediately, and the cops will begin pursuit.

In order for you to get a Wanted level for the majority of your crimes in GTA 6, though, someone will actually have to report them, and the police will have to respond. This means you may be able to get away with murder in more isolated areas, but even a minor crime in a densely populated area could result in the cops being called immediately. Using weapons with suppressors can make it harder for bystanders to notice.

So, say the cops are chasing you: you'll see one to six stars in the top-right corner of the screen, reflecting the intensity of the police response. On your mini-map, you'll likely notice that you're within a dark red circle. To end the pursuit, you'll need to get out of this circle and stay out of it until it disappears, thus breaking the line of sight.

Looking at the color of the stars in the top-right corner of your screen can help you understand what the police are doing:

So, when the stars turn white, you want to run until they turn fully red, then stay hidden until they're only outlined in red or disappear completely. You'll also hear the cops say that they've lost you over your in-game police scanner.

In general, the best way to do this is by driving. If you're in a fast, maneuverable vehicle, you may just be able to lose the cops by driving in a straight line. But if you continue straight for long enough, cops may try to trap you by setting up a barricade. Your best bet is to swerve down side streets and change up your direction to confuse them.

Generally, it's not advisable to try and fight the police directly; you'll always be outnumbered and outclassed. The more crimes you commit in front of them, the higher your Wanted level will get, and the more squad cars, SWAT teams, and helicopters they'll throw at you.

Now, things get a little more complicated. In GTA 6, the cops don't give up looking for you after you escape the search area; they'll continue actively investigating around the area where the crime was committed. If you're seen again, the chase will resume. You could lay low and avoid the area until their search is over, but you'll eventually have to get used to clearing your Heat so you can get around freely.

So, after you've gotten away from the cops, look below the stars in the top-right corner of your screen. You should see a series of red circles with icons in them — so-called "Heat Icons." These tell you what, if any, details the police will recognize about you. If you change them, you'll have no problem going incognito.

If you're caught by the cops again, your Heat Icons may reset as they get a fresh look at your appearance, your clothes, or your partner. Try to make sure you stay hidden until you're all clear.

However, if the area is still under active investigation, there's likely a larger police presence than normal. Any further crimes committed there will likely build on your previous Wanted level, and cops will start chasing you immediately. Better to keep your head down until the investigation is over.

Subagents, Built Live: Reviewer, Tester, Researcher, Doc Writer

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at youtube.com

The video version of this article, "Subagents, Built Live: Reviewer, Tester, Researcher, Doc Writer," goes public on YouTube on Friday, November 20, 2026, and shows all four configs written live in a real repo. This article is the written version, laid out so you can copy the structure.

Most people create a subagent by writing a personality. Friendly. Helpful. Expert at everything. Then they give it every tool in the box and wonder why it drifts, edits files it should only have read, and reports success nobody can check. That is a coworker with no job description and a master key.

A subagent is not a second generalist. It is a narrow brief with permissions attached: one job, a defined set of files it may read or write, a fixed report shape, and a list of things it must never do. The personality is irrelevant. The boundaries are the product.

This article builds four of them on the demo project from the earlier videos: a reviewer, a test writer, a researcher, and a doc writer.

Permissions are what separate roles, not tone of voice. If two subagents can both edit everything, you do not have roles. You have copies.

Two rules sit underneath all four configs. The reviewer is read only. And the one writer rule: exactly one agent has write access to a given file at a time, so subagents that need the same file run in sequence, never in parallel.

The reviewer's job, in one sentence: check a finished diff against the original packet and the repo rules, and report what is wrong.

Its packet has four fields: the original packet, the diff, the files changed, and the test command, from the repo rules file, not invented on the spot.

Its permissions are the whole design. Read any file. Run the test suite and read the output. Write nothing. No edits, no formatting fixes, no helpful little commits. Its report: findings first, by severity, each naming the file, what is wrong, and the evidence. Then what it checked, what it did not check, and a verdict: accept, or send back. Never do: never fix a finding yourself, never soften one to be polite, never approve work you did not read, never edit tests.

The test writer's job: write tests for a stated behavior, from the spec in its packet. Tests only. Never the implementation.

Its packet: the behavior spec with examples, the function signature, and the existing test file to copy style from. Permissions: write only in tests, only the file named in the packet. Read the app code, run the suite, touch no implementation.

In the demo, its new tests fail, because the function under test is still a stub. That failure is the correct result, and its report says so plainly: tests written, command run, real result, cases left out. Never do: never edit a test to match wrong output, never write the implementation to make its own tests pass, never delete or weaken an existing test, never report a skipped test as passing. Tests are the spec.

The researcher's job: answer one specific question from named sources, and return a short brief the orchestrator can act on.

Its packet is one question in one sentence, the decision that answer will feed, and the sources it may use, official docs first. If the question takes two sentences, it is two packets. Permissions: read only on the repo, plus the named sources. It writes nothing in the repo. Its only product is the brief.

The brief has a fixed shape: the answer in the first two lines, then evidence with each source named, then what it could not confirm, labelled unconfirmed and never guessed at. Never do: never turn a brief into a code change, never present an unconfirmed claim as a fact, never widen the question, never bury the answer. Bulk reading is a cheaper model job, safe only because the format does the disciplining.

The doc writer's job: turn finished, verified work into documentation that matches what the code actually does: a README section, a usage example, a changelog entry.

It runs last, and its packet proves it: the accepted diff, the reviewer verdict, and a usage example that has actually been run. No accepted diff, no docs. Permissions: write only in the documentation files named in its packet, read code and tests freely, run examples, change no code, ever. If code and intended docs disagree, the disagreement goes in its report, not papered over. Its report is the doc change, the example with its real run output, the disagreements list, and what it left out.

Never do: never document a feature that is not in the accepted code, never invent example output, never describe planned behavior in the present tense. The example run is not optional.

Now the four on one task, in order, with who writes what.

The test writer goes first, writes only the test file, and its tests fail honestly against the stub. Then one worker implements the change in one file. The reviewer goes next and writes nothing: it reads the diff, runs the full suite, and returns findings or a verdict. If a factual question came up, the researcher answers it in a brief; it never enters the code path itself. The doc writer goes last, after acceptance, with an example it ran itself.

At every step you can point at exactly who was allowed to write the file that changed. Fail that pointing test and you do not have subagents. You have a crowd.

The pattern to design against is a reviewer with write access. It finds a real problem in a diff. Instead of reporting it, it quietly fixes the code itself, because fixing feels more helpful than reporting.

Three things are lost at once. Nobody learns the worker made the mistake. The fix was never reviewed, because the reviewer checked its own work. And the report says clean, so the record shows a diff that never had a problem. A lie by omission.

You do not fix this with a sterner prompt. You fix it structurally, by taking away the write permission. Read only is the first permission decision in every config here.

When We Forgive, What New Meaning Do We Find in Life?

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

Suffering could lead to despair, depression, hopelessness, and even not liking oneself (Yu et al. 2021; Reed & Enright, 2006). Yet one aspect of the forgiveness process, especially as one nears the end of the forgiveness journey, is the sometimes surprising development of finding meaning in that suffering and a new purpose in life. Finding meaning centers on an important question: What have I learned from going through this suffering, and what have I learned as I walked the journey of forgiveness? This is a cognitive exercise that can lead to new short-term and long-term goals (Enright, 2015). In other words, the cognitive exercise of finding meaning can lead to behaviors that support new purposes in one’s life.

The psychotherapist and Holocaust survivor, Viktor Frankl (1969/2014), pioneered this idea of finding meaning in suffering as he endured the persecution of his imprisonment. His motivation was to resist being destroyed by the abuse. By finding meaning, one can transcend suffering and make it more bearable.

When I discuss the theme of finding meaning in suffering through forgiveness, I am not focusing on the injustice but on its aftermath. The basic question is this: What are you learning about life as you extend mercy to those who were deeply unfair to you? This post explores the different meanings people tend to generate as they forgive.

Let us first start by examining what finding meaning in suffering is not, because it can be misunderstood.

Consider three themes that sometimes are confused with finding meaning in what an unjustly acting person has done to the forgiver.

First, the forgiver is not denying the disappointment, sadness, anger, or grief in what was suffered. The injustice did happen, and the negative response is real. In other words, finding meaning is not some kind of non-feeling, interpreted, at least temporarily, as relief from the effects of the injustice.

Second, the person does not play games with themselves, stating, for example, “Oh well, things happen for a reason, and I’ll just make the best of it by accepting it.” Yes, people can “make the best of it,” but if this is an exclusive meaning of suffering, the one who forgives is not going after the woundedness inside, which may linger for a long time. The “oh well” approach is passive. To heal from the effects of the injustice, a person needs a more active approach to the pain.

Third, the forgiver does not distort reality by thinking, “Well, all things happen for good reasons, and so I will try to see the good in what happened to me.” The injustice was not good. Some aspects of what happens after the injustice may be good, but the injustice itself is not.

1. Being more sensitive to the suffering of others, even those who never hurt you: James, upon forgiving, realized he had what he called a “short temper,” in which he imposed his anger on unsuspecting others. He has now committed, for example, to being more patient and kind to cashiers in grocery stores and pharmacies as he waits in line. He is more sensitive to the fatigue and stress of those working behind the cash register. This gives him a new sense of purpose, as he recognizes his intemperance in these contexts and now works to overcome his anger.

2. Being motivated to help others in their suffering: When Dr. Freedman (Freedman & Enright, 1996) worked with female incest survivors on a long-term forgiveness intervention, she saw that many of them, upon forgiving, became motivated to help other incest survivors overcome their deep resentment. They were not choosing as a goal to become mental health professionals, but instead to be of assistance to others who have suffered as they have.

As another example, I talked with a man who is imprisoned for life. Upon forgiving those who abused him, he said to me that he knows he will never get out of the correctional institution, but now he is motivated to help his cellmates forgive and to be unburdened of their anger. This led to a new purpose, or the behavioral manifestation of this desire to assist others.

3. Standing in the pain so you do not throw that pain onto others: The paradox of standing in the pain is this: As people bear the negative effects of unjust treatment by not throwing that pain back to the offending person or to unsuspecting others, the pain-bearers realize they are stronger than they thought. They see the positive outcomes of not seeking revenge. As Gandhi said, if we keep taking an eye for an eye, the whole world will be blind. The pain-bearers realize they can now translate this into a new purpose of contributing to a more peaceful world.

4. Forgiving helps us think more deeply about justice: If people are continually treated unjustly, they can find meaning in living a life of mercy and fairness integrated together. Thus, their understanding of justice can become wiser, and therefore their appropriation of justice can become more helpful to others.

5. Eventually, you can develop an overall philosophy of life, such as committing to be a conduit of good in this world: This is what Dr. Frankl called supra-meaning, or a new philosophy of life that encompasses an overall approach to how one interacts with others and why one does so in this way. For example, a person can commit to living a life of greater respect, kindness, and love toward both offenders and others. For example, Sally decided to resist impatience and negative emotions toward her own children, as a way of life in her family, so that these life-giving moral virtues can become norms there.

6. As people find meaning in their suffering, they can put their future purpose in life into sharper perspective: Finding meaning, in other words, becomes the cognitive rationale and emotional motivation to seek what matters to them.

In summary, our suffering is not for nothing. People who suffer and forgive can develop as they think carefully about what they are learning about suffering, their own humanity, and life itself. As Dr. Frankl learned and passed his wisdom on to us, finding meaning in suffering opens unexpected doors for survival and growing in our humanity.
