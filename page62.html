GTA 6 Could Save You $5,000 According To Self-Made Millionaire

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

GTA 6 is the first new game since Mario Kart World to have an $80 standard edition, with a $100 ultimate edition if you want access to all of its in-game stores. That’s a lot of money for just one game, except a self-made millionaire has explained how the one-off, $100 expenditure could end up saving you thousands in the long run.

The millionaire in question is Mark Tilbury, a British millionaire who, among other things, uses social media to share investment and money-saving tips. His latest video has quickly gone viral as it explains why buying a $100 copy of GTA 6 could end up saving you $5,000.

Tilbury begins by breaking down how much entertainment you’re getting per hour in GTA 6, based on the reveal by Rockstar that its story will take 80 hours to play through. Dollars-per-hour isn’t a metric I typically like to consider when weighing up the value of a game, but for the sake of this fun exercise, let’s hear Tilbury out.

If you were to play GTA 6 for 80 hours and then never touch it again, you’d have paid $1.25 for the experience. That’s not bad at all. This is where Tilbury loses me a little, though. He then notes that once you add in side content and map exploration, the price-per-hour for GTA 6 drops to just 50 cents. That’s 200 hours, and while I’m sure many, if not most of us will spend that much time in Leonida, if not more, he doesn’t explain where that 200-hour figure comes from.

Regardless of how much you end up paying per hour for GTA 6, Tilbury still hasn’t explained how buying it for $100 could end up saving you $5,000. The start of the road to that number begins with the assumption that GTA 6 owners will play the game in four hour chunks. Divide that 200-hour total by four, and you get 50 play sessions.

That means, at least according to Tilbury, that your GTA 6 playthrough will result in you opting to not go out and paint the town red at least 50 times. The millionaire hypothesizes that you might spend $100 on any given night out, multiply that number by the 50 nights out you’re not having because you’re at home running around Vice City, and that’s how he concludes that a $100 copy of GTA 6 could end up saving you a cool 5k.

There are a lot of assumptions being made here. Assumptions that not only won’t apply to everyone playing GTA 6, but probably won’t even apply to the average GTA 6 player. From the hours spent with the game to how long they’re playing it each time. Most of all, though, the idea that someone is choosing to stay in and play GTA 6 over going out and spending $100 each time not once, not twice, but 50 times - that’s just not happening. The big takeaway here, again, as much as I don’t like to boil a game down to dollars spent vs. hours played, is that for $80, or $100, you are almost certainly going to get your money’s worth out of GTA 6.

Rising temperatures may have a dangerous effect on mental health

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

New research reveals how extreme heat may increase suicide risk by disrupting sleep and emotional regulation, highlighting a hidden mental health threat.

Extreme heat can do more than exhaust the body. It may also disrupt sleep, weaken emotional control, and increase vulnerability to serious psychological distress.

A new study has identified temperatures at which extreme heat is associated with increased psychological distress, revealing how disrupted sleep and emotional regulation may contribute to suicide risk.

The research was led by scientists from Georgetown University and the World Bank. It draws on a nationally representative panel study of nearly 7,500 adults in Bangladesh.

The researchers found that extreme heat was associated with an increased risk of suicide-related thoughts, with disrupted sleep playing a surprisingly important role.

“Rising global temperatures threaten human mental health on a massive scale,” said lead author Syed Shabab Wahid, an assistant professor of global health at Georgetown.

“In climate-vulnerable nations such as Bangladesh, this heat stress can translate into severe psychological strain and elevated suicide risk.”

The study tracked two interconnected pathways between heat and suicidal cognition: disrupted sleep and emotional dysregulation.

The association between temperature and suicide-related thoughts became statistically significant at 28°C and was strongest at 35°C.

Temperatures this high are common in Bangladesh during the summer months.

At that upper threshold, nearly 30 percent of the total association between heat and suicide risk ran through poor sleep quality alone. Emotional dysregulation accounted for a smaller but still significant 1.8 percent.

Together, poor sleep and difficulties regulating emotions statistically accounted for roughly a third of the observed relationship between heat and suicide-related thoughts.

The study design is worth understanding, because it shaped what the researchers could and couldn’t conclude.

Participants were interviewed twice – once in winter (January–February 2024) and once at the height of summer (May–June 2024).

Suicide risk was measured using the Brief Suicide Cognitions Scale, a validated tool that captures the sense of unbearability, unlovability, and unsolvability that precedes suicidal behavior.

Heat exposure was operationalized as the number of days with maximum temperatures at or above 35°C in the two weeks prior to each interview.

The panel structure meant the same individuals answered both rounds, which allowed the team to compare the same person’s mental state across seasons.

Summer scores were meaningfully worse: average suicide cognition scores rose from 10.99 in winter to 12.96 in summer.

Sleep quality declined. Emotional regulation worsened. Both effects were consistent across age groups, though older adults and women showed the steepest deterioration.

“Imagine a smartphone with a drained battery, overheating processor, and critical apps crashing all at once. Sustained ambient heat acts on the human brain and body in the same way,” Wahid explained.

“The body cannot cool down at night, leading to broken rest and depleting mental reserves. When the emotional processor overheats without restorative recovery, coping mechanisms collapse under stress.”

The study found that risk was notably higher during summer among less educated and poorer populations.

These differences may reflect unequal access to cooling, greater exposure to outdoor heat, and housing conditions that make it harder to escape high temperatures.

Individuals with disabilities showed consistently elevated risk across seasons. Unemployed people reported higher suicide cognition scores than those in most other employment categories.

Urban residents were more susceptible than rural ones. This may reflect the urban heat island effect. Concrete and asphalt absorb and radiate heat in ways that push nighttime temperatures higher.

There’s a global dimension to these findings that goes beyond Bangladesh.

Previous meta-analyses have found that a 1°C increase in monthly mean temperature is associated with a 1.5 to 1.7 percent increase in suicidal outcomes.

Bangladesh sits in a region the IPCC projects will experience some of the worst heatwaves of the coming decades. But the mechanisms the study identified are not unique to South Asia.

“Rather than treating climate-related mental health crises as unpreventable events, public health systems can now focus on intermediate, modifiable targets,” Wahid said. Sleep is one of them.

“Health agencies can monitor heat waves alongside sleep quality disruption indicators to mobilize preventative interventions. Interventions centered on sleep preservation, household cooling access, and community-based emotional regulation training should serve as effective suicide prevention tools during heatwaves.”

The paper points toward green roofing, passive architecture, and low-cost cooling technologies as structural interventions with documented effects on indoor temperatures in Bangladesh.

A pilot study found that buildings with vegetation-covered green roofing maintained temperatures 3.5°C lower than bare rooftops.

Such reductions could help make indoor environments more comfortable during extreme heat, potentially protecting sleep and reducing heat-related stress.

The study has some important limitations.

First, it relies on ambient outdoor temperature rather than indoor measurements. That’s a known proxy problem in Bangladesh, where air conditioning is rare and indoor heat tracks outdoor heat closely.

Humidity data wasn’t available, meaning the full thermal stress experienced by respondents – particularly relevant in a country where wet-bulb temperatures can reach dangerous levels – is likely underestimated.

What the study offers is new evidence that poor sleep and difficulties regulating emotions may help explain the relationship between extreme heat and suicide-related thoughts.

These findings point toward practical ways to reduce the psychological toll of rising temperatures.

Protecting sleep, improving access to cooling, and supporting mental health could become increasingly important as extreme heat becomes more common.

The study is published in the journal Environmental Research.

Is it time to admit that Newcastle United have made a huge mistake?

“Any man can make mistakes, but only an idiot persists in his error” – Marcus Tullius Cicero

After another shocker of a Newcastle United performance against Everton last night, there is a growing anger and frustration in the fanbase with the direction (or lack of) of the team on field.

Consistent underperformers, no clear strategy, and players proven not to be good enough are selected regardless, though to be fair, managing to be toothless up top as well as all over at the back is impressive.

The knives are sharpening for Matthias Jaissle and he’s looking to be all mouth and no trousers. But for me, appointing Jaissle, isn’t the biggest mistake we’ve made in the last year or so, that dubious honour goes to the appointment of Ross Wilson (pictured at the top with David Hopkinson).

You might think and say well Wilson actually had a pretty decent CV, on paper it looks good, he was involved at Southampton when they brough Van Dijk in, was at Rangers when they broke Celtic’s grip on the SPL and was at Forest when they stayed up and then qualified for Europe.

Delve a bit deeper though and Rangers fans tend to give credit to his predecessor and are still trying to get rid of some of his flops and elsewhere while he’s had successes his overall recruitment strategy is scattergun at best. Theres no clear player type of strategy picked up, very opportunistic and he’s got good players at good prices (particularly getting Anderson from us) but it seems the strategy is slinging a lot at the wall and seeing what sticks.

If you look back since the takeover we’ve had two highly thought of Directors of football come in and go not long afterwards due to ‘issues’ with others at the club, so despite having a reasonable CV he didn’t come in with as big a reputation and to this observer his primary attribute was he was affable to Eddie and his nephew, which at the time made some sense as Eddie had put together an all time season in 24/25 and looked like he’d be our manager for years to come even after that summer of 25.

So Ross Wilson came in after that summer, which now with the benefit of hindsight has been nothing short of disastrous, with Elanga, Wissa and Ramsey being total overpays (as well as two of them debatably being our biggest wastes of money ever) and Woltemade being a glass slipper when we needed a steel toe capped boot.

Of course, we can’t put any of that on Wilson as he didn’t join till afterwards and it would be a challenge for even the best directors of football to deal with the fallout of it all.

The first warning signs for me came in the January 26 transfer window, when the team was on the ropes, Eddie was struggling and we didn’t bring in any first team recruits to assist a struggling squad. Now I know some of you will bring up the likes of PSR, FFP, SKY 6, Ashley, the Illuminati as reasons why we didn’t spend, but even one signing could have reinvigorated the squad and at least limited the late season slide we went on.

The risk is we’d have to sell at the end of the season to cover ourselves but we had to sell in any case because we plummeted down the league and didn’t even get any European football in one of the weakest Premier Leagues I can remember. It showed very little foresight for Ross Wilson to sit on his hands when the team was visibly on the ropes.

We then come to the end of the season where we have plummeted, put together some Bruce level performances and lost another derby which allowed our dear rivals to experience Europe for the first time in a lifetime. The performances were so poor and we looked totally bereft of ideas. Eddie Howe looked a shell of the man who’d once had us putting it up anyone we came up against.

The decision was there to be made, all logic pointed to a departure of a club legend but as Eddie Howe didn’t quit, Ross Wilson didn’t make the decision to let him go. There’s always the loyalty argument but at that stage it was just ignoring the evidence your eyes presented to you to keep him.

This eventually backfired as by mid pre-season Eddie took the decision out of his hands and left for some well-earned rest and relaxation. This put us further on the back foot as a lot of managers had already moved at the end of the previous season and we’d already started our season’s preparations expecting Eddie Howe to be our manager.

The next managerial move was to bring in Matthias Jaissle, who talked one hell of a game, got to give him and the club credit as the way he was presented looked amazing and had most of us very excited for the potential but then the football started. The ‘vertical football’ promised turned out to be an Allardyce like hoofball without the defensive solidity and if they’d played Stelios as their CF rather than Davies.

As a toon fan Ive had plenty of opportunity to make judgement on God awful managers and all I can see with Jaissle is another one.

Could Wilson have been duped like the rest of us? Of course he could of, but even if he was his actions with 50m+ in the bank to go and sign a left winger who thinks they are a forward when we needed a proper physical CF, a 10, pacy ball playing CBs and an inverted RW was just negligent. Don’t get me wrong, Matias Fernandez-Pardo could turn out great, but it was such a bad use of resources.

In a similar note the signing of Ewen Jaouen is looking like excess which wasn’t needed, again he could be great but spending eight figures on a back-up goalkeeper is just wasteful at best. Signing a bunch of youngsters for a manager who won’t give them minutes in general is foolish.

Overall, regardless of the many flaws Matthias Jaissle has shown, Ross Wilson hasn’t built a squad that’s fit for purpose for the system that Wilson hired the new Head Coach for.

Looking at what we did on outgoings we can also see issues with the way Wilson worked, our pet media liked to say we are trying to use the Brighton model, ignoring that the Brighton model is to bring in younger players who have time to settle in before selling your bigger names. This year we sold all three of Bruno, Gordon, and Tonali before bringing in replacements, putting us straight on the back foot.

We have different opinions on the value we got for those three players (personally I think we sold too early) but having a replacement ready for none of them just shows Ross Wilson hasn’t prepared properly.

Looking at other decisions made over the summer, giving Harvey Barnes an extension rather than cashing in seems similar to previous decisions not to cash in on the likes of Miggy/Wilson after they’d had hot streaks. Barnes puts up great goals and assists stats, and you’ll get the argument ‘where would we be without his goals,’ but I disagree and think a better all-round player will benefit the team more eventually.

The Palace game is the prime example of what Barnes is, great finish for his goal but the number of attacks of ours that broke down because of his unwillingness/inability to beat a man, poor passing and poor decisions was in double figures. I think he’s an effective sub but the time to cash in was this summer gone.

The worst decisions though were to come and that was the decisions not to take the cash for both Murphy and Willock at the end of the window, the decision just tells everyone in the squad that they are the standard.

Murphy’s hot spell is long in the past, he can put in the occasional good cross (maybe one in three or four) but his all-round games makes Barnes look like the complete winger, he’s often terrified in possession and will put teammates under pressure or just play it to nobody so he doesn’t have it. You can also add his age, so to turn down the claimed £15m transfer fee is ridiculous. You may say ‘but he’s a good presence’ turning down £15m (as well as keeping Premier League wages) for someone who smiles and claps is a bit much. As for NUFC needing options on the right – Elanga, Pardo, Osula, Barnes and Wissa have all played on the right at some point and Joelinton and Ramsey have played on the left.

Joe Willock was an afterthought for Eddie Howe, but to his credit this season he’s popped up with some key goals and his chaotic style seems to suit our headless gameplan a little bit at least, though to turn down whatever transfer fee could have been banked in the summer is a failure in long-term squad building.

Overall, we may not quite be a sinking ship just yet due to riding our luck so far, but we can’t continue to go on like this. To fire Matthias Jaissle without also firing Ross Wilson would be like getting rid of the monkey without the organ grinder, so we need to remove Wilson from his position too, to try and get back on the right track.

I’m sure some will say you need to give directors of football time, but sometimes when something is wrong you just need to bite the bullet and move on, rather than persisting with the mistake.

Comment: Some feel it was harsh to sack Nuno now, but truthfully, he could have gone in the summer. The bigger question is whether his reported replacement is the right pick

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

“I am always under pressure, every day,” Nuno Espirito Santo said in the wake of Saturday’s 3-1 loss to West Brom. Not any more, he’s not.

An optimistic outlook on his removal as West Ham coach is that it offers respite. Respite from that pressure, which has never truly waned in his 13 months in charge. It might seem a ruthless move by the east London club to fire him “so early” into the season, but West Ham were right to be ruthless.

But let’s not forget that the Championship season is already a quarter of the way done, and true ruthlessness would have entailed sacking the Portuguese over the summer, after he failed to stop West Ham from plummeting into the Championship – despite taking over from Graham Potter in late September.

The former Nottingham Forest and Tottenham coach never truly steadied the ship in the Premier League. There was barely even a new-manager bounce to be buoyant about, never a moment when Hammers fans could breathe easily, and even the seas of the second tier (which he navigated so well by winning the league with Wolves in 2017/18) proved too choppy this time around.

That is not to say his West Ham side have been disastrous this season, but with immediate promotion essential from a financial standpoint – and in order to keep the best of this squad together – six wins, four draws, and three losses were never going to instil adequate confidence. Eighth place, one quarter of the way through the season, is too risky a spot.

It is OK to drop points intermittently in such a long, arduous campaign, but the move to sack Nuno was motivated by West Ham’s fear of those points practically spilling out underneath them, like marbles.

It would, of course, have been nice for fans to see a move not motivated by fear; perhaps a change in the summer would have felt harsh, but it would have been proactive, and there is an argument it would have felt encouraging in a different sense.

On the topic of encouragement, West Ham’s free-scoring start to the season was a refreshing sight after recent times at London Stadium, and they remain the highest-scoring team in the division, but their goal difference has already been matched.

And for Nuno, 52, and his players, the issue has not just been conceding too many goals, but also the timing of those errors: they have conceded first in nine out of their 16 games in all competitions. That has signalled a concerning fragility, which has tasked West Ham with fighting back far too often – in a season when many expected them to lead from the front, no less.

Admittedly, there was a degree of naivety to this sentiment. Over the summer, it was all too easy for neutral fans to assume that West Ham would walk the Championship, when supporters of the east London club knew how misguided these claims were. You need look no further than West Ham’s previous outing in the Championship, in 2011/12, when they let a top-two finish slip away before going life-and-death with Blackpool in the play-off final.

Again, that is far too risky a spot this time around, and cannot be justified when you own the most expensive squad in the league (at an estimated £282m, with the £18m gap to Wolves proving substantial at this level).

There is surely greater quality at West Ham than any other club in the Championship, and some observers will feel a coach would not need to get too much right to promote the Hammers. In that regard, Nuno was doing too much wrong.

With Scott Parker immediately emerging as the frontrunner to replace him, perhaps the conversation can move away from risk. But only in the short-term, you would think.

Parker, a former Hammer, secured promotion with Fulham, Bournemouth and Burnley in his first season in charge of each club. That record is remarkable. It is simply a shame the stories didn’t end there. Fulham and Burnley were immediately relegated in their subsequent seasons, while Parker left Bournemouth by the end of August after a shocking start to life in the Premier League.

Of course, as a West Ham fan, you might not care to look that far ahead. The priority is purely promotion to the Premier League.
