8 foods that may help you sleep, from tart cherries to chamomile tea

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Tart cherries, night milk, kiwi, and chamomile tea appear among 8 foods the Sleep Foundation links to better rest.

Anyone who has gulped a second cup of coffee to survive a long afternoon, or dozed on the couch after Thanksgiving dinner, has felt food tilt a person toward alertness or drowsiness. The same plate that props up a workday can shape the hours after dark. Diet and sleep are both intricate systems, and each one reaches into the other. No single snack guarantees a quiet night, and nothing on a grocery shelf works as a magic switch. Certain foods and drinks may make a full night of rest easier to reach. Some contain nutrients and compounds that influence sleep-related hormones and brain activity, from melatonin production to blood sugar levels that hold steady overnight.

The body's sleep chemistry depends on what arrives on the plate. Melatonin, a hormone the pineal gland produces, signals when sleep should begin and keeps the circadian rhythm on schedule, and its output tends to fall with age. Tryptophan, an essential amino acid, must come from food because the body cannot make it. The body converts it into melatonin and into serotonin, a neurotransmitter tied to sleep, mood, and pain. Carbohydrates help the brain process tryptophan. The wrong late-night choices can undercut these processes. Citrus, tomatoes, and other acidic foods can raise stomach acid and bring on reflux. One study found that caffeine a person drank six hours before bed still affected sleep quality, and the stimulant has an average half-life of five hours in most healthy adults. Whole foods may hold advantages over pills. They deliver vitamins, minerals, and micronutrients that work in concert, along with fiber, which tracks with deeper sleep, and antioxidants that help ward off disease. Anyone weighing a supplement to fill a nutritional gap should consult a health care provider before starting it.

Sleep Foundation built its guide to sleep-friendly foods and drinks on 24 references, most of them published studies, and reports no original testing of its own. Eric Suni wrote the piece, which bears an Aug. 5, 2025, update date. Lulu Guo, MD, a board-certified sleep medicine physician, reviewed it for medical accuracy. Guo urges readers to focus on healthy eating patterns across the whole day instead of pinning their hopes on one food or drink. The guide sorts its picks by the nutrient they supply, with sections for melatonin, tryptophan, and magnesium and a fourth set of foods that work through other compounds. It lists foods to skip before bed and answers common questions about supplements and caffeine timing. Advertising supports the site, which says it does not endorse third-party products. The eight foods that follow form a selection from that longer guide, in the order the source presents them, and they appear without any ranking.

Tart cherries hold above-average concentrations of melatonin, the hormone that cues sleep, and the Sleep Foundation names that trait as one possible reason the fruit may help people doze off. Some shoppers know them as sour cherries, a label that captures how their flavor departs from the sweet varieties. The group spans several cultivars, among them Richmond, Montmorency, and English morello.

Grocers stock the fruit whole or pressed into tart cherry juice. Bakers reach for the tart type in pies and other baked goods, and fresh tart cherries can be tough to find, according to Cleveland Clinic. Scarce fresh fruit explains why most recommendations steer people toward the juice over the whole cherry. A bottle gives the easiest entry point for anyone hoping to test the fruit at bedtime.

Several studies have found that people who drink tart cherry juice sleep better. Melatonin may not account for the whole benefit. The Sleep Foundation adds that the cherries may exert an antioxidant action that favors sleep, a second possible route to a better night.

A Cleveland Clinic expert noted that tart cherries also contain tryptophan. The expert called it plausible that the amino acid and the fruit's melatonin could help people relax and sleep better. The body converts tryptophan into melatonin, which means each serving may deliver the hormone itself along with a building block for more.

Cleveland Clinic reports that early research on the juice has been small and short on rigor. The small studies suggest the drink may lengthen sleep and improve its quality, and scientists need more work to confirm the signal. The Cleveland Clinic expert cautioned that strong scientific evidence for a sleep benefit is still lacking. Two candidate mechanisms and a string of encouraging findings make tart cherries a credible bedtime option, one still built on early evidence.

Milk drawn at night holds nearly 10 times more melatonin than milk collected during the day. The evening yield goes by the name night milk, and the Sleep Foundation identifies milking time as a major influence on how much of the sleep hormone the drink contains. Flip the comparison and a daytime glass carries about one-tenth of the melatonin in an equal pour of night milk.

The gap turns milk into a drink whose sleep chemistry shifts with the clock. Two glasses can look identical yet differ several times over in the hormone that cues the body to wind down. Among the foods the Sleep Foundation credits with melatonin, milk stands out because the hour of collection helps set the dose.

Night milk produced calming, sedative, and sleep-promoting effects in mice, and researchers likened its impact to diazepam. The result comes from animal research and cannot predict how people will respond. Even so, the mouse findings suggest the extra hormone in evening milk can do measurable work in a living body.

Warm milk at bedtime has a long history as a home remedy, and the Sleep Foundation argues that real science supports the drink as a sleep aid. A heated mug may also help through comfort. If a warm cup relaxes someone as part of a nightly routine, it could ease the way to sleep even when the benefit amounts to a placebo.

Milk offers two separate paths to rest. One runs through the melatonin it carries, a supply concentrated in milk drawn after dark. The other runs through the soothing habit of a warm drink before bed, which can work whatever the hormone count. A sleeper chasing the chemical benefit would gain the most from night milk, while the ritual benefit asks for nothing more than a warm mug and a bedtime routine.

Fatty fish supplies vitamin D and omega-3 fatty acids, two nutrients researchers believe can help people sleep. Each one has a hand in how the body releases serotonin and keeps its levels in balance.

Fish also turns up on the Sleep Foundation's list of tryptophan sources, next to turkey, chicken, eggs, cheese, edamame, tofu, peanuts, quinoa, and pumpkin seeds. The amino acid serves as raw material for serotonin, so a single serving touches two stages of the same chain. Tryptophan builds the neurotransmitter, and vitamin D and omega-3s help govern its release.

Pairing fish with carbohydrates may give its tryptophan a clearer path. The guide says a moderate portion of tryptophan-bearing protein eaten alongside carbs may help the amino acid reach the brain. It traces the drowsiness people blame on turkey to that same pairing. A fish fillet over rice fits the pattern the source sets out for poultry.

The guide presents the fish benefit as a belief researchers hold and attaches no serving size or expected effect to it. Fatty fish earns its place through nutrients that act on serotonin from both ends, though the guide leaves the size of any sleep payoff unmeasured.

Cheese delivers a high dose of tryptophan, and the Sleep Foundation credits that amino acid with most of the sleep benefit linked to dairy. Some research has found a strong connection between dairy foods such as cheese and the quality of a person's rest, the guide reports.

Cheese appears on the guide's quick-reference list of tryptophan sources, alongside turkey, chicken, fish, eggs, edamame, tofu, peanuts, quinoa, and pumpkin seeds. It stands as the lone milk-based entry among those 10 foods. The listing gives people who skip meat and fish one more option, beside eggs and plant foods such as tofu, for an evening meal or snack.

Tryptophan gives cheese a sleep pathway separate from milk's, whose appeal in the guide hinges on the melatonin it holds. The guide's entry on cheese names no melatonin content and leans mainly on the amino acid, which hands the body raw material for building its own sleep chemistry. The entry also points to serotonin, calcium, and selenium, a mineral whose antioxidant properties may improve sleep. Cheese thus arrives with one finished sleep compound, serotonin, alongside the building block, plus a mineral that works on a separate front.

Calcium turns up in milk and yogurt as well as cheese, and the body draws on the mineral to turn tryptophan into melatonin. AARP reports that nutritionist Bonnie Taub-Dix called cheese a protein-rich snack and said its calcium, magnesium, and tryptophan are all needed for good sleep. Her list adds magnesium to the nutrients a wedge of cheese brings to the plate.

Carbohydrates may ease the trip cheese's tryptophan makes into the brain, according to the guide, which says a moderate serving of tryptophan-bearing protein eaten with carbs may smooth that route. Its turkey section explains that carbohydrates help the amino acid cross the blood-brain barrier and lift serotonin levels. One bedtime snack idea tops whole-grain crackers with cheese. Such a pairing places protein and carbohydrate in the same bite, the mix the guide describes. Cheese eaten alone may do less for sleep than cheese eaten with a starch.

One cup of firm tofu holds 593 milligrams of tryptophan, which equals 212% of the recommended daily intake. A single cup of the firm block more than doubles a full day's target for the amino acid.

Soy foods appear twice on the Sleep Foundation's quick list of tryptophan sources, as edamame and as tofu. The list also names turkey, chicken, fish, eggs, cheese, peanuts, quinoa, and pumpkin seeds. Plants supply half of its 10 entries. Diners who skip meat or dairy can reach the amino acid through soy, which the guide describes as a group of foods with solid amounts of it.

Tofu's tryptophan may go further with a starchy partner. Elsewhere in the guide, the Sleep Foundation notes that a moderate amount of tryptophan-bearing protein eaten alongside carbohydrates may help the amino acid reach the brain. Its turkey entry makes the same point from another angle, crediting carbohydrates with helping tryptophan cross the blood-brain barrier and lift serotonin levels. Tofu served with rice, another food the guide covers, fits that pattern.

Soybeans carry isoflavones, a class of phytoestrogen. In the guide's account, these plant compounds may mimic human estrogen, a hormone that helps govern how long and how well people sleep. The source treats this hormonal link as a possibility and stops short of calling it proven. Its wording puts the isoflavone claim on softer footing than the tofu figure, which arrives as a measured quantity.

Soy may act on rest through two separate routes, supplying tryptophan in quantity while its isoflavones work on a hormonal pathway of their own. Among the eight foods in this selection, it stands alone in reaching sleep through an estrogen-like compound.

One cup of uncooked oats packs 66% of the daily value for magnesium and 130% of the recommended daily intake for tryptophan. The tryptophan share overshoots a full day's target by 30 percentage points, which means a single bowl meets the day's entire need for the amino acid and leaves a surplus. The magnesium share falls a fraction short of two-thirds of a day's requirement in the same serving. Oats hold close to double the magnesium and five times the tryptophan of a cup of boiled spinach, which the guide credits with 37% of the daily value for magnesium and 26% of the recommended intake for tryptophan.

Complex carbohydrates give oats a second sleep mechanism. The Sleep Foundation reports that these carbohydrates lift serotonin and reduce cortisol, a stress hormone capable of interfering with sleep. Turkey, by the guide's account, needs carbohydrates beside it on the plate to produce its familiar drowsiness. Oats bring the amino acid and the carbohydrates to the table in one food, with no side dish necessary. The guide tempers the carbohydrate case with a caveat, noting that research on carbohydrates and sleep has produced mixed results.

Old-fashioned, quick-cooking, and instant oats all belong to the rolled oat family, and thinner rolling shortens the time on the stove. Old-fashioned oats take the longest of the three and reward the wait with a heartier, chewier texture. Steel cut oats, also known as Irish oats, skip steaming and rolling and require more simmering than any other variety. Water and milk both work as the base liquid, while stock or broth turns the pot savory.

Overnight oats call for little or no heat. Old-fashioned or quick-cooking oats soak through the night in low-fat milk, then take a topping of fruit.

Oats supply plenty of fiber and several B vitamins, a profile they hold in common with barley, farro, and brown rice. The Sleep Foundation counts fiber among the whole-food advantages tied to deeper, more restorative sleep. Measured against the guide's own nutrient framework, oats reach three of its four sleep pathways: tryptophan, magnesium, and complex carbohydrates.

Two kiwis eaten an hour before bedtime helped people drift off sooner, stay asleep longer, and give their rest higher marks, according to the Sleep Foundation. The gains reach three separate stretches of the night. Falling asleep, staying asleep, and the perceived quality of slumber all improved in the same finding. One small snack touched the start, the length, and the feel of a night in bed.

Kiwifruit packs numerous vitamins and minerals. The Sleep Foundation singles out four standouts: vitamins C and E, potassium, and folate. A fruit eaten before lights-out brings that payload along with whatever influence it has on sleep.

Nobody has pinned down why kiwi helps. Researchers suspect antioxidant properties that suppress inflammatory markers. The fruit may correct folate deficiencies or act through serotonin, and some mix of these could account for the effect.

Folate links two threads of the evidence. The nutrient appears among kiwi's featured contents, and one candidate explanation credits the fruit with making up for a shortage of that same nutrient. People low in folate could gain the most if that theory holds.

Serotonin connects kiwi to a pathway the guide traces through several other foods. Cheese, tofu, and oats supply tryptophan, the raw material the body uses to build that neurotransmitter. Kiwi carries a high concentration of serotonin itself, and scientists count the trait among the possible reasons for its benefit. If the idea proves out, the fruit would arrive with the finished compound in hand, while those foods offer the building block.

Among the eight foods in this selection, kiwi alone comes with both a set portion and a set time to eat it. Tofu and oats list serving sizes, yet neither includes advice on when to eat. With the cause unsettled, the timing becomes the most usable part of the result. Anyone can copy a pair of fruit an hour before bed without waiting for the science to catch up.

Chamomile tea owes its drowsy reputation to apigenin, a flavonoid the Sleep Foundation identifies as the compound thought to bring on the effect. The plant chemical has anxiolytic and sedative properties, which allow it to act on the central nervous system. A cup may ease anxiety and help the body unwind.

Apigenin sets the drink apart from most other foods in this selection. Tart cherries, milk, tofu, and oats earn attention for melatonin, tryptophan, or magnesium, nutrients tied to the hormones and brain chemistry that govern sleep. Chamomile follows a separate path. Its flavonoid targets the restless, keyed-up state that can hold off slumber.

The Sleep Foundation frames apigenin's role as a working theory, and research in people lends that theory some weight. AARP reports that a 2017 study tested chamomile extract on adults age 60 and older. The group taking the extract posted significant gains in sleep quality over a placebo group. Hunnes, an expert in the same AARP feature, also credits apigenin with encouraging sleepiness, which puts the outlet's account in line with the Sleep Foundation's.

The study measured an extract, not a brewed cup, so the dose a mug of tea delivers remains an open question. Its participants also came from a single age group. Chamomile's clearest promise lies in calming an anxious mind before bed, and the firmest human evidence for it so far comes from older adults.

Pennsylvania is way ahead on NEVI charging – now comes another $37M

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Pennsylvania is putting $37 million in federal NEVI funding toward what PennDOT says will be 515 new public EV charging ports across the Philadelphia region, including curbside chargers.

The awards cover Bucks, Chester, Delaware, Montgomery, and Philadelphia counties. PennDOT says the projects will expand public charging availability by 27% across the region and 53% in Philadelphia.

For drivers without a driveway, curbside charging gives them somewhere to plug in while parked near home.

It’s Electric PA received $2.93 million for 157 on-street Level 2 ports across 13 Philadelphia neighborhoods. Voltpost received funding for another 62 curbside ports across the four suburban counties.

Fast charging is getting funding, too. PositivEnergy received $7.2 million for 54 DC fast-charging ports at 11 Philadelphia Parking Authority lots. Tesla received two $392,000 awards for eight ports each at Royal Farms in Broomall and Wawa in Plymouth Meeting.

The money comes from the federal National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure (NEVI) program, created by the Biden administration’s Bipartisan Infrastructure Law. Pennsylvania’s five-year allocation is $171.5 million.

NEVI initially focused on fast charging along designated highway corridors. States can spend the money more broadly after the Federal Highway Administration certifies that their corridor charging requirements have been met. Pennsylvania received that certification on September 22, 2025.

In fact, Pennsylvania has built 54 EV charging stations using NEVI funds so far – more than double the number of any other state.

PennDOT’s community charging program supports both Level 2 and DC fast charging. Its published program minimum requirements include at least four ports per proposed station, at least 6 kW per port, two ADA-compliant parking spaces, and J1772 or CCS connectors on every port. Equipment must also meet Buy America requirements.

PennDOT lists 71 awards in this southeastern round as part of its roughly $100 million statewide community charging effort.

Applications for the western region close November 20. Central and eastern Pennsylvania applications close March 12, 2027.

For the southeastern projects, PennDOT’s estimated schedule puts environmental reviews in November 2026 through January 2027, followed by agreement execution in January and February. Drivers will still have to wait for construction and commissioning before these awards become places to plug in.

The search giant is giving schools more control over AI, but there's a big catch

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

Google says it's giving schools more control over AI, but there's a meaningful catch that parents can't ignore. Schools that want to keep AI out of the classroom will also have to give up Google Search through student accounts.

In a Tuesday announcement, Google said it will disable access to several products, including Gemini and Google Search, for K–12 student accounts unless school administrators turn them on.

But there's one thing schools won't be able to do: Keep Google Search while blocking its built-in AI features.

"If a school is not comfortable with that and simply wants AI off, then they have the choice to not have Search on at all," Chris Phillips, Google's vice president of education, told Chalkbeat.

The changes, expected to roll out in early 2027, could leave school districts choosing between allowing AI-generated answers in Search or finding another way for students to research their homework.

Google has spent years integrating AI into its search engine, most visibly through AI Overviews, which provide generated summaries above traditional search results.

Now, those features are becoming a sticking point for schools that want students to research independently without relying on AI-generated answers.

"Search and AI are deeply integrated," Chris Phillips, Google's vice president for education, told The New York Times.

According to Phillips, students in schools that prohibit AI entirely will not have access to Google Search through their school accounts. Instead, they'll see a message indicating that the service is unavailable.

The restriction applies to school-managed accounts under the new controls,

Google has also made it clear that administrators can enable individual products, including Gemini and Google Search, rather than accepting every AI service as a package. But the problem is much greater because schools still cannot offer an entirely AI-free version of Google Search.

Alongside the changes to school controls, Google is expanding Guided Learning, an AI-powered feature designed to help students understand subjects rather than simply giving them answers.

Guided Learning is already available in Gemini, and Google announced that the feature is now available globally in Google Classroom.

The company is also bringing similar capabilities to Google Search, beginning with a pilot in selected U.S. schools later this month.

For example, a student who searches for help with a physics problem might receive questions and explanations that guide them through the solution rather than an immediate final answer.

Someone researching a book report could receive prompts encouraging them to think through the material. Google calls this approach "productive struggle," the idea that students learn more when they work through challenges rather than immediately receiving solutions.

In Google Classroom, teachers can assign Guided Learning activities based on class materials and receive feedback about where students might need additional support.

Google says its research shows that Guided Learning can improve learning outcomes. However, the effectiveness of these tools in everyday classrooms will depend on how students use them and how well the AI avoids simply completing assignments for them.

There are already questions about those safeguards. In testing reported by The New York Times, the consumer version of Guided Learning produced a detailed essay when given an Advanced Placement history prompt. Google says the school version includes additional restrictions.

The changes come amid growing concerns about how AI tools affect children and teenagers.

AI can be useful for explaining difficult concepts, brainstorming ideas and helping students study. But educators also worry that students may become too dependent on chatbots, use them to complete assignments or trust answers that aren't always accurate.

In September, New York City and Los Angeles, two of the country's largest school districts, introduced restrictions on student access to generative AI tools on school devices.

Some school officials have specifically wanted the ability to disable Google's AI Overviews while preserving conventional web searches.

Google's new policy does not provide that option. The company is reversing an earlier decision to enable certain AI features by default in Google Classroom for schools that had already permitted Gemini access.

That reversal gives administrators more control over which Google AI products students can use. But it stops short of letting schools completely separate Google Search from its built-in AI features.

For families, the most important thing to understand is that these changes are not taking effect immediately. Google says that it will share more details with schools in the coming months, with the new controls expected to arrive in early 2027.

Whether a student loses access to Google Search through their school account will depend on the choices made by their district or school administrators.

Schools can enable specific Google products individually, but those that want to keep AI entirely out of Google Search may need to consider alternative search engines or research tools.

Parents concerned about AI in their children's classrooms can ask their schools which Google services are currently enabled, whether students can access Gemini, and how the district plans to handle the upcoming changes.

It's also worth asking whether students will be using Guided Learning and what oversight teachers will have over those interactions.

Why my offline zakat app ships a Hijri lookup table instead of a calendar algorithm

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I'm the developer of Zakat, a small offline Android app (Kotlin + Jetpack Compose + Room) that helps Muslims track the zakat they owe each year. Zakat falls due once per lunar year, so the whole app depends on one question: what Gregorian date is "1 Dhul Qi'dah 1448"?

Android does have options: java.time.chrono.HijrahDate, and ICU's IslamicCalendar with civil, tabular and Umm al-Qura variants. I didn't use them, for four reasons:

The rule in the codebase is short: the app never computes a Hijri date at runtime. Every date comes from a table lookup.

A small Python script, run on my machine and never shipped, uses the hijridate package (Umm al-Qura) to write the Gregorian date of the first day of every Hijri month from 1440 to 1500 AH:

That is 61 years × 12 months plus one sentinel row: 733 entries, about 52 KB of JSON, covering 11 Sep 2018 to 16 Nov 2077.

1. The table does not store month lengths. A month's length is the gap between its first day and the next month's first day. The table can't disagree with itself, because it only says each fact once. The sentinel row (1 Muharram 1501) exists only so the last real month has a "next month" to measure against.

Across the table there are 389 thirty-day months and 343 twenty-nine-day months. Remember that ratio.

2. The JVM module and the APK read the same file. The JSON lives in core/src/main/resources, and the app module adds that folder as an asset source, so there is one file and no copy to drift:

The reverse lookup (Gregorian → Hijri) is a binary search over the month starts. It is only used once, to suggest the user's first year; after that, each year follows from the previous one.

Umm al-Qura is a calculated calendar. If your mosque announces Ramadan from a local sighting, its first day can differ from the table by a day. So a per-year due-date override always wins over the table:

The app does not try to know better than the user's own community.

The due-date override was a normal date picker, so nothing stopped a user from picking, say, 27 March 2098, which is outside the table.

The result: the app crashed on every launch, and the only way out was clearing the app's data. That is the worst outcome for an app whose whole job is keeping your records.

Apple's 10-K lists reputational harm, regulatory action and legal liability among risks tied to its AI efforts

David Paul Morris / Bloomberg via Getty Images

Apple $AAPL warned in its annual report on Tuesday that AI agents, whether built by the company or by third parties, may take actions that were "not directed, instructed or authorized."

The 10-K, for the fiscal year ended Sept. 26, says such agents can operate on their own, for instance by reaching systems they may not have permission to enter, running or changing code, sending messages and finalizing purchases. Apple said the agents may exceed permissions, defy instructions, circumvent security measures or be manipulated through malicious inputs.

The company said agents may conceal actions, withhold or manipulate information, or generate deceptive outputs. Many of the technologies are nondeterministic, so their behavior can be difficult to anticipate, control, test and audit, and errors can compound across multistep workflows, Apple said. Some consequences, such as deleting data or completing transactions, may be difficult or impossible to reverse.

Apple said it may have only limited power to supervise and direct third-party AI agents, and that its policies and controls may fail to keep pace with advancing technology. The company said these issues could expose it to reputational and competitive harm, regulatory action and legal liability.

The filing says AI agents that require access to personal data create privacy and data security concerns that could erode customer trust. It describes legal and regulatory frameworks for AI as divergent and evolving, and says Apple may have to spend significant sums to adapt to new laws.

Apple has no agentic AI product, according to CNBC. On an earnings call on Monday, CEO John Ternus said agentic tools look promising for users but that Apple wants them to know what access they are granting. "We want to make sure this is done in a way that protects security and privacy," Ternus said.

Last month, Apple announced changes to full disk access on Macs, with the aim of lowering "risks related to this level of access" for AI agents, according to CNBC.

Meta $META launched its Muse agent on Sept. 8, and OpenAI introduced its Dots agents at its DevDay event, as AI agents reached a broader consumer audience. Amazon $AMZN blocked Muse from its store and said it never authorized the agent.

In another case, a Muse agent managing the Facebook Marketplace listings of tech reviewer Matt Robb replied to a buyer as Robb, agreed on a price and sent his Toronto home address. Robb had clicked "Allow Always" on a permissions prompt. Meta said it would make the prompt clearer.

French Museum’s ‘Wanted’ Poster Campaign for Stolen Art Leads to Return of Missing 17th-Century Dutch Painting

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Thanks to an inventive “Wanted” campaign by the Musée des Beaux-Arts d’Orléans in France, aimed at tracking down hundreds of WWII-era looted artworks, one painting has just been found, the museum’s director told ARTnews.

A 17th-century painting by Dutch artist Willem Cornelisz Duyster, titled Un Buveur (The Drinker) and stolen in 1940 from the Orléans museum, was located last month at an auction house in Switzerland and is on its way back home to the French museum, Le Journal de l’Art reported earlier. Related Articles Italy Looks to Join Its European Counterparts With Restitution Legislation Swiss Government to Fund Research into Holdings of Cultural Artifacts

After being presented with the evidence of the theft, “the possessor of the work immediately agreed to restitute it at no cost,” said Olivia Voisin, the museum’s director. The painting is currently being held in customs at the French border. Voisin said the museum was able to find the missing painting thanks to a recent campaign that uses a bit of humor to spread awareness about some 350 artworks that were plundered from the Musée Paul Fourché, an annex of the Beaux-Arts museum, after the city was attacked by Nazis in June 1940. It is believed people on both sides of the conflict took advantage of the opportunity to ransack the museum.

The poster campaign features the words “WANTED” written in chunky, all caps in American Old Western font, across a selection of black-and-white images of the museum’s lost artworks. These have been shared on social media, the museum website, and other news platforms, and are bolstered by a two-year-old digital catalog of the stolen works.

So far, it seems to be working. One local from Orléans, who saw the “Wanted” posters, decided to spend his free time searching for the missing paintings. “He wanted to help us, and that was the goal of the campaign: that these searches are shared or collective,” said Voisin.

While randomly sifting through online auctions, the unnamed Orléans resident came across Un Buveur in a catalog for an October 17 sale at the Gloggner auction house in Lucerne. It was estimated to be worth about $3,400 and thought to be a study of the original. It depicts a jolly scene of a seated man, dressed in contemporary garb and holding out an empty glass; he raises his other arm and appears to be caught in midsentence.

The museum was tipped off about the curious painting and contacted the auction house, which immediately cooperated and “was very transparent throughout,” said Voisin, noting that attitudes have changed in that regard. After she presented the painting’s holder with an image of the original superimposed on a digital image of their painting, they saw it was a perfect match. He had purchased it from a London auction house in the 1990s.

“He immediately proposed to restitute the painting,” she said. “It was pretty natural for him to return it, because he felt that he had been able to enjoy it for 35 years, and it was time for it to go home.”

The painting is considered one of Duyster’s finest, and in many ways represents the museum’s stolen collection, which once held several similar Dutch paintings from the 17th century showing scenes of revelry. However, the painting appears to be damaged by several layers of overpainting and cleaning attempts. If in good condition, Voisin estimated it to be worth between €25,000 and €30,000 ($28,000 to $33,600).

The Orléans museum has been trying for years to recover the artworks lost during World War II, with little luck. To date, 424 paintings are still missing from its collection, counting additional paintings that have gone missing or were stolen at different periods. They include works by Édouard Manet, Hans Holbein the Younger, Anthony van Dyck, Lorenzo Costa, and one attributed to Raphael’s Renaissance workshop. Since the creation of the digital catalog and the “Wanted” poster campaign, four paintings, including Un Buveur, have been returned over the past few years.

The current dispute turns on a technical point. Sun chose to sue in federal court, which can hear cases between parties from different places but generally can’t hear a foreign plaintiff’s case against a foreign defendant.

Sun argues the late disclosure was a tactic to move the case out of federal court, and his motion seeks to make World Liberty and its lawyers bear the cost. Magic: The Gathering Is Reprinting a Powerful $30 Sword With Perfect Pixel Art

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Magic: The Gathering is reaching into its inventory of powerful artifacts to reprint a sword worth nearly $30, complete with gloriously nostalgic video game artwork. Sword of Truth and Justice, a popular Equipment first released in 2019, is returning in a new Secret Lair collection that draws inspiration from the blocky, polygonal graphics of the Nintendo 64 and original PlayStation era.

Announced by Wizards of the Coast on November 3, the Rise of the Polygons | Extra Life 2026 drop features four reprinted cards and an Elemental token, with half of the purchase price benefiting children's hospitals. The $39.99 non-foil collection also includes another valuable reprint in Balefire Dragon, making this a potentially enticing proposition for Commander players, collectors, and anyone who still gets misty-eyed thinking about Ocarina of Time (or its remake).

Originally released in 2019's Modern Horizons, Sword of Truth and Justice belongs to Magic's illustrious cycle of “swords,” many of which command considerable sums on the secondary market. According to recent market prices, the original non-foil printing hovers around $29, while certain premium versions fetch even more.

The three-mana artifact gives its equipped creature +2/+2 and protection from both white and blue, already a formidable combination. However, its combat damage ability is where things get particularly nasty. Whenever the equipped creature damages an opponent, its controller places a +1/+1 counter on a creature they control, then proliferates, adding another counter of each existing kind to any number of eligible permanents or players.

For decks revolving around +1/+1 counters, proliferate, or even planeswalkers, that represents an abundance of value from a single successful attack. And since the Equipment costs just two mana to attach, it's easy to keep the sword swinging away.

The artwork is arguably just as appealing as the card's impressive abilities. Whereas 2024's Extra Life collection, Pixel Perfect, embraced the 16-bit era, this year's installment advances a console generation with chunky, low-polygon character models and distinctly late-'90s RPG aesthetics. The result looks like something that might have occupied a treasured memory card back in 1998.

Given how collectible Magic's more unusual Secret Lair treatments have become, this particular sword seems destined to attract attention.

Sword of Truth and Justice might be the proverbial final boss of this collection, but Balefire Dragon is hardly a pushover. Originally printed in 2011's Innistrad, this seven-mana, 6/6 flying Dragon has become a beloved Commander menace, with ordinary non-foil copies generally commanding between $15 and $20.

Its ability is delightfully straightforward: Whenever Balefire Dragon deals combat damage to a player, it deals that much damage to every creature that player controls. One successful attack from an unmodified Balefire Dragon can leave an opponent staring at six damage across their entire army. Ouch.

Together, Sword of Truth and Justice and Balefire Dragon represent roughly $45 or more in existing card value, depending on the printings being compared. That already exceeds the $39.99 asking price of the non-foil drop, before accounting for the remaining cards.

Those include Young Pyromancer, the popular two-mana "spells-matter" creature that produces Elemental tokens whenever its controller casts an instant or sorcery, and Big Score, a useful red instant that converts discarded cards into fresh draws and Treasure tokens. Neither is particularly expensive, but both are eminently playable.

It's another welcome round of reprints from Wizards, which recently announced the long-overdue return of the roughly $18 Soul Spike after two decades without another printing.

Rise of the Polygons | Extra Life 2026 goes on sale November 16 through Magic's official Secret Lair storefront, with the sale ending November 30 at 1 a.m. PT. Non-foil copies cost $39.99, while traditional foil versions retail for $49.99.

Collectors attending MagicCon: Atlanta from November 13–15 will also have an opportunity to purchase the collection early, although quantities will be limited.

The charitable component deserves attention, too. Half of the online sales price, minus third-party transaction fees, will support Extra Life and Seattle Children's Hospital. At MagicCon, Wizards says all proceeds from its Extra Life merchandise and the Rise of the Polygons drop will go toward the cause.

It's an admirable initiative with some genuinely desirable reprints, especially for Commander enthusiasts. And for anyone who spent their childhood battling suspiciously angular dragons with impossibly oversized swords, the artwork is a rather charming bonus.

At least this time, beating the dragon won't require blowing into a cartridge.
