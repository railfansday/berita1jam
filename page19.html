<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/finance/hims-hers-reports-8904m-q3-revenue-tightens-2026-outlook" hreflang="en">Hims &amp; Hers reports $890.4M Q3 revenue, tightens 2026 outlook</a>

📅 2026-11-10  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Hims & Hers reports $890.4M Q3 revenue, tightens 2026 outlook" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Digital health and wellness company Hims & Hers tightened its full-year 2026 revenue outlook after missing Wall Street analysts’ revenue and earnings estimates.

Andrew Dudum, co-founder, chairperson and CEO, pointed investors toward the increasing scale of Hims & Hers' pharmacy and lab infrastructure. That growth is driving efficiencies for the company, as is its artificial intelligence-native experience, he said.

“That infrastructure positions us to launch treatments of increasing complexity, including testosterone replacement therapy this quarter and our Peptides Plus offering later this month,” Dudum said.

Peptides have been a focus for the company as of late, with Dudum telling investors last quarter it plans to offer already-permitted peptides, like sermorelin and NAD+.

Dudum said that Hims & Hers’ long-term strategy focuses on “three critical commitments:” being a leader in metabolic health domestically and globally, leading major markets globally and being "at the forefront of category expansion.”

“Each of these reinforces the other, and together they describe how Hims & Hers becomes the global destination for consumer health,” he said. “We are investing aggressively against all three because this opportunity is growing quickly, and we believe it will become more valuable every quarter.”

The company reported $890.4 million in revenue in Q3, up 49% compared to revenue of $599 million the year prior, according to financial results published Monday. Net loss was $33 million, compared to $15.8 million in profit for the same quarter a year ago.

Chief Financial Officer Yemi Okupe said that revenue excluded $18 million from the “scaling of membership revenue” in the company’s weight loss offering but will be recognized in the fourth quarter.

“This revenue deferral has no impact on free cash flow generation,” Okupe noted.

Adjusted EBITDA was $82.4 million compared to $78.4 million the year prior.

Ethan Feller, stock strategist at Zacks Investment Research, told Fierce Healthcare the company’s revenue was “roughly what Wall Street expected,” adding that U.S. “growth sped up to 21%.”

“But the company lost 14 cents a share against an expected gain of six cents, its third big miss in a row,” Feller said. “It is also keeping less of each sale, as gross margin fell to 57%, down from 74% a year ago, as more of the business shifts to lower-margin brand-name weight-loss drugs.”

Feller added that the company’s “growth story is still a real long-term tailwind.”

“Demand for weight-loss drugs is strong, and having both Novo and Lilly on the platform helps,” Feller said. “But as long as margins keep shrinking, the stock will likely stay under pressure until either the price falls to a level that reflects the lower margins or the company shows it can turn a steady profit.”

As a result, Hims & Hers tightened its full-year 2026 revenue guidance to a range of $3.13 billion to $3.15 billion and reduced its adjusted EBITDA guidance to a range of $257 million to $277 million.

“This is a reflection of the more aggressive strategic pricing and marketing actions that we expect to take in the U.K. and Germany, and the weight loss membership deferral noted above,” Okupe said.

The earnings report comes on the heels of an announcement that the company struck a deal with Eli Lilly to offer its branded obesity treatments directly through the Hims & Hers platform. The drugs were previously only available to patients if they left the site and went to Lilly's direct-to-consumer platform, LillyDirect.

“Nearly 3 million people across four continents rely on us to access care,” Dudum said. “That trust has made us a valuable partner to global pharmaceutical leaders like Novo and Lilly, and positions us to keep bringing the most innovative treatments to our customers as they come to market.”

Dudum added that the company is planning to launch “flexible multi-month subscriptions” across its assortment to make it easier for customers to address health goals.

“We will continue to prioritize partnerships across the industry, with the goal that whenever an innovative therapy reaches the market, our customers can access it here,” Dudum said. “That assortment will sit alongside an AI-native experience that will soon be available to nearly every weight loss customer on our platform.”

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/digital-health/oura-acquire-ai-virtual-primary-care-partner-counsel-health-625m" hreflang="en">Oura to acquire AI virtual primary care partner Counsel Health for $625M</a>

📅 2026-11-10  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Oura to acquire AI virtual primary care partner Counsel Health for $625M" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Smart ring maker and health tracking company Oura plans to acquire AI virtual primary care company and business partner Counsel Health for $625 million, the health tech companies announced Tuesday.

The all-cash deal is expected to close before the end of this year, pending customary closing conditions. Bringing Counsel’s platform and capabilities in-house will allow more Oura members to bring their biometric data into a virtual, in-app care encounter, the companies said in their announcement.

“Oura helps members understand their physiology and, with Counsel, they can take action on their health across a range of needs, from quick questions and prescriptions to lab results, chronic care and referrals,” Tom Hale, CEO of Oura, said. “Together, we can create a new model of care that extends the current healthcare paradigm by delivering an experience that’s more immediate, personalized, and proactive. This is a massive step in our mission to empower people to live healthier, longer, by delivering on the promise of a 24-7 doctor in your pocket.”

Counsel’s model uses text-based, records-informed medical AI encounters to help field and triage users, providing a path to telehealth encounters with the company’s in-house physicians, who are also supported on the backend by a proprietary “Clinician Cockpit” that surfaces relevant clinical data for the encounter. The two-year-old company boasts enterprise partnerships with health plans, provider organizations, employers and other consumer health organizations, for whom it says its approach can reduce costs and improve access to timely care.

Oura is a digital health giant with 5.7 million paid members, whose recently postponed IPO was targeting a $15.6 billion valuation. The company says it is profitable and “growing meaningfully” with 92% year-over-year revenue growth (nearly $1.8 billion) for the fiscal year ended Sept. 30, but ultimately delayed the offering due to “uncertainty” in the market. Its most recent fiscal quarter, ended Sept. 30, included 153% year-over-year revenue growth and an adjusted EBITDA of $87 million.

Oura has partnered with Counsel since June, and in September, announced a tighter collaboration centered on the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services’ ACCESS (Advancing Chronic Care with Effective Scalable Solutions) Model, set to begin early next year. The companies have said early data from their partnership show strong care satisfaction among those who saw a physician (4.6 out of 5 average rating), widespread agreement among Counsel members (90%) that use of their Oura data helped improve care, and general Oura member satisfaction (70%) with the onboarding to Counsel’s services.

“At Counsel, we’ve built medical AI around trust, safety and responsibility, keeping physicians in the loop for clinical decisions,” Muthu Alagappan, M.D., founder and CEO of Counsel, said in the announcement. “Oura’s data gives clinicians objective context, trended biometrics, and member engagement to deliver more informed care. From the start, our teams bonded over a joint vision, shared culture and mission, further solidifying as we negotiated our deal over the past few months.”

Under a new owner, the company will continue to serve its existing enterprise partners while expanding to new customers and broadening its clinical capacity. For the latter, Oura and Counsel said they plan to bolster preventive care via continuous monitoring and proactive guidance; acute care via timely intervention for episodic health issues; and specialty and chronic care management in areas like reproductive health, cardiometabolic health and aging.

“Oura tells members what’s happening in their bodies, and Counsel helps them act on it,” summarized Oura Executive Chairperson David Shuman. “This combination expands our addressable market and deepens the value we deliver to every member. This not only increases lifetime value, but Counsel’s enterprise partners also open the door to millions of people beyond our existing member base.”

While medical cost trend remains a pressure point for health insurers, leading companies cited some positive trends, too, heading into the final months of 2026.

UnitedHealth Group was once again the most profitable company among payers in the quarter, bringing in $4.1 billion in profit for Q3. That's up from a $2.3 billion haul in the prior-year quarter.

Through the first nine months of the year, profits were $15.9 billion at UHG, rising from $12 billion a year ago.

The healthcare giant has been in the midst of a critical financial turnaround since mid-2025, and in the most recent quarter, it highlighted some of the progress that it's logged over the past year at one of its biggest pain centers: Optum Health.

When the company posted an uncharacteristically poor quarter to start 2025, Optum Health was cited as one of the main pressure points. During its most recent call, Optum CEO Patrick Conway, M.D., said that the transition centered on a "continued return to value-based care principles" that sit at the core of Optum Health.

UnitedHealth also led the way on revenue in Q3, posting $112.7 billion. That's down slightly from $113.2 billion the company, reported in the third quarter of 2025.

CVS Health had the second-highest profit and revenue in Q3, bringing in $2.2 billion in earnings for the quarter. This marks a significant reversal from the prior-year quarter, when the company posted a nearly $4 billion loss.

CVS' revenue in the quarter was $108.4 billion, increasing from $102.9 billion in the third quarter of 2025.

The company's financial performance wasn't what dominated the headlines in Q3, however, as CVS shocked the industry in announcing plans to split its pharmacy business off and form two companies. The break-up is expected to close in the first half of 2028, and would establish CVS Pharmacy as its own company while Aetna and Caremark would remain together as CVS Health.

CVS CEO David Joyner said that the shift represents "a structure and plan that are built to win and represent the best path to create value over the long term."

The Cigna Group and Elevance Health were on par with one another in Q3 on profits, both earning $1.4 billion. Cigna wins out on revenue, though, with $73.7 billion, while Elevance reported $51.4 billion.

By comparison, Cigna posted $1.9 billion in profit and $69.7 billion in revenue in Q3 2025.

Core to the third-quarter conversation was the company's newly-unveiled Lead to One healthcare transformation strategy, which puts an emphasis on building a personalized experience for its members and customers.

Cigna said it plans to lean on all the segments under its umbrella to drive toward meeting individual member needs.

Elevance Health, meanwhile, increased its profit slightly year over year from $1.2 billion in Q3 2025. Revenues in the prior-year quarter were $50.7 million.

The company was one of several insurers in the quarter that noted positive signs in its Medicaid plans. While its leaders told investors that 2026 remains a "trough year" for its Medicaid business, improvements are likely in the cards for 2027.

That trend is supported by state rate setting that more fully accounts for the rising cost of care and the acuity of people enrolled in Medicaid, executives said. The member mix is only expected to increase in acuity as states roll out work requirements at the beginning of this year.

Similarly, Centene CEO Sarah London said the company sees an opportunity for "long-term growth" in the Medicaid market as part of its Q3 report. She noted similar trends around rate setting from states.

London added that the insurer has made significant progress on focus areas for improvement, such as behavioral health.

Centene posted a $71 million loss in the quarter, slimming its losses significantly from $6.6 billion in the prior-year quarter. That massive loss a year ago was backstopped by a one-time impairment charge of more than $6 billion.

Revenues in Q3 were $50.6 billion, up slightly from $49.7 billion in the third quarter of 2025.

Humana offered color on early signals in the Medicare annual enrollment period, which suggests that the insurer is poised for further growth in Medicare Advantage for next year. CEO Jim Rechtin said sign-ups through the early parts of the window indicate 2027 membership growth.

The insurer has had momentum around new sign-ups and has also done well in recapturing enrollments that were impacted by market exits, Rechtin said.

Humana posted a $162 million profit in the third quarter, down from $195 million a year ago. Revenues were $41.15 billion, growing from $32.6 billion in the prior-year quarter.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/digital-health/color-health-launches-clinical-collabs-nci-designated-cancer-centers" hreflang="en">Color Health launches collabs with NCI-designated cancer centers to streamline intake</a>

📅 2026-11-10  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Color Health launches collabs with NCI-designated cancer centers to streamline intake" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Color Health, a virtual oncologist-led cancer clinic, has announced formal clinical collaborations with 10 National Cancer Institute-designated cancer centers.

Color and collaborating cancer centers aim to streamline time to treatment through coordinated workup (like imaging and labs) and records preparation, as well as custom intake workflows. These elements help ensure a timely start to care when weeks can be the difference between life or death, executives say. Additional partners will be announced in the coming months.

“Speed to diagnosis, speed to treatment initiation, is one of the single biggest determinants of outcomes in cancer," Caroline Savello, president of Color, told Fierce Healthcare. “Where can we make sure that there is the right clinical coordination, the right navigation coordination happening so that things can happen swiftly through the right processes."

Cancer is among the top drivers of healthcare costs and mortality. Because of its complex and variable nature, the quality and timeliness of care are the best ways to improve outcomes and manage costs. That means less duplicate testing, fewer treatment changes later and fewer avoidable ED visits and hospitalizations

Among the collaborating centers are City of Hope-Los Angeles, Memorial Sloan Kettering, Moffitt Cancer Center, Mount Sinai, Stanford Medicine Cancer Center, UC Davis Comprehensive Cancer Center and UCSF Helen Diller Family Comprehensive Cancer Center. National Cancer Institute (NCI)-designated cancer centers go through a robust evaluation for meeting quality, safety and access standards.

When care at a specific center is determined to be a good fit for a patient, Color coordinates directly to match patients with the right team, completes the clinical workup, reviews medical records and completes authorizations to each center’s specifications. First-appointment targets for most patients are set at seven days or fewer for oncology referrals placed by Color Medical. Additionally, Color verifies insurance coverage to ensure the referral is in-network. Occasionally, Color works with payers or centers on single-case agreements if something is otherwise out of network.

“Color is, above all, a very patient-centered company. When an individual faces a cancer diagnosis for the first time, Color helps to provide the information and navigation to top cancer centers. Their process balances technology with a human touch,” Karen Lu, M.D., physician in chief at Moffitt Cancer Center, told Fierce Healthcare over email.

For any patient issues, Color clinicians escalate to the collaborating centers. Patients also get ongoing wraparound support from Color during and after treatment, including nutrition, mental health, palliative and survivorship care. Additionally, Color will collaborate with the centers to identify any potential clinical trial candidates, gather the needed information and support institution-led eligibility assessment and enrollment.

UCSF Health has already worked with Color for years, so the latest collaboration is an expansion of the partnership.

“[Screening or diagnosis] can be incredibly stressful moments, and navigating a complex healthcare system should not add to that burden,” Laurel Bray-Hanin, VP at UCSF Helen Diller Family Comprehensive Cancer Center, told Fierce Healthcare in emailed comments. “This partnership allows us to connect Color's cancer screening and care coordination efforts directly with UCSF Health's expert patient navigators, who can help patients quickly identify and schedule the appropriate next steps.”

Color “expanded dramatically” this year, per Savello, teaming up with Independence Blue Cross and planning to serve millions more new lives in the new year through employers. Those employers continue to be laser-focused on oncology, per Savello, and are asking Color to set up partnerships with cancer centers in their regions.

“They’re all seeing, obviously, rate increases," she said. "The disease space and the market is very active."

Color partners with local providers to clinically manage all patients through its oncologist-led care team. Its virtual cancer clinic is a national center of excellence certified by the American Society of Clinical Oncology, meaning it meets rigorous standards and is subject to annual audits and reviews. The company, which started working in the cancer space in 2023, says its patients see 66% faster diagnosis and a 77% increase in screening adherence. It also claims to save $62,000 per patient in treatment and closes over three-quarters of costly care gaps.

“Cancer care isn't always a straightforward path. Sometimes, patients need answers about a possible diagnosis, and other times, they need a fresh perspective or access to new treatment options,” Bray-Hanin at UCSF said. “We want patients to feel that someone is looking out for them, helping them understand their options, and connecting them with the expertise and care they need. That commitment to providing compassionate, coordinated care is what makes this partnership so meaningful to us.”

The 10 Most Hopeless Movies of the 21st Century, Ranked

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There are some movies that leave you feeling uplifted, inspired, or even ready to take on the world. Sadly, these aren't those movies. These are the most hopeless ones of the 21st century that take a much bleaker view of the human experience, whether that be exploring grief, addiction, cruelty, loneliness, or the terrifying possibility that some situations simply can't be fixed.

From the backdrop of a dying planet to stories that focus on ordinary people watching society completely collapse, these movies aren't interested in reassuring happy endings. They're often difficult to sit through, all while attaining their unforgettable emotional impact. So buckle up, because these are some real heavy ones.

Set in a dystopian future where Earth has been rendered uninhabitable by environmental destruction, a group of passengers boards a luxury spaceship for a routine voyage to Mars. Unfortunately, the ship soon collides with space debris, leaving it knocked permanently off course, without a way to correct its trajectory. Now, drifting further into the vastness of space, the passengers are forced to face the terrifying possibility that they may never reach their final destination.

Aniara is a chilling mediation on mortality, denial, and the human need to believe there's something waiting for us at the end of the journey. For one, throughout this forgotten sci-fi flick, the survivors initially cling to distractions, relationships, and artificial comforts—only for those to eventually become increasingly meaningless as the reality of their circumstances set in. And by the time its final moments arrive, the film has made the sheer indifference of the universe feel almost unbearable.

After receiving a prestigious grant, theater director Caden Cotard (Philip Seymour Hoffman) begins an ambitious stage production that recreates an entire city inside a warehouse. But as the project grows increasingly elaborate, the boundary between production and reality begins to disappear, all while Caden becomes consumed by his own existential anxieties and relationships.

Few films capture the exhausting weight of simply being alive quite like Synecdoche, New York. Its surreal structure reflects Caden's inability to make sense of his existence, as time slips away and his grand artistic undertaking becomes an increasingly futile attempt to understand himself, those around him, and mortality itself. Funny in places, with an overwhelming sadness beneath the absurdity, this unsettling portrait of a struggling man is a stellar directorial debut by the visionary Charlie Kaufman.

When hunter Llewelyn Moss (Josh Brolin) stumbles upon the aftermath of a drug deal gone wrong, he makes the fateful decision to take a case filled with millions of dollars. Unfortunately, that puts him in the crosshairs of Anton Chigurh (Javier Bardem), a ruthless hitman who will stop at almost nothing to retrieve the money. But as Sheriff Ed Tom Bell (Tommy Lee Jones) tries to track them both down, he finds himself confronting a level of violence and cruelty that seems increasingly beyond his understanding.

The Coen brothers' adaptation of No Country for Old Men is a masterclass in tension, but its bleakness comes from something more unsettling than its brutal violence. Chigurh feels less like a conventional villain than an unstoppable force of chaos, while Bell's growing disillusionment reflects a world that no longer seems to operate by any moral code he recognizes. There are no easy victories or comforting explanations here, just people trying to make sense of a cruel and indifferent world.

Four films, four completely different ways of handling money, trouble and other people. This is the one you would survive, why it suits you, and what it would cost you to be there.

You have quietly arranged your life so that very little can get to you, and a surprising number of people find that infuriating. You would be completely at home here.

You talk your way through life and it works far more often than it should. Give you a bad situation and an audience and you will find a way out of both at once.

You would survive Fargo because you are the only kind of person who does: patient, decent, and completely unimpressed by anybody trying to be clever.

You do not expect the world to be fair and you stopped being surprised about it a long time ago. That clear-eyed quality is exactly what this film asks for.

With civilization collapsed and most of the world's survivors reduced to desperate scavengers, an unnamed father (Viggo Mortensen) and his young son (Kodi Smit-McPhee) travel in search of warmer weather and a semblance of safety. But their journey is a constant struggle as they scramble for food, avoid hostile strangers, and hold on to the small sense of humanity that separates them from the surrounding horrors.

What makes The Road so crushing is that its bleak world is never just a backdrop for survival thrills. Yes, the father's determination to protect his son is deeply moving, but it also comes with the terrible knowledge that he may not be able to keep him safe forever. Their bond offers the film its only real source of warmth, making every encounter with the cruelty of the outside world feel that much more devastating. It's a story about love enduring in the worst possible circumstances, but one that never pretends love is enough to fix everything.

Béla Tarr's The Turin Horse begins with a mysterious incident involving a famous philosopher, who is said to have broken down after witnessing a horse being beaten in the street. From there, the film follows the horse's owner, Ohlsdorfer (János Derzsi) and his daughter (Erika Bók), as they go about their daily lives on an isolated farm. But when the horse suddenly refuses to work, increasingly strange events suggest that even their small, familiar world is beginning to come apart.

Shot in long, hypnotic takes and told through a deliberately repetitive rhythm, The Turin Horse makes the slow collapse of existence feel almost physical. Meals become a mechanical ritual, the weather grows more hostile, and the characters' options seem to shrink with every passing day. There's no dramatic catastrophe or last-minute revelation to offer relief. Instead, Tarr builds an atmosphere of total exhaustion, as though the world is simply running out of reasons to continue.

After the death of his brother, Lee Chandler (Casey Affleck) is forced to return to his hometown, where he discovers that he has been named guardian of his teenage nephew, Patrick (Lucas Hedges). Unfortunately, the arrangement means putting his solitary life in Boston on hold and confronting a community filled with painful memories.

Manchester by the Sea is heartbreaking because it understands that grief doesn't always lead to healing, closure, or some grand emotional breakthrough. Lee's past has left him carrying a guilt so profound that even the possibility of a fresh start feels impossible, and Affleck's performance makes that pain all the more affecting. Not every wound can be fixed, and the film is simply brave enough to leave that truth unresolved.

Lars von Trier's psychological drama follows two sisters, Justine (Kirsten Dunst) and Claire (Charlotte Gainsbourg), as a mysterious planet approaches Earth. The film is split into two parts: first Justine's lavish wedding reception, which descends into utter chaos, then, the days leading up to the planet's predicted collision, when the sisters react to the impending apocalypse in very different ways.

Rather than building towards a frantic race for survival, von Trier uses the looming catastrophe to explore depression, anxiety, and the strange comfort that can come from knowing there's nothing left to fight against. Dunst gives a remarkable performance, capturing Justine's despair without reducing her to it, while the film's haunting imagery makes its cosmic destruction feel undoubtedly terrifying. Its hopelessness is total, and yet there's an eerie serenity in the way it embraces the inevitable.

Selma Ježková (Björk) is a Czech immigrant working in a rural American factory, who is gradually losing her eyesight due to a hereditary condition. Determined to prevent her young son from suffering the same fate, she works tirelessly to save enough money for an operation—occasionally escaping the hardships of her daily life by retreating into elaborate musical fantasies. Sadly, a devastating betrayal threatens everything she's worked towards.

The contrast between the film's whimsical sequences and its increasingly brutal reality makes Dancer in the Dark almost unbearably sad. Selma's optimism and selflessness are admirable, but they're also exploited by the people around her, so much so that her maternal determination becomes the very thing that destroys her. Björk delivers an extraordinary performance, bringing a fragile, almost childlike sincerity to a character whose kindness is met with astonishing cruelty. And by the time the film reaches its final act, its refusal to offer an easy comfort feels downright merciless.

Oh Dae-su (Choi Min-sik) is a man who's mysteriously imprisoned in a private cell for 15 years without being told why. And when he's suddenly released, he sets out to discover who abducted him and what motivated his captivity—only to find himself drawn into an increasingly elaborate game orchestrated by a strange man.

Oldboy may be famous for its ferocious action, but its true horror lies in the devastating revelations waiting at the end of Dae-su's journey. Park Chan-wook turns a seemingly straightforward revenge story into a vicious examination of guilt, obsession, and the destructive power of secrets—with Choi anchoring this film with a masterful performance. Better still, the final twist doesn't simply shock. It leaves the audience questioning whether survival can even be considered a victory.

Darren Aranofsky's psychological drama follows four people whose lives are gradually consumed by addiction. Harry Goldfarb (Jared Leto) and his girlfriend Marion (Jennifer Connelly) dream of building a better future together, while his friend Tyrone (Marlon Wayans) hopes to escape the poverty and instability of his upbringing. Meanwhile, Harry's mother, Sara (Ellen Burstyn), becomes obsessed with losing weight after receiving the prospect of appearing on television, even as that turns her to prescription pills.

Few films are as relentlessly bleak as Requiem for a Dream, which charts the slow destruction of its characters with an intensity that's difficult to shake. The frantic editing, distorted visuals, and haunting score make their spirals feel almost suffocating, with stellar performances (like Burstyn's) amplifying the devastation. There's no romanticizing addiction here, nor any suggestion that good intentions can protect people from its consequences. By the end, all four are left with utter wreckage, making this one of the most harrowing films of the century.

Better Buy Right Now: Cardano vs. Solana

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Both of these coins showed a lot of promise in 2021, but only one has delivered on it.

Cardano (ADA +1.03%) and Solana (SOL -1.27%) are both major cryptocurrencies, yet in my view, only one of them is worth owning. With that said, both coins have been a bit frustrating to hold; as of Nov. 6, Solana is 69% below its all-time high set in early 2025, while Cardano's price is 94% below its peak set in late 2021.

So which of these two bruised blockchains is better positioned to perform better over the next few years?

In crypto, a network's transaction fees are important to track because they're a clear sign of how much users are willing to pay to use the network.

For October, Solana brought in $23.1 million in fees, which is typical for it, and Cardano brought in $141,330, which, for it, is unusually high for 2026. Furthermore, Solana's per-transaction fees are typically much lower than Cardano's. A basic Solana transfer costs about $0.0005 as of Nov. 6, while a simple Cardano transfer costs roughly $0.03 and is also tens of seconds slower.

Additionally, in late July 2026, Solana boosted its transaction throughput by increasing each block's computing capacity by 66%, and then, on Oct. 21, its latest Alpenglow upgrade reduced transaction finalization time from 12.8 seconds to roughly 150 milliseconds. That speed makes Solana highly suitable for processing payments on a massive scale.

And that's part of the reason why, as of October, the network has a new collaboration with Samsung, which, starting Nov. 5, lets U.S. users on 82 million compatible Galaxy devices send the USDC stablecoin to each other, to outside wallets, and to bank accounts in more than 60 countries through Samsung Wallet, with Solana as a network partner. In contrast, Cardano has no collaborations or partnerships on Samsung's scale.

Another domain where Solana is far ahead is in its tokenized real-world asset base.

It currently holds $4.9 billion in tokenized real-world assets, including stocks, bonds, and funds recorded as blockchain tokens. Cardano doesn't even have a presence in that market, which means it'll miss out on the growth stemming from financial institutions seeking to manage their holdings via tokenization over the coming years.

Cardano does have a small decentralized finance (DeFi) ecosystem, which holds $54.1 million in total value locked (TVL), or deposited capital. Solana's DeFi ecosystem has $5.6 billion in TVL. Worse yet, Cardano's ecosystem of blockchain applications has shrunk by around 21% in just three months.

Solana is by far the better coin to buy compared to Cardano.

Aside from the diverging trajectories of their ecosystems, Cardano does not appear to have a clear idea of how to arrest its ongoing decline. Nor does it have any real areas of strength to consolidate around; it lacks on-chain capital to power ecosystem growth. That could well change in the coming years, but it doesn't seem probable -- the current state of affairs is not new for the network, and there still isn't any compelling reason for anyone to add its coin to a diversified crypto portfolio.

Solana, on the other hand, is still not exactly a safe purchase, even if it's preferable to Cardano.

While Cardano's inroads into asset tokenization are accelerating its exposure to growth, and its deals with outside companies to find more uses for its rails are bullish, there still isn't a strong link between its network activity and the returns that holders actually see from buying the coin. That could also change in the coming years, and it probably will -- but one big risk is that it won't.

So, be aware that Solana, the blockchain, could win big without Solana, the coin, winning much at all, even if it's highly likely to do vastly better than Cardano.

'Tough Questions' Ahead: Warren Targets Prediction Markets, Trump Crypto as Banking Chair

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

'Tough Questions' Ahead: Warren Targets Prediction Markets, Trump Crypto as Banking Chair

Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-MA), one of the crypto industry's most prominent critics, is preparing to use the chairmanship of the Senate Banking Committee to launch an oversight campaign aimed at Trump family crypto businesses, Big Tech and Wall Street, Politico reported on Monday.

"Big Tech CEOs, Administration officials, Trump family crypto businesses, Wall Street executives, and giant corporations should preserve their documents and prepare to answer tough questions," a Warren Banking Committee aide told the outlet.

Warren is expected to take over the committee in January after Democrats won control of the Senate in last week's midterm elections. The panel oversees banking, housing, cryptocurrency and the Federal Reserve, and its chair has broad powers to call hearings and launch investigations.

According to the aide, Warren also wants to end a "Wild West-style lack of oversight" of prediction markets that she says has allowed insiders to profit, and plans to warn about threats to financial stability, including a potential AI bubble.

She intends to keep working with Republican China hawks to restrict exports of advanced AI chips, and Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang is expected to be a top oversight target, Politico reported. The report came a day after Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation into reports that Huang had been the target of a Chinese intelligence effort, and as Nvidia shares slid as much as 8%, wiping roughly $350 billion off the company's value.

Warren is also likely to investigate the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's August decision to grant a conditional bank charter to Trump-backed World Liberty Financial, as well as the administration's firings of financial agency staff and its efforts to shut down the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, Reuters reported, citing a person familiar with her thinking. "You're going to see a lot of investigations," Brian Gardner, chief Washington policy strategist at Stifel, told the outlet.

In an essay for Time last week, Warren argued that Democrats won by "being populist economic fighters," and said "Big Tech CEOs, Wall Street executives, and giant corporations should prepare to answer tough questions and face meaningful consequences if they have broken the law."

Warren has been a persistent critic of the crypto industry and of the Trump family's business interests in it. She has argued that federal regulators' approval of national trust charters for crypto firms, including Coinbase and Ripple, is illegal, and has said the CFTC has been "steamrolled" by the prediction market and crypto firms it oversees.

She has also questioned Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick over a Tether loan to a trust tied to his children, and joined Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-VT) in urging the Labor Department to drop plans that would make it easier to hold crypto in 401(k) retirement plans.

Warren was one of the leading opponents of the Clarity Act, the crypto industry's top legislative priority, which stalled in the Senate this year. Democrats are now planning to push a broader crypto market structure bill when they take control of Congress.

The White House has pushed back. A spokesperson told Reuters that Trump is willing to work with anyone on behalf of Americans, but that "sham investigations and radical left policies" would undermine any such effort.

Republicans have cast Warren as the main obstacle to crypto legislation. Sen. Tim Scott (R-SC), the current Banking chair, has argued that without market structure rules, crypto is left in "the wild, wild West."

Warren's ability to pass legislation will be limited while Trump remains in the White House. In her Time essay, she acknowledged that Trump has embraced the nickname "Mr. Veto," but argued that Democrats should send him bills anyway.

Warren is also eyeing a more senior role in Senate Democratic leadership, potentially the No. 3 position, Politico reported last month.
