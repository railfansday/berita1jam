It’s a tale as old as time: amass a fortune with hard work, lose the fortune with reckless spending. That’s certainly true of Johnny Depp, whose latest movie, Ebenezer, is in theaters now on the 2026 movie schedule. Depp has gone through a lot of ups and downs in the last couple of decades, since becoming one of the highest-paid actors in Hollywood with the Pirates of the Caribbean franchise, starting in the summer of 2003. Now, a new biography about Depp is getting into some of the details of his fortune made and lost, and the numbers, as you might expect, are staggering.

In the new book, The Many Faces of Johnny Depp, author Mark Seal notes that court documents from 2017 let us in on just how staggering the numbers are. Depp has admitted to being hundreds of millions in the hole, but the new book touches more on where that money went and how he spent so much, so quickly. Of course, there are quite a few reasons, but they can be summed up in two, according to Seal. He writes (via PageSix):

His vast wealth had been gutted, fittingly, in a Jack Sparrow-like bacchanal of excessive spending and financial mismanagement.

That spending, according to PageSix, included buying a luxury yacht, 14 homes, pricey cases of wine, jewelry, and a private island in the Bahamas. He also spent lavishly on travel and the people who surrounded him at the height of his earnings and fame. Seal writes:

[He] didn’t move without an entourage and could never again fly on a commercial airline … He was no longer merely Johnny — he was Johnny Depp, mega-movie star, icon, brand, and franchise, with an expanding army of people depending on him for their livelihoods.

As Depp has admitted, he was also on the hook for $100 million in back taxes owed to Uncle Sam, a debt he blamed on the accountants and advisors he thought were paying the taxes when they apparently weren’t.

In the years since his court battle with Amber Heard, Depp’s reputation has taken a major hit, and that has also impacted his finances. For Ebenezer, Depp took a major cut from what he was earning for movies a decade ago. He apparently earned a “mere” $5 million for the new take on A Christmas Carol. That’s a lot of coin, for sure, but when you compare that to the $90 million Depp reportedly earned for Dead Men Tell No Tales, and the $16 million paycheck he received for Fantastic Beasts: The Crimes of Grindelwald (a movie he was quickly cut from), you get the full picture.

How To Get The Legendary Crabshack VF5 SMG In GTA 6

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Here is where to locate the giant crab, get the Crabshack VF5, and unlock the safe in GTA 6.

In Grand Theft Auto 6, you will spend quite a bit of time ascending the criminal ladder, maintaining Jason and Lucia's relationship, and exploring Leonida. Unsurprisingly, there are a lot of weird things and people you can come across while exploring in and around Vice City.

One of the strangest is a large mutated crab you can find in the swamps that feels like something ripped out of the Fallout games. If you're looking to find this mutated crustacean and wondering what you will get for your troubles, here is how to find the giant crab, get the legendary Crabshack SMG, and find all the codes for the research lab.

To start, make your way south to Pelican Valley in the Gullet Marsh region. Located around the center of the swamp (circled above) is a lone research station that you can dock your boat next to. If you're struggling to find it, I recommend looking for the building with all the tall antennas sticking out of it, as this can act as a visual marker for where the station is.

Additionally, you can visit this area as soon as you can freely roam around as Jason. It's relatively close to his home, making it a nice place to grab a great early-game legendary weapon; however, I recommend leaving the Stash Bag there since you won't be able to sell Marked Bills till later in the game. There's no combat or anything required to enter, as you won't actually be fighting the mutated crab.

If you want one of GTA 6's best shotguns for free from the start of the game, look no further.

Once you arrive at the research station, don't bother heading inside just yet. Instead, go around the back of the large house and look for a breaker box on the wall. Approach and interact with the box to restore power to the building. Now go inside the research station and head down the short hallway into the scientist's bedroom.

On the floor near the door are some words carved into the wood. Hold down the Left Trigger when near it to examine the writing and make note of the word CLEAVES that is at the bottom. Once you've examined it, return to the living room and approach the computer in the corner.

Interact with the computer to be prompted to enter the password on the right side of your screen. Select the word "CLEAVES" to gain access to the scientist's notes. There will be a section that says "SAFE CODE = DOUBLE SUCCESS." Once you're done reading, exit the computer to trigger a cutscene where a monstrous crab will attack you.

Fight it off via mashing the Square (PlayStation) / X (Xbox) button, and then leave the main building. Go back to where the breaker box was and look for a shack behind the house. Inside, you will find a bunch of terrariums where the crabs were being studied.

Specifically, look for a room with a much larger terrarium and examine the clipboard on the table next to it. You'll see that crab 36 was the one that was a success, meaning the code to the safe inside of the research station is 0072.

We get this number because the note on the computer states that the code is DOUBLE SUCCESS, meaning you need to double the number of the crab that survived the experiment. Inside the safe, you will find another note about an emergency protocol and $1,100 to spend.

We're not done yet. After you open the safe, head outside of the main building and look for a metal gate you can walk through along the wall (shown below). Follow the path to another shack on the water and enter the code 0212 at the door to open it.

Next to the body of the scientist, you'll find a Stash Bag with $10,000 Marked Bills and the Crabshack VF5 legendary submachine gun. If you're visiting here early, leave the Stash Bag to grab once you can sell the Marked Bills. You can then open the garage door, allowing you to drive the jet ski out!

Remember, once you grab this legendary weapon, it's part of your arsenal forever. You can always pick it up again at any weapons locker throughout the game, so it's a great firearm to snag early to make combat a little easier.

If you want to build a collection of vehicles, you'll need Vice City Real Estate as soon as possible.

A Party to End All Parties—Fashionable London Fetes the Life of Antony Price

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Antony Price always said that if anyone should ever think about giving him a memorial, “Make it a fabulous party!” The legendary and beloved British designer’s wishes were honored by his friends in London on Wednesday night. With glamour on the invitation and his gasp-making shows and images playing on vast screens, the man who forged the look of Roxy Music and Duran Duran and conjured couture dreams for decades was fully celebrated in an epic Antony Price appreciation night thrown for him at 180 Strand.

Tributes flowed from his multi-generational fans and closest people. Among them were models who’d walked his London shows, or rather stalked and prowled them, per his direction: Marie Helvin and Yasmin Le Bon, as well as Marilyn Cole, who brought along a glorious reminder of her role on the cover of Roxy Music’s 1973 album Stranded, printed on a t-shirt. There she was, lying looking seductively sweaty in a jungle wearing an artfully shredded red dress that Antony Price had concocted. Thanks to Price’s creative direction and styling (terms unheard-of then), the lives of a generation of British teenagers were changed, just from staring at the fantasies of Jerry Hall as a blue-painted mermaid, Amanda Lear as a gleaming patent-leather dominatrix, and Kari-Ann Muller as a 1950s pin-up on Roxy album covers. It went from scruffy long-haired hippy to sexy slicked-back glamour overnight.

Thirty years later, Alexander Fury discovered the credit ‘Antony Price: clothes, makeup and hair’ on the back of the Roxy albums as a student at Central Saint Martin’s. “I didn’t care about the music—it was the clothes, the look! Who was this genius? ” It started an obsession that led Fury to fashion journalism; he also became an Antony Price collector and friend. From the podium, he contextualized Price’s contribution as the first London designer to fuse fashion, music, and spectacle together in live events—selling tickets to the public. His 1970s fashion shows, dramatically stylized productions staged with supermodels in music venues like Camden Palace and the Hippodrome, “were pinnacles of that approach, early examples of fashion breaking out of its niche to be truly democratic. He was a revolutionary, a pioneer,” Fury said, but added, “yet he was also, and he remains, the most criminally overlooked designer in British fashion history.”

Nick Rhodes of Duran Duran was celebrating his friend’s life in the crowd, while his fellow band members Simon Le Bon and John Taylor circulated elsewhere. Rhodes remembered how he and Taylor came across Price’s wildly stylish store Plaza in the King’s Road, and how that led to Price making the band the vividly colored suits they wore on a yacht slicing through the Caribbean on the video for their massive 1982 hit “Rio.”

“ John and I went in there and blew our entire EMI clothes budget for our whole album on one suit. As soon as we told him we were planning a shoot in the Caribbean, he said, “Oh, all those beautiful pastel colors! And he started dyeing things in his bath. Everything was hand-made with Antony. When you hear people saying, he was a true artist—well he was. He did everything, could do everything. He was up there with Thierry Mugler in Paris, and John Galliano or Alexander McQueen, after him.”

Price was an ace tailor, sculpting broad-shouldered, narrow-hipped suits based on his own rediscovery of matinee idols and screen goddesses in 1940s Hollywood movies when he was a Royal College of Art fashion student in the 1960s. Born in Keighley in the idyllic Yorkshire countryside in 1945, he went to Bradford school of art, just after David Hockney, then followed him to the Royal College of Art at the time when London was swinging. He was the son of “a Spitfire pilot who left my mother with me and my sister and twins,” he once told me. “We were not a straight-up-and-down family. My sister was a poor man’s Sophia Loren. I’ve always loved that curvaceous type.” A lot of that background stuck with him and imprinted his tastes for life. One thing was his liking for uniform; he’d style himself “like a Star Captain beamed down to earth for an evening’s rock ’n’ roll, often in a military cap ,” as I wrote once in a British Vogue profile.

Then there was his passion for birds, keeping and breeding peacocks and fancifully-feathered chickens. He never said whether his idea of molding clients’ torsos from chicken-wire (as couture dress-forms) had cross-fertilized from this practice, though it was a suspicion. Philip Treacy saluted Price, who sculpted bombshell dresses in black velvet for his millinery shows, remembering just what a brilliant collaborator he was. One season, the young Treacy talked about his idea to use feathers to finish an upcoming collection. “So Antony bred a hundred white Yokohama chickens—from the egg. Then brought them live to my studio so I could clip the tail-feathers off before putting them on the hats,” he laughed, adding, “no chickens died for fashion.”

Price delighted in collaborating with younger creative people, who adored him back for his genius, his delight in making women look more amazing than they’d ever felt, his mind-blowing techniques, and, yes, his “lethal mix of humor and pessimism,” as Treacy put it. There were, indeed, decades when—though he was still operating a couture service for private clients—Price would moan at all and sundry that he’d be forgotten. That was not to be. In what was to be the last year of his life, Marco Capaldo, the young designer of 16Arlington, a fan who idolized Antony Price’s glamour, asked him to collaborate on a show of 16 curvy demi-couture looks. On November 17 last year, they were flamboyantly posed in high-1980s style by a whole new generation, including Lily Allen, wearing the ‘Avenge’ dress, Lila Moss in the feathered ‘Pollo,’ and Lara Stone in the zebra-stripe ‘Jerry.’ The warmth of the ovation as Capaldo brought him out at the end finally showed Price just how much he was adulated and loved.

Kit Butler in 16Arlington and Antony Price

Isaac Ferry, son of Bryan, grew up seeing melees of creative people coming in and out of the house. Price was his godfather. Four years ago, while Price was living in a cottage in Sussex with Isaac, his now wife Elizabeth and brother Tara, he witnessed the couturier’s tireless routine in action. “He’d rise at five, go straight down to the workroom, ‘because I’ve got to get on,’ and he’d be down there, watching the early birds at the feeders. He’d sit at his machine or cutting table, working away while watching Cleopatra, Spartacus, or some other Hollywood spectacular.”

Shortly after the 16Arlington show, Antony Price passed away at his sewing machine at the age of 80. Wednesday’s party was an emblematic finale to a legendary life, toasted with oceans of Moet & Chandon by the constellation of stars of pop, London club-life of the high glam years, clients of two generations who partied for him in exactly the way he might have dreamed up himself. Now, just one thing remains to memorialize the full stature of Antony Price: An exhibition and/or a documentary about him are long overdue.

As traditional giants rush into digital dollars, USDD’s Yvonne Chia explains why 24/7 access, self-custody, and transparent yield give decentralized stablecoins an edge banks can’t replicate. 20 November – Road Town, Totorla, British Virgin Islands – Decentralized stablecoin protocol USDD is accelerating its multichain utility across TRON, Ethereum, and BNB Chain—combining automated sUSDD yield via its Smart Allocator with GasFree zero-TRX transfers—as traditional financial giants race to build competing stablecoin rails. In a recent industry interview with instant exchange partner ChangeNOW, USDD Head of Communications Yvonne Chia outlined why self-custody, 24/7 settlement, and transparent onchain reserves give decentralized digital dollars a structural advantage over bank-issued alternatives. 1. Permissionless Access vs. Gatekept Accounts For billions of people globally, opening and maintaining a traditional bank account still involves paperwork, minimum balance requirements, geographic restrictions, and approval delays. Even when banks integrate stablecoin rails behind the scenes, those services remain gated behind legacy compliance walls and intermediary control. Crypto-native stablecoins flip that model on its head. As Yvonne Chia highlighted during the interview: “Compare this to a stablecoin like USDD, where all you need is a phone, a working internet connection, you can immediately start a wallet and you have access to your funds right away.” With USDD, users can access and manage their digital dollars directly through their own wallets, without depending on traditional financial intermediaries. This represents a fundamental shift in financial access: users are no longer limited to the services, operating hours, or account structures of a single institution. Instead, they have the freedom to hold, manage, and transfer their assets on their own terms. Self-custody also gives users greater autonomy over their financial decisions. They can choose which wallets to use, where to transfer their assets, and which supported decentralized applications to interact with, without needing to route every transaction through a centralized financial institution. With USDD, users’ digital dollars can move with them across an open blockchain ecosystem, rather than remaining tied to a particular bank or service provider. 2. 24/7 Global Settlement vs. “Banking Hours” Legacy payment networks still operate on decades-old schedules: weekend cutoffs, bank holidays, and multi-day cross-border settlement delays accompanied by hefty wire fees. Blockchain networks never close. Whether it is a Sunday night or a public holiday, USDD moves globally without depending on traditional banking operating hours or settlement windows. “We are able to have transfers 24/7 and we’re not limited to any traditional banking hours. So I think that in some ways, stablecoins are not just becoming banks, but exceeding what traditional banks can offer to users.” To make everyday transfers even smoother than fintech apps, USDD introduced GasFree integration on TRON, allowing users on supported wallets to pay network fees directly in USDD without needing to hold TRX. This removes another practical barrier to stablecoin adoption: having to acquire and maintain a separate gas token just to transfer digital dollars. With GasFree, eligible users can pay approximately 0.5 USDD per transaction, compared with around 1.5 USDT for a standard USDT transfer under the referenced fee conditions. These improvements matter because the usefulness of a stablecoin is not determined by its peg alone. For Years, Doctors Blamed My Mysterious Symptoms on Running. A Simple Blood Test Proved Them Wrong.

All along, running actually helped me feel better.

This article originally appeared in the Spring 2025 issue of Runner’s World.

Running and menstruation entered my life around the same time, at age 13. I got hooked on running the mile, and, swayed by a persistent and impactful track coach, I eventually left soccer behind to join the cross-country team. My first period arrived right before my first track season; however, while the training cycles progressed, my menstrual cycle didn’t—my second period wouldn’t come until five months later.

Throughout my years on the high school cross-country team, this irregular pattern stuck: I only got about four periods a year and never knew when they would arrive. I felt confused and left out whenever my teammates asked me for a menstrual pad I had no use for, like my body couldn’t do what it was supposed to do.

When it came to running, though, my body felt strong, capable of carrying me through interval workouts and lifting heavy weights during weight room sessions. On the track, I felt more equal to my teammates. The training cycle was one I could control.

At my yearly physical exams, I expressed concern that my periods weren’t occurring monthly, and the feedback was the same every time: I was still young, and sometimes it takes time to become regular. That I accepted. What didn’t sit well with me was what followed: “You’re missing periods because you run a lot. Try eating more.”

Neither felt like an answer to the problem. My 20-something-mile weeks kept me physically fit and helped me deal with anxiety, especially when my dysfunctional home life felt out of control. Not only did the exam show I kept a healthy weight, I had been putting on muscle mass from deadlifting 200 pounds. But hoping the doctor was right, I took a year off from distance training.

At 19, I started seeing a new primary care physician. My doctor was surprised when I recounted my prior experiences. “So you’ve never had any blood tests done?” they asked. I underwent the test and a pelvic ultrasound, and it didn’t take very long to get an answer: The likely culprit was polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).

PCOS is the most common endocrine disorder in people assigned female at birth of reproductive age, yet so much about the condition is still unknown, including the cause of PCOS, according to the American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology. The National Institutes of Health requires only two of the following symptoms for PCOS diagnosis (all of which I’ve experienced): ovulatory dysfunction (irregular, heavy, or missed periods), high androgen levels (often manifesting as excessive body hair or acne), and/or one or more cysts on the ovaries. Besides irregular menstruation, PCOS can cause, for example, infertility and weight gain. Although there isn’t a permanent cure, some patients are able to treat their symptoms by managing their lifestyle: eating well, exercising regularly, and getting enough sleep.

The diagnosis came at a turbulent time in my life. I was swept up in another cycle: emotional abuse stemming from controlling relationships. I escaped this abuse through schoolwork and decided to take extra classes over the summer so I could graduate from college a year early and find my own place.

But I also got back to running, which has always helped me build a sense of agency and played a big role in my postgrad transition—I spent that summer running across the empty campus and habitually relying on a few miles to calm me when faced with chaos. I started taking an herbal supplement that others with PCOS raved about. I moved in with roommates. I found a therapist to help me unpack the trauma I’ve experienced.

And at 21, I finally started to get a regular monthly period.

Every menstruation since has felt like Christmas morning…followed by some gnawing cramps.

What I am only now starting to understand is just how much my environment affected my mental health and how that in turn impacted my physical health. I recently read research studies linking PCOS and emotional abuse, and that’s when I found what felt like the last missing link.

I still mourn for the runner I was in high school, who felt lost and frustrated without any answers, dealing with conflicting feelings around her body image. I remember lying on cold, uncomfortable tables, intimidated by informational pamphlets—none of which covered hormonal health—all while doctors were delivering confusing medical advice or dismissing my concerns with oversimplified notions. A 2022 health survey found that 29 percent of women and 21 percent of men felt doctors disregarded their worries during visits. I was told my problem existed “because I ran.” In reality, it’s far more complex than what many people are told, and I am just one example.

I’ve since run two half marathons while maintaining regular periods throughout each training cycle. Running was never the problem. On the contrary, the very thing doctors blamed my symptoms on brought me relief all along—and I will never give it up again.

Rainforest ferns from an ancient lineage have a surprisingly young family tree

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

An ancient lineage of rainforest ferns reveals how rising mountains and retreating wetlands may have shaped plant diversity across tropical America.

Some rainforest ferns belong to a lineage that stretches back to the age of dinosaurs. Yet roughly half its living species arose within the past six million years, as mountains rose and landscapes changed.

Researchers at the University of Turku and Aarhus University have reconstructed the history of tropical American Danaea ferns.

The findings draw on decades of work identifying ferns and establishing where they grow. That groundwork made it possible to investigate how the group spread and diversified.

“We have conducted extensive research at the University of Turku in order to understand the biodiversity of the Amazon rainforest,” said senior author Hanna Tuomisto, a professor of ecology.

“In the last 25 years, we have discovered and described 47 new Danaea species previously unknown to science, and have clarified the distribution and phylogenetic relationships of the species in the Danaea genus.”

“We have now been able to link this information to the geological and climatic history of tropical America.”

The team combined DNA evidence from 78 species with 2,289 verified records of where the ferns grow. These records helped reconstruct where ancestral ferns probably lived and the elevations they occupied.

The genetic analysis included 11 species that had not yet been formally described.

The researchers’ preferred estimate suggests that the Danaea lineage split from the lineage of its closest living relatives about 128 million years ago, during the Early Cretaceous.

The common ancestor of its surviving species lived much later, around 53 million years ago. The researchers traced that ancestor to Amazonia.

But an ancient lineage need not consist of ancient species. About half of today’s Danaea species originated during the past six million years.

This complicates a familiar explanation for tropical richness: favorable conditions allowed species to accumulate over immense stretches of time.

Persistence mattered, but the landscape also kept changing, creating opportunities for new species.

Fern diversity generally reaches its greatest heights in tropical mountain forests. Danaea follows a different pattern: Amazonia has consistently held its largest pool of species in the reconstruction.

“Mountain rainforests are home in particular to an abundance of epiphytes – plants that grow on top of other plants. Danaea ferns, on the other hand, grow on the forest floor,” said lead author Venni Keskiniva, a postdoctoral researcher at Turku.

“These research results are, in fact, similar to those obtained for trees, which are also the most species-rich in lowland rainforests.”

Where a plant lives within a forest may therefore help explain where its group becomes most diverse. Findings from one fern group cannot automatically describe them all.

The Andes still played an important role. Although Amazonia contained more species, the mountains became the leading source of dispersal to other regions.

Mountain formation affected far more than the slopes themselves. As the Andes rose, they changed rainfall patterns along with the soils developing across neighboring lowlands.

Vast wetlands once occupied much of western Amazonia. Their drainage near the end of the Miocene opened additional land for forest plants to colonize.

The resulting western Amazonian soils were generally richer in nutrients and more variable, than soils in central Amazonia.

Those differences may have encouraged plants to specialize in different environments.

The timing fits the fern history. Most new Amazonian species arising within the past six million years appeared in western Amazonia during the last three million.

New land in southern Central America also offered room for expansion. Arriving lineages frequently gave rise to additional species as the region’s landscapes developed.

The first mountain-adapted Danaea lineages appeared around 20 million years ago, following an intensification of Andean uplift. Several later spread into regions farther from the equator.

Their earlier move uphill may have helped them cope with the conditions they encountered there.

“As the plants had already adapted to the cooler climate of the mountains, it may have been easier for them to spread to areas where the temperature differences between the seasons are greater,” Keskiniva said.

Lineages that remained adapted to Amazonian lowlands tended to stay within that region.

The repeated sequence of mountain colonization followed by expansion suggests that climate tolerance influenced which routes were available.

The mountains could both separate populations and help others reach new habitats. Their role changed as the landscape rose and different fern lineages established themselves.

Assigning dates to this history was difficult. Many relevant fossils are fragmentary, and researchers disagree about where they belong in the family tree.

The team compared three dating approaches, which produced substantially different ages. It favored the reconstruction most consistent with other biological and geological evidence.

The dates remain estimates, and coinciding events do not establish a cause by themselves. Still, the findings connect the survival of ancient lineages with much younger episodes of diversification.

“The biodiversity in rainforests is the result of a long history, but its formation has also been greatly influenced by events that are relatively recent on a geological time scale,” Keskiniva concluded.

The study is published in the journal Frontiers of Biogeography.

Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge claims he has proved the Riemann hypothesis, one of mathematics’s most notoriously difficult problems, with the help of Claude, cracking a puzzle that has eluded some of the world’s best mathematicians for decades. If verified, it would mark yet another seismic achievement for AI in mathematics and add to the upheaval already unleashed by OpenAI’s previous, often inelegant forays into the field.

Alpöge’s solution has yet to be verified independently. Anthropic has not commented or involved itself in Alpöge’s announcement. In a document on his website, Alpöge says he texted Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei about the solution “late Sunday night” and suggests he held off publication until after the company’s IPO on Thursday.

The Riemann hypothesis is one of seven Millennium Prize problems, which are widely considered some of the most formidable open challenges in mathematics. First proposed in 1859, the Riemann hypothesis concerns the distribution of prime numbers and how zeroes of a function are involved. It’s regarded as one of the most important unsolved problems in mathematics.

A statement on Alpöge’s website says Claude was integral to developing the proof:

I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof1 of the Riemann Hypothesis. In fact, the method easily extends much further, resolving the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and I expect, with more time, the Extended Riemann Hypothesis too. Claude and I have also formalized the arguments without assumptions. It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light, but what I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater.

The Verge reached out to Anthropic but didn’t immediately hear back. Anthropic said it was working on the Riemann Hypothesis with Claude in August, but noted at the time: “We don’t expect that the techniques Claude used will lead to proving the Riemann hypothesis.”

Alpöge’s solution, presented in Lean, has only been published on his website and has not yet been publicly accepted by the Clay Mathematics Institute, which administers the Millennium Prize and offers a $1 million bounty for each problem solved. Under the institute’s rules, a proposed solution must be published and withstand at least two years of scrutiny before it can be considered for the prize, during which time it must have “received general acceptance in the global mathematics community.”

In the quarter-century since the prize was established, only one problem has been officially solved: The Poincaré conjecture, a topological problem concerning three-dimensional spheres. In September, OpenAI claimed to have cracked another, the Navier-Stokes problem, which concerns the flow of fluids.

OpenAI said it pursued Navier-Stokes after hearing rumors that rival researchers were closing in on a solution. Alpöge had been working on the problem with New York University mathematics professor Tristan Buckmaster. Buckmaster claimed OpenAI produced a solution for Navier-Stokes using the route he and Alpöge were working on with OpenAI’s Codex and Claude.

OpenAI spent millions of dollars and deployed 10,000 agents in the attempt. The result is widely considered credible, though it has yet to receive formal recognition. But the achievement has been marred by allegations of questionable research practices, including claims it used unpublished work from mathematicians who had shared their research with its AI tools. OpenAI has strongly denied wrongdoing, but this has done little to quell the uproar.

Though most of the spotlight has been on OpenAI’s movement in math, Alpöge said in July that he solved the Jacobean conjecture using Claude. The deluge of findings has left mathematicians scrambling to digest the material and figure out where they belong in what is rapidly becoming a profoundly different mathematical landscape.

If you live in a hilly area, you need an electric scooter that has enough muscle to get you to the top. However, if you’re a commuter, or just someone on a budget, you also need a scooter that’s relatively light and affordable.

The Apollo Dash hits those notes well. While its 600W motor won’t be competing in any Mr. Universe contests, it’s muscular enough to power you up all but the steepest inclines at a reasonable pace. It’s also very customizable via Apollo’s app, and even has Apple FindMy, so you can keep tabs on its whereabouts. Even better, you can often find it for around $500, making it very reasonably priced for what it offers.

But how does it compare to the best electric scooters? I tested the Apollo Dash for a couple of weeks to find out.

The Apollo Dash, which went on sale in 2026, is normally priced at $699, but for Black Friday and other sales, I've seen it as low as $529 at Apollo's site. If you can get it for that price, it's a pretty good bargain.

Thanks to its 600W motor, the Apollo Dash is pretty good at getting up hills. While it’s not a speed demon like the Apollo City, it did manage to zip me up a 10% grade at around 8 MPH, which is much better than the similarly-priced Segway E3 Go. On a 6% grade hill, the Dash dashed me up at around 12-14 MPH.

Apollo says that the Dash is good for up to 25 miles, using its lowest speed setting; that’s not for me. You can set the Dash to “Ludo” mode, which boosts its top speed to 22 MPH, but at that setting, the max range drops to a still-respectable 17 miles.

Of course, range depends on a lot of factors; after a 5.5-mile jaunt, I had 55% battery remaining, so realistically, I would be able to get a little over 10 miles before the Dash would need a recharge.

Apollo’s app gives you a ton of control over how you want the Dash to perform. For starters, you can set the speed for each speed mode, so if you want to cap Sport mode at 15 MPH, you can do so.

But, you can also adjust things such as the throttle response — how fast you want the scooter to start moving — as well as the amount of regenerative braking. You can also specify how long the turn signals remain on, and how long it takes to activate cruise control.

It’s a lot of granularity that in truth, I’ll rarely touch, but it’s nice to know that it’s there.

One of the things I worry about most when riding a scooter is that cars won’t be able to see me, especially around dusk. So I really like the very large lightbar that extends the length of the Apollo Dash’s downtube; it’s bright enough to really stand out.

If that weren’t enough, the Dash also has a dedicated headlight that’s also quite powerful, and you can adjust it, so that it hits the road where it’ll be the most effective for you.

While the Dash has 9.5-inch air-filled tires, its lack of suspension was definitely noticeable compared with scooters which had some sort of shock absorption. When I was riding over bumps and potholes, it was a much more jarring experience, especially when I was moving at full speed.

I was testing the Apollo Dash the same time as I was testing the Segway E3 Go, and there were a few things that seemed small, but that I appreciated on the Segway. For instance, the Segway’s handlebars are angled back slightly (Apollo’s are straight) and are about three inches longer, which gave me more control when going over bumps.

I also liked that the Segway’s turn signal switch is on the left handlebar. On the Apollo, the left turn signal is on the left, and the right is on the right, so you can’t activate the right turn signal if your thumb is on the throttle.

I’ve rode plenty of low-cost scooters that have struggled to make it to the top of hills in my town; the Apollo Dash is not one of them. It proved more than capable on my tests, and while it’s not going to win any long-distance contests, its range is more than suitable for a few runs to the train station or office and back.

I do wish the ride were more comfortable — the Segway E3 Go has it beat there — but as long as your commute doesn’t involve pothole-strewn streets, you should be ok.

If you want something with more zip, the Apollo Go has dual 750W motors, a higher top speed, and twice the range as the Dash; it’s also a few hundred dollars more. If you need a great mid-range scooter that punches a bit above its weight, then check out the Dash.
