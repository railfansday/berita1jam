10 Mad Magazine Mascot Alfred P. Neuman Quotes To Live By

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The humor periodical Mad started off as a comic in 1952, before taking on its more recognizable magazine with 1955's Mad #24. That cover featured a prominent appearance by the caricature of a character who would come to be known as Alfred E. Neuman. A few issues later, on the cover of Mad #30, Alfred took center stage, officially becoming the magazine's figurehead and mascot.

From its inception, issues of Mad included an epigraph, a quote from a historical figure, politician, or celebrity that set the tone. The first time Mad opened with a quote attributed to Alfred E. Neuman was Mad #38. From that point on, Alfred has had the first, and definitive, line in practically every issue of the magazine since.

Sometimes Alfred's quotes are bawdy, and sometimes they are politically charged. Often, though, they are low-key life lessons, the kind of wisdom that only a comic character can get away with sharing.

"It's a good idea to keep your words soft and sweet because you never know when you'll have to eat them!" Here, Alfred E. Neuman riffs on a centuries-old idiom. Be careful what comes out of your mouth, Neuman warns, because if what you say ends up getting shoved back in your face, down your own throat, you might find yourself choking on the tone and the meanings of your words.

It is sensible advice, especially for comedians, but knowing Alfred, and knowing Mad magazine, this is really a "do as I say, not as I do" case of cautionary advice.

"You'll never get rid of a bad temper by losing it!" There's another saying, which is that you can't control your immediate reaction to something, but you can control your reaction to the reaction. If you're prone to getting angry, giving into it is only going to make the problem worse, that's what Alfred E. Neuman is saying here.

It is a slyly clever bit of wordplay. In fact, this bon mot could be remixed into a riddle. "What can you never get rid of by losing it?" "A bad temper!" The cover for this issue, Mad #71, shows Alfred's mug at the center of a dartboard, and with its opening quote at least, the magazine hit the comedic bullseye.

"Learn from the mistakes of others." That is age-old wisdom. But why? That part is usually left unsaid, but leave it to Alfred E. Neuman to say the quiet part loud. It is "'cause you'll never live long enough to make 'em all yourself!"

Before it was a film franchise, National Lampoon was a groundbreaking American humor magazine; these are the greatest covers from its 3-decade history

Life is finite. There's only so much failure and foible the average person can cram into one lifetime. Recognizing and avoiding others' failures isn't about being more likely to succeed in your own endeavors. It is simply a time-saving exercise. Plus, in some cases, those mistakes might have been fatal, and avoiding them is the only way to guarantee a long life full non-lethal errors in judgment.

The art of the one-liner is trickier than people give it credit for. (Just ask Henny Youngman.) In just a few words, the speaker has to set up and execute the punchline, but there is more to it than that. The line has to conjure an image in people's heads. And that image can be vague, or it can be concrete.

Here, it is literally the latter. "Some minds are like concrete," Alfred E. Neuman says in Mad #82, in the sense that they are "all mixed up and permanently set." In other words, some people are defiantly confused. Obstinately misguided. Flat out wrong and unwilling to revise their opinions. Don't be like those people, Alfred suggests. Be willing to admit your wrong.

Long before Gordan Gecko told the world "greed is good" in Wall Street, Alfred E. Neuman gave Mad readers the real truth about cold hard cash. "Sometimes, the best scheme for doubling your money is to fold it in half and stuff it back in your wallet," Neuman declared in Mad #87.

Meaning, get-rich-quick schemes and seemingly lucrative investments are often not all they seemed cracked up to be. Everyone wants to hustle, and maximize their earnings, but sometimes the smart thing to do is just stack paper. Save up. For Alfred, a folded dollar is worth more than one that is tied up in escrow.

The funny thing about Alfred E. Neuman's advice is that he was often both the angel and the devil on readers' shoulders. His line in Mad #145 exemplifies this. "Sometimes it pays to resist a temptation," he begins. Sounds totally reasonable, and also morally sound, right?

Well, let him finish. Because Alfred also answers why it pays. Not because having will power and self-control are important. Not because resisting temptation is the right thing. No, because temptation is a gamble. Why take the one in front of you when "a better one may be coming along." This hilariously, and cheekily, takes the matter from an ethical equation to a practical matter of when it is the right time to do the wrong thing.

"Experience is something you never have until just after you need it!" This quote from Mad #204 is the perfect companion to the issue's cover, which parodies the Jaws 2 poster, with Alfred E. Neuman piloting a mechanical shark to sneak up on a jet-skiing girl in a bikini.

In the movie, only Chief Brody has the ability to save the day, because only he has the experience of having fought a killer great white before. For most people, most of the time, the toughest moments of their lives are trial and error, giving them the experience that would have made it easier on them only after the fact.

This quote from Mad #221 evokes the earlier entry on this list from Mad #54. It is another way for Alfred E. Neuman to remind readers: "Watch what you say." Especially if you've already gotten yourself in trouble by talking too much.

"When you're in deep water, it's a good idea to keep your mouth shut!" It is a great case of applying a literal lesson to figurative language. If you're in proverbial deep water, or hot water, or any kind of water, talking, or shouting, is only going to result in one thing. Drowning.

Mad #267's cover features a Top Gun parody, and Alfred E. Neuman's quote in the issue makes it clear what kind of people he'd like to strap to the nose of a fighter jet and send flying. "People on ego trips," who, Alfred says bluntly, "should buy one-way tickets."

It is a slick play on words, but the point doesn't mince any. If you are ego-driven and willing to do whatever it takes to feed your own sense of self, take a hike. Take a vacation. Take off and don't come back. For Mad fans, it is a reminder to check themselves before they wreck themselves, and ruin someone else's day too.

The theme of Mad #293's Alfred E. Neuman quote is the same as the prior entry. And it contains essentially the same valuable life lesson. Which is, life is hard for everyone, so don't go around making it harder.

Or as Alfred puts it: "It's not just the ups and downs that make life difficult. It's the jerks." People who go around being miserable all the time need to do more than just sit down and read a Mad magazine, or a Far Side comic, or even a Garfield, and try to lighten up. They need to think about how they are impacting other people. That's what Mad's irreverent, but often on-point mascot is saying here.

The 6 Most Outrageous GTA 6 Myths, Debunked

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The internet is already drowning in GTA 6 misinformation

After a wild opening weekend, Grand Theft Auto 6 is off to the races. Millions are playing Rockstar’s enormous open-world game and discovering its complexities in real time. There’s a lot to unpack in a game that’s filled with intersecting gameplay systems across a long story, and some players are already taking advantage of that. At the same time, the opening days of GTA 6’s launch have been filled with misinformation, much of it fueled by culture warriors and content creators looking to turn outrage into clicks on social media platforms that incentivize such behavior.

Because of that, there are a lot of myths about GTA 6 currently floating around the internet. Some come from harmless misunderstandings, while others are more deliberate misrepresentations intended to scare off potential players. To help you navigate the noise, we’ve rounded up some of the most rampant myths currently circulating around GTA 6 and fact-checked them accordingly.

The claim: GTA 6’s story forces you to do chores for Lucia instead of crimes.

If you spent launch day watching reactions to GTA 6’s early hours roll in instead of playing, you might have walked away thinking that Rockstar made a chore simulator. That’s thanks to a few early missions that revolve around Jason getting his home ready for Lucia as she’s about to be released from prison. Players will paint a bedroom for her, fix a leaky roof, and repair some stairs. There’s also an optional quest where players can run errands to prepare for Lucia's arrival, like picking up groceries or buying her a necklace. All of this happens in the first few hours of the game. GTA 6 quickly ramps up after that, getting into the series’ usual flow of cop chases, heists, and shootouts. You can call it a slow start, but know that any mundane missions you’ve seen make up a very small amount of story that you’re likely to see in your very first play session.

The claim: GTA 6 doesn’t allow you to steal cars, or heavily restricts what you can steal.

While there’s a grain of truth to this, it has been heavily exaggerated on social media. There are some select cars that you can’t steal in GTA 6, like those around Jason’s neighborhood or in an NPC’s car lot. But for the most part, you can jack just about any vehicle you see in Leonida. The catch is that you’ll need a device that can copy keys to get into some of the more expensive cars, and you won’t get that until Chapter 3. Being able to steal anything requires some patience as you play through the story, but be rest assured that you can do as much grand theft auto as ever in Grand Theft Auto.

The claim: Airplanes will bounce off of buildings instead of exploding on impact.

This one is a little tricky to unpack because there seem to be two weird things colliding here. After videos of airplanes bouncing off of skyscrapers like rubber began to emerge on social media, players deduced that content creators were sharing videos where they had their Reduced Vehicle Damage setting turned on. It seemed like a textbook case of outrage farming, though there may be a slight bit of truth here. Some players have speculated that GTA 6 may have a few physics bugs that make it tough for certain airliners to blow up as expected. That may be an edge case, but I can confirm that I have crashed plenty of planes into buildings in GTA 6, and they have all exploded into a fiery wreck so far.

The claim: GTA 6 features no autosave, and you will lose progress if you don’t manually save.

When players started reporting that they were losing hours of gameplay because GTA 6 didn’t have an autosave feature, I was very confused. I can open my save file right now and see a dedicated auto save slot that is regularly refreshed as I play. I initially wrote it off as players either lying or not realizing that the auto save only triggers after completing major missions, but it turns out they weren’t hallucinating. GTA 6’s first patch fixed an issue where auto save wasn’t triggering properly. So, yes, GTA 6 does have autosave; it just wasn’t working correctly for some players at launch. It shouldn’t be a problem now, but always remember to save early and often just in case.

The claim: Lucia is cheating on Jason during the story.

This one is downright bizarre. One of the first talking points that came out about GTA 6 was that Jason is a “cuck” who does not realize that Lucia is cheating on him. As someone who has played through the story, I spent days trying to figure out where in the world that blatant lie could be coming from. It turns out it was a good old misinterpretation. Early in the story, Lucia sends a text to an unknown character, which she rushes to hide from Jason when he enters the scene. While some have interpreted that as Lucia having a secret lover, it actually turns out that she was texting a friend from prison. To players’ credit, the scene does feel like it’s built to make you think Lucia is hiding a big secret from Jason, so that reaction might have been exactly what Rockstar was hoping to provoke.

The claim: GTA 6 does not allow you to open fire and kill people in a gay club.

The ugliest rumor to come out of GTA 6 so far is that the game will not allow you to walk into a gay club in Vice City and kill people. There was even a clip going around social media that seemed to support that, sparking some grotesque reactions. The truth is that the location in the video isn’t specifically a gay club. It is a club, and it’s true that you can’t open fire in it, but that’s only because you can’t shoot inside any establishment in GTA 6. This claim would technically be true if there was a gay club in the game, but only as a side effect of the wider systems working the way they do. What is true, though, is that the game features an unkillable non-binary character, according to the actor who portrayed them.

Why Ultra-Long-Haul Flights Are Easier On An Aircraft Than Short Hops

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Services like Qatar Airways' flight from Doha to Auckland and Singapore Airlines' route from Singapore to New York are among the most challenging flights in the world. They push modern aircraft to their limits, testing their range, performance, and fuel burn like no other. There's been a noticeable increase in ultra-long-haul flights in recent years, driven by the arrival of new aircraft like the Airbus A350 and Boeing 787 Dreamliner, which are more capable and efficient than earlier widebodies.

However, while these flights push planes to their technical limits, they aren't particularly physically demanding on the aircraft. Longer flights are actually much less stressful on the airframe and the engines than operating multiple shorter services in a given day with few breaks in between. Naturally, multiple factors go into how stressed an aircraft is during a flight, and time between flights also matters.

The lifespan of large transport aircraft is really determined by two measurements: cycles and hours. The hours metric is as the word implies, while cycles measure the number of times an aircraft is pressurized and depressurized. This roughly corresponds to the number of flights the aircraft takes, although airliners do occasionally operate flights without fully pressurizing the cabin (these are almost always ferry flights flown when the aircraft has a pressurization issue).

Widebody aircraft operating long-haul and ultra-long-haul flights tend to rack up hours very quickly, while short-haul airliners rack up cycles quickly. However, while hours are one measure of an airframe's lifespan, the number of cycles/flights operated is a more important metric for determining how stressed the aircraft is overall. Operating multiple flights means the landing gear and brakes are used for multiple landings, while the fuel and hydraulic systems also wear with each flight.

In addition, each flight involves the engines being pushed to takeoff power and, typically, the reverse thrusters being activated upon landing (this doesn't occur with every landing, but it occurs onalmost every landing). This puts strain on the engines, whereas an aircraft operating a long-haul or ultra-long-haul flight only performs one takeoff and one landing. The number of hours an aircraft flies certainly stresses the airframe, but its cycles and the number of flights it takes physically stress the plane even more.

Hawaiian Airlines currently operates 19 717-200s. These planes are used exclusively to operate between the Hawaiian islands, which means that they routinely operate eight, nine, 10, or even 11 flights per day. Most of these flights last under 40 minutes, so they're not racking up hours as quickly as a typical widebody. However, despite the short length of these routes, the Hawaiian interisland operations are considered some of the most demanding in the world.

The aircraft's engines are routinely pushed to takeoff power, then reverse thrust is used on the runway, and then this process is repeated multiple times per day. The engines are constantly being used and rarely have time to cool down. The landing gear and brakes are used frequently, and short turnaround times mean the plane rarely rests. What's more, the environment exposes the aircraft to salt particles in the air, raising corrosion concerns and increasing stress on the airframe.

737 MAX 7, 737 MAX 8, 737 MAX 9, 737 MAX 10

One of Hawaiian's biggest challenges was determining how these aging planes would be replaced. The engines on newer types like the A321neo and 737 MAX would not take kindly to the constant stress, while their fuel efficiency would provide little benefit. Ultimately, Alaska Air Group (which now owns the Hawaiian brand) opted to use older 737-800s from Alaska's fleet to replace the 717s, presumably scheduling extra downtime due to the aircraft's size and to allow the planes to rest more.

The A350's reputation as a 'passenger favorite' masks a more complex truth for flight crews, whose rest experiences can vary widely.

Hour and cycle limits are mainly used for pressurized aircraft because of the stresses pressurization introduces on the airframe. While other components of the aircraft also get stressed, pressurization is one of the largest stress factors. Each time the aircraft is pressurized, the fuselage expands slightly as the high pressure air inside the cabin pushes the fuselage against the low-pressure air outside. Over time, this can fatigue the fuselage, potentially resulting in microscopic cracks.

Regularly scheduled maintenance inspections uncover any potential fatigue cracks before they become a safety issue, but the pressurization cycle nonetheless puts significant stress on the fuselage. The pressurization cycle and these stress forces were a major factor in two mid-flight breakups of the de Havilland Comet in the 1950s, the world's first jet airliner. While it was not the first pressurized aircraft, no other airliner flew so high, and the intense forces from pressurization cycles fatigued the fuselage far faster than anticipated.

Metallic fuselages are particularly susceptible to the stress forces caused by pressurization, while newer carbon-composite designs are much stronger. This has allowed manufacturers to lower cabin altitudes by using higher-pressure air, which would ordinarily require a much heavier metal fuselage due to the added strengthening needed. Meanwhile, some business jets have even lower cabin altitudes while flying higher than airliners, which is justifiable as weight and fuel burn are less of a concern in private aviation.

Aircraft are designed to serve for upwards of 30 or even 35 years, so aircraft engineers design them with a specific service life in mind that covers typical usage across multiple decades. Manufacturers perform fatigue analysis to establish safe limits and inspection intervals for the aircraft, while working with regulators to determine the aircraft's lifespan. This is designed around the plane's intended mission, with widebodies and narrowbodies having vastly different hours-to-cycles ratios.

In addition to the aircraft's overall lifespan, various components may have their own maintenance intervals, with engines in particular largely on their own schedules. Overall, aircraft designers set goals for the expected service life of the aircraft and its components, and then work diligently to achieve them. In addition, the original limits set for an aircraft are often increased by the manufacturer later in the type's service life, usually via an upgrade that can be retrofitted onto pre-existing examples.

The A320 was originally designed with a service limit of 60,000 flight hours or 48,000 cycles, whichever occurs first. Later on, Airbus was able to increase these limits to 120,000 hours or 60,000 cycles, whichever occurs first. The 767, meanwhile, is designed with a limit of 50,000 cycles, fewer than the A320, but more hours. In addition, some aircraft types are converted to freighters later in their lives, and these projects often come with post-conversion limits as well.

Is there a possibility that Boeing will make an extended-range version of the 787 Dreamliner?

An aircraft's age is often quoted in terms of years, but this doesn't have much of an impact on its actual remaining lifespan. While age can determine factors such as reliability and which parts need to be used, planes can easily operate for upwards of 40 years if they don't exceed their hour and cycle limits. Instead, however, planes are largely retired for economic reasons, as maintaining an old aircraft that has operated for decades is possible but rarely cheap.

As aircraft become older, they become less reliable, and maintenance costs tend to rise. In addition, airliners require multiple types of maintenance overhauls, including what are often dubbed 'D checks', where basically the entire aircraft is taken apart, which come at a huge cost. Older aircraft also burn more fuel than newer ones, which makes it much more expensive to operate aging planes. Oftentimes, airlines will retire their oldest planes right before their next major overhaul, and then they get sent for scrapping.

Because aircraft are retired for economic reasons, it's actually rare to see them near their service limitations. What's more, aging planes tend to be used less often towards the end of their lives due to their high operating costs, which further slows the pace at which the aircraft approaches its service limits. As such, some aircraft remain in service for upwards of 40 years with small cargo operators and in nations such as Iran, as well as older 737s and A320s that are nearly 40 years old, since they remain within their hour and cycle limits.

'Blade Runner 2099' Creator Reveals the Biggest Challenge for Prime Video’s New Sci-Fi Series

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

In just a few days, we will be going back to the dystopian future of replicants with a new limited series from Prime Video. Blade Runner 2099 jumps the story forward 50 years and places us in a world where the replicants have finally come out on top and humans have been turned into second-class citizens. The series follows Cora (Hunter Schafer), a human who has gone undercover as a replicant, who works as a blade runner alongside Olwen (Michelle Yeoh), a dying replicant in the last days of her life.

Collider got to speak with creator and showrunner Silka Luisa and director Jonathan van Tulleken about this brand new entry into the sci-fi franchise and how the series differentiates itself from the previous two films in the universe. Van Tulleken reveals the films he drew from as reference for the visual palette of the show, and Luisa reveals the process of what it takes to transfer a story into a new format. Luisa also talks about the big change between replicants and humans and how Cora and Olwen's story gives us insight into the new dynamic between the two groups. Finally, she gives some insight into the show's connection to both Blade Runner and Blade Runner 2049 and why the show decided to lean away from drawing stronger connections to the previous film.

Going into this, what's the most daunting task when it comes to formulating the world of Blade Runner 2099?

JONATHAN VAN TULLEKEN: I think Silka had written on the page this concept of the sunshine noir, and it was beautiful because Ridley [Scott] described his movie as a 40-year-old movie set 40 years in the future. And [Jordan] Cronenweth had looked to Citizen Kane, his cinematographer, and he described Harrison Ford as a Bogart detective story. And I think, for me, when reading what Silka had written, I was going, "Well, what is our touchstone then? What is our 40 years, 40 years ago?" So I was really thinking of those movies from the 70s and 60s, you know, The French Connection by [William] Friedkin and [Alan J.] Pakula's paranoia trilogy.

What makes those films so connected to what Ridley does as well is they've so much tactility; they're based in vérité. It was where cameras were small enough that you could take them out onto the streets. I think that really led into how we wanted to approach the work, which was something that felt very human, in a way that Ridley's feels so human; it's about what it is to be human, and so we wanted to make it feel human. So we built places that were real; we used original old lights; we used the original glass from the films, the lenses. I think that allowed us to build on what Ridley started and what Denis [Villeneuve] started.

Now the replicants are the ones doing the hunting.

SILKA LUISA: I think just from the narrative perspective, obviously, there's a shift from something that is so cinematic and theatrical. It's a really different experience going into a theater and being locked in a room and letting this world wash over you. We knew we were going to be watching this on TV, and so we really had to consider what is the pace of our Blade Runner? There is a certain tempo to both films that is very meditative; I love it, it puts you in a mindset that is that world, so we were always trying to calibrate both in the writing and in the shooting and ultimately in post when we were editing, how do we pace the show?

Can you have it be this much faster heartbeat? Do you also then need to create moments of breath so that you can actually do the score, which is so iconic? Early on in editing, we had cut the episodes really, really tight just to get that propulsion of Cora's story. But we realized we really needed those breaths because without them, it's not really Blade Runner, and so we were always balancing that.

Talking about the show and the specifics of the narrative, I really like that you flip the script on what we know before, making replicants into Blade Runners who hunt humans. But how did switching the narrative of the story change the approach to how you might structure the story with the focus being on the replicants more than on humans, or at least more than in previous stories?

LUISA: Well, I think the biggest shift was that it was an undercover story. I knew because we were doing a TV show, we would have more time, and that meant that there could be a relationship at the center of the show, not just a single character and their journey. And if you want to tell a relationship story, you know, there's been so many great undercover [stories], that Donnie Brasco dynamic felt like that was going to give you a human and a replicant side-by-side, both of them hiding secrets, that would be really rich terrain.

It's interesting that in this show, you're right, you do have more room to develop things, and you have dual protagonists in the form of Cora and Olwen. Can you talk a little bit about how these two characters have a meeting of the minds as the story progresses? Because Olwen is getting older, she's dying, whereas Cora is forced to go undercover and has to be forced to think more like a replicant.

LUISA: Yeah, at the beginning of the season, both of them are really lacking empathy. Olwen because she's a replicant, and that is just something; without childhoods, they don't develop it in the same way. It's one of the defining characteristics between humans and replicants that we know from the films. And Cora is just a human who is just been stripped of her soul, because sometimes you're in these circumstances where you just are, "Go, go, go," and you can't really take a breath to even think about other people because you just are trying to live the next day. [We] wanted to tell a story of two people who really have lost this fundamental part of what the human experience is, and how can they rediscover it together? Or can they? Can a human and a replicant being side-by-side... can that awaken empathy in both of them?

I'm curious because I know this story does have connections to the original film and 2049. So considering the reveal that we get from 2049 about replicants being able to have children, I was always thinking that in the back of my mind. Why didn't you guys lean into that? Are we going to get more of that, or are there hints trickled throughout the show? Because I have thoughts.

I've seen them all, but I don't want to get too deep into spoilers, but I'm curious about your thoughts on that.

LUISA: Well, I definitely wanted to leave 2049 with enough space to tell their story, because what I love about the end of that film is it felt like it left me wanting more. I could imagine different directions for how all those characters' lives continue, and what it meant for the city, what it meant for them individually, which I think is something that Blade Runner always does. In terms of is the focus on replicants being able to have babies? No, because I feel like that is connected to the 2049 story in such a fundamental way that it felt like touching it would be making decisions for how they were to finish that film.

In a West Texas rock shelter, archaeologists found a hunting kit that was left behind 6,500 years

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Archaeologists in Texas uncovered a roughly 6,500-year-old hunting kit with darts, an atlatl and a rare preserved pronghorn hide.

Deep inside San Esteban Rockshelter, south of Marfa, Texas, archaeologists recovered parts of a hunter’s weapon kit that someone left behind thousands of years ago.

The Center for Big Bend Studies at Sul Ross State University presented the find in its spring 2025 newsletter. The center puts the kit’s age at about 6,500 years and calls it “potentially the oldest example of a full weapon system in North America.”

The kit as a whole has not yet been described in a peer-reviewed study. Only one of its pieces, a spear-thrower known as an atlatl, has been the subject of a journal paper.

The kit includes six stone-tipped dart foreshafts – the front section of a dart – and four self-tipped hardwood dart foreshafts

There are also four dart nock-ends, the rear tips that fit against the atlatl. A broken hunting boomerang and a partial atlatl complete the weapons.

The excavators also found a folded pronghorn hide. It had been tanned, and its hair was still intact.

Spaced holes ran around the hide’s perimeter so it could be tied to a frame, as Plains groups often did.

“We just sat there and stared at it in wonder,” said Bryon Schroeder, then director of the center. Someone had folded the hide and set it on top of a rock, he explained.

“And nobody touched it for 6,000 years,” Schroeder said.

An atlatl is essentially a wooden stick with a handle on one end and a hook or socket on the other. It acts as an extension of the human arm, increasing leverage against the dart, the National Park Service explains.

The San Esteban atlatl is described in a 2023 atlatl paper by Schroeder and Devin Pettigrew, an assistant professor at the center.

A radiocarbon date indicates that the atlatl is more than 6,000 years old. That made it the oldest directly dated atlatl in North America at the time, the authors reported in the Journal of Big Bend Studies.

In a paper published in 2026, Pettigrew and other archaeologists at the center measured how fast modern throwers launch darts.

That research shows that heavier darts, paired with strong and skilled throwers, become lethal to very large animals such as mammoths, the center said.

“In most archeological sites in Texas, only things made of stone survive,” notes Texas Beyond History, a project of the University of Texas at Austin. The dry Lower Pecos rock shelters are an exception.

Those shelters have yielded hardwood dart foreshafts and rabbit sticks, as well as bow fragments and arrow foreshafts from the bow-and-arrow era. Complete atlatls are very rare even there.

Unique depositional conditions favored the preservation of the Schöningen wooden spears in Germany. A 2025 dating study placed them at about 200,000 years old.

A mammoth-ivory boomerang from Poland is one of the oldest known in Europe, an Obłazowa Cave analysis concluded. A statistical model built on dated bones from the same layer places it between 42,290 and 39,280 years ago.

“We can use the wood to reconstruct the environment and learn more about the amount of time they spent working on tools,” Schroeder said.

Schroeder’s reading of the scene is that a person came to the back of the cave and sorted through the gear piece by piece. Some parts were judged good and others not, and then the person went on their way.

“But that one small act is going to have profound implications in understanding the environment,” he said.

The shelter sits in Presidio County along Alamito Creek, a tributary of the Rio Grande. A spring-fed rock pool, or tinaja, lies in front of it.

The center has worked there since 2019 with the Odyssey Archaeological Research Fund at the University of Kansas. The partners were looking for traces of the region’s earliest people.

The Odyssey fund was established in 2003 with an endowment from Joseph and Ruth Cramer. Its director is Rolfe Mandel, a distinguished professor of anthropology at the university.

In 2020, the excavators uncovered a hearth-like feature with burned and unburned bird eggshells dated to about 15,200 years ago.

“Though this site was pot-hunted in the past, the many large boulders that spalled from the ceiling preserved pockets of shelter deposits,” the Kansas team’s site summary states. Pot-hunting means digging for artifacts to collect.

The atlatl foreshafts and darts came from intact deposits in an area the excavators call the Sandal Shaft. The Kansas team dates them to about 6,200 years ago, a little younger than the center’s figure.

The pronghorn hide lay about 2 inches (5 centimeters) above them, capped with a metate, a grinding stone.

The hide yielded a raw radiocarbon age of 5,621 years, plus or minus 24, not yet converted to calendar years. That makes it about 600 years younger than some of the foreshafts and darts, the Kansas researchers report.

The information was obtained from the Center for Big Bend Studies.
