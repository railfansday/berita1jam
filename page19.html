15 Best American Movies Of All Time Ranked, Including Once Upon A Time...In Hollywood

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

There have been approximately 500,000 feature-length films released to date, spanning several genres including action, drama, comedy, sci-fi, and more. This total includes titles released in Hollywood, along with other major cinema markets like India (Bollywood) and China (with its ever thriving box office). Of this total, around 50,000 of these are major American movies (which includes studio films, independent projects, and more).

The United States is often considered to be the hub for cinema, with the longstanding presence of this business in cities like New York, Chicago, and Los Angeles (especially the latter). Some of the biggest movies ever made have been in the U.S., with franchises like Star Wars and the Marvel Cinematic Universe both being funded and made by Americans.

Not only does the United States have some of the biggest movies, but the country is also home to some of the very best movies made as well. Titles like Citizen Kane, The Godfather, and the original Star Wars trilogy are all products of American auteurs from U.S.-based studios. Not only that, but these successful projects span nearly a century, with films like the aforementioned Citizen Kane debuting roughly 85 years ago (and with plenty of amazing movies still being made in modern times, too).

When looking at the best American movies ever made, other titles come to mind as well. Films from directors like Steven Spielberg, Quentin Tarantino, and the Coen brothers, along with classics like The Wizard of Oz and It’s a Wonderful Life, all deserve to be in contention for this prestigious list.

Once again, one of the very best films ever made is Citizen Kane, which was released in 1941. Although it may have made its debut before the United States even officially entered World War II, and despite its lack of color, this Orson Welles masterpiece is the kind of movie that is way ahead of its time in virtually every aspect.

Citizen Kane, which was written, directed by, and starring Orson Welles, tells the story of the life of Charles Foster Kane. Kane’s life, from childhood to old age, is portrayed in the film, and includes his rise to owner of the New York Inquirer and an ambitious run for Governor. All of this is told within the framework of a mystery presented in the film’s very first scene, which shows Kane’s death, and his very famous final words: “Rosebud.” Citizen Kane then explores that mystery and teases the cryptic meaning behind them.

There are many reasons why Citizen Kane is such a classic. Welles’ acting is exceptional, as is his innovative and industry-changing direction. There’s also the usage of non-linear storytelling, which had seldom, if ever, been done in mainstream movies up until this point. Citizen Kane popularized this tool, thus paving the way for countless classic non-linear movies (Christopher Nolan’s movies come to mind here, as the director is known for his affection for this kind of narrative).

Stanley Kubrick was an English director, but one of his most famous films, 2001: A Space Odyssey, was made by an American studio and stars American actors. As such, 2001 more than belongs on this list, and deserves its place among the best American movies ever made. This movie is truly extraordinary, with visuals that are still impressive decades later. Additionally, Kubrick’s hard-to-wrap your head around story deserves great praise, too, as it paved the way for other filmmakers, like David Lynch, to explore the abstract in their own projects.

Some movies on this list were overlooked upon their initial release, only to receive acclaim in the years that followed. Others, meanwhile, were praised right away, and 1991s The Silence of the Lambs belongs firmly in that category. The film, which stars Jodie Foster and Anthony Hopkins, won the “Big Five” awards at the Oscars — Best Picture, Best Actor, Best Actress, and Best Adapted Screenplay.

And, just like many other films on this list, the story of The Silence of the Lambs is likely very familiar. The film follows FBI trainee Clarice Starling as she’s tasked with meeting the infamous serial killer Hannibal Lecter, with the hope that Hopkins’ character will offer some insight that may lead to the capture of another killer, Buffalo Bill, who’s still on the loose.

This movie remains one of the best American films ever made, even 35 years after its release, in large part because of the performances by Foster and Hopkins. Both give some of the best turns in recent Hollywood history, and performances that have greatly influenced the future of the industry, too. For example, Hopkins’ portrayal of a serial killer has become the basis for actors playing murderers ever since.

Halloween is one of the simplest films on this list, with the story of a masked killer returning to his hometown and wreaking havoc among the residents, who fight to stay alive amidst his murder spree. Yet, that simplicity doesn’t go against John Carpenter’s film, which is not just one of the best horror movies ever, but one of the best films ever, too.

The straightforwardness of Halloween’s narrative is actually one of the film’s greatest strengths, as it allows the characters, like Michael Myers, Dr. Loomis, and Laurie Strode, the chance to truly shine. In turn, the suspense and terror of the film’s story is allowed to grow, too. Ultimately, Halloween (1978) is a masterpiece in horror storytelling, with expertly executed scares and a main villain that remains one of the best movie characters ever.

Steven Spielberg has made so many classic films in his career, but one that stands near (or at the very top) of the list is E.T. The Extra-Terrestrial. The 1982 film focuses on a young boy who discovers a gentle alien stranded on Earth, and the two then bond as they evade government officials and attempt to send E.T. home.

This movie truly has it all — it’s funny, relatable (with an excellent portrayal of kids at this age), and, above all else, extremely emotional. The film’s ending, where E.T. goes home on his spaceship, is simultaneously heartwarming and heartbreaking at the same time, even all these years later.

Actor James “Jimmy” Stewart served in the United States military during World War II and, just one year after the end of the conflict, starred in what proved to be the biggest film of his career (and one often watched during this time of year) — It’s a Wonderful Life. The film, which is loosely based on Charles Dickens’ A Christmas Carol, is essentially the perfect holiday movie.

The story follows George Bailey, a down-on-his-luck man who plans to take his own life on Christmas Eve. However, his plans are thwarted when he’s visited by a Guardian Angel, who shows him why life is still worth living. It’s a Wonderful Life is one of the most emotionally touching movies ever made, making it one of the best movies ever made, period, in turn.

Casablanca may be named after a city in Morocco, but it’s firmly an American film, and one of the country’s very best, in fact. The film, which was released in 1943 (at the height of World War II) and stars Humphrey Bogart, has the classic redemption arc for its leading character. Bogart’s Rick Blaine claims that he won’t risk his life for any cause (or anyone), but, over the course of the movie, changes his mind and becomes a staunch anti-fascist.

It’s the kind of character arc that has been repeated countless times since, perhaps most notably in the very first Star Wars film, which saw Han Solo go from being ambivalent towards the Empire to becoming a full-blown Rebel hero. This makes Casablanca not only an incredible movie, but very influential as well. Not only that, but the film is also beautifully made, with exceptional acting, writing (excellent dialogue in particular), and cinematography.

Bar The Godfather trilogy, Francis Ford Coppola’s most famous work is arguably 1979s Apocalypse Now. The film had a tumultuous production, but these hardships certainly paid off in the end. Apocalypse Now is, in so many ways, the perfect war movie. It shows the harrowing realities of the conflict in Vietnam, and gets deep into the psyche of the characters at the heart of the story.

The Coen Brothers’ best film, Fargo remains a masterclass in tone, suspense, and humor, even thirty years later. The film, which is set in the frozen town of Fargo, North Dakota, focuses on a world of crime that comes to the small town after a car salesman hires hitmen to kill his wife. The plot may sound dark, but the Coen Brothers inject the film with so much life that it actually ends up being very funny, emotional, and, above all else, a true whirlwind of emotions.

The Wizard of Oz had plenty of reported issues during production, including drug use, infighting among the cast and crew, and going over budget. Yet, none of that affected the final product, which remains one of the best American movies ever made. The story of Dorothy and her dog Toto traveling to Oz, meeting a group of friends, and attempting to get back to Kansas is simply timeless. It feels like a modern-day fairy tale in this regard, and this movie is so good that it’s no wonder that the franchise is still going nearly 90 years later.

Every Frame Of Disney+'s 27-Episode Sci-Fi Masterpiece Is A Painting

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Many shows claim to be visual marvels, but few of them deserve that title as much as Disney+'s science fiction anthology with 27 episodes and a spinoff show. As the budgets of TV shows continue to grow and the emphasis on prestige television continues to be popular, the list of the most visually stunning shows in history is constantly being updated. From beautiful animation to amazing landscapes and astonishing CGI, TV shows have only gotten more visually pleasing with time.

Some of the best shows on Disney+ also belong on that list, especially considering how vast the streaming service's catalog is. One of the most absolutely beautiful shows on Disney+, however, doesn't get the recognition it deserves. A Star Wars show, Star Wars: Visions, features some of the most breathtaking animation on Disney+ in general, to the point where every one of its frames is basically a painting.

Though it's not considered part of Star Wars canon, Visions is still well worth watching. Visions is an anthology series that focuses on showing every corner of the galaxy far, far away from a different perspective. Each of the show's 27 episodes has its own subject ranging from battles between Jedi and Sith to a punk rock band that performs for Jabba the Hutt. The most impressive part of Visions, however, is the stunning animation.

As previously mentioned, few shows deserve the title of "visual marvel" as much as Star Wars: Visions does. That's because each of Visions' 27 episodes, with a few repeats, was animated by a different studio from around the world. Volumes 1 and 3 primarily feature Japanese anime studios, but Volume 2 features animators from Ireland, India, Chile, South Korea, France, England, Spain, and more.

This Sci-Fi series from the biggest genre franchise is a masterpiece with a near-perfect Rotten Tomatoes score that could go on forever for 1 reason.

Each of those distinct studios also has their own distinct animation style. One episode of Visions, for example, is a 2D Japanese anime, another is 3D animated, and another still is stop-motion. Even within the Visions episodes that fall under the anime banner, there's still a huge amount of variety; one studio might use an ethereal inkblot style, while another uses more traditional full-color techniques. Every episode offers something unique and visually stunning.

The fact that Visions is an anthology series also adds to the visual spectacle of it. At under half an hour per episode, each studio gets to devote resources and attention they can't typically spare for an entire season of television. As such, every episode of Star Wars: Visions is essentially a short film, and every frame could be a painting of its own. The care and passion these animators had for their work drip through every second of television, and the end product is simply amazing.

One of the very few problems with Star Wars: Visions' format of featuring different animation studios and styles lies with its episode lengths. Visions episodes typically last less than half an hour, meaning a single studio and animation style will only get a handful of minutes to display their work, with a few exceptions. If you find a style, story, or aesthetic you really enjoy, there's only one or two episodes to watch.

Luckily, Star Wars recently began fixing that issue by giving Visions its first full-length spinoff show: The Ninth Jedi. Based on Production I.G's episodes from the original show, The Ninth Jedi is a direct continuation of Lah Kara's story featuring the same style of animation, albeit with a few minor updates. With eight full-length episodes, Production I.G got to expand their visual style to new planets, new characters, and thrilling new lightsaber battles.

The Ninth Jedi also highlights the sheer narrative potential of Star Wars: Visions. The story of The Ninth Jedi began as two short episodes, yet it grew into a grand tale about the dangers of apathy, the importance of resistance, and the need to discover one's darkest depths before they can reach their brightest heights. It's one of the best Star Wars shows in general, and it wouldn't have been possible if Star Wars: Visions hadn't made it possible.

The campaign occurred over a period of 48 hours beginning on September 8, and the prompts were written in Simplified Chinese, with timestamps in executed scripts configured to China Standard Time.

Before the AI agents found and exploited the zero day, however, they tried to break into another university’s Redis server over about 16 hours and 148 sequential actions, including abusing Redis's replication feature to load unauthorized code, escaping its scripting sandbox, writing to its configuration files, and corrupting its memory.

None of these were successful, and when those actions failed, the agents also attempted a Log4Shell injection against a different service, a server-side request forgery (SSRF) attack against a vulnerability in a co-located Qdrant instance, and several credential sprays against PostgreSQL, SSH, and other tools.

To deploy an AI-built app to your own server, you do five things: get the code into a Git repository, find out whether it is static files or a server, decide where its backend lives, build it into a container image with the right environment variables, and run that image behind a reverse proxy that serves your domain over HTTPS. Lovable, Bolt.new, Replit, v0 and Claude Code all take this path; what differs is the stack each one produces and the backend it leans on.

The Dockerfiles and the Caddy and Docker Compose files in the builder guides were built and run in Docker the same day, on Node.js 22, against fresh apps from the Vite, TanStack Start and Next.js starters and a small Express and PostgreSQL app.

Every builder hosts what it builds: Lovable publishes to lovable.app with custom domains and a managed backend, Bolt has Bolt Cloud hosting with .bolt.host addresses, Replit publishes to its own Google Cloud infrastructure, and v0 publishes to Vercel.
