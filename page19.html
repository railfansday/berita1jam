Persona 6 Doesn't Have A Date Yet, But Atlus Says You Won't Have To Wait As Long For Persona 7

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There's been a lot of talk around how long GTA fans have been waiting for the latest entry to launch, but Persona isn't actually that far behind. Persona 5 – in its original state – released over a decade ago on 15th September 2016, and although Persona 6 has now been officially announced, we're none the wiser as to when it will actually come out.

It's likely to finally land at some point in 2027 after the dust has settled from Persona 4 Revival, but according to a recent interview with general producer Kazuhisa Wada (thanks, Nintendo Everything), fans won't have to wait quite so long for the inevitable Persona 7.

"We will continue to cherish what makes Persona unique, while also embracing changes in the times and needs, and taking on new challenges. We aim to be a brand that can grow for 5, 10 years, and beyond.

"Among those projects, the one we’re currently working on is Persona 6. While there’s still limited information I can share, we want to prove the results of all the challenges we’ve faced so far through the game itself. With that in mind, we’re creating something even better than before, and we’re feeling a real sense of accomplishment as we work on it.

"And Persona 6 is not the end. We are working on various things to ensure that you don’t have to wait as long for the next installment as we have in the past. And in order to make Persona an even bigger IP, we are constantly thinking about creative ideas and developments that will not disappoint your expectations. Please look forward to what’s to come from Persona.”

We will look forward to it, Wada-san, thank you sir. Let's keep the focus on Persona 4 Revival and Persona 6 for now though, hm?

It's not a surprising statement, mind you. Game development is taking longer than ever these days, which also makes it more expensive than ever. So a lot of publishers are looking into potential solutions to cut down on dev time and increase the cadence of releases. As long as it doesn't involve handing everything off to AI, we're fine with that.

Persona 4 Revival is currently scheduled to launch on Switch 2 on 20th May 2027, a few months after other platforms. As for Persona 6, an imminent reveal has been rumoured recently thanks to a backend update on the official website. Hardly concrete, but it's a start.

‘Cameron Winter at Carnegie Hall’ Review: PTA’s Spellbinding Concert Doc Is a Gorgeously Tender Performance

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

[Editor’s note: This review was originally published on October 2, 2026 during the 2026 New York Film Festival. Janus Film will release the film in limited theaters on Friday, November 20.]

Paul Thomas Anderson‘s eye for vivifying music onscreen dates back to the late 1990s when, after “Boogie Nights,” he made Technicolor mini-masterpiece music videos with his ex-girlfriend Fiona Apple — or made a San Fernando Valley-sized shrine to Aimee Mann with his third feature, “Magnolia.” Since then, he’s kicked around with HAIM while turning one third of its Los Angelino triumvirate, Alana Haim, into a screen actress. He’s shot for Radiohead and made a regular collaboration with band member Jonny Greenwood to create some of the 21st century’s most memorable scores. Many young cinephiles now associate the mischievously lilty notes of “Phantom Thread” with the poison promise of new love and obsessive creation. Related Stories Box Office: ‘Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping’ Scores $21 Million in Previews, Second-Best Result of 2026 ‘Josephine’ Review: Another Heartsick Horror Film About Real Monsters from ‘Soft & Quiet’ Filmmaker

Last December, PTA teamed up with Geese frontman Cameron Winter to film, while operating his own Panavision camera, his solo stage performance at Carnegie Hall, running through sparse piano arrangements of tracks off his solo album, “Heavy Metal.” The result, a 72-minute film now being distributed roadshow-style by Janus Films before inevitably living on the Criterion Collection, is a stunning arrangement of its own: a deeply achey, sincere but never cloyingly earnest concert movie that’s tender to the point of frailty. (Benny Safdie counts as a co-producer, and the film’s scratchy New York quality and setting can’t help but have his imprint.)

The matted-hair, lithe Winter can seem a bit uncomfortable in his own skin in the public eye, as he did onstage ahead of “Cameron Winter at Carnegie” at its New York Film Festival world premiere at Alice Tully Hall. But on the piano, laser-beamed into his songcraft, he’s quietly electrified, at seemingly at peace as his tenorous vocal glissando and nimble fingerwork on the piano produce shiveringly tender folk ballads that echo Harry Nilsson and Van Morrison. (The usual comparisons to Tom Waits and Rufus Wainright are right, too, but Winter is his own strange thing.) If you weren’t previously sold on the wily Winter antic rock chaos of Geese — rapidly becoming one of the most popular contemporary art rock bands of these times — you’re about to. Paul Thomas Anderson has created a rhapsodic shrine to the man that is nonetheless ballad-sized in its emotional scale.

Winter, in December, played and sang to a sold-out crowd of more than 2,700 at the stately cultural temple that is Carnegie Hall, and this film can at times feel like a religious experience, an incantation, a lonely vigil. The singer/songwriter was 23 at the time of the performance, one year older than Bob Dylan when he headlined Carnegie Hall, and similarly at the precipice of major fame. Fixed on a mobile rig that will at times careen and whoosh across the stage when not otherwise holding on Winter, Anderson’s camera catches the expressionism and chiaroscuro of stage lights illuminating and fading off the singer’s face.

The result is hynoptic, a concert film up there with “Stop Making Sense” but for introverts. Songs like the popular single “$0” acquire an even more wrenchingly somber quality as a lovelorn protagonist rues being exploited by a relationship (“You’re making me feel like a dollar in your hand / You’re making me feel like I’m a zero dollar man”). Stripped of production, his indie hit “Love Takes Miles” plays even better. While many of the songs sort of hover in the same key and bleed into one another, that makes for an all the more transfixing experience. The lack of an interstitial anything save for a sweet prologue and moving coda make for experience also that’s immersive, deep, and the kind whose truth you can feel somewhere in your body even if you can’t quite articulate it.

EXCLUSIVE: In a competitive situation, HBO has landed Tracy Oliver and Issa Rae‘s provocative drama Open for development.

The deal, which is said to include penalty, caps a lengthy sales process that started in August when the open marriage drama was taken out by Paramount Television Studios, quickly garnering seven offers.

The field was eventually narrowed down to three suitors, HBO, where Rae has a long history, PTVS sibling Paramount+, and Peacock, where Oliver has a Bel-Air spinoff in the works.

Interestingly, HBO and Paramount+ both competed for Open up until the Oct. 6 completion of the Paramount-Warner Bros. Discovery merger, which saw them become corporate siblings at the combined Skydance company, with HBO/HBO Max Content chief Casey Bloys and his team taking charge of both. HBO stayed in the race alongside Peacock, eventually winning the project. Open is co-written by Rae and Oliver a decade and a half after they worked together on Rae’s Web series Awkward Black Girl, which inspired Rae’s acclaimed HBO comedy Insecure. In Open, when a married couple who did everything “right” opens their marriage to save it, the decision sends shockwaves through their Houston friend group, igniting affairs, exposing secrets and forcing everyone to confront the lies holding their relationships together.

Oliver will serve as showrunner on the potential series, which she executive produces alongside Rae and Jax Clark via Rae’s Hoorae banner, E. Brian Dobbins for Artists First and David Becky and Jonathan Berry for 3 Arts Entertainment.

Rae co-created, headlined and showran HBO’s Insecure, which earned 14 Emmys and a Peabody over its five-season run. With the 2017 Girls Trip, Oliver became the first Black woman to write a film that grossed more than $100 million. In TV, she created and exec produced the series First Wives Club and Harlem. Oliver also has in the works The Wedding Date, a movie for Netflix based on Jasmine Guillory’s book, Girls Trip 2, The Blackening 2, the holiday film Deck the Y’alls starring Octavia Spencer and Jessica Chastain as well as thriller drama series Paper Gods at Netflix and Not a Happy Family at Starz. Gemini Notebook's New Interactive Site Builder Is Much More Useful Than I Expected

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

David Nield is a technology journalist from Manchester in the U.K. who has been writing about gadgets and apps for more than 20 years.

He has a bachelor's degree in English Literature from Durham University, where he also spent a term as editor of the award-winning student newspaper Palatinate. His journalism career started in print media, where he contributed to and edited several technology magazines and bookazines sold in the U.K. and internationally. More recently, he has worked as a freelancer for some of the biggest technology publications on the web, covering everything from on-the-ground reporting about product launches, to detailed explainers and how-to guides on apps, gadgets, and platforms. His expertise covers broad areas of consumer tech, including smartphones, laptops, wearables, and AI.

Gemini Notebook, Google's AI tool for study and research, already lets you do a lot with your notes: You can turn them into flashcard quizzes, interactive podcasts, video explainers, mind maps, and infographics, as well as interrogating the sources you've collected through AI-powered prompts and responses. Now there's something new. You can also build interactive websites from your notebooks.

That might not immediately seem like the most obvious fit, turning your research notes into a mass of HTML code. But it gives you another way to look at your material and to get insights from it. Here's how it works, what you could potentially do with it, and how impressively it worked when I put it to the test on some early 20th-century Irish literature.

Website building is rolling out now for Gemini Notebook's web app for all users, and is "coming soon" to the mobile app.  You won't see a website option in the Studio panel alongside Audio Overview, Reports, Quiz, and the rest; instead, you type something along the lines of "make me a website" in the prompt box.

To make a website, you need sources, so I set Gemini Notebook to collect information about James Joyce's 1914 collection of short stories, Dubliners. As I studied Dubliners many years ago (I highly recommend it), I was hoping I'd spot any AI errors and hallucinations, and be able to properly assess the quality of Gemini Notebook's analysis.

With a suitable amount of information absorbed into the AI brain, I got Gemini Notebook to make me a website. Google doesn't give too much official guidance here, but says you should add "a description of what you want to build for work or study," so if you have a particular style or angle in mind, include it.

Here was my prompt: "Build a website using all the sources and information collected so far. Make it an interactive guide to the individual stories in Dubliners, as well as the overarching themes and context. I want to use it as a study aid to explore different aspects of the story collection."

The finished website pops up in the Studio panel, and in my case it took around five minutes to put something together from 26 different sources. Click on the website, and it opens in a new window—you can use it from there, open it in a separate tab, or download the code to your computer.

I was honestly impressed by how much Gemini Notebook managed to pack into a single website: I got plot summaries, analysis, critical context, and some of the main themes and motifs outlined. There was an interactive quiz at the bottom of the page to test my knowledge, and even a graphic I could click through to reveal different elements of how the stories fit together.

Everything was well laid out and functional, albeit with the standard "sheen" you get from anything made by AI (which tends to always pick the same colors and fonts). I wouldn't have necessarily thought that I needed a website if I was still studying Dubliners today, but I can definitely see how it would have helped.

For something like my project, a website really works well as a study aid, since it offers a different way of interrogating the information. You can already do that in the main chat, and with tools such as the audio and video overviews, but this is different. It's a bit like having a mini-Wikipedia focused just on whatever you're researching.

The interactive elements that are included—so in my example, I could click from story to story, and theme to theme, within the same page—make a site more engaging than a standard report document and a static wall of text, and should help the information stick if you're trying to study it.

I can also see how this might be useful for sharing data with others. Maybe you need to analyze monthly report figures, or you want to chart how your organization has developed a product over time—perhaps an interactive website will relay that information in a more compelling way than a slide deck or PDF.

Maybe you have a notebook stuffed with details of a big family vacation. Putting together a website through Gemini Notebook would give your family a hub that they could use to easily check up on the details of the trip—where you're going and when, what you'll see there, what they need to pack, and so on. Another idea might be a family tree, if you have ancestry data and sources packed together in a notebook. Putting together a website would be a great way to visualize it, and, again, it's something you could share with relatives. If there were updates to the data you've collected, you could generate a new website.

Bear in mind that you can do a lot with your prompt. My website turned out the way it did because that's what I asked for, and if you need more interactivity or specific types of sections, then you can ask. Based on my follow-up prompts, you can also specify colors and certain layout choices, though it's not quite a full-scale website builder.

It looks like these sites are single-page creations, though they do have interactive elements and sections that change as you click around. It might be better to think of them as mini apps rather than sites. As of yet, there's no built-in option for publishing a site online or sharing it with others, so you need to download the HTML and do that manually.

Yellowjackets: What is Melissas plan?!

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

If three seasons of Yellowjackets has taught us anything, it's that you don't mess with Shauna Shipman (aka Shauna Sadecki). Melissa learned that the hard way last season, when she sent a tape that put the Yellowjackets on her scent.

Shauna (Melanie Lynskey) didn't just find her, attack her, and bite off a bit of Melissa's arm. She also tried to feed it to her. Well, Season 4 of Yellowjackets came back with a vengeance, and so did Melissa (Hillary Swank).

Fresh from killing Van (Lauren Ambrose), Melissa begins episode 1 on the run with her wife, Alex (Jaylee Hamidi), and their child. In a grimy hotel room, she confesses to her partner the truth (or some of it). Her name is not Kelly, it's Melissa. She was a Yellowjacket. She was on the plane that crashed.

Melissa says she "rebooted her life" — which is a wild way to reframe faking your death. "I'm still the same person you married," she insists, "just with a different name and way, way shittier past than I told you about on our first date."

Then this conversation reveals something huge:

Alex responds to Melissa's partial confession with: "My mom was out there. She's the one who called for help. She's the reason they got rescued. "

So, there were signs last season about how Tai (Jasmin Savoy Brown), Van (Liv Hewson), Shauna (Sophie Nélisse), and Misty (Samantha Hanratty) talked about Hannah (Ashley Sutton) that suggested she didn't make it out of the Yellowjackets camp alive. Alex's revelation further cements that Hannah died out there. When Alex asks her wife if she met her mom out there, Melissa lies, "No. I didn't know her."

Melissa goes on to say that it was because she wish she'd known Hannah that she moved to the late scientist's hometown to start over. "Maybe I could create a life in her honor," she says. That might be true. But even as Melissa comes clean, she's still lying to Alex. And by sharing any of this, Melissa could be putting Alex in danger from the others. At least she's honest about that.

The episode begins with the Yellowjackets in a Canadian hospital, seeing their injuries tended to. But there's no sign of Hannah.

Trump initially accepted the midterm results, but most GOP voters doubt they were fair

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A firefighter walks past voting booths at a firehouse serving as a polling location in Detroit on Nov. 3. Spencer Platt/Getty Images hide caption

President Trump may have initially accepted the results of his party's midterm losses, but data released Friday shows there is still broad distrust in the elections process among Republicans.

Less than half of Republican voters nationwide considered the midterm elections "free and fair," according to new polling by the University of California San Diego. Just 52% of Republicans said they trusted votes were counted accurately.

In comparison, after the 2024 election that Trump won, more than 80% of Republicans said they had confidence in the vote-counting process.

While trust in elections often dips for voters of the losing party, the GOP has seen bigger swings in confidence in recent years, as Trump has falsely claimed that U.S. elections are corrupt.

"There are still a lot of people who remain unconvinced that they can trust elections," said Thad Kousser, a co-director of UC San Diego's Center for Transparent and Trusted Elections. "And they're predominantly located in one [political] party."

Overall, a wide majority — 66% — of Americans said they think the midterms were fair. That represents an improvement from before the election, when less than half of U.S. adults said they expected free and fair contests.

But that post-election increase in trust was buoyed by Democrats and independents — many of whom worried before Election Day about potential federal interference at the polls that didn't come to pass.

"It would be a mistake to [look at the 66%] and think, 'Oh, everyone has a pretty good view of the election,'" said Lauren Prather, also a co-director of UC San Diego's election center. "It really buries the lede a little bit about the partisan gap."

The widespread doubt among Republicans may be a reality check for those wondering whether Trump's brand of election denial has run its course in American politics.

On the one hand, there will be fewer politicians who deny or question election results in office next year. And the vast majority of election-denying candidates running for positions involved in overseeing statewide elections lost their races this year, according to tracking by the advocacy group States United Action.

Election experts have also celebrated as Trump and high-profile losing candidates on the ballot this year conceded their losses.

"Sure, I'll accept [the results]," Trump told Fox News' Bret Baier on election night, as it became clear Democrats would retake the House and Senate. "Why wouldn't I?"

But just a few days later, he began conspiracy theorizing about those same results.

"Does anybody really believe that this 'Election' was honest," Trump posted online.

The new polling from UC San Diego shows that among Republicans, there still may be a receptive audience to such claims. When asked if politicians are discussing voter fraud too much, too little or about the right amount, just 15% of Republicans answered "too much."

"There is both demand among the Republican base for continued focus on this issue, and as we saw even after the election, politicians are continuing to supply it," Kousser said. "As long as those two dynamics are there, there's still going to be a market for these concerns."

To be clear, in most elections — domestically and internationally — supporters of the losing party usually see a dip in trust.

"It's sort of like asking people after an overtime basketball game, do you trust the referees?" Kousser said.

But in an ideal world, he said that oscillation would be more like 10 percentage points in either direction. Not the 30-point swing seen among Republicans between 2024 and 2026.

The numbers do still represent an improvement from 2020, however. At that time, leading up to the Jan. 6 riot at the U.S. Capitol in 2021, just 22% of Republicans said they had confidence in the nationwide vote count, according to similar polling done by MIT's Election Data and Science Lab.

Darius Acuff is showing flashes of NBA stardom, but he has one massive problem

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Flip on a Sacramento Kings broadcast and you're likely to be inundated with graphics, statistical queries or fun facts framing Darius Acuff Jr. in an illustrious ensemble.

Names like Michael Jordan, LeBron James, Oscar Robertson and Luka Dončić are frequently mentioned alongside the rookie guard, who is averaging a robust 26.2 points, seven assists (3.9 turnovers) and 3.6 rebounds through the first 14 games of his career. It's an esteemed neighborhood for Acuff to reside in and certainly an encouraging omen as he progresses further into his NBA tenure.

Skeptics, however, will peek beyond the box score and point to Sacramento's 1-13 record, Acuff's relative scoring inefficiency (53.3% true shooting, 24th percentile, per Dunks & Threes) or his ball-dominant tendencies to downplay his raw production. They'll note how much of the Kings' 27th-ranked offense is channeled through him and posit his true impact pales in comparison to those All-Star-level statlines by citing a host of metrics and data.

Stretching back to his sensational freshman season at Arkansas a year ago, Acuff has divided the masses with his play. The first month of his rookie campaign has done little to assuage that polarization, a reality existing independent of anything he's responsible for but rather captures the cantankerous discourse enveloping the sports world more than ever these days.

Nonetheless, there is plenty to examine, praise and critique about Acuff's first 14 games. He's flashed loads of promise and indicated various areas of concern as the Kings presumably build around him moving forward. So, what about his skill-set may suggest future stardom and what may offer reasons for worry in an NBA ecosystem increasingly cruel and unforgiving to smaller guards?

Every star player needs a superpower. Some are more consequential or prominent than others but they all have one. Though not a star at this stage, Acuff's superpower is his ability to maintain a live dribble wherever and whenever he desires. That may sound rudimentary. There are a horde of guards who can haphazardly dribble the air out of the ball. But Acuff makes it immensely practical.

As the league continues to evolve and grow sharper fangs defensively while also upsizing its offensive hubs, dribbling — especially inside the arc or against pressure — is a bit of a lost art. So many players are unable to initiate offense while enduring full-court physicality or slalom through gap help. Acuff can.

He's already one of the most dextrous dribblers in the NBA, equipped to prime screens however necessary, vary the height of his dribble to best suit his intentions at any given moment, wind through treacherous limbs and preserve his handle in the face of point-of-attack pestering. If he wants to avoid stunting defenders or insulate the ball from pressure, he'll keep it low. If it's time to rise for a jumper or make a delivery, he pounds the rock to speed up its arrival into his shooting/passing pocket. The toggling between them is seamless, too.

Acuff is not solely a modern Curly Neal, he also touts an incredibly elastic upper body. Such a trait is evident in his dexterity — he can dribble and pass from a variety of arm and hand placements — but spans beyond it, too.

It empowers him to duck low underneath stiffer defenders or coil around them to find opportunistic angles. The dude gyrates, and it's integral to his offensive signature. Any potential stardom will hinge, in part, on his rare intersection of ball-handling and upper-body flexibility that fuels his playmaking and bucket-getting.

That superpower grants Acuff access to virtually any part of the court. The broader the scope a player can access on the court offensively, the better. It renders him a grander threat to defenses as a scorer and passer. Yet right now, Acuff is not an omnipresent threat. His wondrous ball-handling unlocks doors for him he cannot utilize.

He lacks the burst and vertical explosion to create separation as a driver or aptly elevate as a finisher, issues borne through insufficient lower-body strength that prevent him from generating force downhill or through the air.

He is not an imposing scorer around the rim, consistently reduced to attempts well below the basket or unable to discard help inside, even if momentum is in his corner. His handle and upper-body pliability mitigate some of these troubles but not all of them, which rear their head enough to disrupt his game and narrow the avenues where he's a daunting offensive conductor.

According to Cleaning the Glass, 17% of his field goal attempts occur at the rim (30th percentile among point guards) while he's converting just 58% of those attempts (35th percentile). Similarly, he's currently unable to overwhelm defenders and reliably draw fouls. Despite touting the league's sixth-highest usage rate (34.7%) and having taken the third-most shots, he's 39th in free throws attempted (68).

Just look how groundbound he is on these drives, missing both the pop to ease his finishes or the ferocity to challenge bigs inside:

A lean diet of shots at the rim and charity stripe makes for a tough life in building an efficient scoring repertoire. Until or if Acuff adds significant lower-body strength (and gradual increments could facilitate this, it's not all or nothing), the path toward efficient high-volume scorer is to embrace the 3-ball.

He's already doing so off the bounce, launching the league's third-most pull-up triples per game at 6.9. Although he's only netting 32% of those, it's clearly a shot he's hunting and cozying up with, largely against drop coverage around Domantas Sabonis' bludgeoning screens. But he'll also dance into them over switches and still hoist when a trailing point-of-attack defender is re-entering the play.

Thirty-two percent cannot suffice long-term but it's far from a disastrous launching pad. As he approaches his prime, it's entirely plausible the newly minted 20-year-old levels up as a shotmaker to better suit the volume with which he's evidently found comfort.

If Acuff ascends to a requisite level as a long-range pull-up shooter that warps defenses into dissuading those looks, it should benefit his interior pursuits. Deep drop coverage is an arduous tactic for him to attack inside because of aforementioned physical limitations. But seeing more bigs switch or play around the level — and away from the paint — could allow him to weaponize his handle and flexibility into drives without the fear of a rim protector lurking.

Ideally, the Kings will also add another bona fide creator alongside Acuff, whose off-ball game has yet to be spotlighted much because of the rickety ball-handling carousel surrounding him. Among starting guards, his 49.8% on-ball rate ranks third behind Jalen Brunson and Andrew Nembhard, the latter of whom has played much of the year without Tyrese Haliburton (per databallr).

In the rare moments he's been afforded to operate off the ball, his instincts locating space, traversing screens or curling into dribble handoffs lend credence to the notion his sprawling ball dominance is a product of Sacramento's cumbersome infrastructure more than his individual nature.

Even in the small sample he's shared the floor with Malik Monk — who's struggled mightily this year but is still the Kings' most viable non-Acuff creator as someone who can dribble, initiate possessions and operate a ball-screen — Acuff's efficiency spikes without suppressing his volume. Whether it's spotting up, being deployed as a screener or piercing a tilted defense, he's yielded the rewards of defenses fixating on someone else.

Through 14 games, Acuff is shooting 41.7% (15-of-36) on catch-and-shoot threes. An environment promoting more of those chances and opportunities for his quick-witted, malleable decision-making — Acuff adapts his shooting and passing processes on the fly quite well — would simplify his responsibilities without dimming his light.

That doesn't mean Acuff is ill-suited for an on-ball role, he's exhibited reasons to believe in him there. But the extent to which Sacramento relies upon him for it seems detrimental to himself and the collective offense.

Between occasional minutes next to Monk and his screening/handoff camaraderie with Sabonis, the Kings do present a few helping hands for their rookie point guard. That all evaporates defensively, though. There's hardly any safety net.

They're a horrendous rim protection team; opponents shoot a league-high 75.6% at the basket against them, per Cleaning the Glass. They don't have established point-of-attack stoppers or slippery screen navigators. They switch the majority of actions 1-4 because most of their rotation players cannot fight around picks and that makes Acuff a moving target for opposing offenses.

Despite a litany of debilitating factors, Acuff does himself no favors either. He is, thus far, a very, very poor defender. He applies scant physicality on the ball, gets trapped in screens like a never-ending maze, hardly ever forces turnovers or grabs rebounds and is often ill-positioned off the ball without the tools to recover punctually.

Habits like these gleaned from the eye test are reinforced by various datapoints. Per Cleaning the Glass, with Acuff on the floor, opponents take 6.9% more of their shots at the rim, log a 2.1% higher offensive rebounding rate and turn the ball over 3.6% less of the time. His STOP% (steals, blocks recovered, offensive fouls drawn per 100 possessions) is 1.6 and ranks in the 6th percentile, according to databallr.

Playing for the league's 30th-ranked defense exacerbates the magnitude of Acuff's woes but he is culpable for the vast majority of his shortcomings and must improve in the coming years. He does not make offenses feel his presence and appears to simply drift through possessions instead of committing to sustainable defensive efforts.

As defensive-minded players blossom into loftier offensive duties, we sometimes see them regress defensively. Maybe, through an optimistic lens, the opposite can unfold for Acuff. He shoulders a titanic offensive load and has displayed the capacity to function off the ball in a lower-stress role.

For that to ignite meaningful defensive growth, though, Acuff probably has to infuse substantial lower-body strength. It'll make him sturdier and more liable to anchor against contact, which he's mostly shied away from altogether, and accelerate his lateral mobility, where he concedes breakdowns because he cannot slide quickly enough to cut off ball-handlers.

Achieving this could address glaring limitations on both sides of the ball and amplify his superpowers — the very ones propelling him into graphics of glory alongside Hall of Famers. Maybe someday, Acuff, too, will be the sort of complimentary company rookie guards hope to join if they burst onto the NBA scene.
