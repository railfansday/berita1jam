A drug-free diabetes treatment? An implant lowers blood sugar in mice

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The device banishes extra blood glucose by converting it to a compound excreted in urine

Excess blood glucose (shown in white in this illustration) is the target of a new experimental device. In mice, the sugar gets converted into a compound which the kidneys can excrete in urine.

A wireless implant that clears excess sugar from the blood could someday offer a drug-free way to treat type 2 diabetes and obesity.

In mice with both conditions, the under-the-skin device brought high blood sugar back to normal within 60 to 90 minutes of being switched on, researchers report October 22 in Science. After six weeks, the treated mice lost up to 10 percent of their body weight and had lower triglycerides, a type of fat in the blood.

Diabetes affects about 830 million people worldwide. Conventional type 2 diabetes treatments like insulin and GLP-1 drugs require careful dosing. Even experimental diabetes-controlling implants still rely on the hormone insulin, which clears sugar from the bloodstream but doesn’t rid the body of it, says Martin Fussenegger, a bioengineer at ETH Zurich. The new implant instead removes glucose from the body without added drugs, genes or living cells.

The 3-centimeter-long device contains a catalyst made of gold flakes and platinum particles on spongelike graphite. When electricity powers it, the catalyst converts glucose into gluconate, a compound that is easier for the kidneys to flush out in urine. With less glucose available for energy, the body may turn to its fat stores instead, Fussenegger and colleagues suspect. In mice, that shift showed up as higher blood levels of chemicals released when fat breaks down, such as ketones. “This system kind of targets the root cause, which is the energy surplus by glucose in the body,” Fussenegger says.

The implant works like an automatic sugar-removal system: A wearable glucose monitor detects high blood sugar levels and sends readings through a smartphone that switches the implant on. When levels return to a safer range, it switches off. The automated system ran for two weeks in eight mice. The catalyst itself remained able to convert glucose after up to three months under their skin, though the study didn’t establish how long the entire device would last.

And it’s still unknown how well the device would work in people. “This is a potentially promising method,” says David Klonoff, an endocrinologist at Sutter Mills-Peninsula Medical Center in Burlingame, Calif., who was not involved in the study. “But the risks are significant and must be carefully ruled out before it can be safely tested on humans.”

Researchers need to determine how much gluconate the kidneys can clear, Klonoff says. If it builds up, it could disrupt the body’s acid-base balance, causing symptoms such as nausea or confusion; excreting it could also deplete electrolytes such as sodium and potassium, he notes.

Scaling up the device is another challenge. Humans consume about 250 grams of carbohydrates daily, compared with two grams for mice, Klonoff estimates. Meeting that demand could require a much larger implant or multiple devices, he says. In a lab test, a larger device cleared nearly all glucose from about 5 liters of solution, roughly an adult’s blood volume, though not a full day’s glucose intake.

Fussenegger’s team plans to test the device in pigs and sheep before considering human trials. If proven safe and effective, it could offer an alternative to existing diabetes treatments and a new weight-loss option for people regardless of whether they have diabetes, he says.

Supermetrics announced on November 3, 2026 an expanded Claude marketing workflow covering analysis, campaign creation and editing, and reported that more than 6,000 customers have used the connector.

The announcement names campaign creation or editing for Google Ads, Meta, Microsoft Advertising, TikTok, LinkedIn and Snapchat. Claude can draw on connected advertising, CRM, SEO, ecommerce, data-warehouse and other sources, with Supermetrics supplying normalized marketing data when campaign information is requested. New campaigns remain paused until they are reviewed and explicitly enabled, and marketers can review proposed edits before they are applied.

Supermetrics said active users have grown by an average of 250% month over month since the connector’s February 2026 launch, with paid-media reporting currently the dominant use case and the connector ranking among its fastest-adopted product launches. That makes analysis the entry point for execution: performance investigation and review of a campaign proposal can happen within one Claude conversation. Co-CEO and founder Mikael Thuneberg’s stated rationale is to reduce the operational work required after Claude identifies an opportunity, without surrendering control of campaigns or budgets.

The current campaign-management documentation labels the feature as early access. It requires an active Supermetrics trial or subscription with an AI chat destination, and campaign write access must be granted separately for each ad account. The integration does not increase a user’s permissions at the ad platform, so view-only platform access still prevents changes.

Teams can require approval before a campaign goes live, before budget changes on live campaigns and going live, or for every change to a live campaign. A separate no-human-approval setting applies changes immediately, so human review is configurable rather than universal.

Every change is logged in Campaign History. Supported previous values can be restored, including budget, status, targeting and ad settings, but removed campaigns cannot be recovered. Editing also varies by platform; Snapchat permits changes only to an ad’s name and status, not its creative.

The Context Layer carries targets, reporting rules, client-to-account mappings, naming conventions and events into AI analysis and campaign execution. Teams can define these rules in natural language, avoiding repeated explanations of their marketing setup in each conversation.

The Business Context documentation describes shared team memory built into the Supermetrics MCP server and used by Claude, ChatGPT and Supermetrics Studio. Saved guidance can cover reporting time zones, currencies and attribution models; analysis exclusions; campaign naming; brand language; client mappings; and known events such as tracking outages or budget changes. Context can apply to a whole team, a data source or an account. Only team owners, admins and editors can add, change or delete it, and account-level context follows the same access restrictions as the underlying data.

These components predate the November 3 announcement. Business Context documentation was published on September 24, 2026 and updated on October 7, 2026. A May 14, 2026 workflow post had already described starting, pausing and ending campaigns, refining targeting and adjusting budgets through Claude, with previews, paused campaign creation and action logging. The new development is therefore the expanded workflow and adoption disclosure, not the first appearance of campaign actions or saved business context.

A Supermetrics case study supplies an adoption example on the analysis side. Norwegian agency Layer reported that some bespoke client reports requiring up to 10 hours of manual spreadsheet work now take approximately 20 minutes with Supermetrics and Claude. Seventeen of those minutes are spent placing the prepared data into the client’s final Google Sheet. Layer continues to use Looker Studio dashboards alongside conversational analysis for custom and multi-channel requests.

Before broader client use, Layer’s Morten Kleven spent several weeks testing and validating the AI output. The case study reports that this process found no hallucinations or incorrect data points. The example concerns reporting rather than campaign execution, with a manual handoff and existing dashboards still part of the workflow.

The practical shift is a conversational path from cross-platform data to a proposed campaign change, with saved business rules carried into the exchange. The reported adoption figures establish use of the connector, while the documentation sets the conditions for action: early-access campaign tools, explicit write access, platform permissions and the approval level each customer selects.

Healthcare technology company LeanTaaS is expanding its iQueue platform beyond traditional hospital operations and capacity management into ambient intelligence, using computer vision to monitor patient rooms and turn signals into actions.

As part of this strategy, the company plans to launch iQueue for Virtual Care, using AI-powered patient-room monitoring to detect events such as fall risks, patient movement and other environmental signals, then route those insights into operational workflow.

The system is designed to turn events like an attempted bed exit into prioritized tasks rather than additional alarms. AI monitors rooms and surfaces events to a virtual observation team, which can assess the situation, remotely engage the patient or escalate to bedside staff when needed, Mohan Giridharadas, founder and CEO of LeanTaaS, told Fierce Healthcare in an interview.

Urgency and routing are based on the patient’s context and the health system’s established protocols. Human clinicians remain responsible for clinical decisions, Giridharadas said, while iQueue provides context, prioritizes events and helps route information to the appropriate care team.

The company’s software and services are being used by nearly 200 health systems in more than 1,200 hospitals, centers and clinics in the U.S.

To accelerate this strategy, LeanTaaS acquired LookDeep Health, its second acquisition in about two months following its purchase of care transition platform Aidin. The deal brings computer vision technology, an open platform approach and virtual care deployment experience into LeanTaaS.

LeanTaaS provided Fierce Healthcare with a first look at the acquisition news. Financial details of the deal were not disclosed.

Giridharadas said the company is building the "central nervous system of the hospital," and computer vision will be a key data source for this broader hospital operations platform.

"It is a move beyond retrospective dashboards toward continuous operational awareness and execution," Giridharadas told Fierce Healthcare.

"A great operational brain needs more signal, so it can decide faster and decide better. Health systems generate hundreds of thousands of data points each day - but the vast majority of them are discarded after they have served their immediate purpose. The next frontier is building a robust and scalable approach to capture high-value signals from the physical care environment that may never reach the EHR, or may reach it too late to be useful. That includes whether a patient is alone, trying to stand or hasn't been out of bed in days, when a nurse was last in the room, and whether a room is ready for the next patient, from compatible cameras and in-room devices, and potentially other device-generated or workflow signals," Giridharadas said.

"The test is whether a signal is sufficiently accurate and timely, and whether it can be converted into a clear action for the care team. We intend to continuously expand the sources and uses of data from the entire end-to-end patient journey," he added.

Narinder Singh, former CEO and co-founder of LookDeep who joined LeanTaaS as senior vice president of ambient intelligence, said the technology can support stretched care teams by monitoring patients when clinicians can’t be present. "Nurses and physicians cannot be in every room at every moment, and too often technology has added to their work instead of taking it away. Ambient intelligence keeps watch when they can't be there, so staff finally feel the technology works for them, not the other way around," he said in a statement.

LeanTaaS chose to acquire LookDeep rather than partner with a computer vision or virtual care company to integrate ambient intelligence technology deeply into the iQueue platform, executives said.

"We are building the central nervous system of healthcare operations—a highly functional nervous system must have audio and video sensory capabilities in order to be effective. This is why we needed to acquire the capability rather than rely on an arms-length partnership. We already had the intelligence that drives the car, 15 years of it. What we wanted was the best perception ever built for hospitals. Nobody confuses the cameras with the car, but there is no self-driving car without them," Giridharadas said.

LookDeep brings three capabilities to LeanTaaS' platform: peer-reviewed vision AI, an open technology platform that works with hospitals’ existing infrastructure and experience deploying virtual care. Its approach also focuses on using AI to ease care team capacity constraints rather than adding to clinicians’ workloads.

Health systems can build on compatible cameras and in-room devices already in place or deploy supported hardware as needed. This open approach helps preserve existing investments while creating a foundation for new capabilities, according to the company.

"The acquisition gives us the opportunity to make ambient intelligence a native part of iQueue and extend the technology across our broader portfolio. We also share a conviction that in something as complex as a hospital, before you can be smart, you must be open. Vendor-specific camera silos will not last as health systems demand greater interoperability," Giridharadas said, noting that a camera alone "does not generate an ROI." "The value comes when what it sees changes what happens next—earlier, with the right person acting. Nobody expects the barcode scanner to redefine the supply chain, yet no supply chain runs well without one."

The new virtual care offering includes three services: virtual observation for AI-assisted patient monitoring and escalation, virtual nursing to support admissions, rounding, patient education and discharge preparation and virtual medicine to connect patients with remote physicians and specialists.

These offerings build on LookDeep’s experience deploying the technology in health systems. Across different deployments and operating models, reported results include a more than 70% reduction in fall rates, twice as much nursing contact, an 80% reduction in medication errors and threefold cost savings compared with traditional virtual sitting solutions, according to the companies.

Beyond virtual care, LeanTaaS plans to incorporate ambient intelligence into additional iQueue products and workflows to support capacity, patient flow and care coordination.

"We will bring the vision capability into all of our products beginning with inpatient flow and operating rooms and then moving on to infusion centers and other areas of the hospital," Giridharadas said.

The company aims to find areas in hospital patient care where important signals are either missed or received too late by current systems. "We will prioritize use cases where the signal is reliable, the response is well defined and the impact can be measured," he noted.

As adoption of AI in healthcare grows, LeanTaaS says its competitive advantage comes from a proprietary knowledge base built from more than 15 years of health system deployments. "We have accumulated an enormous knowledge base of thousands of corner cases that make operational improvement and change management difficult to accomplish. We have built a multi-layered knowledge architecture of tens of thousands of 'knowledge nodes' that enables us to deliver an enterprise-grade AI capability where the data and knowledge is tightly curated to the unique needs of each site at each customer rather than relying on generic recommendations from foundation LLM models," he said.

A quartet of medical organizations has published a comprehensive set of reports calling for a coordinated plan of action to improve heart healthcare for women throughout their lifetimes.

The reports detail how cardiovascular health issues can be prevented, monitored, and treated from the early years of menstruation to peak fertility age to pregnancy to perimenopause to postmenopause stages.

The report authors call for healthcare professionals to identify heart health risks early in girls and women and to coordinate care across medical specialties.

They stress that females have specific biological factors that increase their risks of cardiovascular disease. Those components include hormonal and emotional factors.

The authors note that heart disease is the number one cause of death for females in the United States, as well as the number one cause of death during pregnancy. Yet, only 44% of females are fully aware of the dangers they face from heart disease.

The organizations issuing the reports are the American Heart Association (AHA), the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine.

The groups presented their findings in a trio of reports published in the AHA’s flagship journal, Circulation on October 26.

“Women experience their healthcare across clinical specialties, but it often is fragmented or disconnected,” said Sadiya Khan, MD, the chair of the collaborative writing group for the reports and a professor of cardiovascular epidemiology at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine in Chicago, in a news release.

“Specifically, these reports connect the dots for clinicians, for patients, and across health domains so that care is holistic and begins in adolescence, recognizing that women’s heart health is uniquely shaped by biological transitions not experienced by men — periods, pregnancy, perimenopause, and menopause.”

Experts not involved in the reports told Healthline the updates were a long time coming.

Nicole Weinberg, MD, a cardiologist at Pacific Heart Institute’s Women’s Heart Center in Santa Monica, California, said the reports present a positive framework for women’s health.

“Women have unique cardiovascular risk factors related to pregnancy and menopause, and certain traditional risk factors, including diabetes, may increase their risk of heart disease more significantly than they do in men,” noted Nissi Suppogu, MD, a cardiologist and medical director of the Women’s Heart Center at MemorialCare Heart & Vascular Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California.

For me, it comes as either a break in my working day or a signal that it’s over, depending on how hectic things are.

For the kids, it’s an all-too-rare chance to get me all to themselves, no deadlines, screens, or other distractions to pull me away from being anything but 100% present.

For all three of us, it’s a literal (and literary!) warm blanket — a chance to escape under the covers to familiar worlds and familiar faces. It is, in short, incredibly good for our well-being.

And yet, in the world of health information — including on this site — thousands, if not millions, of reading hours are dedicated to the benefits of nutrition, sleep, and exercise as the building blocks of good health. Reading itself rarely makes that shortlist.

For me, it unequivocally deserves a place on it. Research seems to support that case. Even if we need more of it to really understand how and why.

A recent editorial in Psychological Medicine co-authored by Barbara Sahakian, Professor of Clinical Neuropsychology at Cambridge University, draws on a broad range of research linking reading for pleasure to better mental health and greater empathy.

The positive impact of reading on young people — just like Amelia and Henry — lso carries evidence.

Among more than 10,000 US adolescents, those who started reading for pleasure early had fewer mental health problems, spent less time on screens and slept more. Although, per an earlier column of mine, I can’t vouch for the sleep side of things when it comes to early bird Henry.

For many, reading can be a form of therapy that improves well-being. Bibliotherapy is a complementary therapeutic tool for emotional release and reduced stress and anxiety, with its roots traced back to ancient civilizations.

There’s even research suggesting a link between a love of reading and a lower risk of cognitive decline.

In a 2003 study of people aged 75 and over, reading was associated with a 35% lower risk of dementia over roughly five years.

A separate 14-year study from 2022 found that people who read at least weekly saw less cognitive decline, whatever their level of education.

A happy (and healthy) by-product of this love of being read to is that both my kids have now started reading to me at bedtime. Henry recently read “Jeffy the Burglar’s Cat” to us, a lesser-known but no less compelling chapter book by Ursula Moray Williams that was a staple of my own childhood. The research above links reading to many health benefits: better attention and memory, fewer mental health problems, more sleep, less time on screens, and greater empathy. I haven’t measured any of those impacts empirically in our house. Nor am I naive to the fact that as they get older, they’ll ultimately grow out of me reading to them, with school, extracurricular activities, their friends, and inevitably screens competing for more and more of their time.

Come what may, though, I know that a child who considers missing a reading session as the most cardinal of cardinal sins, and who scrambles for the chance to read aloud to their dad at bedtime is, at that moment, concentrating, empathizing, discovering and growing.

Researchers are clear that studies espousing the benefits of reading show association rather than proof, and it’s evident that more experimental work and deeper research are needed to ensure the complex cause and effect stands on firm scientific ground.

Regardless, it’s a healthy habit Amelia, Henry and I are very happy to keep, and with a bit of luck, I’ll get to pass it on to their kids one day.
