Almost 22 years after it was launched, NASA’s Swift Observatory is now about to fall back to Earth on Wednesday after attempts to push it back into a higher orbit failed. NASA announced that the aging space telescope is anticipated to re-enter Earth’s atmosphere at approximately 3:30AM ET (give or take 11 hours), and that it expects most of the spacecraft to burn up, but some components will likely survive re-entry.

The observatory weighs around 3,200 pounds and measures in at 5.6 meters (18.4 feet) high and 5.4 meters (17.7 feet) wide, though much of the latter includes its deployable solar arrays. The risk of surviving components causing any harm is low, according to NASA:

“Re-entry locations of any surviving components can vary from hemisphere to hemisphere and depend on when it re-enters. Due to Swift’s low inclination, re-entry locations of surviving fragments are limited between 20.6 degrees south latitude and 20.6 degrees north latitude. The risk of harm coming to anyone on Earth is unlikely — approximately 1 in 2,100. NASA and Space Force will continue to monitor the re-entry and update predictions.”

The agency gave up on trying to save the falling telescope earlier this year, after the Link spacecraft that was attempting the rescue experienced technical issues with attitude control. Still, Swift far outlived its original two-year prime mission to study the origin of gamma-ray bursts, and went on to serve as a general-purpose multi-wavelength observatory until solar storms pushed it into a lower orbit. You can follow Swift’s descent by monitoring NASA’s blog for continued updates.

As the year winds down, we can find ourselves consumed with the busyness of the holiday season, in the thick of responsibility at work and school, and perhaps wondering if we are just spinning our wheels, glancing back to see where the time has gone.

For productivity expert and author Tiago Forte, the Annual Life Review has become an anchor in his yearly rhythm, providing clarity and direction for a life lived with intention. Grab a journal and a pen, a copy of Forte’s latest book, Life in Perspective: The Art and Power of the Annual Life Review, and make deep self-reflection a gift you give to yourself each year.

Q: Share a bit about your background and what inspired you to write Life in Perspective.

Tiago Forte: I've been a content creator, coach, teacher, and author for the last 13 years, focused on helping people become more productive with technology. I decided to write this book because I realized over those years that being productive is not helpful if you don't have a clear sense of where you're going and why.

Modern technology is an incredibly powerful force for getting what you want, but you have to know what you truly want in the first place; otherwise it will just fill your time with distraction and noise. My personal answer to this need for deeper intention has been a practice called the Annual Life Review, which I've followed every year for the last 17 years, and this book is my attempt to make it available to others who feel the same need for clarity and balance in our rapidly changing world.

Q: How has practicing an annual life review impacted your own life? In today’s fast-paced world where many of us are seeking answers online, what is the value of looking within for the validation, direction, and inspiration we need?

Tiago Forte: There are endless sources of external information that we're flooded with every day, all year long: podcasts and videos we consume, the advice of gurus and media personalities, and the digital feeds that seek to dominate our attention on every platform.

The annual life review is about turning down the volume on all those external sources and tuning in to internal ones instead – our inner voice, our intuition, our desires, our dreams. No one else can tell us what is right for us, in our unique life circumstances, or what we need to feel fulfilled and joyful. The only source for those kinds of answers lies inside.

The benefit of finding those answers, which I've experienced again and again, is a sense of deep calm, peace, certainty, and self-confidence that I know where my life and career are headed.

Q: What is the method that you disseminate in this book, and how can readers apply it to their own reflective practice?

Tiago Forte: When I first started conducting annual reviews, there was no method or framework to help me structure them. So my process was haphazard and filled with mistakes and wrong turns. Later on, when I learned that many others also practiced year-end reviews of some kind, there was still no definitive book or method to follow.

Annual Review Course (ARC) is the one I developed over the years, first for myself, and later for the students in my course. It takes you on a learning journey from the past, through the present, and finally to the future, where all the insights and realizations you gleaned from the past and present can inform your future plans.

Q: What role might reading and journaling play in this process of annual life review? How have reading and writing helped you to grow, personally?

Tiago Forte: Reading and journaling play a crucial role in the annual review, alongside many of the most proven practices that many people are already familiar with, like meditation, reflection in nature, visualization, and goal-setting.

What the annual life review does is place all those practices into a framework so they build and compound on each other. This leads to an experience that is more than the sum of its parts – a crescendo of insight and meaning that no single one can produce on its own.

Q: How do you hope Life in Perspective changes readers’ lives for the better?

Tiago Forte: I hope it gives them a counterbalance to the fear, uncertainty, chaos, and mental fragmentation that modern life often creates in our lives. A special time and place that they know they can set aside once per year, or anytime they want to return to themselves, and reliably reach a state of balance and clarity. I hope it introduces them to a wider global community of reviewers, from whom they can learn and be inspired. I hope it becomes a cornerstone of their self-reflection and provides a foundation of intention for years to come.

Pickle Juice Vs Buttermilk: Which Marinade Is Best For Fried Chicken?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

When it comes to down-home Southern cooking, buttermilk and pickles both have a prominent place thanks to their flavors and how they affect the chemistry of cooking. Todd Johnson, Proprietor of Strips Chicken & Brewing, insists that both have a place in fried chicken — although he asserts that buttermilk is the better all-around choice.

"If you're going to marinate your chicken, buttermilk wins," Johnson told Food Republic. "It's gently acidic, so it softens the surface of the chicken without turning it mushy. It's also thick enough to leave a tacky layer that flour clings to. That's the foundation of a craggy, crunchy crust." Just about any marinade can infuse the muscle fibers with moisture and keep it more tender, but it's that combination of subtle acid, sugars, and milk solids that gives it a more well-rounded, heartier flavor and makes it such an essential ingredient in fried chicken. Since it lacks the potency of a pickle brine, it also tends to absorb other seasonings quite well, so you don't have to rely solely on your breading.

While Johnson admits to being "not a fan of pickle juice as a marinade," he noted that it can have its place. "It's already salty and acidic, so it seasons and moistens the meat quickly," he explained. "You'll notice results in a couple of hours instead of overnight." Still, its high acidity means you have a narrower window between tender and mushy chicken, so it's not an ideal choice for beginners. Brine is a pretty potent ingredient, so while it's effective, it also calls for an experienced hand unless you're willing to experiment.

Good fried chicken has three components: meat, breading, and oil. Buttermilk is such a mainstay in recipes because it has a delicious effect on all three of these, promoting flavor, browning, and texture.

One of many major mistakes everyone makes with fried chicken is creating a breading that doesn't stick, and dry ingredients aren't the only contributor to this. "Oil and fat act like a release agent between meat and crust," Todd Johnson explained. "That's why we use fat-free buttermilk." For this aspect, it may seem like pickle brine is the right choice, but Johnson said that its lack of fat comes at the cost of a thinner consistency. Fat-free buttermilk is far more adhesive, giving your dry ingredients a prime surface to cling to, so they won't fall off the meat once they hit the oil.

Johnson conceded that buttermilk has a tendency to "over-brown" if your oil gets too hot, burning the milk sugars and solids, fouling the flavor. This is a pretty hefty disadvantage, but one that can be turned to an advantage with a careful eye. If you maintain Johnson's recommended oil temperature of 350 degrees Fahrenheit, you can toast, rather than burn, those solids, delivering a robust flavor profile focused on a hearty, umami-rich breading. Buttermilk is also a more convenient option, as you can just remove the excess drippings and drop your meat directly into your breading.

When you're looking to make fried chicken on the fly or don't have a surplus of seasoning, there are few options better than pickle brine. Todd Johnson said it's indispensable for achieving that "sandwich shop flavor" that some folks prefer, even if it has its quirks.

While you can always add some salt to your buttermilk, it won't have quite the same effect as using a jar of leftover pickle brine. "It's already salty and acidic, so it seasons and moistens the meat quickly," Todd Johnson clarified. Its extremely high and well-dissolved sodium content loosens up muscle fibers, making them far better at retaining moisture. While it lacks the rich solids and sugars of buttermilk, it brings far more tang, so it'll cut through even the heartiest, most marbled pieces of fried meat quite easily.

The biggest downside to pickle juice in your marinade is inconsistency and lack of other seasoning options. Not all brines are made the same, so unless you work with the exact same brand and product every time, there's always some amount of guess work involved. While pickle juice is one of the most powerful vehicles for flavor, it also leaves very little room for anything more subtle or nuanced. After marinating your meat, you'll also need to carefully pat dry each piece before tossing it in a batter or dry ingredients. Excess surface moisture creates a steam barrier between your chicken and breading, so even a bit left on the surface can ruin your food.

Gossamer Gear The Free Tent Review: A Freestanding Double-Wall Tent Under 2 Pounds?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Gossamer Gear The Free is a one-person, double-wall, freestanding ultralight backpacking tent. Unlike the brand’s traditional trekking-pole shelters, The Free uses a DAC aluminum hub-and-pole system to create a freestanding structure that can be easily repositioned at camp.

Constructed with Gossamer Gear’s exceptionally tear-resistant, PFAS-free nylon fabric, The Free aims to deliver durability and weather protection without unnecessary weight. At just 29.8 ounces, this tent joins the Durston X-Dome 1 and the Nemo Hornet Osmo 1 as one of the lightest freestanding, double-wall tents in the world — and it’s the most affordable of the three, to boot.

But does its minimalist design strike the right balance between weight savings, comfort, and practicality on the trail?

After taking The Free on a section hike of the Continental Divide Trail in northern New Mexico, I came away impressed by its weather-resistant construction and sturdy design. However, its narrow dimensions and some setup quirks left me wishing Gossamer Gear had not sacrificed quite so much interior space in the pursuit of ultralight performance.

I tested The Free during a section hike of the Continental Divide Trail in northern New Mexico, during which the weather was frequently miserable and wet. The conditions gave me an excellent opportunity to evaluate the tent’s weather protection, condensation management, and overall durability.

One of the most noticeable differences between The Free and my previous shelters was its double-wall design. I appreciated being able to avoid the persistent dampness that comes from brushing against a single-wall tent’s interior in wet conditions. In addition, a few nights were particularly windy, and I was impressed by how solidly The Free held up.

However, my overall experience was mixed. I felt that The Free lacked the interior roominess I’ve grown accustomed to in The One. Despite being a relatively small user, I often felt cramped and occasionally claustrophobic.

The Free’s DAC aluminum hub-and-pole system distinguishes it from Gossamer Gear’s traditional trekking-pole shelters. The tent can stand without stakes when pitched with its inner mesh body, making it possible to move the shelter to a different spot before securing it.

Like most freestanding tents, the fly requires staking out the vestible for proper tension, and additional stakes for guylines in less-than-ideal conditions. While the freestanding design sounds wonderfully convenient, I found the initial setup more complicated than expected.

Gossamer Gear developed a custom ultralight shelter fabric called ClearSkies. It combines 15D recycled Robic fabric with a 20D dope-dyed ripstop silicone/polyurethane coating. The company designed this material to improve strength and waterproofing while avoiding PFAS.

The fabric has a reported hydrostatic head rating of 4,923 mm — waterproof enough to withstand heavy downpours and wind-driven rain. The seams are factory-taped.

The fabric is manufactured using a dope-dye process, which incorporates color into the polymer before the fibers are formed. This can improve colorfastness compared with conventional surface dyeing and reduce water and energy consumption during manufacturing.

In the field, the fabric felt reassuringly robust for such a lightweight shelter. During wet and windy conditions, I appreciated the protection it provided without the bulk or weight of a heavier tent.

Although the double-wall construction kept me from directly touching the wet fly, moisture accumulated on its underside, particularly when I kept the vestibule closed to retain warmth overnight. By morning, the fly could be soaked and noticeably heavier.

The fly also sits close to the inner mesh body, helping maximize the available interior space. Unfortunately, that limited separation meant that contact between the inner tent and the wet fly could transfer moisture inside. The Free offers the advantages of a double-wall shelter, but its compact geometry makes condensation management especially important.

The Free’s inner mesh body must be pitched first. Two aluminum poles connect through a central pivoting hub that attaches to the inner tent’s ceiling, creating the tent’s basic structure.

This inner-first setup became a drawback in rainy weather. While I worked to get the fly in place, the mesh interior was exposed to the elements. Since the fly was often still damp from the previous night, assembling the shelter without adding more moisture wasn’t always easy.

The color-coded poles and corresponding corner attachments help guide assembly, but I struggled to keep everything in place while inserting the poles into their designated corners (often with comical results). In breezy conditions, the lightweight tent threatened to slither away before I could finish setting it up. I initially tried holding the corners down with my feet, but eventually resorted to staking them down. That rather defeated the convenience I had expected from a freestanding tent.

The Free is reasonably straightforward once you become familiar with its pole arrangement, but I wouldn’t describe the setup as effortless, especially when the weather is uncooperative.

As I mentioned above, The Free felt more cramped to me than Gossamer Gear’s The One, which I have used extensively. Although the two tents have similar floor dimensions (The Free is even a few inches longer and wider), The Free has a significantly lower peak height and the angle of the side walls feels more restrictive.

The Free has a tapered floor measuring 34 inches wide at the head and 21.5 inches at the foot. At 86 inches long, it’s long enough for most solo backpackers, but the narrowing floor is a significant consideration when choosing a sleeping pad.

My pad fit comfortably, with a little room to spare. However, anyone using a wide sleeping pad should check the dimensions carefully. Per Gossamer Gear’s website, “while The Free’s floor dimensions don’t technically support a traditional 25″ wide pad, it does fit — it’ll just push the side walls a bit. For the best fit, we recommend going with a wide tapered pad instead.”

At 5 foot 7, I could sit upright and even stretch my legs inside. Still, I frequently brushed against the sides or top of the tent. When the fly was wet, those accidental touches could bring moisture into the living space. The compact dimensions are understandable in a shelter designed for minimal weight, but the trade-off was more noticeable than I expected. I often felt confined, particularly when spending extended periods inside during bad weather.

Gossamer Gear lists a maximum user height of 6 feet 2 inches, but height alone doesn’t tell the whole story. Sleeping-pad width, sleeping position, and how much room you want to sit up or move around all influence how comfortable this tent will feel.

The D-shaped doors open into a modest vestibule with enough room for my backpack and a few smaller items. This was useful for keeping gear protected from the weather without cluttering the sleeping area.

My main complaint was the vestibule door’s toggle system. When rolled back and secured, the door tended to hang into the tent rather than staying neatly out of the way. One morning, I cinched it closed with the toggle, only to have accumulated ice fall directly onto my sleeping bag. Magnets or a more effective door-retention system would have made this detail less frustrating.

The Free includes two interior pockets: one near the ceiling and another beside the door. While I appreciated having somewhere to stash small essentials, I found the pockets less useful than expected. Items tended to slide out, making it difficult to keep things securely organized. For an ultralight tent, the storage options are welcome, but I would have preferred pockets that hold their contents more reliably.

The Free impressed me with its sturdy pole structure and durable-feeling fabric. It handled windy conditions well, and the double-wall design provided a welcome barrier between me and the wet fly.

However, the fly’s tendency to collect condensation and its close proximity to the inner mesh deserve consideration. Proper ventilation and careful pitching are important, especially in cold, damp conditions.

This was the tent’s biggest weakness for me. Although I could sit up and move around to a limited extent, the tapered floor and close-fitting walls made the interior feel cramped. Taller users and anyone carrying a wide sleeping pad should pay particular attention to the dimensions.

The color-coded pole system makes the design reasonably intuitive, but pitching the inner tent first can be inconvenient in rain or wind. I also found myself staking the corners during setup to keep the lightweight body under control.

The freestanding structure is useful, but it doesn’t eliminate every need for stakes or make the pitching process as simple as I had hoped.

At 29.8 ounces, The Free is impressively light for a double-wall, freestanding shelter. Its compact packed dimensions make it an appealing option for backpackers who want to keep their pack weight down without switching to a trekking-pole shelter.

The ClearSkies fabric feels like a thoughtful balance between low weight and ruggedness. Despite its delicate-sounding 15-denier thickness, the material is designed for improved tear resistance, and I was impressed by the tent’s overall robustness during my hike.

The use of recycled Robic fabric and PFAS-free materials is a welcome feature. The dope-dyed fabric construction is also designed to improve colorfastness. These choices make The Free appealing to backpackers who want a lightweight shelter without overlooking material considerations.

The Free comes with six aluminum stakes, four guylines, a pole-repair section, a stuff sack, and a pole bag.

The Gossamer Gear The Free makes a compelling case for an ultralight, freestanding backpacking tent. And while it isn’t the featherweight champion when compared to minimalist trekking pole shelters, The Free is still one of the lightest tents in the freestanding double-wall category. At $400, it wins heavily on its value-to-weight ratio. However, its impressive weight savings come with noticeable compromises in livability and convenience.

If minimizing pack weight is your top priority, it deserves a look. If comfort and easy setup matter just as much, I would consider a roomier alternative.

The countries with the most islands — and why Sweden beats Indonesia

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Sweden, Norway, and Finland report 704,773 islands between them. See how 10 countries compare, from Canada to the Philippines.

A scrap of land ringed by water can become a world of its own. Cut off from the mainland, species follow separate evolutionary paths, and many island plants and animals exist nowhere else. Seabirds raise their young on island nesting grounds, and migrating flocks stop on them to rest during long journeys. Humans depend on these places as well. More than 600 million people live on islands, where distinct cultures and ways of earning a living stay tied to the surrounding ecosystems. Each island, however small, can matter to wildlife and communities out of proportion to its size.

Most readers who picture a country of islands imagine Indonesia or the Philippines, with their warm seas and sprawling tropical archipelagos. The image holds some truth, yet it skips a harder question about what qualifies as an island at all. A bare rock in a lake, a skerry off a jagged shore, and a landmass large enough for towns can all carry the same label in everyday speech. Governments do not agree on where the label starts. Some set a minimum size, some measure the shoreline, and some ignore freshwater. Each choice can swell or shrink an official tally by a wide margin. These standards open a gap between a country's reputation as an island realm and what it reports on paper. Making sense of any national number means knowing the rules behind it.

Visual Capitalist compiled island counts for 94 countries in an analysis by Bruno Venditti, published Oct. 31, 2026, that covers 945,247 islands in all. The publication warns that this sum does not amount to a uniform global census, since every country writes its own definition. Sweden admits any island of 9 square meters or more, and Norway draws its line at about 10 square meters. Both include inland islands. Japan requires a circumference of at least 100 meters and excludes artificial islands as well as those in lakes and rivers. Canada's figure leaves out everything but its coastal islands. The analysis adds that these differences keep the national counts from lining up for direct comparison. This article examines 10 of the 94 countries, moving from the largest reported count down, and treats each count as a record that local definitions shape as much as geography does.

Sweden reports 267,570 islands, which gives it 28.31% of the dataset's total and a larger tally than any other country on the list. More than one of every four islands on record lies within Swedish borders. Norway, the nearest challenger, falls 28,513 short. Sweden and Norway together report 506,627 islands, more than half of all islands counted. The margin between the two Nordic neighbors alone outnumbers Indonesia's islands.

Sweden's total dwarfs Indonesia's 17,508 by a factor of about 15.3, a difference of 250,062 islands. The gulf between those two countries is wider than Finland's whole tally, the third-highest in the table. Sweden surpasses Canada, Chile, the U.S., Indonesia, Japan, Australia, and the Philippines put together. The seven report 171,871 islands between them, nearly 100,000 fewer than Sweden. Indonesia's reported figure equals less than 7% of Sweden's.

Some 30,000 islands, islets, and skerries fan out from Stockholm into the Baltic Sea, forming the Stockholm Archipelago. This single cluster exceeds every island the U.S. reports and tops Indonesia's total as well. Still, the archipelago supplies about one-ninth of Sweden's islands, leaving more than 230,000 scattered beyond Stockholm's waters.

Sweden's other islands span rocky shores, protected seas, and freshwater lakes. Smooth rock formations and fishing villages with red boathouses define the Bohuslän coastline. Kosterhavet National Park holds the distinction of being Sweden's only marine national park. Inland, Lake Vänern, Sweden's largest lake, contains the Lurö archipelago, the biggest freshwater island group in Europe.

Rising land reshapes the High Coast, a Unesco World Heritage site, where existing islands grow larger and new islets break the surface. Sweden's island map there remains unfinished. Sheep and cattle graze several islands in the Östgöta archipelago, and sea eagles are a common sight there. Sweden's lead draws on islands across sea and lake alike, and the High Coast shows the land itself can keep adding to them.

Norway's 239,057 islands make up 25.29% of every island in the dataset. About one in four counted islands falls within Norwegian borders.

The 91 countries outside Sweden, Finland, and Norway report 240,474 islands among them. Norway trails that sum by 1,417 islands, a gap smaller than Thailand's whole tally. One country comes within a whisker of matching the combined holdings of 91 others. The group on the other side of the scale stretches from Cuba and the Bahamas to Fiji, Tonga, and the Solomon Islands.

Norway outnumbers the seven countries next in the table by 67,186 islands. Canada, Chile, the U.S., Indonesia, Japan, Australia, and the Philippines hold 171,871 between them. Canada, the strongest of the seven at 52,455 islands, reaches about 22% of the Norwegian figure. Norway's surplus over the group exceeds Canada's entire count. Indonesia and the Philippines, the two countries the source credits with reputations for vast tropical archipelagos, sit inside that outnumbered group.

Rocky islands divided by narrow channels define the Øygarden Islands west of Bergen. Thin strips of water run between the stone and split the shoreline into many separate pieces of land. A coast fractured this way can pack a large number of distinct islands into a short stretch of sea.

Remove Norway from the list and 706,190 islands would remain, with Sweden and Finland still holding about 66% of them.

The Case For Pre-Breach Intelligence: Why Buying Access Before Attackers Do Beats Cleaning Up

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Austin Berglas is Managing Director and Head of Cyber Operations at Nardello & Co. and the former head of FBI New York Cyber.

​For decades, enterprise security spending has followed one pattern: Detect the intrusion, contain it, investigate and recover. The breach occurs, and the security team responds as fast as it can.

That approach is expensive and increasingly avoidable.

IBM’s 2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report put the global average cost of a breach at $4.44 million and the average time to identify and contain at 241 days. Yes, this was a nine-year low, yet it still amounts to nearly eight months in which an attacker can sit inside a network, expand access and live off the land to conduct a larger-scale attack.

This is the limitation of post-breach incident response: It’s reactive. A SIEM can only correlate logs that exist, and an EDR platform can only flag malware after it’s been deployed. The industry has put enormous effort into faster detection and response and far less effort into stopping the incident from happening in the first place.

Most modern intrusions don’t begin with a zero-day exploit or custom malware. They begin with a transaction. Initial access brokers are criminals who specialize in breaching a network and selling that foothold to the highest bidder. These brokers supply a large share of the initial access used by ransomware crews and other individuals and groups conducting cyber-enabled crimes.

A gbhackers report noted that ​Rapid7 researchers tracked initial access listings across five major underground forums and found the average base price “rose from $2,726 in 2024 to $113,275 in the second half of 2025.” Additionally, Rapid7’s earlier dataset showed that more than 70% of listings “included some degree of privilege,” which means buyers were getting more than just a low-level login—they were purchasing a foothold from which to move laterally and expand access.​​​​​

Once access is listed, it doesn’t sit there for long. KELA Cyber research from 2022 found that access was often sold within one to three days of listing and that victims were listed on ransomware leak sites an average of 23 to 36 days later. This is a fairly significant window to conduct an attack.​

By the time the tech stack flags unusual activity, the access has already changed hands on a criminal forum. The attack that eventually triggers an alert is frequently the second or third step in a chain that began with a transaction on some dark web forum, completely unknown to the security team.

Pre-breach intelligence inverts the model. Instead of waiting for, and reacting to, a compromise, it involves direct monitoring of and, in some cases, legally acquiring access to the criminal marketplaces where access and credentials are bought and sold. In this way, an organization can learn if access to its own network is for sale before an attacker completes the purchase and exploits it.​

This changes what “detection” means. Pre-breach intelligence tells you that a specific method of access to your organization’s network is actively being marketed to buyers who intend to use it for a variety of crimes, such as ransomware, fraud or corporate espionage, while there’s still time to rotate credentials, patch the stolen access route and notify law enforcement before anything nefarious happens.​

Pre-breach intelligence is also significant for third-party risk. Vendors and partners are often the weakest link in an organization’s security posture and are frequently the last to know when their own access has been compromised. Monitoring the marketplaces where access to those vendors is sold can surface a third-party compromise weeks before the vendor discloses it or it becomes public.

None of this replaces traditional security operations and best practices. Endpoint detection, patch management and a competent incident response plan are still nonnegotiable. Pre-breach intelligence adds a layer that sits earlier in the attack chain. This is the difference between finding out your house key was copied and finding out someone already used it to get inside.

Insurers have started to take the approach seriously. In March 2025, Reinsurance News reported that AXA XL’s specialty risk division had partnered with DarkwebIQ, a pre-attack intelligence provider that intercepts access sales by infiltrating forums, marketplaces and private channels.​

Post-breach response has dominated budgets because its value is easy to explain. Here was the incident, here’s the cost and here’s what we spent fixing it. Prevention is a harder sell internally because success looks like nothing is happening. However, given how long it can take to remediate and recover from a data breach, and the significant cost associated, nothing happening is exactly the outcome worth paying for.

An organization that intercepts access before an attacker uses it avoids the costs that follow a breach. This can include months of containment, legal fees, incident response engagements, remediation and recovery, regulatory exposure and reputational damage.

Security leaders should ask whether access to their network is for sale today. If their current tools, vendors or partners can’t answer that, there’s no question that they’ll learn about their exposure on the attacker’s timeline rather than their own.​
