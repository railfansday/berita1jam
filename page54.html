The week wraps up for a major alternative crypto project with a sudden Glamsterdam hard fork stop after testnet launch revealed a dangerous bug in the fee recalculating mechanism, brutally breaking the logic in smart contracts.

As the Ethereum community debated the ability of the network to sustain on old power, Vitalik Buterin showed up with a manifesto which completely zeroes out all the panic around mainnet's decreasing revenue. The "red line" behind Vitalik's updated vision is that the community should stop being fixated on the idea of ETH as "fee-burninng token". For a basic blockchain layer worth $270 billion, attempts to build evaluation on the amount of gas burned are a dead end. For Vitalik, the novel of "ETH as store of value (SoV)" is much more viable amid all the global uncertainty around.

This U-turn, taken by a key visionary of the ecosystem, happened at $2,240 for ETH and may drastically change all the plans developed with a focus on Glamsterdam architecture. The hard fork was initially projected for the logic of blockspace surplus, not the deficit. Ecosystem builders are already readying expansion of L1 capacity with the help of EIP-8037 (tariffs calibration) and EIP-7928 (parallel production) with an aim to raise gas limits up to 200 million units. The logic is that the excess of free space will cheapen the transactions, while token burning rates will gradually be cut.

The market saw a risk of deflation in this concept. Vitalik took a front-step. By his estimates, the historical peaks of burning through EIP-1559 "never were as strong as they seemed". They were dependent on inner cyclical activity of crypto speculations. And it itself was falling once every bear market started.

Attempts to squeeze the premium out of the protocol by any means would push the system toward overly aggressive management and interventions, says Buterin. Ethereum would be required to become a centralized commercial project with inflated costs on research and development in other words. What the market needs meanwhile, amid the rise of AI and regulatory storms, is not a "corporate shitcoin", but what Vitalik calls CROPS - "censorship and capture resistance, open source, privacy and security".

EIP-1559 is not going anywhere, and Vitalik openly says "don't be sad" if the volume of gas burned will forever stay below the 10% mark. The source of value for Ethereum lies in completely another dimension.

Solana's co-founder, Anatoly Yakovenko, unexpectedly aligned with Buterin on this matter. "You can't focus on fees as the products", he said, calling such an idea "absolutely meaningless". According to Yakovenko, the blockchain network must generate real value for the end users, not control the price of getting into the block.

Vitalik is absolutely right that you can’t focus on fees as the product. That’s absolutely meaningless. You need to pick a product, the thing that brings value to people, and do that really well.

There is lots of technical stuff and philosophy around, but what the missed deadline on the testnet and Vitalik's manifesto reveal is that Ethereum opts for long-term, secure, and even a bit boring stability of the basic layer. Infra doesn't try to flirt with the market through deflationary records.

Why Nvidia Stock Looks Like a Bargain Right Now

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia's shares have stalled, while its business hasn't. Here's the case for buying the dip.

Nvidia (NVDA +2.77%) has made investors a tremendous amount of money over the past several years. But lately, the artificial intelligence chipmaker has been heading in the wrong direction.

Shares have been essentially flat so far in 2026 and have fallen about 22% from their all-time high. That's quite a reversal for a company whose revenue more than doubled in its most recent quarter.

And I think investors are getting an opportunity with Nvidia stock.

Nvidia reports its fiscal third-quarter earnings on Nov. 17, and I'm expecting another blowout performance. The company's financial results and the extraordinary demand for AI computing suggest its growth story has plenty of room to run.

In its fiscal second quarter, which ended July 26, Nvidia generated $96.2 billion in revenue, up 106% from a year earlier. Data center revenue reached $89 billion, an increase of 117%.

Those are remarkable growth rates for a company operating at Nvidia's scale.

Management expects third-quarter revenue of approximately $108 billion, representing another 12% sequential increase at the midpoint. Nvidia also forecasts a non-GAAP gross margin of 74%, suggesting it expects to maintain substantial pricing power despite its enormous sales volume.

And there are good reasons to believe demand remains strong. SK Hynix, a major supplier of high bandwidth memory used in AI accelerators, has indicated that its advanced memory capacity is effectively sold out for years. That matters, because Nvidia's GPUs need that memory to handle increasingly demanding AI workloads.

Meanwhile, Nvidia's Vera Rubin platform is already in full production, with systems being deployed by major cloud providers and AI infrastructure companies.

Customers aren't waiting for AI computing demand to disappear. They're spending billions to secure more capacity.

The latest weakness in Nvidia stock reflects concerns about the enormous cost of AI infrastructure and the possibility that political opposition could slow data center construction.

Those aren't imaginary risks. If companies struggle to obtain electricity or construction permits, some GPU deployments could be delayed.

But there's a difference between a delay in building data centers and a collapse in demand for AI computing. Nvidia has already demonstrated that it can sell enormous quantities of increasingly sophisticated chips while maintaining exceptional margins. Its next earnings report will provide another opportunity to see whether that demand is holding up.

I'm expecting the results to exceed management's already aggressive outlook.

A 24% decline doesn't automatically make any stock cheap, and Nvidia still faces risks from customer spending cuts, competing chips, and restrictions on AI infrastructure development. But with revenue doubling and a new generation of products entering production, I see a stronger business than what the stock's recent performance suggests.

The easiest way to measure app launch time is to start a stopwatch, open the app, and write down the number.

It is also one of the easiest ways to produce a measurement that cannot be reproduced, compared, or trusted.

An iOS launch depends on more than the code in the repository. The result changes with the process state, system caches, device model, thermal state, available memory, network behavior, stored user data, app version, and the destination the user is trying to open. Two measurements can both be technically correct while describing different experiences.

In Part 1, we separated cold launch, warm launch, resume, scene creation, and entry points. We also defined three possible endpoints: First Draw, Meaningful Content, and Time to Interactive.

Now we can turn that model into a measurement system.

The goal of this part is not to find a single perfect number. It is to build a production measurement system that describes what real users experience - and then use controlled tests and profiling to explain why that distribution changes.

Instruments collects thread states, samples, stack traces, allocations, signposts, and launch phases. That visibility has a cost. Apple explicitly demonstrates that CPU time and wall-clock time in an instrumented trace can differ because profiling itself introduces overhead. It makes Instruments a diagnostic tool rather than the final stopwatch.

Use production data to discover and quantify the problem. Use Instruments to explain its root causes. Use XCTest to make the reproduction scenario repeatable and protect the issue from returning. Then, refer back to production data to verify the result.

As soon as production data reveals which cohort or user journey is performing slowly, create a benchmark that reproduces the situation. This serves as a tool for diagnosing and preventing regressions, rather than as a primary description of the user experience.

Launch tests are sensitive to the state of the whole device. A useful benchmark removes unrelated variance while preserving the characteristic that made the production cohort slow: device class, data volume, migration state, authentication path, or entry point.

The simulator is useful for development, lifecycle experiments, and fast feedback. It is not a reliable representation of iPhone launch performance because it runs on Mac hardware, shares host resources, and does not reproduce the same storage, memory, thermal, scheduling, and rendering environment.

A global p90 can remain unchanged while the p90 on the oldest supported devices regresses by several seconds. It can also appear to improve simply because adoption shifted toward newer devices. Segmentation is part of the metric, not an optional dashboard filter.

Do not use production maximum as a release KPI. In a sufficiently large population, isolated extreme values are expected and may reflect interruptions the application cannot control. Use high percentiles, SLO compliance, and sample count to quantify the tail; use diagnostics to investigate pathological individual cases.

For a controlled test, preserve every raw iteration and report the median, p90, and maximum. A small local dataset answers a different question: whether the scenario is stable enough to reproduce a change.

The median describes the center, while the p90 and maximum reveal instability that an average would blur. Do not delete the slow runs merely because they are inconvenient. Investigate whether they came from the application or from a documented failure of the experiment.

Ten local runs can provide quick feedback, but they do not make a production-quality p99. High percentiles require many samples.

Do not compare a p90 from ten local launches with a p90 from millions of production launches as though they were the same experiment.

Production data is the primary description of startup quality. Controlled tests answer whether a code change affected a reproducible scenario, but they cannot represent every real user.

Xcode Organizer aggregates launch performance from users who share diagnostics and allows teams to compare distributions across app versions and devices. It is the fastest way to establish a system-level production baseline before building any custom pipeline.

The following protocol is a useful starting point for a production team. It begins with user devices and introduces laboratory measurements only after the production metric identifies a problem.

Select the launch scenarios that represent meaningful user traffic and risk:

Keep launch and resume in separate series.

Before creating test fixtures, define the production segments needed to explain the journey: app version, device tier, OS, authentication state, entry-point category, and migration state. Do not collect dimensions that have no analytical purpose.

Use Organizer or MetricKit for system launch and resume distributions. Add lightweight custom milestones for Meaningful Content and Time to Interactive. Buffer and upload the data outside the launch critical path.

Collect a complete, stable rollout window. Report p50, p90, p95, sample count, SLO compliance, and the important segments. Treat resume as a separate metric.

Determine whether the regression belongs to a device tier, OS, app version, entry point, user state, or migration path. This cohort becomes the specification for the controlled experiment.

Choose stable physical devices based on the affected production population. Create deterministic fixtures that reproduce its data volume, authentication state, feature flags, cache state, and network contract.

Use XCTApplicationLaunchMetric for First Draw or responsiveness and signposts for application-owned stages. Use Instruments to connect the slow interval to threads, stacks, dependencies, and launch phases.

Measure after every meaningful change. Once the improvement is reproducible, run the benchmark regularly on fixed hardware and alert on a sustained regression or budget violation rather than one noisy sample.

Compare Organizer, MetricKit, and custom product milestones by app version and the original affected segment. A local win is complete only when the relevant production distribution moves in the expected direction.

Correct launch measurement begins with real users.

First define the production population, lifecycle path, entry point, start boundary, and meaningful endpoint. Collect enough samples to report p50, p90, p95, SLO compliance, and the device, OS, app-version, and user-state segments that can explain the distribution.

Use Organizer and MetricKit for system-observed production launch and resume metrics. Add privacy-safe custom telemetry for route resolution, Meaningful Content, and Time to Interactive. When a cohort regresses, reproduce that cohort on physical hardware, use XCTest to make the scenario repeatable, and use Instruments to understand the phases, threads, and stacks behind it. Finally, return to the same production segment to validate the fix.

A benchmark tells you whether the code changed. Production telemetry tells you whether the user experience changed.
