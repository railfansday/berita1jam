‘Passing Strange’ Comes to Pasadena Playhouse: Creators Stew and Heidi Rodewald on the Rock Musical Reaching L.A. Almost Two Decades After Tony-Winning Broadway Run

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Senior Music Writer and Chief Music Critic

“Passing Strange” will seem just a little less strange to theatergoers now than it might have when it opened on Broadway in 2007 and scored big wins at the Tonys, the Drama Desk Awards and the New York Drama Critics’ Circle Awards. Without naming any names, there are plenty of successful shows since then that have taken cues from the rock musical, from how it put social and racial issues up front for its fouth-wall-breaking protagonist to having a live band on stage. The only truly strange thing, maybe, at this late date? Why it took so long for the show to get an L.A. production. After all, Stew and his creative partner Heidi Rodewald used to be prominent figures on the Los Angeles music scene, as members of the critically hailed band the Negro Problem, before the development of this show took them to New York in the early 2000s. So they’ve always a loyal audience of rock fans in SoCal eagerly waiting for the show to take root here, too, on top of Angeleno theater buffs who never got to see the show back east. Now it’s finally happening, thanks to director Zhailon Levingston, who won the Tony this year for best direction of a musical for “Cats: The Jellicle Ball.” When he sat down with the top folks at Pasadena Playhouse to discuss what he might like to do here, giving “Passing Strange” its proper SoCal premiere was at the top of his list.

Stew and Rodewald will not be performing in the show in Pasadena, as they did throughout its entire Broadway and pre-Broadway runs. (Neither will Colman Domingo, who had a supporting role, pre-fame, on Broadway. You can see all of those performances in the film version Spike Lee directed.) Vintage Trouble singer Ty Taylor stars in this production as the Stew stand-in known only as Narrator, along with Brittany Campbell (pictured above), Britton Smith and Saycon Sengbloh. Stew and Rodewald are as eager as anyone to see what Levingston has done with a project that takes elements of Stew’s real coming-of-age and sets them to rock ‘n’ roll. The pair Zoomed in with Variety to discuss the history and impact of the show, as well as reveal some projects now in the works.

The two of you aren’t directly working on this Pasadena Playhouse direction, but more like watching appreciatively from afar how it’s going, right?

Stew: Oh, yeah. No, we wouldn’t dare. Zhailon Levingston was sweet enough at one point to invite us to peek in, but he’s a total visionary, so all we could do at this point would be drag it down. First he knows the play better than we do. Secondly, if you’ve ever seen his work, like the work he did in “Cats: The Jellicle Ball,” he’s honestly the last person I would give any advice to.

Rodewald: The way he talks about “Passing Strange” just makes us think: Whatever you’re going to do, you get it.

Stew: As I was just telling him just a few minutes ago, it was made for him. We don’t make art for the contemporary world. The stuff that’s moved me and Heidi for the most part has always been those early morning moments when you go home with somebody and they wanna show you that they’re cool, so they put on a record that you’ve never heard before. I call that 3 a.m. music, and I think that’s what we make. I think the Negro Problem are a 3 a.m. band. “Passing Strange” is a 3 a.m. musical. It’s not something you’re gonna get fed by the mainstream, but somebody very cool is gonna turn you onto it. You don’t necessarily make a lot of money that way, but that’s not why we’re in this, obviously.

Here in L.A., remembering your band as an L.A. band originally, I remember hearing of the show when it was first playing at the Public and then coming to Broadway, thinking that it’d be great to see when you brought it to L.A. I couldn’t have realized that would be a 22-year journey.

Stew: I know. We didn’t either. “Passing Strange” has been done in almost every major city except L.A. It’s been done in South America; it’s been done in Europe — so, yeah, it was starting to stick out in a kind of a weird way. But, see, we’ve always had a adversarial relationship with both Los Angeles and to the L.A. press, particularly the L.A. Times. So we were sort of not surprised at all, but other people would always come to us and go, “What’s up? The play won a Tony! Why aren’t you guys in your hometown?” And we’re like, “Uh, I don’t know.” … It’s certainly nothing we’ve lost sleep over. Having a London production to me was a dream come true, so it’s all good. But the good news is that this amazing director is doing it in L.A., so who cares about time? It’s gonna be worth the wait.

Going back to the very beginnings of the show, weren’t you kind of developing some kind of preliminary version of it in clubs?

Stew: Yeah, we had this thing called “Travelogue” that we did at some places in New York, which was sort of a cabaret version of us talking about different places that we had lived and different experiences — a very primitive version in Joe’s Pub and a couple of other clubs where we did stuff in New York, and that’s when the word started to get around. We were approached by the Public Theater. We weren’t petitioning to make a play. They approached us and said, “Would you like to make it a theater (piece)?” We said no three times, and then finally said yes.

You’ve said that Broadway was not an aspiration of yours, ever. It was not your dream come true.

Rodewald: No, and that’s why it ended up being what it is, because we weren’t thinking that way at all. We weren’t trying to please people. We were just pleasing ourselves. I think we really held onto that. … It was this crazy surreal thing. And then we did have choices of doing it a different way or being on Broadway, and we went with that. We didn’t have to go to Broadway; we could have been one of those cool shows that goes on and on forever, off-Broadway. But it wasn’t like “Oh my God, this is all we’ve ever wanted.”

Stew: It really helped being a rock band, because I guess there must be some rock bands that get really overly excited about things, but I think a lot of rock bands are like us: we’re very jaded. Don’t forget, we’re old enough to have experienced the implosion of the music business itself. So we remember the point where people were trying to get record deals, and then we also were there when people are like, “Fuck a record deal. Let’s make the record ourselves.” Which is what we did.

We were in the office with Lenny Waronker, talking about a record deal, and they did give us $10,000. Woo, right? And we took that $10,000 and instead of making their demos, we took that $10,000 and made our record with it. So technically our second record is a DreamWorks project, but they don’t know it. Their accountant probably knows it, but they don’t.

At what point did you go east from L.A. to New York?

Stew: Yeah, we were playing in New York a lot when we were touring a lot, but I guess 2000 was probably where we really started spending more time. The best thing you can do in New York is not tell people you moved there. When you’ve moved there, you tell people you’re just visiting so they treat you better.

Rodewald: We haven’t talked about this in a long time. With “Passing Strange,” we get this commission, and then we’re working at the Public Theater, and they’re sending us away to do workshops and it’s like, “Wow, they’re giving us money, so we can just keep going.” In the middle of this, Stew and I are still making albums and touring, so we’re not banking everything on it; we’re just doing this other thing. And one of the great things that happened was, by the time Broadway happened, we still didn’t officially live in New York, so they had to put us up. Otherwise, how would we have done that?

Stew: Sort of like all the luxuries we never got with a big record deal, suddenly theater people were giving it to us, and they were much nicer.

Rodewald: At one point, we were at Sundance, and I remember looking at you and going, “They actually care! Like, no, they actually do.”

And that was not your experience with the record business.

Stew: I feel like record business was just a lot of suits pretending that they get you, but not really. Theater people are really into lyrics, for instance. So that for us was something very astonishing. People liked our stage show and our outlandish name and our ambitious music or whatever, but people in L.A. didn’t really say much about lyrics. Some people did, but not like press and stuff. Then suddenly we go to New York, and the theater people are like, “Whoa, we love these lyrics.”

Also, it’s a touchy subject because I know a lot of our L.A. people feel funny about the fact that we really did have to go to New York in order to make a living from this. Because we were selling out Spaceland on Saturday night, but there was a glass ceiling, and we couldn’t get that big record deal that was no longer around, or no longer around for a band like us. That wasn’t a possibility anymore. Suddenly in New York we had a career and could make a living. It’s always weird when hometown people leave and go elsewhere, and things happen.

I have a lot of friends, and Heidi has a lot of friends, who, back in the day, would get a big advance from a record company, and they’d be crawling the carpets with all the cocaine and Gil Turner (high-end liquor) deliveries. And the thing is, theater doesn’t give you Gil Turner money. It doesn’t give you cocaine money up front. But it does provide you with everything else you need to create. “We’re gonna send you away on a nice retreat in the cool countryside or the hills or Berlin.” They give you everything you need in assistance, but you’re not gonna become a coke addict on a theater budget. And that’s good!

Rodewald: It was amazing, but kind of embarrassing, to tell peole, “Well, we have a week, and we’re working on this, and we’re getting paid fairly.”

Let’s talk about the impact “Passing Strange” had on Broadway. Did you arrive at a point where you were able to recognize the influence it had?

Stew: For sure. After us, there were two things that were very clear. One is that every show wanted to have a band on stage. That was almost immediate. And the micro thing that only theater people would know is our lighting design, which was considered so weird at the time. Our lighting designer was like, “I want to use these lights that’ve been rejected from, like, seven shows.” And we saw it, and it looked like “Liza With a Z,” and we were like, “Hell yeah, we want this.” And then suddenly everybody started copying that look once they saw it. But that stuff isn’t really that big a deal. When I say “of course we would be influential,” I don’t mean that as from an egotistical point. Obviously, when you introduce something new, artists wanna check it out and play with it.

But I don’t think our influence was deep enough, if anything, to be honest. I still think musicals are pretty un-rocking and kind of shallow. And when they say “rock musical,” that just means there’s gonna be a scene where you hear a fuzzy guitar turned up.

Do you know the Iggy Pop thing where he’s [at a show in Cincinnati], he’s covering the peanut butter [all over his body] and stage diving into the audience? I show that to all my theater students, and I say, “For me, this is the epitome of rock ‘n’ roll. Why? Because we don’t know what’s going to happen next, and he is in completely in that moment. He’s high as a motherfucker, but he is completely in that moment.” And that’s what we want to achieve in rock ‘n’ roll. We want to be so present and so there that you want to come there with us. And then I ask them, “Can you show me moments in musical theater that are like this?” And when they do, they are usually scenes that are not even approaching that level of nakedness and frankness and vulnerability.

So what I try to do in my class is get people to write musicals that are more rock ‘n’ roll. And I don’t mean rock ‘n’ roll music necessarily; I mean the spirit. So when you talk about “Passing Strange’s” influence, I mean, superficially, sure. But I don’t know if it’s been deeper than that. Maybe with some shows it has. I don’t know.

Rodewald: The thing that really hit us at one point… We were touring doing some shows after that, onto the next thing. We’re playing in Detroit and most of the people there were like, “Oh, I saw that show.” When you think about how many shows we’ve played live as a band across all these years, [that didn’t compare with] we then do this show where it’s 1,200 people, eight shows a week. We didn’t go on forever, but when you think of that many people, everywhere we go, they’re like, “Oh my God, I saw you on Broadway.” And yeah, a lot of people in theater will be like, “That’s from ‘Passing Strange.'” It’s really amazing to be part of that world where people have been really influenced — a lot of people.

Stew: Most rock people who are involved in musicals are already really famous. Like Green Day. Or it’s like, “Okay, we’re big. We got nothing else to do. Let’s make a musical,” that kind of vibe. But I feel like what was really cool for us is we weren’t anywhere near that. We were indie famous, if even that. We were able to be ourselves in theater and individual in theater because of that. We weren’t trying to please any fans; we were just trying to please ourselves.

Do you think it broke conventions as well as helped invent some new ones?

Rodewald: I’m thinking back on different workshops over the years, these amazing workshops like Sundance. And as we’re doing the rehearsals, we’re laughing, going, “And if we were on Broadway, we would do this.” At the end of the song, everybody claps, and then the music comes back as everybody marches off the stage, just all those kinds of things. We would just laugh about it the whole time, right? We were well aware of what those things were that we did not want to do. And we had a lot of of conversations about wanting to give people a chance to clap, where we were like, “No, no buttons” —that kind of stuff.

In a video interview you did with Spike Lee right he filmed the last show, you indicated you were glad you were through with starring in it in certain ways, and you didn’t really imagine other people doing it. You didn’t see it being the kind of show where somebody just stepped into your role.

I totally didn’t, yeah. I remember the very first one we saw in D.C. And I thought, “Wow, this is awesome,” because that’s the cool thing about theater — it’s just like doing a cover. You know, the best covers don’t steal completely? The best covers do their own thing. So that was what was really fun. The narrator in the first (Washington) show was completely different than me, and I thought it was fantastic. So, yeah, the different people that have done it… We got a call from Argentina, where they were doing it, and they were like, “We’re thinking about not doing ‘The Black One’ because we just feel like that’d be kind of weird with a bunch of white Argentinian actors. Are you OK with that?” And I’m like, “Yeaaaaah, I’m okay with that.” Same when they did it in Belgium. So yeah, it’s just been fun for us. Heidi said it best — it’s bitter and… what did you say?

Rodewald: Oh, well, I feel like I say that a lot: “Old enough and bitter enough.”

Stew: It’s “old and bitter,” but in a very fun, devil-may-care kind of way… I think we enjoy what has happened, but we never sat around and were like, “Oh my God, this is it.” Because to us, it isn’t; it’s just we just do this. This is our life.

You don’t have a huge, active social media presence where you’re promoting yourselves, so it would be good to catch up on what you have coming up. For one thing, does the band still do concerts?

Stew: Oh, absolutely. Negro Problem will come out with a record in ’27. And we’re constantly writing and doing things. Heidi’s writing this amazing show about Aileen Wuornos. What is it? Is it like a song cycle, or is it gonna be a actual musical?

Rodewald: Well, the beautiful thing about it in theater is: I don’t know yet. We’re writing the songs, and then we’re gonna figure it out. But it’s not gonna be straightforward.

Stew: And she and I have this thing called Stewdio Zero. That’s this thing where, every four months, we make a show, and we collaborate with former students of mine from The New School, from Harvard, from LaGuardia. So it’s us being the age we are, but there’s also six people that are like a third of our age on stage collaborating to make sure things stay fresh. We perform at a wonderful space in New York called Jack, which is like our home. And, yeah, we’re crazy busy. We’re making a film of this show we did on Harlem Stage called “High Substitute for the Head Lecturer.” It’s about Amiri Baraka and Allen Ginsberg. I’m working on a Broadway-bound thing on both Muhammad Ali and Thelonious Monk.

Rodewald: “Broadway-bound.” That’s amazing. I love that you said that.

Stew: Yeah, bound in the sense of like prison. And Heidi, you’re doing something else too, right? What are you doing?

Rodewald: Well, I’m gonna be doing the Grace and Jimmy Boggs project with you.

Stew: Oh, yeah — we just got the money for this project about these legendary activists, Grace Lee Boggs and Jimmy Boggs. We’re gonna do this really, I hope, epic live song cycle about these two truly revolutionary activists out of Detroit. They’re already well known to political people, but I hope this musical we’re gonna make about them puts them on the map. They were unapologetically radical and wonderfully charismatic people. We’re busy as fuck. And I teach at Harvard, which is insanely busy, you know?

Are you teaching theater or music at Harvard, or both?

Stew: Both. My class should be titled “How to Make Musical Theater That Doesn’t Suck,” but they wouldn’t let me title it that. So it’s called “Singer Plus Song Equals Story,” and I just try to get them to make musicals that are more informed by what I would consider a rock ‘n’ roll ethos, more than “let’s put on a show” kind of stuff. The students are incredible, and this is what the Stewdio Zero group does, too, that Heidi and I have with them. They kind of teach us what’s going on now, and then we get to tell them what happened. We get to tell them about this little thing we like to call the 20th century, beause they know nothing about that, if you could believe.

Rodewald: The thing that’s changed for me is… Stew insists… I used to have to say “Tony Award-winning Stew,” like, throw that in. [Stew won the Tony for best book for a musical.] And now sometimes he makes me call him “the Professor.”

Stew: I admitted in front of a class that there was a point in my life where I didn’t know the difference between Miley Cyrus, Katy Perry, and… what’s her name, the other one? Taylor Swift. The level of eye roll that happened among my students, it was like teaching zombies. Their eyes turned white because their eyes went so far up into their head, and they groaned this zombie-like groan. I think they all had to go to the eye doctor after I said this. They just could not believe that I couldn’t tell the difference between those three. I never tell them that I still kinda can’t if I’m just listening. This is so embarrassing, because I realize that they’re all fine. They’re great. They even have songs that I like. But when someone comes up to me and goes, “Yeah, I’m really into songwriting, like John Lennon and Taylor Swift,” you know, I just… I’m not criticizing that. I know it’s okay. I’m just not there yet, you know what I mean? Maybe by the time I’m 80. I’m 65 now. When I’m 80, I think it’ll be a Taylor Swift light bulb, you know what I mean? I’m working towards it. I’m working.

Urban Lights & Gucci Nights: An Exclusive Look Inside the LACMA Art + Film Gala

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The night was young. Very young, as I cruised across Los Angeles to this year’s LACMA Art + Film Gala. I decided to arrive before the usual red-carpet mayhem starts, and because I had gotten ready several hours too early, I was starting to entertain the idea of having a quick nap in my glam and gown. I was wearing a 1930s purple satin bias-cut dress that I won at an auction for considerably less than it was worth. Such a purchase can do wonders for one’s self-esteem: I felt like a million bucks.

This year marked the 15th anniversary of the gala, which honors artists and filmmakers while raising funds to support the museum’s programming. The evening, sponsored by Gucci and co-chaired by Eva Chow and Leonardo DiCaprio, would celebrate Latvian artist Vija Celmins and filmmaker Denis Villeneuve. The art world and Hollywood are not always natural bedfellows, although they have certainly become more comfortable with one another over the years. I was interested to see how the two would behave over dinner.

I arrived fashionably early, both because I am extremely punctual and partly because I have developed an aversion to the chaos that accompanies certain Los Angeles events. Anya Taylor-Joy was making her way towards the photographers in an archival Pierre Cardin sculptural gown and jacket looking like a beautiful futuristic loofah. I spotted the sparkle of her Tiffany & Co. Schlumberger Stars and Moons necklace before I even registered who was wearing it. The necklace was doing its own job of announcing her.

As she approached the carpet, which was lined with photographers and press, I was surprised that nobody was yelling. The atmosphere was remarkably calm, a far cry from some of the movie premiere assignments I’ve attended, where you can barely hear yourself think over the screaming of fans and photographers. Behind the carpet was the backdrop that has become something of a celebrity in its own right: Chris Burden’s Urban Light, the monumental installation made up of more than 200 restored cast-iron antique street lamps. It is a Los Angeles sight that never loses its magic.

In the distance, I heard a woman having a meltdown with security. “Am I not famous enough?” she yelled.

I never established what the issue was. Perhaps she had been denied access to the carpet, or she simply wanted to take a photograph and was being told no. Either way, she was making a scene in sparkles. I hurried past the publicists who were watching with mild horror. “Never a dull moment,” I said. One or two laughed as I continued inside.

During cocktail hour, I stopped to say hello to Ed Ruscha, whom I had met a few nights earlier at a birthday party. He would be seated at Larry Gagosian’s table that evening. I made my way around the room, passing Julia Garner, who is invariably one of the nicest people in it. She was wearing Loewe with sculptural gold earrings. She always asks me if I’m an actress, which makes me blush slightly, despite the fact that I have never once given her reason to believe that I am. Nearby, Iris Law was wearing a beautiful pearlescent tiled dress by a brand she told me was called Amaramara. Up close, the dress looked almost as though it had been constructed from mother-of-pearl shells. She explained that it was lined in leather, making it surprisingly soft against the skin.

I walked by a cluster of familiar faces, getting stepped on a few times along the way—a long train is lovely in theory, but less so in a crowded room full of people who have somewhere to be. I spent much of the evening trying to protect my almost 100-year-old archival satin from people’s shoes. Artist Alex Israel told me he was particularly excited to see Rosé perform later that evening. I spotted Jonas Wood in the distance, along with Lauren Halsey, who had recently created the most extraordinary art direction and stage design for Erykah Badu at the Hollywood Bowl. I had been lucky enough to see it, and Halsey had projected her artwork across the venue, making the entire experience feel as though you had stepped directly into her world.

I also ran into Jordan Firstman, whose film Club Kid had just come out. We heard about a rather unexpected screening, which included Oprah. Apparently, she wasn’t entirely familiar with the film’s more specialized vocabulary and required a brief introduction to gay culture, including what a popper was and what people meant by G. (I would have loved to be there for that.)

Meanwhile, waiters were circulating with bite-sized beef tenderloin and miso black cod. I attempted to be a fly on the wall and observe who was talking to whom. Many of the faces I didn’t recognize were presumably museum patrons. I always find it interesting trying to distinguish between those who are famous and those who are simply very rich. At a gala like this, the two categories overlap quite considerably.

Eventually, bells rang and we were ushered across the museum towards the dining room. On the way, I spotted Jennifer Tilly, whom I am a big fan of. I complimented her Gucci ensemble and told her I admired her JAR jewelry collection. She immediately asked if I wanted a selfie. It turned out to be one of my favorite photographs of the night.

Jennifer told me that her dress had been five sizes too large the evening before, but that the Gucci elves had worked their magic and made it fit just right. She was wearing a Jean Schlumberger Hedges and Flowers necklace for Tiffany & Co., with matching earrings from the 1960s. It is one of his most iconic designs, and she lit up as I asked her about it. I found myself thinking that I would happily abandon the rest of the evening to order a martini with her and gossip. She was delightful.

I also introduced myself to another Real Housewife I have a soft spot for, Kathy Hilton, who told me she was wearing vintage Gucci. The bow at her collar looked Edwardian-inspired.

The dining room was arranged with extraordinarily long tables with crisp white tablecloths and florals, giving the feeling of a very glamorous wedding. I was seated with Devon Lee Carlson, who was wearing a sheer Gucci number. Model Alex Consani came over to share some tea with us, and we attempted to make our way to the bar before being gently redirected. The speeches were about to begin, apparently, and the time for wandering was over.

The museum’s director introduced Eva Chow, who announced that this would be her final year helping to put on the gala. After years of bringing together the art and film communities, it was a rather poignant moment.

At a neighboring Gucci table sat François-Henri Pinault and his wife, Salma Hayek Pinault, in a sequin red gown by Gucci. Her waist was so dramatically cinched that she looked as though she had stepped straight out of an eighteenth-century portrait. Further along were Gucci creative director Demna, Kim Kardashian in a Bordeaux-hued sparkling gown, and Viola Davis, who was glowing. I also spotted 2hollis, who confessed that he didn’t know anyone there, so Devon and I immediately offered to adopt him for the evening.

There was also an impressive coterie of Victoria’s Secret Angels, including Elsa Hosk, Candice Swanepoel, and Rosie Huntington-Whiteley. Candice wore a Gucci gown held together by what appeared to be several strategically placed crystal strings in front of her crotch. Rosie had opted for a rather more revealing arrangement including a prominent display of her derrière with the Gucci logo doing some heavy lifting when it came to concealing her modesty. Later, I found several of the models outside smoking. Even angels need a cigarette.

In the bathroom, a woman in sky-high heels and a very tight Gucci gown was applying her lipstick. As she turned to leave, her clutch slipped from her hands and burst open on the floor. Its contents scattered in every direction, rolling underneath the neighboring stalls. Lipstick, a wallet, what I believe was a fake nail, and some medication. I admired her composure as she collected everything, composed her dignity, and returned to the party. The show must go on.

Back in the dining room, I said hello to my friend Noor Alfallah and Al Pacino. They were seated with Millicent Wilner, the senior director of Gagosian’s London gallery, who is one of my favorite people to run into every few months. On my way back to my seat, I passed Parker Posey, dressed in the most beautiful kaftan dripping in crystals, and I told her she looked gorgeous. By this point, I had begun mentally assembling my own fantasy dinner party featuring Jennifer Tilly, Parker Posey, and Kathy Hilton.

Eventually, the lights dimmed and the room fell quiet. A short film spotlighted Vija Celmins and her work. Based in New York, the artist spoke about her enduring fascination with the California coastline, the ocean, and the moon. She also mentioned that she had attended UCLA and, amusingly, had taken nothing from the experience. The room laughed at the accomplished artist delivering such an assessment, before she took the stage to accept the prestigious honor.

Characterize First, Then Refactor: A Golden-Corpus Harness With a Mutation Gate

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Most refactors break behavior that no test ever covered. Adding more hand-written tests is slow and biased toward what you already suspect. A pinned behavior snapshot is faster, and a mutation gate tells you whether that snapshot can actually fail.

The rule: pin first, prove the pin bites, then change the smallest thing. If the pin never fails on a deliberate bug, you have documentation, not a safety net.

Choose a target that is boring and load-bearing. Small input surface, no hidden globals, callable from a script. Write the choice down in the commit message.

If the function reads the clock, calls the network, or draws randomness, stop. You need a seam before a snapshot. Pin a wrapper with the bad inputs injected instead.

Build a JSONL file of real-shaped inputs. One case per line, stable ids, keyword arguments. No customer data, no tokens, no emails.

Three sources work well. Existing fixture files, call sites in the repo, and log payloads you have already stripped. Twenty to forty cases covers most small functions.

Include the ugly ones: empty string, zero, negative, missing key, duplicate id. The golden file should pin the error paths too, not just the happy path.

Raw output rarely compares cleanly. Dict ordering drifts, floats repeat, timestamps move. Normalize before hashing.

Normalization is a tradeoff, not a free win. Rounding floats hides small drift. Falling back to type name hides everything inside slot-only objects. Log both decisions in the harness header.

The harness fingerprints each case and stores a sha256. Exceptions are pinned by class name and message.

Run it twice. Once to write, once to confirm the harness is stable. A pin that drifts on a second run is not a pin.

This is the step most people skip. A snapshot nobody has ever seen fail is unverified. So break the code on purpose, one behavior at a time.

Keep each mutant as a tiny patch file. Each one must change real behavior, not formatting.

A MISSED line is the useful output. It names a behavior your corpus never exercises. Add one case, re-pin, re-probe. Repeat until every survivor is a mutant you can prove is equivalent to the original.

A SKIP line proves nothing. A patch that fails to apply, or a mutant that fails to import, is not evidence of coverage.

Now refactor. Rename, extract, inline, move. Re-run the check after every commit.

Example output after a clean extract-method commit: ok: 34 cases unchanged. If a hash moves, the patch was not behavior-preserving. Revert it, split it, try again.

One extra rule keeps this honest. Any commit that updates golden.json must also carry a BEHAVIOR: line in the message. That line explains why the change is intended. A golden diff without that line is a refactor that leaked.

After the refactor, run the mutant loop again. Every mutant that was CAUGHT must still be CAUGHT. Coverage can survive a move while silently degrading.

Two parts of this loop are tedious to write by hand. First, the normalization layer, which needs to survive real shapes you have not seen. Second, the mutant set, which needs genuinely different behaviors rather than nine cosmetic edits.

MonkeyCode's free model access and free server option are relevant here, both as operator-supplied availability claims rather than measured benchmarks. A free model is good at drafting normalize branches for shapes you forgot. It is also good at proposing candidate mutants across comparison operators, defaults, branches, and validation paths. The free server option matters because the mutant loop is a matrix of full test runs. Running that matrix in a sandbox keeps your local credentials and your laptop's patience out of the loop.

Disclosure: This article was prepared as part of MonkeyCode's product outreach.

Use model output as a proposal, never as a gate. Every suggested mutant needs a human check that it compiles and actually changes behavior. In my experience with generated mutants, the failure mode is equivalent mutants and non-applying patches, not missing ideas. Reject them loudly; a mutant set padded with noise makes the probe look green while proving nothing.

Skip this workflow if you have no reproducible input source. Skipped if the function cannot be imported without side effects, or if the output is dominated by data you cannot legally freeze. Skip it when the real risk is security or concurrency, where a pinned snapshot is silent. And skip it if nobody will maintain the mutant set. A stale probe is worse than none, because it looks like evidence.

The most useful file you will produce is the MISSED list. Save it, and let it drive the next corpus case.
