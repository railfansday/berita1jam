📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Industrial and data center demand pushed sales higher amid tight capacity.

Microchip Technology Incorporated (MCHP -13.92%) reported a shift in the business environment from broad customer inventory digestion to lengthening lead times and supply constraints across several product lines. Management reported that broad-based demand, particularly in the industrial and data center segments, drove a 9.2% sequential revenue increase. The company is managing a transition in its data center portfolio, where it is regaining market share with Gen 6 PCIe products after previously losing share in the Gen 5 generation. Management indicated that while AI infrastructure demand has been explosive, the company is preparing for a potential deceleration in that sector while focusing on its core industrial and automotive markets. Strategic focus has shifted toward integrating recent acquisitions and managing capacity constraints at outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT) partners, which have been impacted by high demand from the AI industry.

Meteorites that may have delivered carbon to early Earth carry an oxygen signature older than their asteroids

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Organic specks in primitive meteorites carry an oxygen signature set before their asteroids formed, likely in the cold outer solar system.

Some of the carbon on the early Earth may have arrived inside meteorites, carried as dark specks of organic matter far too small to see.

Where those specks were first made is still an open question. A new study suggests their oxygen dates from before the asteroids that carried them had formed.

In seven primitive meteorites that were never strongly heated, the organic matter carries nearly the same oxygen fingerprint.

Water on the asteroids barely changed it. That makes the oxygen a record of where the material formed, not of what happened to it later.

Where the organics formed is the team’s interpretation, drawn from a small set of meteorites and two lab tests.

Daniel Crocker and David Johnston of Harvard’s Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences led the work. Their co-authors were Conel Alexander of the Carnegie Institution for Science and Mark Thiemens of the University of California, San Diego.

Oxygen atoms come in three stable forms: oxygen-16, oxygen-17 and oxygen-18. They differ only in weight. Scientists call these forms isotopes, and the mix of the three in a sample works like a fingerprint.

On Earth, almost any process that sorts oxygen by weight changes the share of oxygen-17 about half as much as the share of oxygen-18.

The oldest minerals in some carbon-rich meteorites break that rule. In them, the two heavier forms change by equal amounts.

That odd pattern is usually explained as a blend of two ancient pools of oxygen, one rich in oxygen-16 and one poor in it. Researchers drew a reference line for it in 1977 from minerals in the Allende meteorite, but what produced it is still debated.

The meteorites in the study belong to a group called carbonaceous chondrites. The most primitive ones hold up to 4 percent carbon by weight in their organic matter. Between 30 and 80 percent of that organic matter won’t dissolve in acids or solvents.

Alexander’s lab separated that material from the rock. Crocker then measured its oxygen with a method the Harvard group had recently developed.

Water had altered the seven primitive meteorites on their home asteroids, always below about 302°F (150°C). Their organic matter came out almost identical.

The oxygen-18 readings varied by only about 2 parts per thousand, and every sample was within the margin of error of the reference line.

“What surprised me was how similar the oxygen isotope compositions were across meteorites that experienced very different alteration histories after they formed,” Crocker told Earth.com.

These asteroids once held liquid water, and water can swap oxygen atoms with the material around it.

The team calculated what would happen if the organic matter had traded much of its oxygen with that water. Its oxygen-18 reading would be 27 to 58 parts per thousand above a standard.

The actual readings were 10 to 12. So the organic matter most likely swapped very little oxygen with the water. If it had, samples from several separate asteroids would have needed nearly identical conditions to end up with the same values.

The water still changed the material’s chemistry, and some oxygen-bearing parts of the molecules were lost. But it left the oxygen’s mix of weights mostly alone.

“That preservation gives us an opportunity to look past what happened to the organic matter on the asteroids and investigate the environment in which it originally formed,” Crocker said.

Organic matter from six meteorites heated on their asteroids, or by impacts, looked different. It held less of both heavy oxygen forms, and the readings formed a trend away from the unheated group.

To test whether heat alone could do that, the team warmed organic matter from the Mighei meteorite in a vacuum for an hour. One run reached 932°F (500°C) and the other 1,112°F (600°C).

The samples lost about 59 and 67 percent of their oxygen respectively, and what remained shifted along the same trend as the heated meteorites. Undo the heating, and they would move back toward the primitive group, which fits one shared starting fingerprint.

Water didn’t set the fingerprint, and heat changes it in a different direction. So the authors concluded the oxygen most likely went in when the organic matter itself formed, before it was gathered into any asteroid.

The organic matter holds less oxygen-16 than almost every mineral in these meteorites. In the early solar system, water was the main ingredient low in oxygen-16. So the authors think the organics took their oxygen from ancient water as they formed.

Extra heavy hydrogen and nitrogen in the organics already hinted at a cold place with strong radiation. The team favors a birthplace in the outer solar system, with the same well-mixed supply ending up in several asteroids.

“Rather than starting with the chemistry that occurred on the early Earth, we can ask what organic material Earth and other young planets inherited from the Solar System environment in which they formed,” Crocker and Johnston told Earth.com.

The researchers said they “cannot yet identify a single location or oxygen-bearing molecule as its source.” They also checked for lab effects, such as stray mineral grains and the chemicals used to free the organics. They concluded neither changed the readings in any meaningful way.

The unusual pattern in the ancient minerals, first spotted more than 50 years ago, still lacks a full explanation. “That doesn’t solve the mystery, but it provides a new constraint,” the two researchers added.

Next, the team sees the oxygen as a way to narrow down where, and under what conditions, the material formed. That could show how widely the raw ingredients for life’s chemistry were spread among the young planets.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
