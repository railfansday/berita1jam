15 Scent Control Strategies for Deer Hunting That Actually Work

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Early Black Friday deals are here — Shop hunting gear, fishing tackle, optics, and more!

Published Nov 22, 2026 10:00 AM EST

Get everything from whitetail news and opinion pieces, to advanced hunting tactics and cutting-edge gear reviews.

A heavy-antlered 12-point buck materializes on the trail. He’s only five steps from bow range, but then he throws his nose in the air, goes rigid with nervousness, and waves the white flag. Game over. That’s all, folks.

We all know that any successful deer hunt starts with not getting busted by a whitetail’s incredibly powerful sense of smell. And today there’s a whole collection of gear that’s marketed as being able to beat a deer’s nose. Many of the scent control gear and strategies are gimmicks, but some are not.

With careful scent control, you can give yourself a slight edge that just might mean the difference between getting busted and getting a shot. Here are scent-reduction efforts that work, with 15 steps that hunters can use to minimize scent before, during, and after the hunt.

Hunters must understand they will never completely neutralize the scent they emit. Furthermore, they will never completely beat a whitetail’s nose. However, a hunter can minimize their scent.

This won’t keep a deer from smelling you, but it might buy you a little extra time or a few more yards. It can also make all the difference in a non-target deer just moving along rather than alarm snorting.

You don’t have to use all of the following strategies just to shoot a deer. But, implementing at least some of them will certainly make you more effective in the stand.

Deer hunters should consider a long-term scent control mentality. Rather than just preparing the day of the hunt, start mitigating scent long before. During hunting season, consider cutting the smelly shampoos, soaps, colognes, deodorants, and aftershaves out of your routine. This will help keep your clothes smelling more natural, too.

Washing your hunting clothes is the next step. Treat with scent-reduction laundry detergent. Such laundry detergent doesn’t remove all scent, but it knocks out a lot of it and it doesn’t add unnatural scents. (Also, on a deer vision note, ensure that the detergent does not contain UV brighteners.)

Once your hunting clothes are clean, store them in a dedicated container. A portable plastic tote that has a place at home, but easily loads into a vehicle, is the best choice. This should help minimize odors that might contaminate the garment fibers. A lot of hunters like to put a pine branch in their tote to keep clothes smelling fresh and piney (and not like plastic).

In addition to hunting clothing, hunters should also clean their other gear. Backpacks and boots absorb the scents around them. So, store these and other hunting items in areas that aren’t stinky. Use a dedicated storage bin for this gear, too.

The day of the hunt, take a shower with shampoos and soaps that are designed to reduce human odor without adding artificial scents. This should help neutralize some (but not all) of the existing surface scent on your hair and skin.

Brush your teeth, but not with a super-smelly toothpaste. Instead, use something with a natural scent, or no scent at all. Then, eat an apple to help further mitigate scent that might originate from the mouth and throat. Avoid strong-smelling drinks and foods, such as coffee or spicy grub which lead to bad breath.

A lot of hunters use ozone in the field (like Ozonics), but it’s also applicable for treating hunting clothing. Some products are designed to work in conjunction with boxes to clean clothing and remove unwanted scent.

Used improperly, Ozone can destroy clothing. So Wildgame innovations recently introduced a ZeroTrace tote and device that is designed to treat gear without destroying it.

Read Next: Does Ozonics Actually Work? Here’s What 5 Independent, Diehard Deer Hunters Say After Field Testing

To minimize unwanted scent particles from making it onto your hunting clothes, consider dressing once in the field. This keeps away scent from handling pets, visiting gas stations, or other stops between home and the hunt. Getting dressed in the field during a cold or rainy day can be a hassle, but it’s worth it.

Rubber boots can contain some scent, but they only achieve this if kept uncontaminated from foreign scents. Don’t wear your deer hunting boots around town where they might pick up unwanted odors.

Hunters have many reasons for field wipes. But in terms of scent control, wiping off sweat with a field wipe will certainly help. Just make sure the version you use is scent-free.

Even better than wiping away sweat, is to not break a sweat at all. Sweat just adds an entire fresh new layer of scent. In the field, do everything possible to prevent or reduce sweating. Wait to put on your coat until you get to your stand.. Walk slowly. Remain in the shade. Take more time to get settled in the treestand.

Perhaps the most important aspect of scent control is managing the direction that your scent travels. This means choosing good entry and exit routes, and ensuring your wind doesn’t carry over into areas that deer frequent (especially bedding areas and core feeding areas).

Those who hope to minimize scent even further should deploy hunting blinds that are completely enclosed. Those which have completely sealed doors and windows can hold most, if not all, scent within the blind. Keeping windows closed until it’s  time to shoot is an excellent way to bottle up scent.

Some hunters might cleverly use wind blockers, which are land features that keep deer from getting downwind. Examples include hunting along the edge of a bluff, river, hinge-cut, or lake. Similarly, you can make a mock scrapes on either side of your scent cone, which might stop a buck just long enough before he gets downwind of you.

The rising and falling thermals can be helpful or a huge pain in the butt. When used wisely, and with the right stand locations, thermals will carry scent up and away from deer. For example, hunting up high on a ridge when morning thermals carry scent upward makes it very difficult for deer to smell hunters. Likewise, hunting down low in a bottom when afternoon thermals carry scent downward can achieve the same result.

Remember, no matter how far you take scent control strategies, deer will eventually smell you. It’s important to take all precautions, and implement each step outlined above. Ultimately, careful stand selection that puts the wind in your favor will be the most important step for not getting busted.

But don’t get discouraged when the inevitable happens. Just keep hunting. After all, you can’t really beat a deer’s nose, and it wouldn’t be fun if we won every time anyway.

GLP-1s Like Ozempic May Boost Brain Health, But Can They Lower Dementia Risk?

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

GLP-1 medications have transformed obesity and type 2 diabetes treatment. Now, researchers are investigating whether the effects of this blockbuster class of drugs extend beyond weight loss and affect brain health.

A comprehensive new review published on November 5 in the journal Science evaluated the current evidence on how GLP-1 medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide may affect the brain across aging, cardiometabolic disease, and Alzheimer’s disease.

The authors of the review suggested that GLP-1 medications like Ozempic and Zepbound may offer brain health benefits. However, they were also left with the question of whether the medications act directly on the brain or whether better brain health is actually a consequence of improved cardiometabolic health.

Sheena Aurora, MD, vice president, Medical Affairs, Alzheimer’s Association, who wasn’t involved in the review, told Healthline that more research is needed to better understand the effects of GLP-1 drugs on the brain.

“While research suggests GLP-1s could reduce brain inflammation, improve blood vessel health, and even protect neurons, much broader, randomized clinical trials on GLP-1s and brain health, including in people with Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, are needed,” she said.

GLP-1 medications like semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy, mimic the GLP-1 hormone. Tirzepatide, the active ingredient in Mounjaro and Zepbound, also targets a second hormone pathway called GIP.

These medications have been shown to produce significant weight loss and improvements in cardiometabolic health.

This is important for brain health because obesity, type 2 diabetes, and vascular dysfunction are all linked with cognitive impairment and dementia. Insulin resistance and chronic inflammation may also link metabolic and brain health.

Analyses of large outcome trials and observational cohorts have suggested that GLP-1 medications may be associated with a reduced risk of cognitive impairment and dementia. However, randomized trials in diseases like Alzheimer’s disease have yet to demonstrate clinical benefits.

Around 200 preclinical and mechanistic studies have shown benefits for learning, memory, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal resilience, though results are heterogeneous in design and direction, so a clear consensus on magnitude and mechanism remains lacking.

It is also unclear how well GLP-1 medications reach the brain. They appear to reach certain areas, such as the hypothalamus, brainstem, and circumventricular organs, more readily than regions protected by the blood-brain barrier.

The report authors noted that some studies have shown that GLP-1 receptor agonists may reduce the risk of cognitive impairment and dementia in people with type 2 diabetes. This may be due to cognitive issues being linked to factors such as blood sugar, blood pressure, vascular health, and sleep.

Mir Ali, MD, medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA, who wasn’t involved in the report, told Healthline, “These findings are interesting, but like many of the other benefits associated with GLP-1 medications, a direct causal relationship has not yet been proven.”

“Rather, the benefits appear to be more closely tied to the reduction in obesity and better control of diabetes,” he said.

Aurora noted that growing evidence supports the heart-brain connection.

She cited new findings from the Alzheimer’s Association’s landmark U.S. POINTER study presented at the Clinical Trials in Alzheimer’s Disease (CTAD) conference November 16–19 in Boston, MA.

The research showed that the structured lifestyle intervention program also improved heart health risk factors, such as:

“U.S. POINTER can change those risk factors within 24 months, and better heart health risk-factor control was linked with better cognition,” Aurora said. “We’re also interested to see whether adding a drug like a GLP-1 to a U.S. POINTER-style lifestyle program improves cognition in at-risk older adults more than the program alone.”

To find out, the Alzheimer’s Association is funding the Prevention of Risk for Cognitive Decline through Combined Therapy (PROTECT-Cog) global phase 3 clinical trial.

PROTECT-Cog is a first-of-its-kind trial designed to evaluate whether combining a proven multidomain lifestyle intervention with a metabolism-targeting drug, like a GLP-1, can further reduce the risk of cognitive decline, mild cognitive impairment, and dementia in at-risk older adults.

Dung Trinh, MD, internist at MemorialCare Medical Group and Chief Medical Officer of Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, who also wasn’t involved in the review, told Healthline, “I often remind my patients that what’s good for the heart is generally good for the brain.”

“The brain depends on a healthy cardiovascular system to deliver a constant supply of oxygen, glucose, and other nutrients. Conditions such as high blood pressure, diabetes, obesity, and high cholesterol can damage blood vessels, promote chronic inflammation, and increase the risk of stroke and vascular cognitive impairment,” he said.

“Over time, these changes can also contribute to the development or progression of dementia.”

Trinh added that while GLP-1 medications can improve several cardiometabolic risk factors, these improvements do not automatically translate into proven dementia prevention.

“Regular physical activity, a heart-healthy diet, adequate sleep, and management of cardiovascular risk factors remain essential strategies for maintaining brain health as we age,” he said.

When the report authors turned their attention to more stringent tests on semaglutide in people already living with early Alzheimer’s disease, the picture began to change.

Novo Nordisk’s EVOKE and EVOKE + Phase 3 Trials examined the efficacy of semaglutide in people with early stage Alzheimer’s disease.

The studies showed that while semaglutide produced changes in several disease-related biomarkers, it didn’t slow the progression of Alzheimer’s disease.

This emphasizes that fixing metabolic issues might prevent cognitive decline in someone with obesity or diabetes. But it might not be enough to reverse the damage in someone with established Alzheimer’s disease, where profound synaptic loss and protein pathology have already occurred.

The review authors suggest that future research on obesity and diabetes should also include rigorous and specific cognitive testing.

The Signature Is the Real Decision: What Every TRON User Should Know Before Approving a Crypto Swap

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Swap TRC-20 tokens on TRON with self-custody and open-source smart contracts. Low fees, verifiable on TRONSCAN.

A crypto transaction can look deceptively simple.

Connect a wallet. Select a token. Enter an amount. Press Swap.

Then your wallet opens a confirmation window.

Most users glance at the screen, check whether the numbers look right, and approve.

But this is arguably the most important moment of the entire interaction.

Because connecting a wallet, approving token access, and executing a swap are not necessarily the same operation. Understanding the difference is one of the most useful habits a Web3 user can develop.

One of the most common misconceptions in DeFi is that connecting a wallet automatically gives an application access to its funds.

A wallet connection generally allows an application to interact with a wallet address and request certain actions. The user still needs to authorize transactions that require a signature.

There are three separate concepts worth understanding:

Wallet connection: An application establishes communication with your wallet.

Token approval: You authorize a particular smart contract to spend a specified amount of a token on your behalf.

Swap execution: A transaction interacts with the relevant smart contract to perform the exchange.

Depending on the protocol and token, the exact transaction sequence can differ.

Understanding which operation you are signing is more important than simply recognizing the application's name.

Let's take the TRC-20 standard on TRON as an example.

TRC-20 tokens implement a permission mechanism that includes two important functions:

The first authorizes a designated address to spend up to a specified amount of tokens.

The second lets an application or user check how much spending permission remains.

Before a smart contract can transfer those tokens using the standard delegated-transfer mechanism, it may need an allowance from your wallet.

This is where token approval enters the picture.

However, there is an important distinction between approving 250 USDT and granting an unlimited allowance.

The latter can authorize substantially more spending than the immediate transaction requires.

For users, the practical lesson is straightforward:

Don't treat every approval request as a routine confirmation.

Check the spender address and the requested amount. Understand what you are authorizing.

The official TRON documentation provides detailed explanations of the TRC-20 interface and contract interaction:

https://developers.tron.network/docs/trc20-protocol-interface

They can reduce repeated authorization steps when interacting with the same application.

But convenience and security are not interchangeable.

If a contract has permission to spend a token balance, that permission may remain active beyond the transaction that originally required it.

This creates an important security consideration.

A user who approves a contract once might forget that the authorization still exists weeks or months later.

A better approach is to periodically review token allowances and revoke permissions that are no longer needed.

This is particularly relevant for people who interact with multiple DeFi applications.

Your wallet balance is only one part of your security picture. Existing permissions deserve attention too.

There is another detail that makes TRON interesting from an engineering perspective.

Unlike networks that primarily describe transaction execution costs through a single gas metric, TRON uses two principal resources:

Bandwidth measures the data size of an on-chain transaction.

Energy measures the computational resources required to execute smart contracts.

A TRC-20 interaction generally consumes both.

The amount of Energy depends on the contract execution itself. Different operations can have different resource requirements.

For users, this means transaction costs are not simply a fixed number attached to every swap.

They depend on the operation, available resources, and applicable network parameters.

TRON also supports Energy delegation and mechanisms that allow smart contract developers to cover part of the Energy cost.

This creates opportunities for applications to improve the transaction experience without requiring every user to manage network resources in the same way.

Check the token contract address, not just its ticker or display name.

Is the wallet asking for a token approval or an actual transaction?

Make sure the address requesting token permission corresponds to the contract you intended to use.

Avoid granting broader permissions than you are comfortable with.

Understand whether the operation requires Energy, Bandwidth, or additional TRX.

After execution, inspect the transaction on a blockchain explorer rather than relying exclusively on an application's success notification.

These steps take very little time but can help prevent expensive mistakes.

A good DeFi experience should help users understand what they are authorizing, which contract they are interacting with, what resources an operation requires, and how to verify the result.

I Spent 10x Longer Debugging AI Code Than Writing It — Here's What Changed

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Everyone talks about AI speeding up coding. Nobody talks about the debugging session from hell that follows.

I’m not exaggerating when I say this: last quarter, I spent roughly 40 hours writing code with AI assistance, and about 380 hours debugging it. That’s not a typo. A 10x ratio. I felt like I was using a tool that wrote perfect-looking code with hidden landmines on every line.

Let me walk you through what happened, why it happened, and the exact workflow shift that flipped those numbers around.

It started innocently. I was building a data pipeline for a client who needed real-time sentiment analysis on Twitter streams. The architecture was straightforward: ingest tweets, run them through a sentiment model, store results in a Postgres database, and expose a simple REST API.

I decided to let an AI assistant generate the initial scaffolding. "Write a Python script that connects to the Twitter API v2, filters for specific keywords, and streams tweets to a Kafka topic," I typed.

Within seconds, I had a 200-line script that looked flawless. Proper type hints. Docstrings. Even a retry mechanism with exponential backoff. I was sold. I pasted it in, ran it, and... it worked. Tweets were flowing into Kafka.

The first issue surfaced two days later. The script crashed at 3 AM because the Twitter API returned a 429 Too Many Requests — the rate limit had been hit. The AI's retry logic was there, but it retried immediately after a 429, which made things worse. I hadn't read the code carefully because it looked right. My mistake.

Each of these took hours to find because the code compiled and ran. No exceptions. No obvious errors. Just wrong behavior in edge cases.

I kept a log of my debugging sessions for a month. Here's the breakdown:

The code was correct in the happy path. It was catastrophically wrong in the edge cases. And the more "senior" the AI tried to sound, the more convoluted the code became.

After three weeks of this, I had a conversation with a friend who runs a fintech startup. He said something that stuck:

"You wouldn't let a junior dev push code to production without a code review. Why are you letting an AI do it?"

He was right. I was treating the AI as a senior architect when I should have been treating it as a very fast junior developer. The code quality was junior-level: it worked for the demo, but didn't handle production realities.

So I changed my approach. Here's the workflow that cut my debugging time from 10x to about 1.5x.

Instead of: "Write a complete function to parse this JSON and insert into the DB."

I now ask: "Give me a function signature and a skeleton. I'll fill in the logic."

This forces me to understand the structure before I accept the code. The AI's job is to save me typing time, not to save me thinking time.

This was the game-changer. Before I let it generate any implementation, I'd ask for unit tests that define the expected behavior.

Then I'd look at the tests, approve them, and then ask for the implementation. This caught 70% of the edge-case bugs before they ever hit my codebase. The AI's tests were often wrong too — but it was much easier to correct a test than to debug a runtime error in production.

These were the sources of 90% of my "what the hell is this doing?" moments. The code got longer, sure. But it became readable. And readable code is debuggable code.

Before accepting any AI suggestion, I'd run it through a diff tool against the existing codebase. Not just to see what changed, but to check if the changes were minimal.

The AI has a tendency to refactor unrelated parts of your code "for consistency." That's how you end up with a PR that changes 400 lines to fix a one-line bug.

The biggest single improvement was providing the AI with real data samples and actual error messages from my logs.

Instead of: "Fix the rate limiting issue."

I'd write: "We're getting 429s from the Twitter API. The current code uses time.sleep(1) between retries. Here's the exact response header that comes back with the retry-after value: retry_after: 900. Implement a retry that respects this header and logs a warning."

The quality of output improved exponentially. The AI wasn't guessing anymore — it was solving a specific puzzle.

Here's something nobody told me when I started this journey: the same AI prompt can give you wildly different results depending on the model version, the random seed, or even the time of day.

I had one instance where I asked for a date parser three times in the same afternoon. The first output handled ISO formats. The second handled MM/DD/YYYY. The third was a custom parser that only worked for YYYY-MM-DD but looked like it handled everything.

That inconsistency is a hidden tax on your time. If you're building a serious project, you need a stable API endpoint that doesn't change behavior under your feet.

This is where I landed on using a consistent API gateway for my AI calls. I was experimenting with different providers, and the pricing and reliability were all over the place. One provider would have a 5-second latency spike right when I was on a deadline. Another would silently cap my token usage mid-request.

After trying a bunch of options, I settled on a pay-as-you-go setup that routes through a unified endpoint — I'm using shadie-oneapi.com these days. It's not flashy, but it's predictable. Same model, same response format, no surprise quota resets. That consistency alone saved me from having to re-verify AI outputs every time I changed a config file.

After two months with this workflow, I re-ran my time tracking:

Total time dropped from 420 hours to 137 hours. A 67% reduction in overall time, and a massive drop in the frustration quotient.

The extra 15 hours I spent writing code "manually" (or at least reviewing it carefully) paid for itself tenfold by eliminating the mystery bugs.

AI coding tools are incredible for generating skeletons — the boring stuff you already know how to write. They are terrible at handling edge cases you haven't thought of yet, because they don't know what you know about your domain.

The fix isn't to stop using AI. The fix is to stop treating it as an autonomous engineer and start treating it as a very fast, slightly clueless intern who needs explicit instructions, code reviews, and unit tests.

And if you're going to rely on AI-generated code, make sure your API layer is stable. Nothing kills momentum faster than your code generation tool changing behavior mid-sprint. A consistent, pay-as-you-go API endpoint removed that variable from my equation, and it's one less thing I have to think about while I'm wrestling with the actual bugs.

The code I write now is less "elegant" — but it runs. And honestly? That's all that matters at 2 AM when production is on fire.

A Crypto Swap Is a Distributed Systems Problem Disguised as a Button

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Swap TRC-20 tokens on TRON with self-custody and open-source smart contracts. Low fees, verifiable on TRONSCAN.

Why transaction states, wallet permissions, liquidity and on-chain verification matter more than a beautiful exchange interface

Select an asset. Enter an amount. Choose what you want to receive. Confirm.

But beneath those four actions is a surprisingly complex coordination problem involving wallets, smart contracts, network resources, liquidity and blockchain settlement.

The interface might take milliseconds to respond. The blockchain has its own timing, rules and failure conditions.

And that difference is where good swap engineering begins.

One of the most important distinctions in Web3 application design is the difference between a user interface state and an actual blockchain state.

The application calculates an estimated exchange.

A displayed quote does not mean a transaction has happened. A wallet approval does not necessarily mean a swap has completed. Even a submitted transaction can remain pending before final confirmation.

A robust application should communicate these states separately.

This is particularly important for developers designing interfaces where users expect immediate feedback.

Wallet connections are often treated as a simple onboarding feature.

They should be treated as a security boundary.

There is a fundamental difference between connecting a wallet and authorizing a transaction.

Users should never be encouraged to approve something they do not understand.

Developers should also avoid requesting unnecessary permissions and should explain potentially risky approval scopes.

Blockchain applications have an advantage over many traditional financial applications: their transaction activity can be independently inspected.

But transparency is useful only when users know what to inspect.

For a token swap protocol, several questions matter:

Contract identity: Is the user interacting with the expected contract?

Source verification: Is the published source code available and verified?

Permissions: Can an administrator change important contract parameters or withdraw assets?

Transaction history: Can users inspect actual operations on-chain?

Security review: Are audit reports available, and do they cover the deployed contract version?

An audit badge is not a substitute for understanding the underlying contract.

Likewise, open-source code does not automatically mean that a deployed application is safe.

The relationship between published code, deployed bytecode and actual permissions deserves careful attention.

Developers sometimes focus heavily on exchange rates while overlooking transaction costs.

On TRON, network resources such as Energy and Bandwidth influence how transactions are executed.

This creates an interesting UX challenge.

A user might understand the token exchange perfectly but still be confused by network-resource requirements.

A well-designed swap interface should make transaction costs understandable before confirmation.

The goal is not simply to make transactions cheaper.

It is to make the cost model predictable enough that users can make informed decisions.

A mature swap application should be designed around failure scenarios, not just the happy path.

The wallet disconnects during the process?

These scenarios should have clear interface responses.

A generic error message is rarely sufficient.

Good error handling should explain what happened, whether funds moved, whether another action is required and where the transaction can be inspected.

In Web3, clarity during failure is just as important as speed during success.

Review the exchange rate and price impact.

Verify transaction details through a blockchain explorer.

Never assume that a successful wallet connection means a successful swap.

These checks are useful regardless of which exchange interface someone chooses.

A cryptocurrency swap is not merely a conversion between two token balances.

It is a coordinated process involving multiple systems, each with its own state, timing and security assumptions.

The best Web3 applications understand this distinction.

They do not just make the exchange button look simple. They make the underlying process understandable.

And that is a much more meaningful measure of good blockchain UX.

Following Saturday's dominating win over Arkansas, there was only one unanswered question for the Texas football team.

Will the Longhorns return to Royal-Memorial Stadium this season?

Arch Manning accounted for three touchdowns and Texas seized an early lead that it wouldn't relinquish in a 45-3 rout of the Razorbacks. With the 42-point victory on the UT campus, the Longhorns improved to 9-2 on the season and 6-2 in SEC play.

Arkansas was the last team on UT's home schedule this season. But Texas was ranked fifth in the College Football Playoff rankings this week, which is a notable number.

The fifth, sixth, seventh and eighth seeds will get to host a first-round playoff game in December, so there may be some additional action on Bevo Boulevard this year. A home playoff game isn't guaranteed, though. The Longhorns are still in contention for a first-round bye, and Texas could also slip in the rankings if it stumbles at Texas A&M next week.

Texas is now 6-1 at home this season. The Longhorns were last beaten at home during the month of November in 2022.
