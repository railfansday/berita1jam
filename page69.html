Here's What the Average Man's Social Security Benefit Looks Like by Age

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Men's average retirement benefit runs $409.85 higher monthly than women's, a gap rooted in lifetime earnings with survivor benefit implications.

Men's average Social Security benefits run higher than women's at every age. This breakdown lays out the specific figures across the early retirement years based on the most recent data snapshot.

As of December 2025, the average retirement benefit among men is $2,282.28 per month, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA). The average for women is $1,872.43, which leaves a monthly gap of $409.85.

The SSA bases your benefit on an average of your wages across your 35 highest-earning years. These wages are adjusted for inflation and run through a formula designed to replace about 40% of your pre-retirement income. Most retirees need a much higher share of their prior pay to cover their costs.

These averages represent a snapshot of current beneficiaries, including those who claimed early and those who waited for higher payments. As of December 2025, the monthly averages show consistent differences between genders across the board.

The higher average for men reflects historical differences in lifetime earnings and years spent in the paid workforce. While individual work histories vary, these broader patterns shape the current benefit landscape across every age group.

A man who is 65 today is expected to live about 18 more years, while a woman is expected to live about 21 more years, according to the SSA. This means that men's Social Security benefits typically cover a larger proportion of their retirement costs than women's, who have to pay for more years of living expenses with smaller checks.

A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027. The above figures don't yet reflect that increase, which will add about $91 a month to the $2,529.62 average for 70-year-old men. Full retirement age (FRA) ranges from 66 to 67, depending on your birth year, and it's 67 for everyone born in 1960 or later.

Married people have the option to claim a spousal benefit on their partner's work record while their partner is still alive and a survivor benefit after their partner's death. You only get these checks if they're worth more than your own retirement benefit.

Since women have historically had fewer years in the workforce and lower average earnings, they're more likely to claim spousal and survivor benefits, which means married men also need to think about how their claiming decision could affect their spouse.

Your partner cannot claim a spousal benefit until you are receiving your retirement checks, so your claiming age may affect theirs. Talk your options over with your spouse and make sure you're both on the same page about when each of you plans to sign up. If one of you wants to change things up down the road, keep the other in the loop and adjust your retirement plan accordingly.

Warren Buffett Has 1 Simple Rule for Surviving a Stock Market Crash, and History Says It's Never Once Failed

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If a bear market is coming, Warren Buffett's advice is more important than ever.

It's been a roller-coaster of a week for the stock market. The Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC +1.09%) officially entered correction territory after falling by more than 10% from its October peak, while the S&P 500 (^GSPC +0.31%) and Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI -0.46%) are down by around 6% and 5%, respectively.

Investors are also grappling with the downturn. The Fear and Greed index measures investor sentiment based on multiple stock market metrics, and it quickly plunged into the "extreme fear" category amid this week's turbulence.

But it's not all bad news right now. Warren Buffett has some timeless advice for investing during crashes and bear markets, and history says this strategy has worked out every single time.

When the market is shaky, it can be tempting to avoid investing or even sell your stocks in fear that prices will fall further. According to Warren Buffett, though, that's one of the worst moves to make during a slump.

"A simple rule dictates my buying," Buffett explained in a 2008 opinion piece for The New York Times. "Be fearful when others are greedy, and be greedy when others are fearful." He went on to emphasize that "bad news is an investor's best friend. It lets you buy a slice of America's future at a marked-down price." In other words, when the future is bleak, buy more.

In the years following the publication of that piece, the S&P 500 soared by nearly 1,000%, and the investors who reaped the biggest rewards were those who stayed invested even when it was tough.

"Over the long term, the stock market news will be good," Buffett continued, noting the Dow's extraordinary rise from 66 to 11,497 during the 20th century.

"You might think it would have been impossible for an investor to lose money during a century marked by such an extraordinary gain. But some investors did. The hapless ones bought stocks only when they felt comfort in doing so and then proceeded to sell when the headlines made them queasy."

No matter how severe the short-term volatility is, the market has always managed to recover.

During the dot-com bubble in 2000, for example, the S&P 500 fell by nearly 50% as the tech sector collapsed. It took until 2007 for the index to reach another all-time high, and just a few months later, the Great Recession began.

However, between 2000 and 2020, the S&P 500 still earned total returns of more than 224%. In other words, even if you'd invested at seemingly the worst moment -- immediately before the onset of two of the worst recessions in U.S. history -- you'd still have more than tripled your money over 20 years.

This trend holds up throughout history, too. Between 1919 and 2025, all of the S&P 500's rolling 20-year periods have ended in positive total returns, according to analysis from Crestmont Research. By holding an S&P 500 ETF or index fund for 20 years, it's historically been harder to lose money in the stock market than to make money.

Regardless of what's ahead for the market, continuing to invest is key. However, it's more important than ever to choose your stocks wisely. Weak companies can thrive during periods of economic prosperity, but recessions and bear markets will hit those stocks the hardest. The weakest of them may crash and never recover at all.

Companies with strong fundamentals -- such as a durable competitive advantage, healthy finances, and a competent leadership team -- are the most likely to rebound after a crash. The more of these stocks you own, the better prepared you'll be to not only survive a downturn, but thrive over time.

Construction crews in California accidentally unearthed the bones of a large mammal that lived 40 to 45 million years ago

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Fossils found during construction in Oceanside, California, may belong to a giant hoofed mammal that lived 40 to 45 million years ago.

During routine excavation for a city infrastructure project in Oceanside, California, Joseph El Adli, a principal paleontologist with Helix Environmental Planning, spotted fossil bones about 12 feet (3.7 meters) below the surface. He recognized their importance and secured the area.

The bones belong to a large, hoofed mammal that lived 40 to 45 million years ago, the City of Oceanside announced on September 22, 2026.

The animal may be a titanothere, nicknamed “thunder beast,” or Amynodon, historically called a “swamp rhino.” Laboratory work should settle which.

He was monitoring the dig as the project’s paleontologist. The city places monitors on major infrastructure work as standard practice and to comply with California environmental laws.

The find includes several limb bones, ribs and numerous other bones and fragments. The largest piece appears to be a femur.

El Adli’s team worked for seven days to expose the bones from beneath several tons of hardened mudstone and sandstone.

The fossils were then wrapped in a plaster field jacket and moved to an off-site laboratory.

The exact location has not been disclosed, to protect public safety and the integrity of the site.

The bones came from Member C of the Santiago Formation, a thick sequence of sedimentary rock in northwestern San Diego County.

Paleontologists based the age estimate on the known age of that rock and the types of organisms found in it. The range falls within the Eocene Epoch, in a stretch of time called the Uintan North American Land Mammal Age.

According to the city, the animal roamed ancient floodplains in the area.

The same rock unit in the city has yielded Diegoaelurus vanvalkenburghae, a predator named in a 2022 description of Diegoaelurus. It belongs to the machaeroidines, the oldest group of sabertooth mammals.

Known from a nearly complete lower jaw with most of its teeth, it is the youngest machaeroidine on record. Its authors tentatively tie the group’s extinction to faunal change at the shift from the middle to the late Eocene, part of a wider turnover among mammal predators.

The fossil site that produced it, known as Jeff’s Discovery, also holds a tapir relative called Hesperaletes and a hyaenodontid predator, Limnocyon.

The paper was led by Shawn Zack of the University of Arizona College of Medicine in Phoenix, with Ashley Poust of the San Diego Natural History Museum.

Both candidates belong to Perissodactyla, the order of odd-toed hoofed mammals that includes today’s horses, rhinos and tapirs. Titanotheres are also known as brontotheres.

Oceanside officials describe them as animals roughly the size of a black rhino, with deep chests, a distinct shoulder hump and small horns.

In 2001, paleontologists collected brontothere remains from a sandstone layer at another construction site in Oceanside, near Rancho del Oro Road.

That animal, Parvicornus, stood about 5 to 6 feet (1.5 to 1.8 meters) at the shoulder, according to the San Diego Natural History Museum. It had only a very small horn, more of a bump, near each eye.

Brontotheres began as dog-size animals in the early Eocene and grew larger over roughly 20 million years. Those in South Dakota’s Badlands, about 38 to 34 million years ago, reached around 8 feet (2.4 meters) tall and 16 feet (4.9 meters) long.

The Badlands animals carried blunt, paired horns on their noses, sometimes more than 3.3 feet (1 meter) long. Males had larger horns than females.

These herbivores most likely ate soft leaves in Eocene forests. The group died out at the end of the Eocene, likely as the climate turned cooler and drier, the National Park Service notes.

Amynodon is the namesake of the amynodonts, which the American Museum of Natural History describes as large-bodied, hornless rhinos. Amynodon itself had a squat, hippo-like body, city officials say.

Their nickname comes from an older idea that they spent much of their time in water. Evidence for that is very limited.

Only Metamynodon, the last North American amynodont, may have been semi-aquatic. Its fossils turn up in sandstone laid down by rivers.

Amynodonts lived in Asia and North America from the middle Eocene through the early Oligocene. They have no living relatives and sit far from today’s species on the rhino family tree.

Amynodon had a large hollow in the skull in front of each eye socket. It likely anchored muscles for an enlarged upper lip.

At the laboratory, preparators will remove the plaster jackets. They will then harden and stabilize the fossils with museum-grade glues and resins.

Once the bones are fully freed from the surrounding rock, paleontologists expect to narrow down the animal’s precise identity.

The specimens are expected to join a museum collection, where they will be preserved and available to scientists and the public. The museum has not been named.

Several jacketed blocks have not been opened yet, and a skull may be inside one of them.

The information was obtained from the City of Oceanside.

TL;DR: FinCEN and the Federal Reserve are withdrawing a proposed expansion of US recordkeeping and Travel Rule requirements that would have covered certain convertible virtual currency transfers and lowered a cross-border transfer threshold. The withdrawal removes a proposal; it does not cancel existing anti-money-laundering obligations.

Two announcements issued by the same US agency on November 6 point in different regulatory directions. While FinCEN imposed a targeted financial restriction on MEXC Global, it also announced that it was withdrawing a previously proposed update to the Recordkeeping and Travel Rule for funds transfers.

According to FinCEN’s release, the proposal would have amended requirements relating to convertible virtual currencies and certain digital assets. It would also have lowered a threshold for transfers beginning or ending outside the United States. The Federal Reserve Board joined the withdrawal.

This distinction is central to understanding the announcement. A proposal describes changes regulators are considering. Withdrawing it means those proposed changes will not proceed through that rulemaking in their existing form. It does not mean every reporting, customer-identification or information-sharing requirement currently applying to financial institutions has disappeared.

The Travel Rule generally addresses information that financial institutions must collect or transmit when handling covered transfers. In the digital asset industry, implementation raises difficult questions about service-provider identities, unhosted wallets and transfers that cross multiple systems. A proposed amendment can change where those obligations attach or what thresholds apply, but it cannot be read as the whole compliance framework.

FinCEN explicitly frames this action as the withdrawal of a notice of proposed rulemaking. Companies should therefore update regulatory trackers and comment schedules without treating it as permission to suspend controls that already exist under separate law.

Rules governing transfer information affect the customer experience as well as the institution behind it. More extensive data-collection requirements can add operational cost, create friction during international payments and complicate relationships between banks and crypto businesses. Supporters of stricter rules argue that traceability is needed to investigate laundering and sanctions evasion; opponents can point to privacy, proportionality and practical implementation burdens.

The November announcement does not settle that debate. It signals that the agencies have chosen not to move ahead with this particular expansion. A future rulemaking or different targeted action remains possible, and the exact scope of any later proposal cannot be inferred now.

That sits beside FinCEN’s much harder action against MEXC: the agency can withdraw a broad proposal while using existing powers against entities it believes pose specific risks. The two decisions are not mutually exclusive.

Banks, exchanges and payment providers should review the published withdrawal documents, identify which previously planned systems changes relied on the abandoned proposal, and keep existing transfer-screening arrangements in place unless their legal teams determine otherwise. Cross-border businesses should pay particular attention to the difference between US requirements and rules in the jurisdictions where counterparties operate.

For broader context, NewsBTC has followed how regulatory changes alter stablecoin access in Europe. This is another reminder that regulation often changes the infrastructure customers use without changing the underlying technology of a token.

The next useful development would be a clear timetable for any replacement rule, or fresh agency guidance on affected transfers. Until then, the precise conclusion is modest but important: a proposed reporting expansion has been withdrawn, while the underlying AML regime continues.

If you're in the mood for a discrete dose of folk horror, Hulu recently added Adam Scott's terrifying horror "Hokum." Masaaki Ninomiya's "Gannibal" manga was originally serialized across thirteen volumes from October 2018 to November 2021. Another also highlighted how the show is "beautifully shot and incredibly well-acted." Unfortunately, it seems unlikely there will be more to come. "Gannibal" Season 2 was released on March 19, 2025 and that wrapped up the story told in Masaaki Ninomiya's manga. Thankfully, if you are able to binge your way through both seasons of the series, there are plenty of great folk horror movies to tide you over.

At what length do DisplayPort cables start giving you trouble?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The longer a DisplayPort cable gets, the lower the visual quality will become

Although pure USB-C and Thunderbolt video connections are gaining traction, DisplayPort continues to rule the day for a lot of Mac and Windows PCs, and it's likely just as much about production cost as legacy compatibility. To equal the bandwidth of the best DisplayPort tech, companies need to use USB 4 v2 or Thunderbolt 5. Whether that's inherently more expensive to manufacture or not, people also tend to expect power delivery through USB-C and Thunderbolt, whereas DisplayPort can be exclusively about video.

A major concern with any video format is how far a clean signal can be delivered. With HDMI, for example, connections can get sketchy past 25 feet, if not far earlier. When a cable isn't up to the job, the result can be problems like artifacts, flickering or connections dropping out entirely. Alternately, a display might automatically scale back resolutions and refresh rates.

With DisplayPort, the limitations can be pretty sharp if you want to maintain maximum quality. There are ways around them, but they're probably not worth the money for most computing setups.

The standard copper-based DisplayPort cable should be solid up to 6 feet. By solid, we mean capable of displaying any resolution, color or refresh rate combo you could realistically ask for, such as 4K HDR at 144Hz. You shouldn't experience artifacts or other issues with this setup, barring physical damage or severe interference that would probably affect the rest of your hardware too.

By about 10 feet, resolution and refresh compromises begin to enter the picture. 4K refresh rates may be limited, or else resolution could be forced to drop to 1080 or 1440p. Toward 15 feet, stable 4K is gone and even those lower resolutions might be capped at slower refresh rates.

Luckily, that's not the true limit of DisplayPort. What we've been talking about so far is the performance of passive copper. If you switch to active copper cables that boost signal strength, practical range can extend to between 15 and 30 feet. Even better are fiber optic cables, which can run up to 100 feet.

Expect to pay for the privilege, mind you. Whereas a 10-foot passive copper cable (rated for DisplayPort 2.1) can cost as little as $10, even a 15-foot fiber optic cable can cost north of $50. That second cable should be bulletproof, but it's probably not worth the investment outside of corporate offices.

Most people should stick with passive copper DisplayPort cables measuring 6 feet or less. Although active copper and fiber optic cables provide reliable performance at greater distances, there's usually no need for it since DisplayPort is almost exclusively associated with monitors. If a monitor is sitting on your desk, it's almost certainly less than 6 feet away from your computer.

In fact, if you're daisy-chaining monitors via MST (Multi-Stream Transport), it's better to stick to the shortest cables possible since two 3-foot cables already brings you up to the 6-foot mark. It's all too easy to accidentally cripple one of your screens even if the GPU in your computer is likely going to be the biggest bottleneck. You need powerful hardware to run 4K games at 120Hz on a single screen, let alone two. The requirements soften for most productivity apps, yet the general concern remains.

It'll be interesting to see how much longer DisplayPort hangs around as a separate cable format. Once costs come down and devices are equipped with USB 4 v2 and Thunderbolt 5 as a matter of course, there may not be any incentive to keep DisplayPort in the mix. Not when port space is already at a premium.

Extending LTE Beyond Terrestrial Coverage

For asset trackers, environmental monitoring devices, and other IoT equipment deployed in remote agricultural areas, mountainous regions, or locations with limited cellular coverage, satellite connectivity offers an additional way to keep devices connected.

Traditionally, adding satellite capabilities can introduce additional hardware requirements, power consumption, and development costs. This raises an important question for IoT device manufacturers: Can existing LTE designs be extended to support satellite connectivity without adding dedicated satellite communication hardware?

LTE Cat.1 bis offers a practical balance of data performance, mobility, power consumption, and cost for a broad range of IoT applications. SpaceX Direct to Cell extends the reach of cellular technology by deploying LTE base station capabilities on low Earth orbit (LEO) satellites and operating through partnerships with mobile network operators.

For compatible LTE devices that meet the relevant network access requirements, satellites can provide an additional connectivity option beyond terrestrial cell towers.

This creates opportunities for OEMs to explore satellite-enabled IoT applications while retaining their existing LTE hardware architecture, potentially reducing the complexity of introducing satellite connectivity into established device designs.

Real-World Performance Across Two Environments

Establishing a satellite connection is only part of the equation. For IoT applications, reliable data transfer and the ability to operate under varying coverage conditions are equally important.

Under open-sky conditions, both modules successfully established satellite data connections and demonstrated practical data throughput.

These findings highlight both the potential and the practical considerations of satellite-enabled IoT connectivity.

For applications such as asset tracking and agricultural monitoring, which typically transmit small amounts of data at regular intervals, the priority is not necessarily to match terrestrial LTE speeds. Instead, satellite connectivity can provide an additional path for transmitting location updates, device status, and sensor readings when terrestrial coverage is unavailable.
