Denis Villeneuve Talks ‘Dune: Part Three’ and Rebooting James Bond: ‘We Cannot F*ck It Up.’

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

“I’m out of a job,” Denis Villeneuve said, jokingly. Hours earlier, Villeneuve had signed off on the IMAX 70-milimeter print of Dune: Part Three, officially wrapping post-production on that film—and in turn, a decade-long chapter of the Quebecois writer-director’s working life.

Fresh off this milestone, Villeneuve and GQ had come to the Lucas Museum of Narrative Art in Los Angeles for a conversation and a private screening of Dune: Part Two, presented by OMEGA, in honor of his anointment as GQ’s Director of the Year. Global Editorial Director Adam Baidawi introduced Villeneuve before the director and GQ’s Zach Baron dove into a conversation that covered his original Dune plans, where Dune: Part Three breaks from its predecessors, and Villeneuve’s feelings about taking over the James Bond franchise.

GQ’s Adam Baidawi and Zach Baron with Denis Villeneuve

“I feel light,” the director of the year told Baron, then added, “Numb.” He was still processing Dune’s end, he said—while also coming to grips with closing the book on “the most rewarding, inspiring, and best ten years of my life as a filmmaker.”

Villeneuve’s first two Dune films have raked in more than a billion dollars at the box office. Part Three appears likely to nearly double that when it hits theaters (and locks up most of the world’s IMAX screens) a week before Christmas. But at one point during his conversation with Baron, the director pushed back gently on the notion of these movies as a “franchise.”

“There was nothing industrial in the way they were made,” Villeneuve said. They were the work of “a bunch of friends”—a familial crew, out in the desert putting things together.

“In French,” the Quebecois director continued, “there is a word—artisanal. They are very personal. Made with a lot of care. They’re little big things—but in a way, they are delicate.”

After all, as sprawling and cosmically grand as they are, these movies are also a deeply personal project for the director, born of images that took root in his head when he first read Frank Herbert’s thick-as-a-brick, notoriously adaptation-resistant 1965 novel Dune at age 13. Later, as Villeneuve racked up credits and clout, people would ask him what his dream project was; the answer was always Dune.

“I kept saying to myself, Someone else will do it one day,” he told Baron. “Another filmmaker will adapt this book again. And I kept waiting, and I kept waiting, until I felt ready to do it myself.”

Herbert wrote five escalatingly bonkers Dune sequels before his death in 1986, but Villeneuve intended to walk away from the table after adapting the first book across two films.

“Trilogies are very dangerous,” he told Baron. “The first movie is a promise, and if you fulfill the promise with the second one, then there’s a feeling of security that comes with that, and you feel that you have a recipe…That’s the trap. Because then you repeat yourself.”

But after wrapping Part Two, Villeneuve found himself unable to stop thinking about Dune Messiah—the second Dune novel, which Herbert supposedly wrote in frustration, believing the audience had missed the point by embracing Paul Atreides as a hero.

Villeneuve said his take on Messiah is “a different beast” than the first two films, built it around the tension between Timothée Chalamet’s Paul and his Fremen consort Chani (Zendaya), who Villeneuve has re-imagined—in a pivotal departure from Herbert’s version—as a nonbeliever who takes a critical view of Paul’s rise to power.

Neel Shah, Timothy Simons, and Michael Lasker

Of course, the specter (S.P.E.C.T.R.E.?) of another franchise hung over the proceedings at the Lucas Museum. Villeneuve, after all, is only technically between jobs; he’s a self-described “monomaniac” who insists that he can only hyper-focus on one project at a time and has been locked in on Dune for a decade, but the as-yet-untitled 26th Bond film is in motion, with Villeneuve signed on to direct.

GQ’s Adam Baidawi, Denis Villeneuve, and OMEGA’s Lucien Jornod

He could say almost nothing in public about the project, or even when he’d begin shooting it—he joked about having “the target of the sniper” on his chest—but spoke of feeling the responsibility not to fail: “We cannot fuck it up. We do not have the choice.”

Over the past week, the casting rumors around Villeneuve’s Bond reboot have reached what our UK colleagues accurately termed the “fever-dream” stage. It might be Callum Turner or Slow Horses’ Jack Lowden, but it might be Robert Pattinson, who’s emerged as a surprise front-runner after months of supposition about the part being earmarked for a relative unknown. At some point, toward the end of Tuesday’s conversation, the subject of Bond 26’s lead could no longer be avoided.

“Has the role of James Bond been cast?” Baron asked the director.

“It’s—it’s in the process. In the process,” Villeneuve said with a smile. “I am pushing the gas.”

“Blink once for Robert Pattinson,” Baron suggested, “and twice for Callum Turner.”

Villeneuve, for the record, did not blink. ✦

NASA's secret weapon in the lunar space race may be ... procurement reform?

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

“The pace that’s occurring, and the nature of the procurements, is unprecedented.”

Senior NASA officials held a lengthy news conference last weekend, laying out a series of lunar contract awards, new contract opportunities, and mission decisions. They followed one after the other: landers, nuclear-powered rovers, scouting drones, power generation, storage, and communication satellites.

“Hopefully, we’re keeping industry on their toes trying to catch up to all the solicitations we’re doing,” said Carlos García-Galán, manager of the Moon Base Program, more than 90 minutes into the news conference. “This is what it takes to do the Moon Base in the timeline we have set forth.”

The rapid-fire announcement of awards tells us a few things about NASA’s efforts to return to the Moon and establish a durable settlement there during the next decade.

First, the agency is serious about this effort—the Artemis Moon Base program represents the most credible effort to establish a permanent outpost on another world in NASA’s history. Second, NASA is reaching out to all corners of the US commercial space industry, casting an extraordinarily wide net to reach companies that have yet to fly any hardware into orbit. Third, NASA is getting creative about how it’s buying these services, and this may matter most of all.

There was a lot packed into the announcements this weekend, so to make sense of it all, I called Mike Gold, a lawyer and former NASA associate administrator who set up the Artemis Accords, who is now the president of Redwire Space. He was at NASA at the beginning of Artemis and understands what it takes to get the government moving. He said he was blown away. During a period of seven days, Gold said, NASA put out five proposals for lunar contracts.

“The pace that’s occurring, and the nature of the procurements is unprecedented,” Gold said. “You are seeing an all-hands-on-deck approach. Big companies, small companies, new companies, old companies.”

What has impressed Gold most about NASA’s strategy since announcing the Moon Base in March, at the Ignition event, has been the agency’s evolving procurement strategy. The space agency has adopted a flexible procurement strategy similar to one used by the US Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency.

There are two components. NASA has largely used a mechanism called Broad Agency Announcements, which ask for a capability such as providing power on the surface of the Moon. This allows NASA to ask industry for ideas without being too prescriptive and levying a bunch of requirements. It’s rather like instead of telling industry precisely what it wants, NASA asks industry what it can do.

Critically, Gold said, the space agency has also used a DARPA-style preliminary proposal process. In response to a traditional procurement, interested companies would prepare a detailed proposal, often running dozens, if not hundreds, of pages. This required an extraordinary amount of time.

With the Moon Base, NASA is employing a two-step process. After NASA announces the need for a service, interested companies submit a short white paper describing their solution. After a short review, NASA identifies the concepts it wants to pursue further, and only then do companies invest the time and resources to prepare a detailed proposal.

Gold said several lunar opportunities interested Redwire, and the company submitted initial proposals in response. But it only had to prepare a full proposal for opportunities that NASA wanted to pursue, including using a variant of its roll-out solar arrays on the Moon to generate power.

“We were able to focus the lion’s share of our resources in what the agency really wanted and what we were best at,” he said.

From this shift, it seems clear that NASA is trying to move away from micromanaging every individual lunar-base subsystem and instead seeks to coordinate independently developed commercial capabilities. The space agency is sourcing them from everywhere, from major contractors like Lockheed Martin, SpaceX, Blue Origin, and Northrop Grumman to relatively new startups such as Impulse Space, ispace-US, Voyager Technologies, Quantum Space, and more.

With questions emerging about how committed SpaceX is to NASA work in the future, the space agency appears to be seeking to diversify the way it gets cargo to the Moon. For example, it is leaning into Relativity Space and its large (but unproven) Terran R rocket, as well as orbital transfer vehicles built by Impulse Space. NASA is prioritizing the creation of a whole ecosystem of potential contractors rather than relying on one or two primes.

In doing so, NASA is taking a big risk. It is attempting to accelerate its lunar program by buying services from companies whose hardware is not yet operational and, in some cases, exists only as advanced engineering designs. For NASA, ultimately, it is a high-risk, high-reward approach. But in a demanding contest with China to reach and establish settlements on the Moon, NASA’s post-Apollo timidity is no longer tenable.

So how will NASA pay for all of the new stuff? Some of the funding is being repurposed from the previously shuttered Lunar Gateway program, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said. For example, as part of the “One Big Beautiful Bill” act passed in 2025, NASA received $2.6 billion for the Gateway. Some of the funding was already appropriated through the agency’s Commercial Lunar Payload Services Program.

Moreover, Isaacman said, some of these contracts are relatively small and will enable NASA to invest more down the road in successful services.

“By doing a lot of littles, and learning from our approach and also putting some good wins on the board, we’re demonstrating that we can be very efficient and responsible with taxpayer dollars,” he said. With this approach, he added, “You give future administrations the option of how they can dial up or down missions. Some of these missions in the, call it, $200 million range between launch, lander, and payload; potentially, you can add those or drop those inside a $25 billion approximate budget per year without putting the entire program at risk.”

‘I’ve had to prove people wrong’: Kane still striving before record England cap

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Captain’s 126th cap against Croatia will top Shilton’s record

Kane aims to increase record for younger players to catch

Harry Kane says he still feels a pressure to prove his worth, even as he prepares to become England’s most capped men’s player, as he outlined his determination to set records that will stand for years.

The captain will make his 126th appearance against Croatia at Wembley on Thursday night, moving him ahead of Peter Shilton on the all-time list. Kane is England’s record goalscorer with 91, comfortably clear of Wayne Rooney, who retired with 53.

Kane will be presented with a memento of his historic cap by Shilton before kick-off against Croatia. Shilton has held the record since 1990 when he finished his 20-year career. Kane’s achievement of winning the Ballon d’Or last month will also be recognised.

“I don’t think it will ever leave me,” Kane said, when asked whether he continued to have points to prove. “It’s probably part of what makes me push myself to the limits that I do because in football, you’ve never completed it. No matter what you’ve achieved, there is always a trophy that someone else has won or a goal record that someone else has.

“Whether it’s noise from the outside, from media or the fans … it just helps motivate me to realise there’s a lot more to achieve. My biggest fear is finishing my career and thinking I could have done a bit more … if I could have just done that or done that. I want to make sure I cross every bridge and do everything possible so that whenever that day does come, I can say I’ve done everything possible. And I can sleep well at night.”

The 33-year-old Kane is conscious that two of his younger England teammates have won a significant number of caps. Declan Rice has 80 at the age of 27; Jude Bellingham 60 at 23. “I’ll try and give them a good number to catch,” Kane said. “The goal record, of course, is going to be one that’s difficult to beat for a while.

“I’ve spoken about leading by example and pushing a lot of these younger players. It’s by setting standards, being available every time, getting the caps and scoring the goals. That should help motivate the younger players. If I was younger and I had a player in front of me doing what I was doing, I know that would push me to think: ‘OK, I can score those goals, I can win the Ballon d’Or, I can break the cap record.’ Whilst I’m in this place, it is to try and set these records as high as I can and one day look back to see if anyone can catch them.”

Kane has been doubted at numerous points of his career, starting when he was released by Arsenal as an eight-year-old. He had difficult loans from Tottenham to Norwich and Leicester in 2012-13 and there were the one-season wonder jibes after his breakthrough campaign for Spurs and England in 2014-15.

Kane has been ridiculed for setting lofty targets. Such as in 2017 when he said he wanted to replicate the numbers of Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo. When he stated his ambition to win the 2018 World Cup Golden Boot. Or when he stood on the verge of his 100th England cap in September 2024 with 66 goals and said he wanted to score 100. There were questions about his place in the team after Euro 2024 when his performances were underwhelming and he was substituted in the final. Every time, he answered back.

“When you look at all the great athletes, I’ve spoken before about Tom Brady [the NFL icon] and it’s that unwavering mentality to be the best. How would the crew of the Wild Blue Yonder fare on a real exoplanet?

Imagine you’ve crash-landed on an alien planet. Your ship is destroyed, your oxygen supply is dangerously low, and the landscape around you is completely unknown. Where would you begin looking for clues to your survival? Surprisingly, the best place to start could be the star overhead.

This is the predicament that three crew members of the Wild Blue Yonder face in Audible’s new sci-fi thriller, "Exoplanet." This sci-fi thriller is written by acclaimed comic writer Benjamin Percy, executive produced by Andy Weir (Project Hail Mary, The Martian), and features an all-star voice cast including Anthony Mackie, Ariana DeBose, and John Leguizamo.

Stranded on an unfamiliar planet, they encounter volcanic wastelands, unknown skies, and environments that seem to defy reason. But behind the extraordinary events of this audio drama is a very real scientific question: what makes a planet habitable?

In “Exoplanet”, the crew view skies that are not only unfamiliar but also seemingly different from one another. One character sees a sun as so dim that he can look directly at it, while another suffers under a blazing heat, surrounded by stretches of sun-scorched red rock.

For astronomers, the light emitted by a star is one of the most crucial tools for understanding planets orbiting it.

Using a technique called spectroscopy, astronomers split starlight into its constituent wavelengths, producing a spectrum much like a rainbow. By studying this spectrum, they can investigate the temperature, composition, and other physical properties of the star.

However, stellar temperature is only part of the picture. The amount of energy a planet receives depends on both the luminosity of the star and its orbital distance.

Astronomers use these measurements to determine whether a world lies in the habitable zone of a star, where temperatures could allow liquid water to exist on the surface given a suitable atmosphere.

A planet orbiting a cool red dwarf would need to be much closer to its star than Earth is to the Sun to be in this habitable zone. Unfortunately, being in this zone is no guarantee of a planetary paradise.

A planet that sits comfortably in the habitable zone is ultimately still at the mercy of its star. Stars emit more than just the light we see. Ultraviolet radiation and X-rays can interact with planetary atmospheres, altering chemistry and even contributing to the loss of the atmosphere.

Violent stellar flares can produce bursts of high-energy radiation. A planet could offer a wonderfully balmy temperature, only to be bombarded with radiation from the host star.

The age of a star matters too. Young stars tend to be more active, with radiation that could erode a planet’s atmosphere over time. Meanwhile, as stars age and evolve, their changing luminosities can drastically alter the conditions a planet experiences.

For the stranded crew in “Exoplanet”, understanding the star overhead may therefore be as important as knowing the landscape beneath their feet.

Even if a planet has survived all the complications we’ve just thrown at it, there’s still a rather important question: could we actually breathe the air?

In "Exoplanet", one crew member discovers that the planet’s atmosphere contains enough oxygen for him to breathe without his spacesuit. On Earth, oxygen makes up around 21% of our atmosphere. However, finding a similar percentage on another planet wouldn’t automatically mean the air is safe to breathe.

Atmospheric pressure matters just as much. At high altitudes on Earth, the oxygen percentage remains the same, but the lower pressure means less oxygen is available to our lungs.

Oxygen isn’t the only concern, either. The fictional planet’s volcanic landscape releases gases such as sulfur dioxide and hydrogen sulfide, both of which are hazardous at high concentrations. Even an oxygen-rich atmosphere could still be lethal.

But what if you don't just need to know if a planet is habitable? What if you need to know if something already lives there?

Before the Wild Blue Yonder arrived at the planet, probes detected dimethyl sulfide, a molecule we associate with biological activity on Earth. This is a beautifully timely detail, as this very molecule has been at the heart of a scientific debate about one of the most intriguing exoplanets discovered so far.

Astronomers have investigated possible signs of dimethyl sulfide in the atmosphere of K2-18 b, an exoplanet orbiting a red dwarf. However, both the detection itself and the biological interpretation remain heavily disputed.

The challenge is that detecting a single molecule isn’t definitive evidence of life. Its presence can be influenced by factors other than biology, like atmospheric chemistry and stellar radiation.

Understanding whether such a molecule could actually indicate life means studying not just the planet’s atmosphere, but also the radiation it receives from its star.

Astronomers have discovered thousands of exoplanets. Understanding whether any could support life is, however, an extraordinary challenge.

The distance between the planet and the star might give us a good clue, but its atmosphere, geology, and stellar environment are all interconnected in determining whether life could survive. For the unfortunate crew of the Wild Blue Yonder, those distinctions could mean the difference between survival and utter disaster.

We might one day identify a planet with Earth-like temperatures, liquid water, and abundant oxygen. But even then, there’s still a huge gap dividing a potentially habitable planet from one where we could safely remove our helmets.

Harry Kane has answered every critic and extinguished every doubt. At 33 he is a double winner with Bayern Munich, England’s greatest goalscorer, and sits on the Ballon d’Or throne. On Thursday night, Kane will reach another famous milestone when he surpasses Peter Shilton’s tally of England caps and collects his 126th against Croatia in the Nations League at Wembley.

From the academy coaches at Arsenal who released him aged eight, to the Tottenham coaches who didn’t think he could handle first-team football, to the fans who labelled him a one-season wonder and the pundits who prematurely called time on his England career, each served to motivate Kane. You might think he would finally feel satisfied, vindicated, ready to drop his guard, to take his foot off the pedal, to eat a Twix or two.

Far from it. Kane is speaking to a huddle of journalists at Tottenham’s training ground, back where his career began, and insists he still has a point to prove.

“I don’t think that will ever leave me,” he says. “It’s probably part of what makes me push myself to the limits because in football, you’ve never completed it. No matter what you’ve achieved, there is always a trophy that someone else has won or a goal record that someone else has. There’s always something. Whether it’s noise from the outside, media or the fans or whatever it may be, it just helps motivate me to realise there’s a lot more to achieve.”

Where does that come from? How can the unassuming man who stood on stage at the London Palladium last month, nervously twiddling his buttons and repeatedly sweeping away specks of nothing from his pristine tuxedo, who spent his 12-minute speech naming dozens of people who had helped his career, also be a ferociously driven world-class athlete who carries a kind of burning wrath inside him?

“I think it’s just my mindset, because I’ve had to prove people wrong my whole career. From early academy days proving coaches wrong, proving scouts wrong, it has just been instilled in me. If someone tells me I can’t do something it just motivates me more to do it. Of course, you have to combine the mentality with being able to produce it, so that’s where the self-belief and the training and all the consistent work I do has allowed me to do that.

“I’ve spoken about Tom Brady before and that unwavering mentality to be the best. Maybe with me, because I’m a calm person, you don’t see the ego of some of these great players and I’m a bit more relaxed, but it doesn’t mean that I don’t want it as much as everyone else. It doesn’t mean I don’t put in the work and drive myself and drive everyone around me to be the best.

“Maybe getting released that early and proving them wrong and then maybe proving my early coaches at Tottenham [wrong], just bit by bit, loan moves that didn’t go quite well, again, same thing – ‘I’m going to show them’ – using all the negative stuff as motivation to push me to another level.”

There is something else behind Kane’s drive, a different kind of emotion: the dread of leaving a single stone unturned.

“My biggest fear is finishing my career and thinking I could have done a bit more if I could have just done that or done that. I want to make sure that I cross every bridge so that whenever that day does come, I can say I’ve done everything possible, I have this record, I don’t have this record – but it doesn’t matter, I can sleep well at night.”

Kane can still remember arriving at his first England camp, in the spring of 2015, and admits he felt “nervous and anxious” about being the new guy. Wayne Rooney was the England captain and was the first person to talk to the 21-year-old Spurs striker. “He told me to just be myself,” Kane says.

But as soon as he got on the training pitch, Kane felt a confidence flood through his body. “I felt like this is my comfort zone, I know what I can do, I can score goals, I can even talk and shout – I felt more comfortable in that sense. I remember training really well and feeling like, ‘OK, if I can do it here I can do it anywhere’, and that gave me even more confidence. I just took that into the game.”

Kane replaced Rooney in the second half and scored 79 seconds later with his third touch in an England shirt. He would assume the captaincy from Rooney two years later, when Gareth Southgate embarked on an overhaul of the squad.

“I was talking to Michael Olise at Bayern and I was asking how old he was, and he was saying at the time he was 23 and I thought, ‘Jeez, I was [England] captain then’, and I looked at him and he looked so young! So I don’t think I realised in the moment how young I was to be named captain.

“I’ve always been quite mature, even from late teenage years, always led by example, always done the right things. It felt quite natural for me. I’ve been lucky enough to do it for a long time now, and honestly the feeling of being captain still feels as good as that first moment I wore [the armband].”

Eleven years and 125 games after his England debut, Shilton will present Kane with a special cap to commemorate the achievement. It will be a busy pre-match, with Kane also expected to find time to parade the Ballon d’Or to the Wembley crowd.

Even in Kane’s world, it has been an extraordinary, whirlwind few weeks.

“I think I struggle to take it all in to be honest,” he says. “I think it is just my natural mindset to look ahead. The fact that I had just equalled the record [last month], it was, ‘right, next camp, let’s go and break the record’. Maybe tomorrow will feel a little different, actually breaking the record and being the one that sits alone on top.

“But in this period especially when there are so many games, I don’t think I’ve taken it all in. What’s happened in the last few weeks, I don’t think I’ve accepted it all, I guess. But maybe during the winter break when I’m away somewhere, on the beach, it’ll be a moment to reflect and then really soak it all in.”

"Nvidia going down would be a pretty seismic event," she said.

Investors still have high hopes for Nvidia, which reports earnings on Tuesday. Analysts expect its revenue to more than double in the current fiscal year, and then to roughly double again over the following two years, according to LSEG data. But with Huang under scrutiny by the FBI, Congress, and Nvidia's board over his relationship with a woman who The New York Times reported was suspected of spying for China, analysts see a wide range of outcomes as suddenly possible, including the potential removal of Huang, who co-founded the company in 1993.

The company didn't immediately respond to a request for comment. Nvidia's board has expressed confidence in its CEO.

Nvidia's stock fell 30% from its Oct. 6 peak through Tuesday's close, amid a broader reassessment of the AI trade prompted by Democrats' surprising victory in the Nov. 3 midterms. Its stock recovered Wednesday, rising more than 4% as of midday. Still, people who closely track the financial flows underlying AI say that even sharper losses by Nvidia likely wouldn't trigger the kind of systemic problems that regulators guard against.

And yet the risks flowing from AI are keeping some finance officials up at night. They are watching for the possibility that many of the factors that have helped the AI boom could reverse simultaneously. If the value of Nvidia's GPU chips fell, for instance, it could prompt investors to ask whether companies that borrowed money to buy them would be able to pay off their debts.

In that case, the financial assistance big tech firms have extended to others in the industry could suddenly become drags on their growth. Tech companies would have credit problems at the same time that their equity is falling. Problems among giant tech firms wouldn't stay contained to tech investors' portfolios, but could cause problems across the economy.

"I think that's a real concern because both the economy and financial markets are standing on the same leg, which means a drawdown could have reinforcing effects between the two," said Jason Wu, an assistant director at the International Monetary Fund who oversees the Global Markets Analysis unit.

Wu was the lead author of a chapter of the IMF's Global Financial Stability report published in October that warned about the potential for growing leverage among AI firms and investors to trigger broader problems. The so-called hyperscalers — the biggest tech firms — have grown from just 1% of bond issuance in the $15 trillion investment-grade debt market to 10% in just two years. Hedge funds and retail investors are making risky bets even riskier by taking on debt to buy stocks or investing in funds that have embedded leverage.

Nvidia is at the center of all of that. The company has repeatedly stepped in to help backstop smaller AI companies known as neoclouds, which build data centers using Nvidia chips and lease capacity to other firms. Among other obligations, Nvidia disclosed in July that it has committed to buy up to $36 billion in cloud services from those companies. That helps Nvidia ensure that there will be more customers for its AI chips, and it helps those companies find easier financing.

Wu and others refer to these kinds of arrangements as circular financing. When business is growing, it can be productive for bigger firms to help their customers buy their products. But if Nvidia couldn't honor its obligations, it would ripple through the firms that borrowed the money and the banks that lent to it. Relatively opaque private credit funds have also lent heavily into the AI boom, and how they would fare is a subject of debate among regulators.

Nvidia didn't reveal its specific partners, but some neoclouds such as CoreWeave and IREN have separately disclosed their relationships with the bigger firm.

Many neoclouds have been under stress in recent weeks. The cost of insuring CoreWeave debt, as measured by credit-default spreads, rose by 94% — nearly doubling — from Oct. 1 to Nov. 5, before settling, according to LSEG data. The price of an IREN convertible bond maturing in 2029 fell 23% from Nov. 3 to Nov. 5, amid a 26% decline in the company's share price.

Nvidia's other obligations include a $105 billion phased guarantee to a major data center project in Ohio planned by OpenAI and SB Energy, subject to certain conditions. Nvidia promised to backstop up to $125 billion of a $500 billion credit program by Wall Street financial firms.

These obligations come in different forms and are unlikely to come due all at once, if ever. Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh, a Columbia Business School finance professor, estimates them at $400 billion overall.

That is a huge sum, but still a small fraction of the $4 trillion market capitalization Nvidia has even after its stock decline. "The company would have to get to the brink of extinction before all these guarantees come into jeopardy."

"I don't want to say it's a safe system, but I do want to say there's a lot of equity in the system right now," Van Nieuwerburgh said.

But in the past few days, Nvidia's troubles have separated it from the pack. Most neoclouds' equity prices have recovered this week as Nvidia's share price has fallen. That suggests investors see Nvidia's problems as specific to the company, for now.

Meanwhile the basic laws of economics are driving other firms to try to move into Nvidia's lucrative chips business. AMD, Broadcom, Intel, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI all have efforts underway to build their own AI chips, Van Nieuwerburgh said.

Over time, they could replace Nvidia's products. That could cap the economic fallout of any potential troubles at the company.

And yet it's impossible to entirely write off the financial risks of the AI trade suddenly hitting a wall, added Van Nieuwerburgh, who spoke to me over Zoom from the sidelines of a bimonthly meeting of finance officials in Basel, Switzerland.

Central bankers "are clearly very, very, very anxious about this and thinking hard about these questions," he said.

Missouri-based BJC Health and St. Luke's Hospital are planning a merger.

The providers announced on Wednesday that they have signed a letter of intent to come together, bringing the community hospital into the fold with the Show Me State's largest health system. The pair expects the deal to improve access and improve local care in the St. Louis region.

St. Luke's said in the announcement that the letter "follows a deliberate and thorough evaluation process," conducted by its board of directors and leaders "to identify the best path forward for St. Luke’s and the communities it serves."

The providers said they intend to spend the next several months building a definitive agreement to merge and complete the needed regulatory approval process. In the interim, St. Luke's patients can continue to see their regular physicians.

BJC said in the announcement that it is committed to supporting local, community care while also allowing St. Luke's to tap into the resources available across its ecosystem. It intends to provide key support for the hospital's clinical technology and equipment as well as backing infrastructure and facility upgrades and provider recruitment and retention efforts.

St. Luke’s is a well-respected hospital, known for clinical quality, patient experience and the personal attention given to everyone in our care,” said Andy Bagnall, CEO of St. Luke’s, in the announcement. “At the same time, the demands of health care delivery and our communities’ needs are changing rapidly."

We believe that together with BJC, we can strengthen patient care in St. Louis in ways we could not easily do on our own," Bagnall said.

Nick Barto, CEO of BJC Health, called St. Luke's "a trusted leader in our region with a reputation for excellence."

"Together, we will build on that foundation through our aligned clinical strengths and complementary services," Barto said in the announcement. "Our shared goal is to expand access to differentiated, high-quality care while ensuring St. Luke’s can continue serving the growing communities of West St. Louis for generations to come.”
