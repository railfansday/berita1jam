10 Magical Christmas Traditions Around the World

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Living far away from family is hard at the best of times, but it becomes particularly difficult around the holidays. If there's one thing that makes it a little easier for me, it's being able to mix and match festive traditions from different countries to create something that's uniquely ours. As an Italian living in France with a British husband, I love bringing together the best of each culture for a rather extravagant Christmas. On Christmas Eve, we have oysters and Champagne, as per French tradition, followed by an Italian feast and plenty of Christmas crackers—although I draw the line at Christmas pudding, preferring a simple pandoro over anything else.

Whether, like me, you spend Christmas far from your family, are planning to chase the sun this December or are driving home for the holidays, one of the great joys of Christmas is making memories and indulging in nostalgic traditions. All over the globe, different countries and cultures celebrate the season in different ways, marking the holiday with long-established rites and rituals or merging heritage to create beautiful new traditions. Below, we run through some of our favourite Christmas traditions around the world, and ask the Traveller editors about their own personal festive rituals. From seafood feasts on Christmas Day in Australia to surfing Santa’s taking to the waves in SoCal, these are the most wholesome traditions for some festive joy this season.

Christmas is not recognized as a national holiday in Japan, but the festive season is still celebrated—albeit slightly differently from how you’d expect. As part of a marketing campaign launched in 1974, a few years after KFC first opened in Japan, the brand came up with the slogan ‘Kentucky for Christmas’, and it stuck. On Christmas Day, many Japanese families tuck into a full feast of fried chicken—a tradition so popular that you often have to order weeks in advance to make sure you’ve got your bucket guaranteed for the big day. The original Christmas meal even came with a bottle of wine. Christmas cake is just as surprising in Japan, too—strawberry shortcake, made with layers of sponge, whipped cream and fresh strawberries, is a festive favourite.

As you might imagine, food is at the heart of Christmas celebrations in Italy. Every region has its own traditions and dishes, enjoyed on Christmas Eve, Christmas Day and throughout the festive season. While northern Italians might tuck into tortellini in broth and other comforting dishes, in the south we go all out. In my family, we have twelve different dishes—mostly fish-based—served for Christmas Eve dinner, before indulging in traditional desserts and an unholy amount of nuts while playing tombola. If there is one tradition that the whole country shares, it's the rather quirky tradition of La Befana – a witch-like old woman who flies through the skies on a broomstick on the night of January 5, leaving gifts for children to discover on the morning of 6 January, known as Epiphany (Epifania in Italian). Well-behaved children receive sweets and presents, while naughty ones get coal – although these days it's usually sweet, sugary charcoal, which is actually delicious.

The real difference between the Scottish festive season and that of much of the rest of the world is Hogmanay. Once Christmas Day is done and dusted, the focus is on New Year’s—or, as the Scots call it, Hogmanay. Street parties in cities such as Edinburgh roll on until the early hours, Robert Burns’ Auld Lang Syne is sung at midnight, and the ‘first-footing’ custom takes place—the first visitor to a home after midnight is believed to bring good luck for the year ahead (along with traditional gifts such as whisky, shortbread or a lump of coal, of course). Traditionally, a tall, dark-haired man is considered the luckiest first-footer.

While much of the Northern Hemisphere is wrapping up against the cold, Christmas in Australia falls in the middle of summer, meaning festive celebrations often revolve around the beach, sunshine and outdoor gatherings. "The traditional Christmas lunch in Australia is usually seafood, a roast ham served cold, lots of salads and then a pavlova for dessert," explains Lauren Burvill, CNT’s senior commerce writer and resident Aussie. "After lunch the classic traditions are to play cricket in the backyard, or if you're lucky, on the beach. It's not unusual to see Santa on the beaches as well, often riding on an inflatable rescue boat from the local surf club. On Boxing Day the country's favourite pastime is to watch the cricket or the Sydney to Hobart yacht race."

Forget snowy landscapes and cosy fireplaces—in Southern California, you might spot Santa Claus swapping his sleigh for a surfboard during the festive season. "I grew up in greater Los Angeles, and though December isn't typically beach weather, something uncanny almost always happens on Christmas," explains Megan Spurrell, Conde Nast Traveler US’s associate articles director. "The temperatures spike to the mid-70s Fahrenheit with shining sun and many families, including my own, end up at the beach. But we also go to look out for a very SoCal figure, known as Surfing Santa. We keep our eyes out at Manhattan and Hermosa Beach, especially near the piers, though I've heard of surfing santas in Malibu and Santa Monica, too. Most are just local dudes with a Santa suit who reliably hit the waves on Christmas morning to entertain their neighbours (and get a surf session in)."

While many Western traditions see Santa leaving lumps of coal in stockings for badly behaved children, in Austria it's the terrifying Krampus who comes to punish them. As the folklore goes, the Krampus is a fearsome horned figure with a long, snake-like tongue, who accompanies Saint Nicholas on 5 December, otherwise known as Krampusnacht, the eve of Saint Nicholas Day. While Saint Nicholas rewards well-behaved children with sweets and small gifts, Krampus is said to frighten or punish those who have been naughty. Today, the tradition is celebrated with Krampuslauf parades, during which people dressed in elaborate horned masks and furry costumes take to the streets, particularly in Alpine towns and villages. The folklore even inspired a Christmas comedy horror film in 2015, starring Adam Scott and Toni Collette.

In Peru, Christmas Eve, known as Nochebuena, is the main event, with families gathering for a festive dinner and celebrations that often continue well past midnight. "We alternate years of spending Christmas in Southern California with my mom and sisters, and going to Lima, Peru, with my husband's family" says Megan. "Though Peru has plenty of traditions tied to Christmas, perhaps my favourite is the sheer fact (shared in many Catholic and Latin American countries) that the whole affair begins at midnight on the 24th. It can be torture waiting until midnight proper to dig into the feast of turkey and arroz arabe that sits out waiting, but the entire city explodes in fireworks when the clock strikes 12. And then the party doesn't stop until the wee hours of the morning, sometimes at a family member's house or at the city's bars and clubs. It almost feels like a preview of New Year's Eve, just a few days earlier."

Dumplings are a major part of Polish cuisine day to day, and Christmas is no exception. The traditional Christmas Eve supper, known as Wigilia, begins when the first star appears in the sky, with families gathering around the Christmas table for a feast that traditionally includes 12 meatless dishes. "My favourite part of seasonal eating has never been the Great British Christmas Roast, but instead 'Polish Christmas', celebrated by my family on Christmas Eve, a haphazard tribute to the Polish side of my family tree," says Olivia Squire, Traveller's features editor. "When I was a child, my grandmother used to make pierogi (Polish dumplings filled with mashed potato and cottage cheese) from scratch, which in more recent years has been taken over by my mum and I and usually involves a lot of swearing and tears. It's all worth it when we sit down to eat them alongside a vegetarian version of bigos (hunter's stew—an insult to my meat-loving ancestors), breaded fish and holy wafers, soundtracked by Carol's at King's, my English dad's addition."

Forget spending a fortune on Christmas presents—in Germany, the tradition of Schrottwichteln puts a rather amusing twist on exchanging gifts, with friends and family swapping unwanted, bizarre, or downright useless objects instead.

"For gifts, we follow the German tradition of Schrottwichteln (literally 'junk Secret Santa'), where everyone brings something completely useless, unwanted or gloriously ridiculous, often something they were given once and never knew what to do with. The more pointless, tacky or absurd, the better," Karin Mueller, Traveller's visuals director, tells us. "Our children have grown up with this ritual, and what began as a cosy dinner has become the highlight of the year. They never miss it. Now that they’re young adults, they bring their partners along, and it’s turned into this wonderfully chaotic, joyful, very-London celebration that I’m pretty sure they’ll keep going long after we stop hosting."

Across England, Christmas is celebrated with familiar traditions, from exchanging gifts to tucking into mince pies and singing Christmas carols. But each county and neighbourhood has its own customs, too. In some seaside towns, brave locals take part in a Boxing Day dip—sprinting into the freezing sea to shake off the Christmas excesses, often while raising money for charity. There's also the age-old custom of burning a Yule log, traditionally associated with good fortune and protection from misfortune. In Cornwall, some communities gather in local pubs for a traditional Cornish ‘shout’, joining together to sing folk songs and sea shanties.

"In the hazy betwixmas period, the days of lounging on the sofa and gorging on Terry’s chocolate orange are punctuated by one magical evening at a local pub for a Cornish 'shout'", explains Lucy Bruton, CNT's social media manager. "A shout refers to the community coming together to sing sea shanties and folk songs in the pub, and the Lerryn (where my family live in Cornwall) shout is a particularly special one to me. The tiny pub feels packed to the eaves with people of all ages belting out some of my favourite Cornish songs – from "Trelawney" to "Lamorna" and, of course, “Cornwall My Home”. It’s dark, cosy and a real slice of the magic of Cornwall."

After a midterm election that saw the Democrats take control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate on Tuesday night, President Donald Trump spent Wednesday ignoring the results and instead stumping for his $400 million plan to build a ballroom for the White House.

Trump’s ballroom has been controversial from the start, not only because he demolished the White House’s East Wing to make room for it but also because he said it would include a military complex beneath the structure. A series of challenges, both from past presidents and US courts, have questioned whether Trump even has the authority to undertake such a project. Whether Trump would be able to fully proceed with the ballroom plan under a Democratic-majority House and Senate remains an open question. King Ghidorah's Origin, Powers, & Godzilla Movie History Explained

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The release of Godzilla Minus Zero will further solidify King Ghidorah's status as the greatest villain in the history of the Godzilla franchise. Hitting theaters worldwide on November 5, the upcoming sequel marks the ninth time a version of the three-headed golden dragon has butted heads with Godzilla in live-action. Ghidorah already had a richer history than all other Godzilla villains, with Mechagodzilla being the only one that came remotely close.

Over the last several decades, Godzilla has accumulated an impressive rogues' gallery made up of a diverse lineup of monsters. He's saved humanity from a giant lobster, a prehistoric dragonfly, all sorts of aliens, and even beings created from his own DNA. And some, like King Ghidorah, had to be defeated more than once. Because of his popularity, the monster has been revisited on multiple occasions, across numerous continuities. As a result, there are several different versions of King Ghidorah in the overarching Godzilla franchise.

Most movies that have included him maintain a high degree of consistency in terms of how he's portrayed. However, the King Ghidorah character is incredibly nuanced, much like Godzilla himself. Some films have offered different explanations about his origins and what his powers are, thus handing a level of flexibility to Godzilla Minus Zero for its take on the King of the Monsters' archenemy.

Although far from the first monster to fight Godzilla, he served as an early opponent to the King of the Monsters, fighting him for the first time in Ghidorah, the Three-Headed Monster. Released in 1964 as the fifth entry into the franchise and a direct sequel to Godzilla vs. Mothra, the movie used King Ghidorah to faciliate Godzilla's first-of-many team-ups in his cinematic history.

In the movie, Mothra has to convince Godzilla and Rodan to stop fighting and join forces to drive away King Ghidorah, an incredibly powerful alien monster who wiped out the alien race living on Venus and has since set his sights on Earth. Following Ghidorah defeat at the hands of Godzilla and Rodan, the villain returned for a rematch with the same two monsters in 1965's Invasion of Astro Monster.

In Destroy All Monsters, Ghidorah had an even more difficult path ahead of him when he had to fight Godzilla, Rodan, Mothra, plus an assortment of other kaiju. His next role, though, which came in 1972's Godzilla vs. Gigan, evened the odds by having Godzilla and his ally, Anguirus, square off against Ghidorah and Gigan.

19 years passed before Ghidorah was utilized again. The Godzilla franchise was rebooted in 1984 with a darker, less kid-friendly feel. Several of his allies and villains were gradually reintroduced into the franchise, with Godzilla vs. King Ghidorah giving Godzilla and Ghidorah their first one-on-one fight in 1991. The movie also famously gave Ghidorah a new mechanized upgrade, courtesy of the human characters - Mecha-King Ghidorah.

Ghidorah's next two movie appearances were significant departures from the past. Ghidorah filled a heroic role in Godzilla, Mothra, and King Ghidorah: Giant Monsters All-Out Attack, a movie in which Godzilla was the true villain. In Godzilla: Final Wars, Toho introduced Keizer Ghidorah, who wasn't technically King Ghidorah himself, but a considerably more powerful member of the same species.

Legendary's Monsterverse tried its hand at Ghidorah by making the villain of the 2019 Hollywood production, Godzilla: King of the Monsters. Recreating the monster cast of Ghidorah, the Three-Headed Monster by featuring Godzilla, Rodan, Mothra, and Ghidorah, the film saw the latter attempt to terraform the planet. Naturally, it was up to Godzilla to stop him.

Nearly every movie appearance from Ghidorah - including his first and only Hollywood film - has defined Ghidorah roughly the same way. Ghidorah, the Three-Headed Monster established Ghidorah as an alien threat, an origin that every movie except Godzilla, Mothra, and King Ghidorah: Giant Monsters All-Out Attack has kept. Being a film where Ghidorah was characterized as one of three benevolent guardians of the Earth, it remains an outlier in regards to the kaiju's portrayal.

Typically, his alien origins are important to the plot, such as when aliens controlled him in Invasion of Astro Monster and Destroy All Monsters. Legendary's King of the Monsters, for its part, didn't discuss it at length but heavily implied that his nature as an extraterrestrial being was the catalyst behind the destructive path he carved.

One particular movie went much deeper with his origin story. In Godzilla vs. King Ghidorah's telling of it, he doesn't begin the story as "King Ghidorah." Rather than exist as a singular entity, Ghidorah was originally a multitude of harmless, golden dragon-like creatures called Dorats. Hailing from a distant planet, the Dorats were collectively transformed into King Ghiodrah when time travel was used to make them take the place of Godzilla at the moment that he was to be bombarded with nuclear radiation during World War 2.

Similar to the situation with his origins, depictions of King Ghidorah's powers are largely consistent as well. Each of Ghidorah's three heads are capable of firing blasts of yellow lightning known as "gravity beams." They're not as powerful as Godzilla's atomic breath, but through continuous use of his gravity beams from all three heads, Ghidorah can indeed use them to inflict serious pain on Godzilla.

Godzilla: Final Wars' central villain, Keizer Ghidorah, possesses a stronger variation of the gravity beam called Destroyed Thunder, which actually allowed him to manipulate gravity.

Just as it is with Godzilla and his atomic breath, the gravity beams are the signature weapon of King Ghidorah. But he has other ways of fighting Godzilla too, such as his ability to fly. He can scoop up kaiju with his talons and has enough physical strength to carry them into the air before dropping them from extreme heights.

As for how dangerous that makes him, that tends to vary. Even though Godzilla had help on his side in Ghidorah's first three movie appearances, it was never proven that he wouldn't have been able to beat him alone. And Godzilla vs. Gigan did demonstrate that Godzilla could win a fair fight against him. The same was true of the 1991 and 2001 versions of Ghidorah seen in Godzilla vs. King Ghidorah and Godzilla, Mothra, and King Ghidorah respectively.

Based on how his fights with Godzilla typically went, King Ghidorah is an incredibly powerful Godzilla villain, but not quite as strong as Destoroyah or SpaceGodzilla. But his relative, Keizer Ghidorah, has a strong case for being the most powerful Godzilla villain of all time. In any case, there's every reason to expect an intense matchup before watching Godzilla Minus Zero.

Meta acquires AI coding startup Conductor, whose app runs several coding agents at once

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Meta Platforms Inc. has acquired artificial intelligence coding startup Conductor in a deal first reported today by Axios. Conductor confirmed it is joining Meta in a post on its website. Terms were not disclosed.

Conductor’s software lets a developer run several coding agents at once, each in an isolated copy of the codebase, and then review and merge what they produce. Supported agents include Anthropic PBC’s Claude Code, OpenAI Group PBC’s Codex and Space Exploration Technologies Corp.’s Cursor, and users pay for them through their own subscriptions and keys. The desktop app runs on Macs. A hosted version, Conductor Cloud, puts agents in sandboxed virtual machines where they can keep working for hours, and users can check on them from an iPhone app.

Engineers at Meta were using Conductor well before the deal. When the startup announced its Series A in March, it named Meta, Google LLC and Spotify Technology SA among the companies with dozens of engineers on the product. Conductor now says on its website that more than 100,000 people use it.

People familiar with the deal told Axios that Meta expects the Conductor team’s speed at shipping products to help it build its next generation of AI coding tools. The acquisition follows two Meta model releases last month that both pitched coding as a strength. On Oct. 15, Meta released Muse Spark Open, an open-weights version of its Muse Spark model aimed at coding and long-running agent work. Meta’s more capable Muse Wonder models stay closed inside its own Muse Code terminal and application programming interface.

The Conductor deal landed the same day OpenAI said it plans to acquire developer tools startup Pierre Computer. OpenAI had already announced plans in June to buy Ona, which runs coding agents in cloud sandboxes.

Local copies of the Mac app keep working until early 2027, when the team expects to release a new interface it has not yet described. New models and critical bug fixes stop arriving at that point, and Conductor will delete the app’s user data. The company said it has never sold Conductor user data or trained models on it, and does not plan to.

Chief Executive Charlie Holtz and Chief Technology Officer Jackson de Campos founded the company, formally Melty Inc., in 2024. Conductor raised a $22 million Series A in March from Spark Capital and Matrix. Y Combinator and the founders of Notion and Linear are also backers.

Why Opendoor Stock Jumped 13.8% Today

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Opendoor jumped as a Democratic sweep pushed oil and Treasury yields lower, but this is a rate-sensitive stock with a model I'm still skeptical of.

Opendoor Technologies (OPEN +13.79%) stock finished Wednesday, Nov. 4, 2026, up 13.8% after a Democratic sweep in the midterms set off a rally in housing stocks.

The S&P 500 (^GSPC +1.58%) and the Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC +1.44%) finished up 1.7% and 1.5%, respectively.

Democrats took both chambers of Congress on Tuesday night in a sweeping victory. The party has said that affordability is a major policy priority, including the cost of housing.

Beyond that, the results led oil prices lower as investors hoped for a quicker resolution to the Iran war now that Democrats control the legislature. That could help cool inflation, which in turn may mean long-term Treasury yields retreat -- exactly what the 10-year Treasury yield did today.

Opendoor is in the business of buying and selling homes, so the more active the housing market is, the better the company's sales. If yields move lower, mortgage rates follow suit, which typically means more buying and selling.

Opendoor also sits on a large, financed inventory. Higher rates eat into its bottom line as it is forced to pay more in interest.

The company reports Q3 results after the close on Thursday, with Wall Street looking for sales growth of about 17%. We'll see if they deliver.

Regardless, I'm still skeptical of the model long term. I'd be careful reading too much into today.

Money you invest in your 20s has about 40 years to grow, so small amounts go far. Contribute enough to your 401(k) to get the full employer match, open a Roth IRA while your tax rate is low, and invest both accounts mostly in stocks through a target-date or broad stock index fund.

Starting early can matter more than the amount you put in, since money you invest at 25 gets 10 more years to compound than money you invest at 35.

A Roth IRA suits your 20s. You pay income tax on what you put in at today’s rate, and in retirement the money and its growth come out tax-free. That trade pays off most when your tax rate is low, and on a first salary it’s likely the lowest it will be in your career.

With about 40 years until retirement, you can hold mostly stocks, which swing more than bonds but have historically grown more. If the market drops 30% when you’re 25, every contribution buys shares for 30% less while prices stay down, and you won’t need to sell for decades. Keep buying through it.

Take the steps in this order. First, get the full match, the money your employer adds when you contribute, as soon as your plan allows. Build a starter emergency fund, a small cash cushion for surprise bills, at the same time. Next, pay off any high-interest debt, such as a credit card balance. Then, if your income is under the IRS’s ceiling for Roth contributions, open a Roth IRA and contribute up to the yearly limit, $7,500. Anything past that goes back into your 401(k).

In both accounts, pick a low-cost target-date fund named for the year you plan to retire. It holds mostly stocks while that year is far off and shifts toward bonds as it nears. A broad stock index fund, which owns small slices of hundreds or thousands of companies, works too.

At a hypothetical 7% average annual return, raising your contributions from 6% to 10% of a $50,000 salary adds $2,000 a year that grows to about $400,000 over 40 years. So each time your pay goes up, save another 1% or 2% of it until you and your employer together put in 15% of your pay.

This Genius Trick Makes Perfectly Portioned Cinnamon Rolls—No Ruler or Guessing Required

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This clever little move turns your knife into a measuring guide for cinnamon rolls.

I have no objection to a shortcut in the kitchen. I’m happy to let boxed cake mix handle dessert or a great jar of pasta sauce get dinner moving. But I just can’t make peace with cinnamon rolls from a can. I want to love them. I appreciate the convenience, and I understand the appeal of having warm cinnamon rolls before the coffee is finished brewing. They just never quite satisfy that craving for a warm, deliciously gooey cinnamon roll.

Some viral upgrades make a compelling case—particularly the heavy cream bath and generous additions of butter and brown sugar. But my long-standing love for cinnamon rolls has much more to do with the soft, pillowy dough, so I’m willing to wait through a rise or two.

Having committed to that, though, I’m still interested in anything that makes the process easier. So when I saw a video of someone flipping a knife over a log of cinnamon roll dough to mark perfectly even portions, I wanted to try it. Cutting equal slices by eye can involve a surprising amount of second-guessing when you’d really just like to get them in the pan. I made our classic cinnamon roll recipe and put the trick to the test.

The clever part of this trick is that the knife becomes its own measurement. Rather than judging the thickness of each slice, you use the width of the blade to mark equal portions along the dough roll before slicing it into pieces.

Once your dough is filled and rolled into a log, gently lay the blade flat across the top at one end, with the knife running crosswise over the log. Then flip it onto its opposite flat side, moving along the dough one blade-width at a time. It looks a little like the knife is doing small somersaults, with its edge leaving a light indentation at each turn.

Continue down the log, using just enough pressure to leave visible marks. Then go back and slice through the dough at those marks.

This worked beautifully with our classic cinnamon rolls, but the same technique would work with just about any cinnamon roll recipe. The turning motion was simple, and the knife left an evenly spaced cutting guide along the dough. Once the marks were there, I could slice the rolls without measuring each one or estimating where the next cut should go.

I particularly liked being able to see all the portions before I started cutting. It made the job feel settled: The spacing was already worked out, and all I had to do was follow it. For something that takes so little effort, it’s a useful bit of precision.

This is the kind of cinnamon roll shortcut I can get behind. I still get the homemade rolls I wanted, but there’s one less thing to fuss over before the pan goes into the oven.

Other spending failed to deliver the intended results, both in primaries and in November. The Fairshake network’s roughly $10 million spent opposing Juliana Stratton in the Illinois Democratic primary failed to block her nomination. She later won the Senate seat. In Massachusetts, John Deaton lost despite roughly $700,000 in spending by crypto-backed Commonwealth Unity Fund to help elect him. Ken Paxton, who received crypto PAC backing in his successful Texas Republican runoff against John Cornyn, went on to lose to James Talarico.

The House brought another setback when digital assets subcommittee chair Bryan Steil lost to Democrat Mitchell Berman despite $1.9 million in crypto PAC funding.

The shift in congressional control could slow crypto legislation, but BitGo CEO and co-founder Mike Belshe said the election results would not alter the industry’s trajectory. “Crypto has continued to move forward under different administrations and different political majorities. There’s an attempt to single out some particularly great classic rock albums below, all of them worth listening to before you die. Or, less morbidly, listen to them all while you're still breathing; before the bucket’s kicked. That’s still pretty morbid. Sorry. But these albums are amazing, and enough so that you’ve probably heard most of them before, but if you haven’t, they're essential to give a spin at least once before… you know…

It feels uncool to call them Creedence Clearwater Revival instead of just “Creedence,” but either way, here's Cosmo’s Factory, which is probably the band’s best album. Creedence (yeah, let’s be cool for a bit; for fun) is entirely classic rock in sound, really being demonstrative of this particular era of rock, and so sure, Cosmo’s Factory is blunt, certainly straightforward, and pretty easy to listen to quite passively.

Sometimes it’s just what you need, though? Something loud, catchy, and very of its time, but all of those things in good ways. Cosmo’s Factory is strong enough that you might not love Creedence, or the particular kind of rock they made, but you're still likely going to, at the very least, tolerate this particular album. And if you are fond of classic rock and/or Creedence, then it’s safe to say you're going to go well beyond tolerating this, since you'll probably love it.

If there’s one flaw with Damn the Torpedoes, it’s that it starts in a way strong enough that you could argue the momentum isn't entirely made, yet that’s a nitpick, since the album after the first three tracks is still strong. It’s more just that the run from “Refugee” to “Here Comes My Girl” to “Even the Losers” is especially great, with three high-quality songs on their own all feeling perfectly sequenced, and thereby made even better.

But Damn the Torpedoes is not a long album, so once you're three tracks down, there are only six to go, with “Don't Do Me Like That” and “Louisiana Rain” among those remaining songs. Tom Petty and the Heartbreakers had a knack for pumping out punchy rock music early on, so the two albums before Damn the Torpedoes and the one following it are also great, yet it’s Damn the Torpedoes that’s the most consistent and memorable of the bunch.

Make no mistake: The Rolling Stones had a great run throughout a good chunk of the 1960s, but there’s an argument to be made that it was their 1970s run that demonstrated the band at their strongest. Sticky Fingers is especially strong, and the one being singled out here, but it wouldn’t be wise to overlook the 1960s, and then ‘80s-wise, the band was still going fairly strong there, even if their music in that decade wasn’t quite as “classic rock” in sound.

Sticky Fingers is great, and most of it holds up incredibly well over half a century later.

Anyway, Sticky Fingers was the album that helped demonstrate The Rolling Stones could well be the new kings of rock, making a great start for the whole decade, right around a time when another legendary British band had broken up, leaving the future of rock temporarily uncertain, to some extent. More on that other band later, by the way. But yes, Sticky Fingers is great, and most of it holds up incredibly well over half a century on from its initial release.

Easily one of the best double albums of all time, Electric Ladyland is probably The Jimi Hendrix Experience’s greatest album, which is a big take, even without too many albums by the band. It comes down to all those albums being phenomenal and influential, and it’s just the case that Electric Ladyland, with its size and also all the wild things it’s going for – and successfully trying to cram into – a single album.

Electric Ladyland is still such a wonderful and strange album to dive into, even today, with it coming up on nearly six decades since it first came out (safe to assume it would've been even more mind-blowing to hear back in 1968). There’s an undeniably interesting atmosphere to the album overall, and it’s surprisingly consistent, all the while saving two of the best songs until the very end: a cover of Bob Dylan’s “All Along the Watchtower” and then “Voodoo Child (Slight Return).”

This spot could’ve easily gone to Who’s Next, which has the singles and perhaps more historical/cultural importance, so consider that an honorable mention for that possibly easier pick (the one that’s slightly more straightforward, not to mention potentially simpler to praise). But Quadrophenia is even better, and maybe ever so slightly underrated, being like Electric Ladyland in the way it sustains itself for twice as long as a normal album, but then it’s also more of a concept album.

Broadly, Quadrophenia is about struggling to fit in while growing up and dealing with intense isolation, and lots of what it tackles lyrically still packs a punch. The story might not be as comprehensible as was the case with The Who’s Tommy, but it doesn’t really matter when there’s so much to feel throughout Quadrophenia, since it’s very emotional, cathartic at times, and then also a bit intense at times thanks to how full-on the introspection gets at other points.

Bruce Springsteen had a remarkable run throughout the 1970s and 1980s, since it’s that period of time that saw the release of the majority of his all-time great albums. There are albums after that era that are still pretty great, but then again, it’s also hard for anything to be close to Born to Run in terms of quality. And even compared to the other Springsteen albums of those two decades, Born to Run is special.

It does a lot to condense everything great about Springsteen into just eight largely phenomenal tracks, and while they'd all be worth mentioning, it’s especially worth mentioning the title track (an all-timer), “Thunder Road,” and “Jungleland,” the former kicking off Born to Run expertly, and the latter being one essentially perfect closing track. Everything here works, and it’s 40-ish perfect minutes of classic rock, in the end.

It says something about how great Led Zeppelin were, as a band, that you could probably put an album of theirs without “Stairway to Heaven” in a ranking like this and it would still feel right. The first half-dozen albums are all very hard to fault, but among greatness, it’s probably Led Zeppelin IV that well and truly is the greatest, and sure, “Stairway to Heaven” helps, but the other songs here are also fantastic.

Technically, this album’s untitled, but it’s easier to call it Led Zeppelin IV after the band put out three self-titled albums in the couple of years before Led Zeppelin IV. There’s also a great variety of tracks here, with some not being particularly rock-heavy, but then other parts do indeed get very energetic and loud. The slower songs make for a nice contrast, and Led Zeppelin IV transitions from song to song incredibly well, with the pacing and overall flow being impeccable.

There are arguments to be made for several other albums by The Beatles that aren’t Abbey Road to be worthy of being called the best, but it’s Abbey Road that feels like a culmination of the band’s entire run. No shade to those other albums, though, since the band was remarkably consistent and boundary-pushing, especially through the last half of the 1960s (see also Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band, the self-titled or "White" Album, Revolver, Rubber Soul, and Magical Mystery Tour, if you want to count that one).

Abbey Road has some all-time great tracks on it, and the whole thing flows incredibly well, too, especially all those short songs in the second half that make up a medley. It’s got almost everything great about The Beatles packed into one album, and is also significant for being the final one the band recorded, even if Let It Be, largely recorded earlier, was the last album of the band’s to be released (and the only one that didn’t come out in the 1960s, since it dropped in 1970).

Pink Floyd is known for making albums that you can really get lost in, and The Dark Side of the Moon is especially dense and immersive in a mostly good way. “Mostly” because this album is pretty heavy-going at times, so maybe it’s not for every mood, yet it’s for a lot of them, and it is overall about life quite broadly, helping it be a little easier to get into than some of the band’s other great (and also ambitious) rock albums.

So, it’s great within Pink Floyd’s discography, and then when judged on its own, The Dark Side of the Moon is still phenomenal and everything. It’s just such a quintessential album – rock or otherwise – that it’s hard to know how to even praise it or convince someone who’s yet to listen to it that they really need to get on it. Also, it’s probably the kind of thing almost everyone’s heard at this point, so maybe it’s an odd recommendation for present purposes. Still, uh, maybe re-listen to it before you die? Yeah, there we go. That works.

If you want to find great David Bowie songs, you can go as far back as the 1960s, since “Space Oddity” came out then and is an obvious classic, but if you want essential albums of his, it’s best to start in the 1970s. He kept going beyond that decade, of course, with his final album (Blackstar) even being a masterpiece, but the most masterful of his masterpieces is probably The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars.

And since no one has really ever done it better than Bowie, why not also call it the best classic rock album of all time? The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars is just too good, with everything being perfect to the extent that it’s hard to know what else to say. Why bother saying anything when you can just listen? Thankfully, The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars never really gets old, either.
