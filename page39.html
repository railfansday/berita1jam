Senegal re-crowned Afcon champions after appeal upheld

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Senegal were stripped of their Afcon title for staging a walk-off and Morocco will appeal the latest ruling

Senegal have been re-crowned the 2025 Africa Cup of Nations champions after the Court of Arbitration for Sport (Cas) unanimously upheld their appeal against the decision to strip them of their title on Tuesday.

Senegal contested Confederation of African Football's (Caf) decision to declare Morocco champions, which wiped the Teranga Lions’ title from the records despite victory in January’s Afcon final 1-0 after extra time in Rabat.

The final was fraught with controversy after Senegal staged a walk-off in protest to a penalty was awarded against them in the dying stages of normal time, with players ordered into the dressing room by manager Pape Thiaw, where they remained for 14 minutes.

Play was eventually resumed before Morocco’s Brahim Diaz missed the spot-kick, sailing a tame ‘Panenka’ effort into the arms of Edouard Mendy. Senegal went on to win the match in extra time through Pape Gueye.

However, they were stripped of the title two months later after Caf’s Appeal Board ruled that Senegal had forfeited ⁠the match by walking off the pitch, overturning the result and awarding a 3-0 ​victory to ⁠Morocco.

Senegal quickly followed with an appeal to sport's highest court, Cas, which was lodged on 25 March and have now successfully fought their case to be reinstated as champions, with the original 1-0 result restored.

The three-man Cas ‌panel, presided over by French sports law ​expert Gerald Simon with assistance from ‌Italian international law professor ⁠Luigi Fumagalli and Hamburg-based ⁠attorney Ulrich Haas, ‌unanimously found that referee Jean-Jacques Ndala “did not commit any error of law”, said Cas secretary general Matthieu Reeb.

Ndala’s decision to resume the match after Senegal’s walk-off fell within his sole authority and was therefore covered by the “field of play” doctrine, something Morocco’s federation argued was not the case.

The court published its reasoning in a press release issued on Tuesday afternoon, which read: “By blowing his whistle to restart play, the referee considered that the conditions for resuming the match had been met – both teams were on the pitch again, and both expressed their willingness to resume.

“In the panel’s view, there is no doubt that the effects of the referee’s decision were limited to the pitch.”

The panel clarified that Articles 82 and 84 of Caf’s tournament regulations, cited in the Moroccan federation’s appeal, apply only to determining match results if team conduct forces a referee to permanently call off a game, and were therefore not applicable in this instance.

"In conclusion, the panel considers that in resuming the match, the referee did not err in law," a court press release added.

“The panel acknowledged that the referee had to make a decision in an atmosphere of extreme tension. However, these circumstances do not justify describing the decision as 'arbitrary'. On the contrary, the referee's judgment did not appear to have been affected by factors external to the game."

Cas noted that disciplinary action has already been taken against then-Senegal boss Thiaw, the Senegalese federation and certain players following separate proceedings by Caf.

Senegal took to social media to celebrate the verdict, posting a picture of the team lifting the trophy with the caption, “Encore et toujours champions d’Afrique” - which translates to, “Champions of Africa, once again and always”.

“The Senegalese Football Federation welcomes this sentence with deep satisfaction,” a federation statement read.

“Far from consecrating the preeminence of one party over another, this decision affirms above all the triumph of sporting fairness and recalls a cardinal principle: the truth of a match is established exclusively on the field, under the protection of sports law.

“On this solemn occasion, the FSF wishes to express its deep gratitude to the Lions of Teranga and their management, to its lawyers and advisors, to the President of the Republic and the Senegalese Government for their support, as well as to all supporters, both in Senegal and in the diaspora.”

The Moroccan federation (FRMF) released a statement confirming its intention to “pursue all available legal avenues” to appeal the decision.

The federation alleged there had been “communication” between a member of the Cas panel and the president of the Senegalese federation 48 hours before the decision was issued.

The FRMF said these practices would “compromise the integrity and credibility of the arbitration procedure”.

Cas has been contacted for comment regarding this allegation.

The FRMF added that the decision “did not take into account the approach recently adopted by both the International Football Association Board (IFAB) and Fifa, regarding players leaving the field of play without the referee’s permission, and the resulting disciplinary responsibilities and penalties”.

Cas initially promised a decision within days of the 8 October in-person hearing in Lausanne but then decided to issue a complete ruling, including legal grounds, known as a Cas Award - a process that requires several weeks.

Cas Awards hold the same value as a national court judgement, enforceable in accordance with the New York Convention on the Recognition ‌and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards.

The full Cas Award will be published in due course.

Chasing the Camino Coast

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

With nearly 14 miles to hike today, we started early at 6:30 AM.

Because Vigo is such a massive port city, our morning walk began right along the waterfront. We passed a mile-long fish distribution facility where workers offloaded fresh seafood from ships into warehouses before loading it onto smaller delivery vans.

Standing guard near the loading zone was a man carrying a live falcon. He explained that his job is to scare away seagulls that constantly try to swoop in and steal the fish.

From here, we officially transitioned onto a mix of the Coastal Camino and the parallel Senda Litoral (Litoral Route). While both follow the ocean, the Litoral Route hugs the shoreline much more closely—even winding all the way out around various peninsulas.

After two miles of walking beside roaring city traffic, the trail finally turned into a quiet, intimate medieval village. We took advantage of the peaceful atmosphere and stopped at a local café for pastries and caffeine.

Leaving the café, we wound through narrow village streets and stepped onto a coastal boardwalk stretching over a marshy area beside the bay. We were officially on the Litoral Route now. Because Vigo is a popular starting point for pilgrims walking the minimum 100 km needed to earn their Compostela certificate, we expected to see far fewer crowds on this quieter coastal stretch.

However, the trail wasn’t very well marked, so we had to rely heavily on digital maps to stay on track. Continuing along the waterfront, we passed through a long, tree-lined park dotted with beachfront bars and nightclubs overlooking the sand—an area that must come alive after dark. Even early in the morning, green parakeets were already out grazing in the grass.

The Camino continued right along the water. A cool breeze blew off the ocean, though the bright sun kept us warm as we passed steep, rocky cliffs broken up by small pockets of sandy beaches. By 11:00 AM, we had already covered 9 miles.

We continued bouncing back and forth between the Coastal and Litoral paths. Eventually, the route ran alongside a busy road at the end of a large bay before leading us down a beachside road past an expansive stretch of sand. Crossing over a river, we finally arrived in the village of A Ramallosa, where our albergue was waiting.

Our accommodations for the night were in a family-run apartment building—the family lived on the top two floors and rented out the bottom two. Conveniently, it sat right behind a beachfront bar where we grabbed dinner. We sat outside enjoying the ocean views and a few drinks until the evening chill pushed us indoors, where we exhaustedly turned in for a great night’s sleep.

New York Just Made It Harder for Automakers to Put Car Features Behind a Paywall

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new law takes aim at subscription fees for features already built into your vehicle, but automakers can still charge for certain services.

These days, the average new car payment in America is over $800 a month. With a payment like that, the last thing you want is to pay even more to access all of your car’s features. The state of New York is now taking steps to ensure that won’t happen; however, like most things in life, there are exceptions.

On Monday, November 23, New York governor Kathy Hochul signed legislation restricting automakers and dealers from charging consumers subscription fees to access certain features already installed in their vehicles. Essentially, if the hardware necessary to operate a feature is already installed in the vehicle, and activating that feature doesn't require continued support or expense on the part of the automaker, dealer, or third-party service provider, it cannot be put behind a subscription paywall.

A few years ago, BMW faced backlash in Europe for charging a monthly fee in order to enjoy heated seats. BMW has since backed away from that plan, and under this new legislation, that subscription approach would no longer be legal. According to a report by the New York Post, companies that violate this new law will face a $250 fine per subscription.

However, this doesn’t mean that New Yorkers can wave goodbye to all automotive-related subscription services; automakers can still charge for navigation updates, satellite radio, infotainment services, in-car Wi-Fi, roadside assistance, and certain connected vehicle services. Also, software-dependent driver-assistance and automated driving features like Tesla’s Full Self-Driving and General Motors’s Super Cruise are also exempt, meaning motorists shouldn't expect their hands-free highway driving subscriptions to suddenly become cost-free too.

Automakers and third-party companies have about 90 days to get into compliance before the law takes effect on February 27, 2027. It will be interesting to see if other states will follow New York’s lead, but the bigger question is if automakers will simply begin charging more for optional equipment at the time of purchase.

Toxic Port Pollution Costs Europe €2.5bn A Year In Health & Environmental Damages — Study

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

New research reveals a single ship emits, on average, as much toxic sulphur at port in a year as 19,300 cars, leaving taxpayers and hospitals to pay the hidden cost of shipping pollution.

Commercial ships docked in European ports inflict €2.5 billion a year in societal and environmental costs, according to new research by T&E. While upcoming EU port rules will cut at-berth damages by 40% by 2030, most vessels remain exempt. Previous research has shown that residents living close to a commercial harbour already face significantly higher risks of cardiovascular and respiratory mortality¹. Tightening port pollution rules would save society billions and protect the health of port communities, says T&E.

Instead of plugging into the electrical grid when they dock, thousands of commercial vessels run diesel generators around the clock to power onboard systems. These idling engines release harmful pollution directly into neighbouring coastal and port communities. Analysis of Europe’s 100 largest ports shows that Rotterdam is the most polluted with costs at €350 million, followed by Antwerp (€220 million), Marseille (€120 million), Hamburg (€120 million), and Genoa (€110 million).

According to the report, removing loopholes in the EU rules would save Europe €1.56bn in health and climate costs. Under current EU rules, outlined in the Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Regulation (AFIR), only large container ships, ferries and cruise ships are required to plug into dockside electricity sockets, meaning oil and gas tankers, bulk carriers, car carriers and other cargo ships are left out entirely. Requirements to connect should be progressively extended to all ship types in the upcoming review to cut emissions and grant ports the necessary long-term predictability.

Eloi Nordé, shipping policy officer at T&E, said: “Port communities are paying the price for ships running their engines 24/7 at port. Fossil fuels only seem cheap because companies offload health and climate costs onto the public. Fortunately, the solution already exists. Plugging ships into shoreside electric power would save society billions and make port cities far healthier to live in.”

On paper, marine fuels appear less costly than drawing electricity from the port grid. However, this is largely because the associated healthcare and environmental burdens are absorbed by the local population rather than ship operators. Once these external costs are factored into energy pricing, generating power from fossil fuels at dock is twice as costly as using shore electricity.

This policy gap creates a lot of planning uncertainty for EU port authorities and electricity grid operators. Without a predictable policy progressively including all vessel types, ports are disincentivised from making long-term investments in green infrastructure, says T&E.

The EU will review the regulation at the end of this year. T&E urges EU policymakers to use the AFIR review to expand shore power to all ships, batteries, and boilers, backed by national port electrification roadmaps before 2032.

Its call is echoed today in a joint letter to the European Commission from the Clean Port Cities (CPC) coalition, uniting industry, civil society, and maritime and energy stakeholders including DFDS and Green Transition Denmark, urging predictable shore power timelines for all vessels, battery charging rules, and faster grid access.

¹ The port-health research cited refers to Mei et al. (2023), “Long-term effects of air pollutants on respiratory and cardiovascular mortality in a port city along the Adriatic sea,” BMC Pulmonary Medicine.

New Option Approved for Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumors

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

— Adding bezuclastinib to sunitinib improved median PFS by 7 months

The FDA approved the selective KIT inhibitor bezuclastinib (Ekitna), in combination with sunitinib (Sutent), for previously treated gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST).

The approval allows use of the combination in adults with advanced GIST previously treated with imatinib.

"With today's FDA approval of bezuclastinib in combination with sunitinib, we have a new standard-of-care treatment following imatinib for patients with GIST," said Andrew Wagner, MD, PhD, of Dana-Farber Cancer Institute in Boston, in an announcement from bezuclastinib-developer Cogent Biosciences. "This approval represents a major step forward for the GIST community and reflects the tireless progress made in improving the lives of people living with this disease."

Sara Rothschild, executive director of the GIST advocacy organization The Life Raft Group, called the approval "an incredibly meaningful milestone for the GIST community that has waited many years hoping, advocating, and helping advance the research for a new treatment option."

Principal support for the approval came from the randomized, international PEAK trial involving 413 patients with locally advanced, unresectable, or metastatic GIST and disease progression after or intolerance to imatinib. Patients were randomized to sunitinib alone or in combination with bezuclastinib, and the primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS) by independent review. Crossover to the combination was allowed after confirmed progression with single-agent sunitinib.

The primary analysis showed a median PFS of 16.5 months with the combination versus 9.2 months with sunitinib monotherapy. The difference translated into a 50% reduction in the PFS hazard (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.39-0.65, P<0.0001). Significantly more patients randomized to the combination achieved objective responses (46% vs 26%, P<0.0001). Overall survival data remained immature.

Adverse reactions to the combination occurring in ≥20% of patients consisted of diarrhea, increased liver enzymes, hypertension, dysgeusia, hair color changes, fatigue, abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting, decreased appetite, stomatitis, gastroesophageal reflux disease, rash, dyspepsia, and pyrexia. The most common (≥5%) grade 3/4 laboratory abnormalities were increased alanine aminotransferase and decreased leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, and hemoglobin.

The approval brings to six the number of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) for GIST. Imatinib remains the only TKI approved for first-line treatment. Sunitinib alone and the bezuclastinib-sunitinib combination are the only approved second-line therapies. Regorafenib (Stivarga) is approved for use in third line and ripretinib (Qinlock) for fourth line and beyond. Additionally, avapritinib (Ayvakit) has approval for unresectable/metastatic GIST harboring a PDGFRA exon 18 mutation.

Bezuclastinib also is being investigated as a potential treatment for systemic mastocytosis.

AFT Boss Randi Weingarten’s Sleazy Book Scam Keeps Growing—and Teachers Paid for It All

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Becoming a teacher is no way to get rich, but becoming the head of a teachers union? That’s a different story.

Take Randi Weingarten, president of the American Federation of Teachers, who received $546,158 in total compensation last year, according to the union’s latest financial disclosure.

That’s over seven times the $74,495 average salary earned by public-school teachers nationwide.

And the new documents, recently filed with the federal Labor Department, show that an AFT president’s cronies can profit handsomely, too.

Outside consultants, a New York law firm, a public-relations firm and others appear to have raked in more than $2.2 million from AFT coffers to help Weingarten produce her 2025 book “Why Fascists Fear Teachers”—up from the more than $1.4 million previously estimated by the Freedom Foundation and reported by The Post.

The payments break down across two filings, covering two separate fiscal years.

>>> Randi Weingarten’s Dirty Back-to-School War on Target

The first, for a one-year period from 2024 to 2025, documented substantial costs connected to the book paid by the AFT—including payments to Weingarten’s literary agent Inkwell Management, to a fact-checker credited in her acknowledgments, and to a photographer for her author’s headshot.

The new form adds further amounts apparently related to the same project.

On Sept. 25, 2025, nine days after the book’s release, the AFT paid Inkwell Management $28,125 for a “publication expense,” bringing combined payments to the agent across both filings to $92,215.

Separately, the union paid Feldman Strategies, a publicity firm, $202,500; shortly after the pub date, the PR group posted, “It has been a true honor for our entire team at Feldman Strategies to help promote” Weingarten’s book.

The new filing also records an $80,000 “royalty payment” sent on Oct. 23, 2025, to a limited liability company controlled by Weingarten.

That sum follows a $125,000 royalty payment to the same entity disclosed in the union’s prior annual report, bringing the combined royalty transfers from AFT to the Weingarten-controlled company to $205,000.

And in its 2026 filing, the union paid Patterson Belknap Webb & Tyler LLP — the law firm credited in the book with reviewing Weingarten’s manuscript—another $591,174, bringing cumulative legal fees to the firm across both years to nearly $1.6 million.

The union says the firm toiled on the book “pro bono,” but the attorneys’ extensive financial ties to the AFT create an obvious incentive to characterize that work as such.

The royalty payments require additional explanation: Public filings show the AFT itself receiving publisher advances connected to the book, then disbursing a portion of those funds as a “royalty payment” to Teachers Want What Kids Need, LLC—an entity controlled by Weingarten.

The publisher did not pay royalties to Weingarten or her LLC directly; the money flowed through the union, in an arrangement that remains opaque.

Weingarten has stated that some of the book’s proceeds would go back to the AFT, the AFT Disaster Relief Fund, and the AFT Educational Foundation.

Even if that claim holds, dues-paying teachers shouldn’t be required to subsidize a book project that profits their union’s president.

In July, the GOP-led House Committee on Education and the Workforce opened a formal inquiry into the matter based on The Post’s initial report, requesting documents on the AFT’s book-related expenditures, contracts and royalty flows.

That inquiry is controlled by the committee majority, however—and once Democrats take control of the House in January, the probe is expected to stall or be closed.

The minority can still request materials and issue reports, but it cannot compel the same level of investigation.

Separately, the Department of Labor’s Inspector General has already opened its own review to probe whether union dues were improperly used for Weingarten’s book project.

That executive-branch inquiry can continue under President Donald Trump’s administration, regardless of which party controls Congress.

The new 2026 filing supplies further evidence that any ongoing review should examine: The additional royalty payment, the fresh publication expense routed to the literary agent, the large PR contract and the further $591,174 to Patterson Belknap all post-date the period covered by the prior report.

Payments to the Weingarten-controlled entity, the literary agent, the PR firm and the consultants raise questions about whether dues money collected from teachers was used for the personal benefit of the union’s top officer.

Federal labor filings exist precisely so that members and the public can review such expenditures.

When these records show repeated transfers linked to an officer’s private project, the disclosures themselves supply the basis for scrutiny.

Teachers who don’t want their hard-earned paychecks to underwrite an officer’s vanity project have a practical alternative.

Organizations such as the Teacher Freedom Alliance offer membership—including free liability insurance—without routing teacher dues to electoral campaigns or left-wing causes.

If enough fed-up teachers leave the AFT, people like Randi Weingarten might think twice about wasting members’ money on personal and political pet projects.

The Rise of Astrosociology: What Can Analogue Astronaut Studies Teach us About the Future of Humanity in Space?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

It's no secret that some highly ambitious visions for the future of space exploration are taking shape today. Beyond NASA and China's plans to send crewed missions to the Moon by the end of the decade and create permanent infrastructure there, both space agencies hope to put boots on the surface of Mars by the 2030s or 40s. Meanwhile, the European Space Agency (ESA) is active in the Artemis Program and plans to send the ExoMars Rosalind Franklin rover to the Red Planet in 2028. India aims to send the first "gaganauts" to space next year (as part of its Gaganyaan program).

On top of all that, there are plans to "commercialize Low Earth Orbit" (LEO), build infrastructure in cis-lunar space (Ã  la asteroid mining), and even proposals for sending commercial astronauts to "space tourists" to the Moon and Mars. All of this begs the question of how humans intend to live in extraterrestrial environments and among the stars. What is needed to ensure that humans can not only survive the hazards of being off-world, but also flourish? As Mars City Design CEO Vera Mulyani would say, "How can we thrive, and not just survive?"

This is where the emerging field of Astrosociology comes into play, an umbrella term that looks at spaceflight through the lens of the social sciences. In addition to questions related to engineering and astronaut health and safety, astrosociology takes a more holistic outlook and examines the social, cultural, and behavioral patterns of humans related to outer space. Consider Abraham Maslow's "Hierarchy of Needs," which begins with the basics (physiological and security needs) and extends upwards to include the immaterial, such as love, self-esteem, and self-actualization.

This challenge motivated Dr. Jim Pass to found the Astrosociology Research Institute (ARI) in 2008, a California-based nonprofit educational organization dedicated to developing astrosociology as a multidisciplinary academic field. As Pass describes on the organization's website, astrosociology is both a subdiscipline of sociology and a multidisciplinary field that includes (but is not limited to) "psychology, anthropology, economics, social psychology, political science, space history, space law, space policy, philosophy, as well as the arts."

At its core, the ARI's mission comes down to a simple but highly relevant question: "How can social and behavioral scientists collaborate with physical and natural scientists to further the success of space exploration and settlement in the future?" In their latest newsletter, titled "Analog Space Missions: The Astrosociological Factors That Affect Analog Space Missions - Part 1," Pass and his colleagues share the perspectives of analog astronauts who participated in their call for papers and expert commentary by educators, space medics, and systems engineers.

As Mia Belle Parkinson, a postdoctoral student at the University of Edinburgh and ARI's editor-in-chief, wrote in the issue:

When we talk about preparing for space exploration and settlement, we too often default to the technical: propulsion systems, life support, radiation shielding. These things matter enormously. But so do the quieter, harder-to-quantify questions â how do people build trust in isolated environments? How do cultural backgrounds shape group dynamics? What does it mean, psychologically and socially, to live on the edge of the unknown?

While analog astronaut training may seem like a relatively new idea, it's actually part of a long-standing tradition almost as old as the "Space Race" itself. Before astronauts landed on the Moon, NASA conducted a series of training missions known as the "Apollo Geology Field Trips" between 1964 and 1972. The first field trip saw the first two astronaut groups travel to Flagstaff, Arizona, for a two-day mission to Meteor Crater and other locations that resembled volcanic features on the Moon.

Other locations included the Grand Canyon, the volcanic fields in Hawaii, the Nevada National Security Site, and mountain formations in the Sonoran Desert, New Mexico, Oregon, Alaska, west Texas, and Sudbury, Ontario. Additionally, the Soviets conducted the BIOS-3 experiment within the closed ecosystem built at the Institute of Biophysics in Krasnoyarsk in 1965. This was followed by Tektite I and II in 1969 and 1971, which studied human behavior in a confined, isolated, pressurized habitat on the ocean floor near the U.S. Virgin Islands.

JAXA (Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency) astronaut Satoshi Furukawa pedals on the upgraded CEVIS system, keeping muscle and bone density levels during spaceflight. (Credit: NASA)

Since that time, research aboard space stations such as Mir, the International Space Station (ISS), and the Chinese Tiangong space station has provided invaluable knowledge about living in space and the effects of microgravity on humans' mental and physical well-being. However, these studies are not directly relevant to proposed missions that will send astronauts to the Moon, Mars, and other celestial bodies. As Parry writes:

While extremely valuable for zero-gravity spaceflights, living and working at the bottom of a gravity well presents altogether different conditions, including an inability to manufacture drugs or lift heavy objects. Planning for bases and settlements on the surfaces of the Moon, Mars, and eventually beyond can only be simulated on the surface of the Earth to humankind's best approximation even in 2026, because gravity matters!

The first analog astronaut (hereafter AA) profiled was former NASA Advisor, science communicator, and space exploration expert Beth Mund. Mund is an especially experienced AA, having participated in the HI-SEAS (Hawai'i Space Exploration Analog and Simulation) program on the summit of Mauna Loa, Hawai'i, the LunAres Research Station, and the Jules Undersea Lodge. The first involved an all-female crew working together during the height of COVID in an uninsulated temporary habitat with minimal privacy and tough conditions.

The second took place in a converted World War II bunker in Poland located inside an old hangar. The hangar itself was filled with sand to simulate lunar regolith, and steel doors sealed the habitat and its six-person crew inside a rather confined space. At the Jules Undersea Lodge, located in Key Largo off the southern tip of Florida, Mund and her colleagues entered a submerged habitat with no escape hatches, accessible only through a "wet room" portal.

Looking back at these three missions, Mund noticed patterns that transcended any single environment. While each had its own geography and logistics, confinement was the common theme. From her experiences, Mund identified four universal human responses that were consistent throughout. The first, she wrote, was the importance of improvisation:

No matter how carefully equipment is designed or schedules are drawn, something always fails... each failure forces us to understand the problem and formulate a response to it in the context of very tight resource constraints. And in adapting, we discover more about ourselves than about the broken machines... These small acts of invention prove that ingenuity is not a luxury in confinement. It is a decisive factor in survival.

Photo of analog astronaut Beth Mund sitting outside the HI-SEAS habitat on the summit of Mauna Kea, Hawai'i. Credit: Beth Mund

The second lesson was the importance of ritual: "At HI-SEAS, our crew naturally gathered each evening for what I came to think of as our 'campfire,' a circle of debriefing, storytelling, and laughter, even in the cold dome with no flame. In Poland, games became our escape valve, a way to puncture the heaviness of the surveys and the claustrophobic walls. Underwater, simply gazing out the porthole together felt like a ceremony. In every analog, the crew instinctively created moments of reconnection that grounded us, even in places designed to isolate."

The third lesson for Mund was continuity, as she and her colleagues inherited experiments started by crews they never met and which would be handled by future analog crews. "It gave weight to our confinement, transforming it from a private trial into a communal contribution," she said. "That continuity across crew "generations" is perhaps the most powerful sociological insight of analog missions. We are practicing not only how to live together, but how to build across time, leaving work - and maybe even some data/ insights - for crews who serve next."

Lastly, there was the inevitability of testing. While every mission includes tests of equipment, plants, and methods for living in space, the experience also tests the limits of human endurance, creativity, and cohesion. This, she wrote, is the true essence of analog astronaut training:

From Mars-like lava fields to a Polish bunker to the ocean floor, I have learned that analog missions are not about pretending to be astronauts. They are about practicing human endurance in places that strip away comfort and certainty. In that practice, in all the different shapes it takes across environments, facilities, missions, and crews, lies the real preparation for humanity to endure no matter the circumstances.

Mund is merely one of many analog astronauts profiled in the Astrosociological Insights Forum's newsletter. To include as many testimonials and experiences as possible, the issue was released in two parts, incorporating perspectives not only from AAs but also from several experts in the burgeoning field of Astrosociology. Why is backflow testing required, and what should property owners expect? Backflow Prevention Experts Jeff and Margie Vlisides of Waterford, Michigan, explain how testing protects drinking water and helps property owners follow water safety rules in a HelloNation article. Jeff and Margie Vlisides are with Backflow Prevention Services, LLC.

According to the article, backflow happens when water moves backward through a plumbing system. Changes in water pressure can cause dirty water or harmful substances to enter clean water pipes.

A backflow preventer helps stop this problem. These devices are often found in businesses, apartment buildings, sprinkler systems, and some homes.

The article explains that water suppliers require backflow testing to help protect public drinking water. Testing schedules vary based on local rules, device type, and property use.

How Should Property Owners Prepare for Backflow Testing?

Property owners may receive a notice from their water supplier with a testing deadline. The notice may also explain how to schedule testing and submit the results.

Before the appointment, owners should make sure the tester can reach the device. Clearing the area around the backflow preventer can help avoid delays.

The article notes that water service may need to be turned off briefly during testing. Owners can ask about possible shutoffs before the appointment.

A trained tester uses special tools to measure water pressure and check whether the device's valves work properly.

These checks help determine whether the backflow preventer can stop water from flowing backward. Testing usually does not require major plumbing work.

Afterward, the tester records the results. If the device passes, the report confirms that it met the testing requirements at that time.

What Happens If a Backflow Preventer Fails?

The article explains that worn parts, damaged components, or dirt inside the device can cause a failed test.

A trained professional can identify the problem and determine whether repairs or replacement are needed. After the work is completed, another test is usually required.

Property owners should keep their reports and submit results to the water supplier when required.

The one-sentence motion filed Tuesday in a Salt Lake City-based court by state Attorney General Derek Brown referenced a “change in circumstance.” Brown’s office noted that it could refile the case if Kalshi resumes offering trades on its platform there.

“Kalshi has pulled out of Utah, and if it returns, so will the charges,” Brown said in a statement.

Utah has been on the front lines of a battle between states and the federal government over who regulates the markets. Litigation has spread across more than 20 states, creating a fragmented legal landscape that analysts say will likely be settled by the U.S. Supreme Court.

The felony charges filed by Utah include 13 counts of violating the state’s prohibition on gambling and another charge involving profiting off illegal activity.

The state’s charging documents name Kalshi and its co-founders, chief executive officer Tarek Mansour and chief operating officer Luana Lopes Lara. They are due in court in Salt Lake City on Dec. 4, unless a judge agrees to dismiss the case.

Utah law prohibits gambling, and Utah considers prediction markets gambling, Brown’s office said. Many other states contend that the platforms’ sports trading activities are flouting their laws that regulate sports betting and casino gambling.

Utah’s felony charges represented a new tactic in how states are trying to assert regulatory authority over the fast-expanding activity. Brown’s office also has asked a court to make Kalshi give up the profits it made in Utah.

Kalshi, like the CFTC, argues that prediction markets offer “event contracts” that should be considered swaps under a federal law that grants exclusive regulation to the federal agency. Utah and at least 44 other states have opposed that view.

Kalshi sued Utah in February, asking a federal judge to prevent the state from enforcing its gambling restrictions on the platform. A federal judge rejected the request, and an appeal is pending.

The nonprofit research firm’s Consumer Confidence Index, which measures American opinions of current economic conditions and their outlook for the next six months, rose by 11.4 points to 103.3 in November, the second consecutive monthly increase after months of glum reports.

The organization’s other monthly indicators—the Present Situation Index, based on consumers’ assessment of current business and labor market conditions, and the Expectations Index, based on consumers’ short-term outlook for income, business and labor market conditions—also both increased, by 13.7 points to 132.9 and by 9.9 points to 83.6, respectively. The survey period for this month’s preliminary results was November 1-17.

“Consumer confidence rallied again in November, potentially due to lower uncertainty following the midterm elections,” said Fergus McCormick, senior economist, Global Indicators, The Conference Board. “After two months of consecutive increases, the headline index is back to levels of confidence last seen in January 2025. The gain in confidence was led by stronger perceptions of present and future labor market conditions. Perceptions of current and future business conditions also improved sharply. Along with the gain in headline confidence, consumers planned slightly higher spending on goods and vacations.”

McCormick said write-in responses in November were slightly less negative than previous months, noting that while consumers continued to express frustration with high prices and rising costs, some comments cited hope for the general economy. He said comments about war and conflict ticked up slightly, along with mixed feelings about elections, politics and the direction of the labor market.

A host of macroeconomic factors continue to hinder consumers who are looking to buy a home. Yet, despite these indicators, the November Consumer Confidence report also shows on a month-over-month as well as on a six-month moving average basis, plans to purchase autos and homes both improving slightly this month.
