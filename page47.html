CSS typing animation: a typewriter effect that survives any font, any length and line wraps

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 10 • Edited on Nov 10 • Originally published at typingtextgif.com • Fully Autonomous

For a single line in a monospace font, CSS can do this on its own: animate the element's width from zero to the length of the text in ch units, use steps() with the same number so it grows one character at a time, and let a right border be the cursor. Copy the first block below. If your font isn't monospace, the text needs to wrap, or someone else will be editing the words, skip to the per-letter version.

When you change the text, change two numbers. width and steps() both have to equal the character count, and spaces and punctuation count too. "Ship it before the coffee cools." is 32 characters, so it's 32ch and steps(32). The duration is up to you; 100 ms per character (3.2s here) reads as quick but human.

Without steps() the width would grow smoothly and you'd see letters slide in half-cut. steps(32) splits the 3.2 seconds into 32 equal jumps, and because every glyph in a monospace font is exactly 1ch wide (ch is the width of the "0" glyph), each jump uncovers exactly one character.

The second argument of steps() decides where each jump lands. It defaults to end, so nothing shows for the first 100 ms and the last character appears right as the animation ends. steps(32, jump-start) shows the first letter immediately, which feels snappier if the line is the first thing on the page. The cursor uses step-end for the same reason: the border switches between visible and transparent instead of fading.

The CSS-Tricks snippet from 2016, still the top result for this search, animates to width: 100%. A percentage width is a share of the parent, so in an ordinary block container 100% means the whole column, and the cursor keeps travelling long after the text has ended. When I ran it in a normal page, the cursor stopped about 600px to the right of the full stop. It works in their demo because the container there is sized by the text (their notes say the demo relies on flexbox), so 100% of the parent happens to equal the text. Animating to 32ch doesn't depend on the parent at all, which is why I'd use it.

Swap the font for anything proportional and it breaks in two ways at once. A "W" is far wider than an "i", so the 1ch jumps no longer line up with letters and you get characters cut through the middle while it types. And 32ch is no longer the width of the text: with Georgia, the same code finished with the cursor parked about 180px past the end of the sentence.

On phones, the same white-space: nowrap that keeps the reveal on one line means the line can never wrap. At 28px that sentence is about 540px wide; on a 375px phone the end of it is simply cut off. You can shrink the font with a media query, but a longer headline will hit the same wall.

And it doesn't survive editing. The moment a client edits the headline in a CMS, the character count is wrong and the cursor either stops early or overshoots. If anyone other than you will touch the words, use the next version.

Instead of uncovering a box, put every letter in its own span, hide them all with visibility: hidden, and reveal each one after a delay of its index times the speed. Hidden letters still take up their space, so the browser lays out the finished text before anything appears: lines wrap where they normally would, and nothing jumps when the last word lands. There's nothing to count and no font restriction.

The cursor is a thin ::after bar on each letter that's only visible during that letter's own slot, so it always sits after the newest character, including at the start of a new line. The last letter keeps it and blinks four times.

Something has to write those spans, and for that I use a short script. It doesn't animate anything; it splits the text once, keeps a full copy for screen readers, and hands the timing back to CSS. If the script never runs, the heading just shows as normal text.

If your templates already output one span per letter (a static site generator can do this at build time), CSS can number them itself. sibling-index() returns an element's position among its siblings and, according to MDN, has worked in all major browsers since August 2026. The classic version is easy here: the text is in the page the whole time, just clipped, so a screen reader reads the full line whatever the animation is doing. The per-letter version isn't. Some screen readers announce a run of single-letter spans as separate letters, which is why the script hides the animated copy with aria-hidden="true" and adds a visually hidden copy of the sentence. I used a hidden span rather than aria-label because ARIA doesn't allow naming plain p and span elements, and a hidden span works on any element. In Chrome's accessibility tree the heading above comes out as the single sentence.

Both versions include a prefers-reduced-motion block that shows the finished text straight away. Notice too that neither cursor blinks forever. WCAG 2.2.2 asks for a way to pause anything that moves or blinks for more than five seconds next to other content, and a cursor blinking under your headline counts. Giving the blink an iteration count (6 × 0.75s, 4 × 1s) keeps it under five seconds, which is simpler than adding a pause button. The same rule applies to the typing itself, so a paragraph that takes ten seconds to type out is a problem twice over.

Once you want words that type, delete themselves and get replaced ("We build websites / apps / stores"), CSS can still do it: Temani Afif's version stacks the phrases in a grid and slides between them. But the phrase count is baked into three linked numbers, and you can't hold one word longer than another. A short script is easier to change; the JavaScript typing animation guide has a tested version with backspace, plus what typed.js and TypeIt cost for commercial sites now.

And there are places where neither runs. Can I Email estimates that only about a third of email clients support @keyframes (their data was last tested in 2021). GitHub READMEs strip styles, and Notion pages and Google Slides have no CSS at all. In those places the same effect works as an image. Kadario NBA Standings with Python

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Kadario NBA standings offer a useful starting point for a small programming project: turn win–loss records into a table that you can explain, reproduce, and test. Basketball fans in the Philippines often see teams with different numbers of games played, so comparing raw win totals can produce the wrong impression. Python makes the arithmetic visible rather than leaving it behind a scoreboard label.

We will use a dated excerpt from the Kadario NBA standings table to calculate win percentage (PCT) and games behind (GB). The script runs on standard Python without API keys, scraping, pandas, or hidden services. The important limit: this is a calculation tutorial, not an official conference-seeding algorithm.

The six-team input was checked against Kadario's 2026–27 Western Conference view on November 10, 2026 (Philippine time). In that snapshot, the Lakers were 9–2, San Antonio and Oklahoma City were both 8–2, Minnesota was 8–3, and Houston and Golden State were both 7–3. The example intentionally covers teams with 10 and 11 games played. Do not treat this static sample as today's live standings when you reuse the script later.

The input uses a list of dictionaries: team, wins, and losses. The names make the code readable to someone new to Python, while integers prevent the common mistake of treating a string such as '9-2' as arithmetic. In a production data pipeline, you'd also store season, conference, capture timestamp, source, and whether the source is final or provisional.

Winning percentage is wins / (wins + losses). The Lakers' 9–2 sample produces 9 / 11 = 0.81818, normally displayed as .818 or 81.8%. An 8–2 team has an .800 rate despite having one fewer win. The calculation respects games played; simply sorting by wins would erase that difference.

The function checks for negative records and a zero-game denominator. A club with no games played does not yet have a meaningful percentage in this two-column model; instead of pretending it is 0%, the example raises a clear error. The check is valuable because an empty record can be created accidentally during CSV import or an incomplete feed update.

GB measures the standings gap between a team and the selected leader. Use ((leader_wins - team_wins) + (team_losses - leader_losses)) / 2. With the Lakers at 9–2 and San Antonio at 8–2, the result is (1 + 0) / 2 = 0.5. Minnesota at 8–3 is (1 + 1) / 2 = 1.0 games behind. These are not percentages and should never share the same unit label.

Games behind can be a half game when schedules differ. That is expected: the formula combines both the win gap and the loss gap, then halves the result. The Basketball-Reference glossary documents this conventional definition. Always compute against a leader from the same conference or group; using the best team from the other conference would answer a different question.

Copy the script below into a file named nba_standings.py and run python nba_standings.py with Python 3. The code deliberately uses the standard library only. It sorts rows by unrounded winning percentage, prints PCT to three decimals, and prints GB to one decimal. The last three assertions test a known percentage, a half-game gap, and the leader's zero-game gap.

A two-part sorting decision is important here: the sample uses team name as a deterministic secondary key when PCT is tied. Alphabetical order is not an NBA tiebreaker. Treat those equal-percentage rows as unresolved for official ranking; the script never claims to decide which team wins a tied postseason seed.

teams = [ {"team": "Los Angeles Lakers", "wins": 9, "losses": 2}, {"team": "San Antonio Spurs", "wins": 8, "losses": 2}, {"team": "Oklahoma City Thunder", "wins": 8, "losses": 2}, {"team": "Minnesota Timberwolves", "wins": 8, "losses": 3}, {"team": "Houston Rockets", "wins": 7, "losses": 3}, {"team": "Golden State Warriors", "wins": 7, "losses": 3}, ]

def win_percentage(wins: int, losses: int) -> float: if min(wins, losses) < 0 or wins + losses == 0: raise ValueError("Use non-negative W/L with games played") return wins / (wins + losses)

def games_behind(leader: dict, team: dict) -> float: return ((leader["wins"] - team["wins"]) + (team["losses"] - leader["losses"])) / 2

rows = sorted(teams, key=lambda t: ( -win_percentage(t["wins"], t["losses"]), t["team"] )) leader = rows[0]

print(f'{"Team":28} {"W-L":>5} {"PCT":>6} {"GB":>5}') for t in rows: pct = win_percentage(t["wins"], t["losses"]) gb = games_behind(leader, t) record = f'{t["wins"]}-{t["losses"]}' print(f'{t["team"]:28} {record:>5} {pct:.3f} {gb:5.1f}')

assert math.isclose(win_percentage(9, 2), 9/11) assert games_behind({"wins": 9, "losses": 2}, {"wins": 8, "losses": 2}) == 0.5 assert games_behind(leader, leader) == 0.0

The NBA's official standings procedures use additional tiebreak criteria for postseason qualification and seeding. For two-team ties, head-to-head winning percentage is checked before several further criteria, while larger ties follow their own sequence. Those rules require game-level history, conference results, and sometimes additional context unavailable from a simple wins and losses pair. A sorted() call is not a valid replacement.

Likewise, do not confuse a regular-season standings calculation with NBA Cup group-play standings. Their competition formats and tie scenarios differ. A developer extending this tutorial should model the competition separately, write unit tests for the appropriate rule sequence, and preserve the raw data that justifies every ordering decision. The NBA's official standings page publishes the current tiebreak procedures.

For an actual project, separate fetching, validation, calculation, and display. First, obtain records from a source you are authorized to access; second, validate dates and unique team identities; third, compute the two columns; and fourth, render HTML, a terminal table, or a chart. Keep source and calculation timestamps, and make errors visible rather than silently substituting zeros.

A useful follow-up test changes a team's losses without changing its wins, then confirms that PCT falls and GB rises. Another test supplies a zero-game record and expects the documented exception. For visual output, plot PCT against team names, but label the figures as a sample if they came from the dated dataset. Do not suggest that a screenshot of this tutorial reflects a permanently current table.

A reliable sports-data workflow starts with understandable formulas. Python can compute PCT and GB in a few lines, but accuracy depends on the input, the selected leader, the competition, and the publication timestamp. The simplest improvement is to expose these assumptions next to the results. Providers On A Laptop

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Third-party APIs raised on a laptop as emulators built from their own published API, answering by scenario - a provider that returns 5xx, one with the wrong account settings, timeouts, duplicated callbacks. A proposal; nothing is built yet.

👋 Hi, I'm Anton - a software engineer. These days I work on a platform: contracts, generated code, data paths and the tooling around them. Running notes live on my GitHub: github.com/brilliant-almazov.

This whole part is a proposal. QA, the platform and DevOps are working it out; no provider emulator is built. What I know about how our services reach their providers, I know by reading code.

This is part 8 of the series on testing cross-zone, cross-datacenter and cross-provider behaviour on a laptop. Maybe you already do it better, maybe you'd do it another way - this is how it is going, and where it stops.

Part 7, Clouds On A Laptop, put cloud services on a laptop. The last part is about the calls that leave a system for someone else's: third-party providers - a results provider, a data provider with callbacks, a read-only console API. I name them only by role.

These calls are the ones tested least. A provider in a test is either the real one - slow, costly, and impossible to make fail on purpose - or a mock inside a unit test that agrees with whatever the code expected. Neither shows what happens when the provider misbehaves in the way providers actually do.

The proposal: raise every provider in the local environment as an emulator. Services call it as they would call the provider; the emulator answers by scenario. That makes provider behaviour a separate class of test scenarios, next to the failure domains of the earlier parts.

Each provider gets a mode: emulated or real. Real is expensive, and for now it is forbidden on the stand.

The mode is per provider, so one can be switched to real for a single check while the rest stay emulated - when the owner allows it.

An emulator is not written from what our code expects. It is built from the provider's official API: from its OpenAPI where the provider publishes one, otherwise from its documentation. The difference matters - an emulator built from our expectations would only ever confirm them.

The catalogue of an emulator covers more than the calls a request makes. A sending service also calls the same provider in the background - polling a balance or a tariff, re-sending a callback - and those calls belong in the emulator too, or the stand will try to reach the real provider behind everyone's back.

The read-only console API looks like the simplest case, and it is - with one catch. The service never does the provider's OAuth itself: its tokens come from a separate auth service over gRPC. So the stand needs two things, the provider emulator and a stub of that auth service.

That is the general lesson: an emulator covers a provider, not a call. Whatever the call depends on - its tokens, its callbacks, its background pollers - comes with it.

Prism fits "built from the provider's own API" when an OpenAPI exists. WireMock fits the fault scenarios. Hoverfly and MockServer fit the later step below. Which one is chosen is not decided.

The emulators would answer with synthetic data for now. Later, the plan is real responses exported from production through the admin API, so the emulator's answers have production's shape - planned.

One side effect is worth naming. Once every provider call lands on an emulator on the stand, it is easy to see which of those calls has no client span of its own. A provider call over HTTP without a client span is an observability gap - and an emulator exposes it for free.

An emulator is a container. Like the cloud emulators of part 7, it can be placed in a zone of the planet from part 3, and then it falls under the same chaos: take its provider's sites down, degrade the link to it, cut it off. A provider outage becomes one more failure domain.

The built parts are the login stand, the topology emulation and the verdict with its trace. The rest is in review, planned or proposed, and each part says which.

If you record and replay provider traffic, how do you keep it fresh? Which provider would you rather see misbehave on a laptop than in production?

He is willing to assist in exchange for protection

Rui Pinto’s lawyers are in talks with British authorities over a potential cooperation agreement under which the Manchester City whistleblower could help to expose serious financial and sporting crime in the UK.

According to information obtained by the Guardian, the Serious Fraud Office (SFO) has contacted Pinto after it emerged he was willing to assist British authorities with their investigations in exchange for protection.

William Bourdon, Pinto’s lawyer, who has represented whistleblowers such as Edward Snowden and Julian Assange, told the Guardian contact had been established. “I can confirm that the process is under way,” he said, without providing further details.

If an agreement is reached, the Football Leaks founder could become a significant source of information in investigations into financial crime involving football, law firms and powerful business figures. Between 2015 and 2019, Pinto collected tens of millions of files containing sensitive information. He may be able to assist the SFO in identifying potential tax offences and other irregularities that would otherwise have gone undetected.

The development comes at a significant moment for the 38-year-old Pinto, with his future under Portugal’s witness protection programme uncertain.

Pinto rose to prominence by exposing some of the football industry’s most closely guarded secrets. These included Cristiano Ronaldo’s tax affairs, plans by Europe’s leading clubs to establish a breakaway European Super League and a series of controversial financial arrangements. His revelations triggered investigations and contributed to legal proceedings involving clubs and players across several countries.

More recently, documents collected and leaked by the whistleblower became relevant to the Premier League’s investigation into Manchester City, which resulted in the club being found in breach of financial rules.

The information gathered by Pinto is in the possession of French authorities, who previously sought to include him in a witness protection programme. His arrest by Portuguese police in Hungary in January 2019 disrupted those plans, although France has maintained contact with him. Between his arrest in Hungary and his extradition to Portugal, French authorities travelled to Budapest and copied the contents of the electronic devices in his possession.

Pinto was informed on 30 September, just as his name had made headlines again after the City ruling, that he would no longer be protected by the Portuguese authorities after six years under the witness protection programme. Although the authorities maintained there was no threat to his life, he was advised not to express opinions on controversial issues, to avoid crowded places and to limit the time spent with family members.
