One BlackRock fund dominates Bitcoin’s billion-dollar ETF exodus

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Fidelity finished positive in the November 2-6 week, but the other funds collectively remained negative without IBIT.

BlackRock's iShares Bitcoin Trust ETF (IBIT) accounted for 94% of US spot Bitcoin ETFs' net withdrawals in the completed November 2-6 week, leaving one fund responsible for most of the category's retreat. That leaves the next recovery test focused on one fund: can IBIT’s withdrawals slow?

The 12 funds tracked by Farside Investors recorded weekly net outflows of $1,282 million. IBIT recorded net outflows of $1,210 million. Fidelity's Wise Origin Bitcoin Fund (FBTC) moved in the opposite direction, recording weekly net inflows of $93 million.

IBIT's share was 94.4% of the aggregate net withdrawal, after positive flows elsewhere offset part of the withdrawals. The denominator is net fund flows across the group, not gross redemptions or all Bitcoin selling.

All funds other than IBIT collectively recorded net outflows of $72 million during the week. Removing IBIT's withdrawals from that calculation would substantially reduce the shortfall while leaving the remaining group negative.

The daily pattern also matters. IBIT recorded withdrawals in each of the five sessions, while the group finished positive only on November 4. US spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded net outflows of $412 million on November 6.

Fidelity was a counterweight across the week, despite recording withdrawals on Thursday. Three other funds also finished the week positive. The picture was therefore more uneven than a universal exit from Bitcoin funds, although the positive products did not offset the category's largest withdrawal source.

Wednesday's Fidelity-led inflows failed to persist: Thursday's withdrawals restored the negative aggregate reading, and Friday ended the week with another outflow.

iShares reported IBIT net assets of about $61 billion as of November 6. The week's withdrawals were roughly 2% of that ending asset base.

Redemptions also need care when connecting fund flows to Bitcoin selling. The IBIT prospectus describes both cash and in-kind settlement. A cash redemption involves the trust selling bitcoin and delivering cash. An in-kind redemption delivers bitcoin to an authorized participant, the intermediary handling the redemption, or its designated agent or client.

Mastering the Sprint Lifecycle: How to Escape Mechanical Scrum

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 8 • Originally published at aiflowpm.com

Too many engineering organizations fall into the trap of "mechanical Scrum." You know exactly what this looks like. Teams robotically go through the motions, execute the ceremonies, and check the boxes, yet entirely miss the value. The result? Exhausted developers, frustrated product owners, and Sprints that feel like a relentless feature factory.

If your team is constantly rolling over tickets, dreading the daily standup, or viewing the retrospective as a pointless venting session, you have a fundamental problem. Your tech team isn't running Sprints. Your Sprints are running you.

The secret to high-performing teams isn't memorizing Scrum steps. It is mastering the Sprint Lifecycle as a synchronized engine for predictable value delivery and team empowerment. Let's break down how elite software teams elevate these core events from tedious calendar invites into a distinct competitive advantage.

The Sprint Lifecycle is the continuous, time-boxed sequence of Agile events—Planning, Daily Scrum, Execution, Review, and Retrospective—designed to deliver working software iteratively. It matters because when executed with intent, this cycle transforms unpredictable software development into a reliable, feedback-driven value engine.

Agile leadership isn't about enforcing rules blindly. It requires creating an environment where this dynamic cycle breeds trust, speed, and continuous improvement. Moving from a beginner team to an elite one requires shifting your focus from compliance to intent. Here is how you optimize each phase of the lifecycle.

Sprint Planning is the critical starting point where the team defines the exact business value they will deliver over the upcoming iteration and how they will achieve it. The goal is to establish a clear, unified Sprint Goal rather than simply cramming a backlog with random, disconnected tickets.

In low-maturity teams, planning looks like a prolonged negotiation over story points. The Product Owner reads off a list of tickets, the developers try to guess how much they can squeeze into two weeks based on historical velocity, and everyone leaves feeling overwhelmed. There is no narrative. There is just a bucket of tasks.

Elite teams start with the Sprint Goal. This is a single, compelling objective that connects engineering work directly to business outcomes. For example, instead of "complete 40 story points," the goal becomes "enable users to securely reset their passwords via SMS."

Define the "Why" first: What is the most important problem we need to solve in the next two weeks?Pull, don't push: Developers must pull work into the Sprint based on their actual capacity, not have it pushed onto them by aggressive deadlines.Leave slack in the system: Aim for 80% utilization. If you plan for 100% capacity, a single production bug will derail the entire iteration.

The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute tactical synchronization meeting held by and for the developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt their plan for the next 24 hours. It is an alignment tool to optimize flow, pivot quickly, and eliminate blockers.

We have all attended painful standups. The Scrum Master acts like a project manager, calling on team members one by one. Developers mumble through the three standard questions ("Yesterday I wrote code, today I will write code, no blockers") while everyone else zones out. It is a micromanaged status report that adds zero value.

Ditch the rigid three-question format. Turn the Daily Scrum into a strategy session.

Walk the board: Start from the right side of your Jira (or equivalent) board—the items closest to "Done"—and work backward. Ask: "What do we need to do together to get this specific ticket across the finish line today?"Focus on flow, not busyness: It does not matter if a developer is busy. What matters is if the work is moving.Take it offline: If a technical discussion starts dragging on past two minutes, call a "parking lot" and move it to a post-sync huddle with only the necessary people.

Sprint Execution is the active building phase where the team writes, tests, and integrates code to create a potentially releasable product increment. The primary focus here must be on continuous integration and maintaining high quality from day one, avoiding late-stage technical debt.

In mechanical Scrum, developers code in silos for eight days. On day nine, they throw everything over the wall to QA. Integration conflicts explode. Bugs pile up. The Sprint ends with five tickets sitting in the "In Review" or "Testing" columns, leading to a massive carryover into the next Sprint.

You cannot bolt quality onto a product at the end of a Sprint. You have to build it in.

Swarm on tickets: Stop having five developers working on five separate features. Encourage pair programming and mobbing to finish one feature before starting the next. Limit your Work In Progress (WIP).Shift testing left: Quality assurance isn't a final phase; it is a continuous activity. Write automated tests alongside the code.Strict Definition of Done (DoD): Done doesn't mean "it works on my machine." Done means code is reviewed, tested, integrated, documented, and fully ready to deploy to users.

The Sprint Review is a collaborative working session held at the end of the Sprint to inspect the working software with stakeholders and adapt the product roadmap based on real data and feedback. It is a strategic pivot point, not just a one-way presentation.

Many teams treat the Review as a passive "demo theater." The team shows off a few features to stakeholders who nod silently, applaud politely, and log off. There is no discussion about market changes, user feedback, or roadmap adjustments. Worse, teams sometimes present slides instead of actual working software.

Treat this event as a vital feedback loop. You want stakeholders to actively engage with the product.

Show real software: Never demo from a PowerPoint or a staging environment that isn't representative of production. Inspect the actual increment.Discuss the misses: Be transparent about what you didn't achieve. If you missed the Sprint Goal, explain why and what you learned. This builds immense trust with the business.Adapt the backlog: Spend the last 15 minutes discussing what comes next. Did a stakeholder's feedback just invalidate an upcoming feature? Great. Adjust the product backlog right there.

The Sprint Retrospective is the most critical event in the Agile framework, designed for the team to inspect how they worked together and identify actionable ways to improve their process, tools, and relationships. It requires psychological safety and a commitment to incremental upgrades.

Teams run out of time and skip the Retrospective, or it devolves into a superficial "Start, Stop, Continue" exercise where the same minor complaints (e.g., "too many meetings") are raised Sprint after Sprint without anything ever changing. It becomes an empty ritual.

If you only have time for one Agile event, make it the Retrospective. This is where your team upgrades its own process engine.

Establish psychological safety: Start with the Prime Directive. Everyone must believe that regardless of what was discovered, everyone did the best job they could given what they knew at the time. No blame games.Focus on one clear action item: Do not try to fix ten things at once. Identify the single biggest bottleneck impacting the team right now. Assign accountability: An action item without an owner is just a wish. Assign a specific person to drive the process change in the very next Sprint, and put that action item physically on the Sprint backlog.

Scrum is not a rigid rulebook meant to micromanage developers. It is a continuous rhythm. When you shift your perspective from just checking off calendar events to genuinely mastering the Sprint Lifecycle, everything changes.

Planning becomes purposeful. Standups become tactical. Execution drives quality. Reviews generate crucial business pivots. Retrospectives build unstoppable teams.

Take a hard look at your current iteration. Which phase of the Sprint Lifecycle is currently your team's biggest bottleneck? Stop going through the motions. Identify the mechanical traps in your current process, apply these tactical fixes, and watch your tech team evolve from a stressed-out feature factory into a highly predictable, high-performing value engine.

MCP prompt injection: limit the agent’s blast radius

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 8 • Originally published at span.observer

MCP prompt injection is an instruction hidden in data an agent reads, which pushes it to call MCP tools without the user knowing. A ticket, a log line, a web page or a tool description is enough. No filter stops it reliably, so the realistic lever for an SRE or platform team is to reduce what the agent can do when it gets fooled.

This guide applies that idea in seven steps: inventory the tools, remove the ones that are not needed, make the rest narrow, bound the token, require human approval, separate reading from acting, then trace the calls. It complements our guide to securing an MCP server by focusing on one goal: the blast radius.

The OWASP Gen AI Security Project, in its LLM01:2025 Prompt Injection entry, separates two cases. Direct injection happens when a user’s input alters the model’s behaviour in ways nobody intended. Indirect injection happens when the model accepts external content, such as websites or files, that contains instructions. With MCP the second case is the common one: every read tool is an input channel.

The same entry lists seven strategies, including « Enforce privilege control and least privilege access », « Require human approval for high-risk actions » and « Segregate and identify external content ». The LLM06:2025 Excessive Agency entry covers the other half of the problem: damaging actions triggered by unexpected, ambiguous or manipulated model output, whatever the cause, hallucination or injection. It names three root causes: too much functionality, too many permissions, too much autonomy.

The SRE reasoning is the same as for an outage: not every cause can be prevented, so the impact is contained. An injected agent that only has a read tool on a narrow scope causes a limited confidentiality incident, not a deleted deployment. For an introduction to the protocol, see our explanation of the Model Context Protocol.

Build one table per agent: MCP server, tool, effect (read, write, send outside), identity used, human approval yes or no. Three questions classify each row:

An agent that answers yes to all three is the dangerous case: it reads text controlled by a third party, then has the means to act and to exfiltrate. The rest of the method breaks that combination.

OWASP words the first measure as « Minimize extensions ». Give the agent only the tools its task needs. A diagnostic assistant does not need to send e-mail; a writing assistant does not need to read production logs. Disable out-of-scope tools or whole servers in the client configuration, and redo the inventory whenever a server is added.

The MCP specification (version 2025-06-18, Tools page) requires servers to validate all tool inputs, implement access controls, rate limit invocations and sanitise outputs. OWASP recommends avoiding open-ended extensions: prefer a dedicated tool over generic shell access. Here is a pod restart tool that is narrow by construction:

The critical lines are the namespace allowlist, the strict name pattern, the absence of a shell and the maximum duration. Even if an injected instruction asks to « restart everything in production », the tool refuses. These guardrails live in code, not in the prompt, which matches the OWASP measure « Complete mediation »: authorisation is enforced by the downstream system and does not depend on the model’s judgement. For locally launched servers, see also isolating STDIO MCP servers.

The token defines the worst case. The « Security Best Practices » page of the MCP specification (draft version, read on 31 October 2026) describes a progressive model: a minimal initial scope set, for example mcp:tools-basic, limited to low-risk discovery and read operations, then targeted elevation through a WWW-Authenticate challenge with a scope parameter when a privileged operation is first attempted. It also lists common mistakes: publishing every possible scope, and using wildcard scopes such as * or full-access.

One token per use, with an expiry date and an owner, also keeps auditing to a readable perimeter. For observability servers, grant read rights on the data that matters and leave write rights to another token used by another agent (see the case of Dynatrace Assist and its guardrails).

According to the specification’s Tools page, there should always be a human in the loop with the ability to deny tool invocations. Clients should ask for confirmation on sensitive operations and show tool inputs to the user before calling the server, to avoid malicious or accidental exfiltration. OWASP says the same thing: « Require human approval for high-risk actions ».

In practice, sort tools into three levels: read-only without confirmation, reversible write with confirmation, irreversible or outbound action with confirmation and the arguments displayed. Approval fatigue is fought by reducing the number of sensitive tools, not by clicking faster.

The last cut in the combination from step 1: an agent that reads uncontrolled content (tickets, web pages, application logs) holds no write or send tool. It produces a summary or a proposal that a second agent or a human carries out. OWASP recommends to « Segregate and identify external content », and wrapping external content in explicit markers inside the context helps. This reduces the risk without removing it: a poisoned summary remains possible, hence the approval of step 5. For booby-trapped tool descriptions, see our guide to MCP tool poisoning.

The specification recommends that clients log tool usage for audit purposes. The OpenTelemetry semantic conventions for MCP (status Development) define mcp.method.name (required), gen_ai.tool.name (when the span relates to a specific tool), gen_ai.operation.name set to execute_tool for tool calls, and error.type set to tool_error when the result carries isError as true. The attributes gen_ai.tool.call.arguments and gen_ai.tool.call.result are opt-in, because they can hold sensitive data. Setup is covered in our guide to observing MCP agents with OpenTelemetry. It works with any OpenTelemetry backend, Dynatrace SaaS or Dynatrace Managed included, without relying on a feature specific to either.

8 SNES JRPGs That Reward Players for Exploring the World More Than Grinding Levels

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Murillo Zerbinatto is a JRPG enthusiast, writer, and editor. He has played over 300 RPGs during his gaming career, with 90% of them being JRPGs.

When the JRPG genre was born, its progression was as basic as it gets: kill enemies, gain levels, become stronger. The in-game economy also revolved around earning gold to buy new equipment, but leveling up was the sole driver of power. Many NES and SNES JRPGs demanded some grinding, and since most battles were random encounters, you naturally did it as you moved along.

From classic Final Fantasy to Undertale, these 10 JRPGs reward understanding your enemy more than stacking up levels

Occasionally, however, games emerged that promoted more horizontal progression, focusing less on experience points or level-ups and instead rewarding players who explored every nook and cranny of the world or engaged deeply with unique mechanics introduced in each title. Today, I will explore several SNES JRPGs that reward players for venturing out into the world or leveraging a game's unique feature rather than mindlessly grinding for levels.

No better way to kick things off than with a JRPG that doesn't even feature traditional levels. Illusion of Gaia is part of what is commonly called the Quintet Trilogy, a set of action RPGs crafted with tremendous care, each bringing its own distinct flavor. In Illusion of Gaia, we control Will, who can transform into two other distinct forms at various stages of the adventure: Freedan or Shadow.

To grow stronger, you need to clear out every enemy in a given room, which grants a jewel that permanently increases the protagonist's core attributes. It's a small bit of grind, but instead of defeating monsters repeatedly, you only need to defeat each enemy once per zone. It feels far more gratifying and rewarding because you maintain a clean, consistent sense of progression whenever you choose to clear rooms.

Star Ocean never received an official Western release on the SNES. Yet, it already featured several mechanics that would become defining staples for the franchise, especially if you look at the remake Star Ocean: First Departure. In tri-Ace's JRPG, gaining levels helps because it grants SP to upgrade crafting skills, letting you synthesize equipment far more powerful than anything you can find in shops. In a sense, this rewards players for exploring system mechanics rather than mindlessly slaying mobs.

The JRPG also introduced the Private Action system to build deeper connections with party members, alongside optional character recruitments and a dynamic relationship mechanic. The more you engage with everything the world of Star Ocean has to offer, the greater your rewards will be.

Terranigma is another standout title from the Quintet Trilogy, but unlike Illusion of Gaia, it actually features a traditional leveling system. Defeating enemies yields experience points, which raise attributes on level-up. Still, because Terranigma is an action RPG, execution and player skill matter far more than raw stats, so you never need to grind endlessly just to advance through the story.

Enemies occasionally drop gems you can use to buy new equipment, items, and spells, which is where the magic of exploration takes center stage. You can convert every Magirock you discover into a spell, with more options unlocking as you progress. After casting a spell, the Magirock reverts to its base form, requiring you to slot another spell into it, which directly rewards those who seek out hidden items. On top of that, the town-building sidequests form the emotional heart of Terranigma's narrative, uncovering their full potential only for players willing to track them down.

Breath of Fire is my favorite JRPG series, and each new entry has only refined its craft. While Breath of Fire II on the SNES requires a bit of early farming, especially during the Colosseum events and when Randy joins the party, a specific mechanic will empower your party members far more than any raw level-up: the Shaman system.

After you build your own village and advance through the story, Ryu can recruit Shamans hidden across the world. Using their power, you can fuse one or more Shamans with a party member, altering their form and making them exponentially stronger. For instance, Spar, who feels somewhat underwhelming by default, transforms into an absolute powerhouse through Shaman fusion, with similar massive power spikes applying to Katt and Sten. While levels remain relevant, tracking down Shamans and executing the correct fusions provides a far greater boost to overall combat strength.

The old school villains really were some of the best, as these SNES JRPGs demonstrate.

Character progression in Final Fantasy VI works completely differently depending on who is in your active party. Certain characters like Cyan, Sabin, and Terra acquire new skills and spells simply by leveling up, but others like Edgar, Gau, and Strago require you to explore the world, purchase specialized items, and battle rare monsters. Also, if you want to min-max your stats, you'll want to hold off on leveling until you acquire Espers.

Leveling up in Final Fantasy VI only increases base HP and MP until you unlock Espers. Once acquired, each Esper grants specific attribute bonuses when you level up, letting you tailor and optimize character builds. While min-maxing is not strictly necessary for a casual playthrough, it adds a fascinating layer of depth. Additionally, during the second half of the game in the World of Ruin, the entire party-gathering mechanic hinges on world exploration if you want to re-recruit your full roster.

One of the most common praises directed at Lufia II centers on its brilliant dungeon design. The game features unique puzzle gimmicks and exploration mechanics reminiscent of The Legend of Zelda, utilizing specialized tools to solve environmental hazards. Every dungeon comes packed with secret rooms containing rare loot. It even carries a touch of Metroidvania flair, as tools acquired in later dungeons can be taken back to earlier areas to uncover hidden rewards.

Dragon Eggs are also scattered across the world and yield stat-enhancing potions, rare fruits, or powerful rings once you deliver them. Most importantly, Lufia II features Capsule Monsters that battle alongside your party. They act independently and evolve by eating items from your inventory, meaning you need a vast hoard of gear to feed your companions. Raising them isn't about grinding levels but about exploring dungeons, accumulating gold, buying items, and tracking down rare drops.

If a JRPG strikes a clean balance between combat progression and world exploration, it is Super Mario RPG. Mindless grinding is rarely necessary. And while leveling up offers significant stat boosts, grinding levels can feel overly tedious. True to its Mario roots, the game packs plenty of environmental puzzles and secrets that make exploration well worth your jumping time.

For example, expanding your maximum Flower Points requires hunting down specific hidden items across the world. You can also hunt for Frog Coins, a rare currency you can trade for powerful accessories and items. Game-changing equipment also hides off the beaten path, such as the Lazy Shell, which bestows absurd defensive stats that practically trivialize combat. It's a prime example of how vital exploration can be in shaping your party's strength.

Among the endless stream of compliments Chrono Trigger has received over the years, praise for its pacing remains one of the most common. Chrono Trigger omitted random encounters entirely, an unheard-of design choice for its era. Furthermore, the game never demanded grinding, letting you push through main-story challenges smoothly without ever looking back. That holds true right up until you acquire the Epoch.

Once you gain access to the Epoch, you can travel between eras seamlessly to visit hidden islands packed with optional dungeons and formidable bosses. These optional challenges present a far steeper curve than the main narrative, but with proper preparation and strategy, they are completely manageable. These side areas contain the absolute best endgame equipment while offering deep narrative payoffs for the party's backstories. On top of that, the game features numerous alternate endings accessible via New Game+, letting you confront Lavos at different points in time and proving that Chrono Trigger's spirit of exploration extends directly into its narrative structure.

How much Netflix cost in 2007 vs. 2026

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The company is notorious for frequent price hikes.

Movie fans of a certain age may vividly remember the sad, somewhat rapid decline of the old video store. Whether your store of choice was the nearest Blockbuster or an independent shop, you probably smile at the nostalgia of heading to the video store on a Friday or Saturday night, anxiously hoping that a copy of the hottest new release was in stock. Or, better yet, just returned by another renter so you could swoop in and snag the last one.

Then streaming came along, and video stores around the world began fading into irrelevance, with locations closing as streaming services continued to grow in popularity. In 2006, Blockbuster — the undisputed king of video rental stores — made a last-ditch effort to stay current by offering Total Access, which allowed customers to exchange their mail-ordered DVD rentals in physical stores.

If you've only ever known Netflix as a streaming service on your smart TV, tablet or phone, you might find it interesting that, in 2007, Netflix could only be streamed on a Windows PC with Internet Explorer. And the video quality wasn't great. After all, high-definition (720p and 1080i) was just starting to take off as a mainstream viewing format in 2007. Netflix's streaming library of roughly 1,000 titles was pretty lean, as well. Its first original series was still five years away, and the first original Netflix movie didn't debut until 2015. But none of this stopped its prices from increasing — a true Netflix staple.

Over the next decade, Netflix added some new plans like Premium, Standard, Basic, Standard-definition-only, Basic with ads, Ad-Free and other iterations. And with those plans typically came annual price hikes, usually ranging from $1 to $3 more per month depending on the plan.

Today, Netflix offers three plans. Standard with ads costs $8.99 per month and includes ad-supported access to most (but not all) shows and movies in the Netflix catalog, with the ability to stream and download in 1080p HD on two supported devices at a time.

The Standard plan is $19.99 per month and includes everything in the Standard with ads package (minus the ads, of course), but with completely unlimited access to Netflix TV shows, movies and games. It also offers the option to add one extra member who doesn't live with you to the plan for another $7.99 per month to let them endure commercials, or $9.99 a month for the ad-free experience.

Finally, the Premium plan costs $26.99 monthly and offers unlimited access to the entire Netflix library, with video available in 4K Ultra HD + HDR with Netflix spatial audio, the ability to watch Netflix on four supported devices simultaneously and download on six supported devices, and the option to add up to two more members who don't reside with you for the same respective add-on prices per month as Standard.

Netflix has become notorious for its near-annual price hikes over the past 20 years. But if you really analyze what you get today compared to what you got in 2007, one could argue that today's plans give you better value for your money. Of course prices have increased since 2007. It's been 20 years, and our economy has been put through the wringer a number of times during that span.

Every time venture capitalists write a check, they’re secretly running numbers in their heads about different exit scenarios. It’s their job as fiduciaries. But nearly all will tell you (publicly, anyway) that every portfolio company is headed for an IPO.

Stephen Ward, the co-founder of Brightmind Partners, a two-year-old cybersecurity fund in New York, says otherwise outright. “You have to sell on the way up,” he says.

Ward’s unvarnished pragmatism is one reason he stands out. Another is the size of his firm’s debut fund late last year: a whopping $250 million fund, which he closed with co-founder Gur Talpaz. A third reason is his résumé, which includes a stint with the Coast Guard, nine years in the Secret Service, and CISO jobs at TIAA and Home Depot before he ever thought about investing.

One proof point that suggests the combination is working is SGNL, the identity security startup CrowdStrike agreed in January to buy for $740 million, 11 months after Brightmind led its Series A.

Now Ward is raising a second fund, targeting roughly the same size as the first one. This time, though, he’ll be doing it without Talpaz, who left in April along with another early colleague, leaving Ward as the firm’s sole general partner. He’s betting on himself at a moment when early-stage cybersecurity startups are raising more money than later-stage rivals for the first time in more than a decade.

His unemotional approach to exits traces back to an unlikely career. Ward says he grew up in northwest New Jersey between two farms, in a family that couldn’t afford college for his older brothers. After high school, he joined the Coast Guard, inspired by a commercial of a man jumping out of a helicopter, though he says he barely knew how to swim at the time. He spent four years in search and rescue.

Afterward, he studied criminal justice at Seton Hall in New Jersey and thought half-heartedly about law school. “I didn’t know what I wanted to do,” Ward told me last week over Zoom, wearing a pale hooded sweatshirt and looking more like a basketball coach than a venture capitalist. “I was kind of lost. I missed the military and the camaraderie and all of that.”

After graduating in 1998, he says, he mailed out some 300 résumés but no one responded. A Seton Hall professor who was also a chaplain to Secret Service personnel, as well as to members of the FBI and CIA, began introducing him around, and the Secret Service called first.

The Secret Service has two primary missions: protecting people and investigating financial crimes. Ward gravitated to investigations, then to “cyber” after the September 11 attacks in 2001. That year, he married a former college classmate, an American-born Syrian Muslim. Colleagues warned him the background check would be “miserable.” (They’re still happily married.)

What came next took Ward’s life down an unexpected path. Stationed in New York, he arrested a hacker who’d pulled off the largest credit card data theft of that era. Instead of charging him, however, Ward flipped him, turning him into an informant and running him for about three years. But the hacker kept hacking behind Ward’s back, and after he was caught again, he told the agency, as a diversionary tactic, that Ward was in on it.

By that time, Ward had left for the private sector, and he ultimately spent roughly 18 months clearing his name. “It was hard to get out of bed,” he said of that period, describing the investigation as “almost like a spouse cheating on you,” given his love of the agency. The hacker ultimately received a 20-year sentence, and Audible bought the rights to the story, turning it into an eight-episode podcast called “Operation Firewall.”

Ward’s corporate career has been a success by most measures, though he describes it as a series of jumps he really wasn’t qualified to make. His first private-sector job at J.P. Morgan, where he was recruited in 2007 to build a cybersecurity unit, was the hardest transition of his career, he says. He felt like “the dumbest person in the room,” missed his Secret Service crew, and twice thought about going back, unaware he was still under investigation. He stuck it out and now calls the investment bank the “best management school.”

Six years as CISO at the financial services company TIAA followed, and then two years at Home Depot starting in 2019. But in 2021, feeling burned out, he quit without anything lined up for the first time since age 16.

That chapter didn’t last long. The founders of Wiz, whose security software Ward had used at Home Depot, put him on a short list for a job at their company. Jeff Horing, a venture capitalist at Insight Partners, which had co-led Wiz’s $100 million Series A round in late 2020, interviewed Ward for the role, then offered him one at Insight instead.

It’s a good-news, bad-news story, given that Wiz was acquired last year by Google for $32 billion. When I asked him about it last week, Ward observed that the friend who took the Wiz job “made life-changing money,” but said it doesn’t bother him.

Regardless, he spent just three years at Insight, where he says he worked on Wiz, Abnormal Security, and Elisity, which IonQ agreed in October to buy for up to $1.3 billion. Then, at age 49, he thought about striking out on his own for the first time.

He says people told him it was a young man’s game. He left anyway and began cold-emailing limited partners. Ultimately, fewer than 20 LPs filled the fund, including Horing.

Ward started Brightmind in 2024 with Talpaz, a CrowdStrike corporate development executive whose deals overlapped with Ward’s during his time at Insight. Tayler Sipperly, another CrowdStrike corporate development alum, was the firm’s third employee.

The partnership ended this past spring. Asked what happened, Ward, using carefully worded legalese, cited “differences in opinions regarding operations and the future of Brightmind.” In August, Talpaz and Sipperly co-founded a new venture firm with Elia Zaitsev, CrowdStrike’s global CTO for the past three years. Called Cognition, it’s now reportedly targeting a $170 million first fund to back cybersecurity startups.

Sipperly says that he and Talpaz “continue to support the existing Brightmind portfolio companies as well as its limited partners” and that they “remain on the board of a number of the Brightmind portfolio companies.”

Brightmind’s first fund has made 14 investments to date, with one exit: that SNGL sale.

Some investors asked why Brightmind didn’t ride out that bet even longer. But Ward says he’s seen enough across his career that he feels confident in warning founders when they’re “going to race past the turn.”

In cybersecurity, he says, the best sales come between a startup’s third and seventh years. Indeed, he says his team tracks every security acquisition and what buyers are building versus buying. If Palo Alto Networks is likely to buy something, he says, CrowdStrike will experience “FOMO, then Zscaler is going to look over and go, ‘What did they just do? I should probably do the same thing.’”

Understanding how the pieces fit together is the trick, Ward explained, offering some hypotheticals. If CrowdStrike is building a product, he said, it’s unlikely to buy a portfolio company that makes something similar. Palo Alto Networks might love a company, but not if buying it would force its customers to switch away from something it bought five years ago.

“It’s very easy to go, ‘Good match,’ ‘terrible match,’” he said. “And that’s really what we spend a ton of time figuring out.”

He has timing on his side, at least. Early-stage cybersecurity startups are outraising their later-stage rivals for the first time in more than a decade, according to PitchBook, which counts $4.7 billion worth of seed-through-Series B deals through September.

That total is already ahead of the entirety of 2025, even though the number of deals fell to 248 from 358 last year. Seed rounds in particular are ballooning, per PitchBook. The average cybersecurity seed round jumped to $8.4 million in the first nine months of this year, from $5.54 million a year earlier.

Ward questions how much of that money is really going to AI security. By PitchBook’s count, 278 deals in the first half of 2026 were bucketed as such, but his team’s manual review found only 30 true ones. He doesn’t think Anthropic or OpenAI will “take over the security world,” but instead will do “just enough.”

For real-time protection, which he terms “zero second,” he favors cyber-specific models that run locally on a customer’s own systems, like that of Empirical Security. He also looks for startups that could trounce established companies that he considers vulnerable, name-checking 27-year-old Qualys and 24-year-old Tenable.

Ward says Brightmind’s second fund won’t be entirely cybersecurity, focusing about 20% on AI infrastructure deals. He expects a meaningful number of teams to come from Israel, too. (Roughly 40% of the debut fund’s startups are based in Tel Aviv.)

Asked about the founders he’s most comfortable backing, the response is immediate: second-time founders who stick together.

Brightmind is comprised of five people right now: COO Iva Messy, also a Seton Hall graduate, came from venture firms Felicis and Andreessen Horowitz (a16z) on the recommendation of Ward’s cousin, Carl Eschenbach, a former Workday CEO who is now back at Sequoia. Sven Wollschlaeger, a Midwesterner whom Ward calls one of the hardest-working people he’s known, spent three years alongside Ward at Insight and is a principal at Brightmind.

CTO Jamie Mneimneh, whom Ward first hired at Home Depot as a distinguished engineer, is the most technical person of the team. He built the cloud computing environment on AWS where the firm tests startups’ products. Mneimneh also built the AI agents that now source most of Brightmind’s deal flow, which is how the firm avoids hiring a bench of analysts and keeps management fees down, says Ward.

Taylor Cleveland, Ward’s chief of staff at Home Depot, joined the firm a few months ago.

For all the career changes, Ward says the real force driving him hasn’t changed. He grew up without money and still worries it could happen again, saying he’s being paid to produce a “3x net fund,” and he’s “not going to celebrate until we get that.”

Said Ward: “I don’t know what’s wrong with me. I have a level of being very naive to like, not being qualified to do something. I’ve just always looked at people and been like, ‘Okay, you know more than me today, but tomorrow, it’s going to be a little less, and the day after that, it’s going to be less, and eventually, I’m going to catch you and surpass you, and I’m going to know more than you.’ That’s how competitive I am.”

Texas A&M and Tennessee start time, TV channel finalized

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The game between the Texas A&M Aggies and Tennessee Volunteers is set to start at 2 p.m. CT on Saturday, Nov. 14, in Kyle Field, the Aggies announced Sunday on social media. The game will air on ESPN.

Texas A&M will be looking to snap a two-game losing streak after losing to the South Carolina Gamecocks 17-9 on Saturday. The Aggies are now 4-5 overall and 1-5 in SEC play. The offense has been unable to get going in SEC play and is averaging just 16.0 points per game against conference opponents.

Tennessee is coming into the week with plenty of momentum after crushing the Kentucky Wildcats 34-6 on Saturday. They improved to 7-2 with the win and continue to make their case for a spot in the College Football Playoff. The Vols are just 2-1 on the road this season, with Texas A&M going 4-1 at home. This game has the makings of a potential trap game, with them set to play the LSU Tigers the week after in a potential playoff elimination game.

It will be the sixth meeting between Texas A&M and Tennessee. The Volunteers lead the series 3-2, winning the last meeting 20-13 in 2023. Texas A&M won the only other meeting at Kyle Field, 45-38 in 2016.
