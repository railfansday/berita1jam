While some lids from decades past were bulky and sometimes looked silly, modern road, mountain, and commuter helmets combine safety, performance, and style. While a helmet’s primary role is to protect your head and brain in a crash, the best bike helmets also look great, and some even improve your speed.

Helmet technology has improved considerably in the last decade. Plus, safety features once limited to high-end helmets have trickled down to affordable models. You probably already know where you like to ride. Perhaps you ride on paved roads, or maybe on gravel and dirt. Other cyclists prefer riding mountain bikes in the woods or on the rail trail with the family.

Helmets are generally grouped by cycling discipline—road, gravel, mountain, commuting, or recreational. But don’t feel boxed in by those labels—You can wear a mountain bike lid for gravel rides, commute in a lightweight road helmet, or don a commuter helmet for weekend rides on the bike path. The best helmet is the one that you will wear—something that fits well, feels good, and meets your budget.

However, it’s still helpful to understand what those category designations actually mean in terms of helmet features, ventilation, and protection:

You can spend as little as $30 for a helmet or more than $400 on a high-end race lid. Generally, pricier helmets are lighter and better ventilated. However, all helmets sold in the U.S. must meet the same CPSC safety standards.

For a deep dive into Virginia Tech’s independent helmet testing program, read Matt Phillip’s excellent story, “Virginia Tech’s New Helmet Ratings Raise the Bar. Here’s How the Tougher System Could Affect Your Next Choice.”

All helmets sold in the U.S. must meet a Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) standard. However, the standard was adopted in 1998. For example, it does not give specifics for mitigating rotational forces on the brain. It also does not lay down any standards for a full-face helmet’s chin bar.

Voluntary and third-party tests go beyond the CPSC standard. We like Virginia Tech’s STAR tests, which were developed with a more modern understanding of head injuries than the CPSC’s test. VA Tech’s protocols evaluate a helmet’s ability to mitigate linear acceleration and rotational velocity, then assign a ranking of zero to five stars. A helmet receives more stars if Virginia Tech’s testing indicates it does a better job of reducing injury. VA Tech only recommends helmets that receive four or five stars.

Outside of CPSC and VA Tech’s STAR, most reputable helmet makers have their own in-house test protocols. The specifics of those tests and the results are often proprietary and not shared with the public.

Remember, no controlled test can replicate every possible real-world crash, but wearing a helmet is always better than not wearing one. Additionally, manufacturers’ understanding of what makes an effective helmet improves every day, which means helmets are always improving.

Sizes vary by brand, and most companies offer fit guides to help you choose the best size for your head. Measure your head’s circumference to determine the best size. If you fall between sizes, choose the most comfortable helmet.

The correct size helmet will sit low and straight on your forehead. The retention system should take up some of the slack so the helmet sits securely on your head—you shouldn’t rely on cinching down the chin strap to hold the helmet in place. The right helmet will fit comfortably, and the retention system will easily snug up the fit.

Consider these other factors when choosing and purchasing a new helmet.

Typically, road helmets are the lightest and best ventilated helmets. In some cases, the vents even also enhance cooling (over wearing no helmet at all) by directing airflow through carefully designed shapes and vents and over your head. This also helps prevent your sunglasses from fogging. Other helmets with increased coverage, such as full-face and mountain bike helmets, often have fewer vents.

However, don’t get tricked into thinking that “more vents are better.” You can’t judge a helmet’s cooling ability only by its number of vents. The Giro Synthe, for example, has only 19 vents but is widely regarded as well-ventilated, while the Kask Valegro has a whopping 37 vents. The size and design of the vents, plus thoughtful internal channels, are more important than the quantity.

Weight needn’t be your biggest concern because when it comes to a helmet, the one you wear will always win out over the one you don’t. It comes down to comfort—you’re more likely to wear a helmet that won’t get in the way or weigh you down.

Lightweight helmets tend to feel more comfortable, especially on long or challenging rides. Both material choices and design aspects contribute to weight savings. High-tech helmets often save grams by incorporating intricate internal bridges and supports that allow airflow and keep the helmet together in a crash. But be prepared to pay a higher price for these innovations. Opting out of “extras” like visors and mounts will also reduce weight.

While we don’t recommend adorning your helmet with too many accessories, extras such as visors, GoPro mounts, and lights certainly have their place and time.

If you find yourself riding in the dark, light mounts can make your life easier and safer by illuminating the path ahead and providing a rear-facing blinker light to help drivers see you in low-light conditions. Bike commuters especially should consider light mounts to make the morning and evening transitions as smooth as possible. Helmet-mounted bike lights work great in conjunction with handlebar-mounted lights because they move with you when you turn your head.

For those who ride during daylight, visors provide a built-in sun shield, especially for mountain bikers or rec riders. Road riders may use a cycling cap under the helmet and eschew any helmet-related extras.

Most modern helmets come equipped with safety technology such as Mips, KinetiCore, Koroyd, and WaveCel.

These technologies are engineered to help reduce the forces transmitted to your brain from impacts and collisions in different ways. However, no system or technology can guarantee a better outcome in every situation.

When researching a helmet’s protection, consider rotational and direct-impact forces. Choose a lid that uses a time-tested safety system. Look for positive reviews from actual users, not only quotes from brands. Full-spectrum protection might cost more than the most bare-bones helmet, but it’s worth the price for frequent riders and anyone who ventures off the beaten path onto gnarly singletrack or into heavy traffic.

Mips (Multi-Directional Impact Protection System) is the most widely know helmet technology. It comes in several versions and is used by numerous helmet brands.The most common Mips tech uses a liner inside the helmet that allows your head and helmet to move relative to each other in the event of a crash. Upon impact, the helmet can move without pulling on your head, which claims to reduce brain trauma. Mips Spherical found on some Giro models is variant. The MIPS SL pads in the Specialized Prevail III is another example.

KinetiCore uses blocks and channels in the helmet’s EPS foam designed to create crumple zones that absorb energy under impact impact.Lazer claims KinetiCore improves ventilation, reduces weight, and limits plastic use in its helmets.

Koroyd uses miniature tubes thermo-welded to create a honeycomb shape. The material crumples in on itself upon impact. Smith typically applies Koroyd in a zonal manner, built into particular places on the helmet to optimize weight and space savings while protecting the most vulnerable parts of your head. Smith’s latest helmet models employ Koroyd and Mips for a double dose of leading-edge protection.

Used in four Trek helmet models, Wavecel is a 15mm-thick grid of layered polyester intended to crumple, flex, and glide on impact. Trek claims this system reduces forces on the rider’s head.

In addition to standard road, mountain, and commuter helmets, some brands offer full-face and e-bike-rated helmets.

Only the Netherlands has an e-bike-specific helmet standard (adopted there in 2017). NTA 8776 is a standard designed to protect against higher-impact speeds and covers a slightly larger part of the head.

In the U.S., there are currently no helmet requirements specific to e-bike use. However, several manufacturers sell commuter helmets built to the NTA 8776 standard. Two of our favorites are the Specialized Mode and the Smith Dispatch MIPS.

The helmets in this story are “half-shells” and cover the top of the head. Their coverage usually ends around the eyebrows and top of the ears, though some—particularly mountain bike helmets—extend to the back of the rider’s head.

Another style of helmet is the full face. As the name suggests, this style covers most of a rider’s head and has a chin bar to protect the rider’s jaw. A full-face helmet covers the ears and the back of the head, with a large opening in front that allows a rider to wear goggles.

Full-face helmets protect more of the rider’s head—particularly the mouth and jaw—but are heavier and less ventilated than traditional cycling helmets. Full face helmets are primarily used by mountain bikers—especially downhill and enduro racers—but all riders can benefit from the extra protection they provide.

Coverage area is one of the most important factors in choosing an air conditioner. Manufacturers rate units in square footage, but that number assumes an average ceiling height (roughly eight feet) and room insulation. If the room is especially hot in the summer (for example, it’s a finished attic or it has large, south-facing windows) we recommend bumping up to the next size rather than buying a unit that will run constantly and struggle to keep up.

An air conditioner’s cooling capacity is typically measured in British Thermal Units, or BTU, per hour. To gauge coverage, figure roughly 20 BTU per square foot of space. Most portable a/c units range from 5,000 to 20,000 BTUs, which puts their effective range roughly between 350 and 700 square feet. So when in doubt, size up—an oversized unit cycles off when it hits your target temperature, while an undersized one never stops trying.

Single-hose units essentially recirculate the room’s air to cool it and have one line that runs outdoors to expel exhaust. The catch is that expelling air creates a slight negative pressure in the room, which pulls in warm air from other parts of the house and from outside through gaps around doors and windows. It works, but it’s working against itself.

Dual-hose units solve this by pulling outside air in through one hose for cooling and pushing hot exhaust out through a second hose. The result is a more self-contained system that’s noticeably more efficient, especially in larger spaces. The tradeoff is price.

Because dual-hose models are more complex, they tend to be more expensive. If you only have a small space to cool, a portable a/c with a single hose may suit your needs just fine. For anything larger, the dual-hose design is worth seriously considering.

Hose length determines how far away from a window you can maneuver your portable air conditioner. If you set it up in a large room, or one where the nearest window is far from where most people gather, hose length becomes more important.

It also plays a key role in room setup. If the hose isn’t long enough to set the air conditioner in front of large furniture pieces, you may have to rearrange the room for the most effective cooling. Most hoses range anywhere from 50 to 90 inches fully extended. This should give you enough space to make adjustments and pull the air conditioner closer to the center of the room if needed.

Most portable air conditioners hover between 50 and 60 decibels while they’re running—that’s roughly the noise level of a normal conversation. In most cases, that’s fine during the day, but if you’re planning to run the unit in a bedroom overnight, those numbers start to matter.

Units that dip below 50 decibels in their quietest modes are on par with most ceiling fans and are easier to sleep through; a few on this list get impressively close to whisper-quiet. Pay attention to the decibel rating at the unit’s lowest fan setting, not just its average operating level.

Much like larger ductless mini-split air conditioners, some portable air conditioners include a heat pump. “If you’re shopping for an a/c unit because you have a space not currently cooled or heated, one of these models may warrant consideration,” says gear editor Bradley Ford. “Similarly, you may have a room that your HVAC system doesn’t reach effectively. Rooms like this are typically either too cold or hot, depending on the season. If this is the case, a unit with heating capabilities may be able to fill your HVAC gap.”

Every portable a/c pulls moisture out of the air, similar to home dehumidifiers. The question is what happens to it next. Budget units collect it in an internal pan that you’ll need to manually drain, sometimes daily in humid conditions.

Other units use self-evaporating technology to push moisture out through the exhaust hose with no bucket to remember. A few high-end models go even further, offering a continuous drainage port where you can attach a hose and never think about water again. If low-maintenance matters to you, the drainage method is a factor to consider.

There isn’t a clear superior option between window units and portable options. Choosing between the two largely depends on your home, windows, and personal needs. That said, window air conditioning units are generally faster at bringing temperatures down and run more efficiently than their portable counterparts.

Portable air conditioners have certainly closed the gap over time in terms of cooling ability, but still lag behind if that’s your number one priority.

Portable units stand out because of their convenience and ease of setup. If you don’t have compatible windows, get a portable unit. If you need quick cooling in a spot that generally doesn’t need air conditioning, get a portable unit. If you want just one machine for your entire apartment, get a portable unit.

Portable air conditioners are compatible with a lot more window openings than window units, so you can use them regardless of your apartment or home fixtures.

New portable a/c units are relatively energy efficient across the board. That said, it’s certainly a sliding scale. There are several ways in which energy efficiency is calculated for air conditioners, but the shorthand is if your a/c unit is Energy Star-certified, it’s worth getting.

For items that don’t have Energy Star certification, pay attention to the CEER, EER, and SEER numbers. In short, look for an air conditioner with a CEER above 10, with anything above 12 qualifying for Energy Star certification. Similarly, look out for a SEER above 14 in the north and 15 in the south as well as an EER above 10. These are general numbers, but serve as a basic guide when choosing between different options.

One of the biggest issues to solve with portable air conditioners is cooling efficiency. While most units generate a ton of cold air, leakage from the hose or exhaust port can work against that. There are two key things you can do: insulate the opening in the window and insulate the hose itself.

Many portable units provide foam pieces that fit in and around the window insert to minimize the amount of hot air that leaks in from outside. The hose itself won’t come with foam insulation pieces, but you can perform a similar trick by buying one online or by making one yourself. Some people use blankets while others have used everything from reflective sun shades to Amazon plastic packaging. The goal is to prevent heat from the tube from radiating into your living space.

There are two types of 3D printer built with home users in mind: FDM and resin. (While other types of 3D printers are available, they’re intended for industrial use, with a learning curve and price tag to match.) As a rule of thumb, FDM printers are faster and produce more rugged prints than resin, while resin printers excel at fine detail.

Fused deposition modeling printers (FDM) are like a hot glue gun for plastic. They feed a strand of plastic filament through a heated metal nozzle, laying out the print onto a heated metal bed. They’re cleaner and easier to use than resin, and require less safety precautions. They tend to have larger print volumes, but they don’t produce prints that are as highly detailed as resin.

Resin printers, on the other hand, create layers of material by dipping a flat bed into a vat of liquid resin, then curing a layer of material with UV light. (It’s similar to the process of making candles, where you dip a wick into wax repeatedly.) They are typically more complicated to use, as the models have to be washed and cured after printing is complete. Printer resins are toxic until cured, and using resin printers requires significant safety precautions. Resin printers produce highly detailed prints, prized by users ranging from tabletop gamers making miniatures to the dental offices, creating surgical implants and guides.

Both types of printing have their pros and cons, depending on your space, your style, and what items you want to print. Generally speaking, FMD printers are good for larger items, items that need to be more durable, or when you need to turn around prints quickly. They are also a good choice if you’ll be printing in areas with less ventilation or if you’ll have children or pets around while printing. Resin printers are a better choice if you want outstanding detail, and have room to set up a cleaning station for your prints.

FDM printers are usually described by the head movement (either CoreXY or bed-slinger) and whether or not it’s enclosed or open frame. CoreXY printers move the head left to right, and front to back. On a bed-slinger, the head moves left to right, but front to back adjustments are made by moving (“slinging”) the bed itself.

Enclosed printers are just that–enclosed on all sides. These are typically CoreXY printers, and allow for higher temperatures and provide filtered vents for filaments that produce more fumes. They also usually cost more than open-bed printers.

To start printing, you’ll need some 3D models and software (called a slicer) to send them to your printer. Luckily, some of the best options are free.

Every 3D Printer uses some type of slicer software, which allows your printer to analyze objects and create a blueprint for making a 3D printed copy. True to its name, it creates a digital model and “slices” it into anywhere from one to a few thousand printable layers, creating the building blocks of a 3D print design. The result is a detailed set of instructions for printing, called G-code. When you load a design, your printer follows the G-code to assemble the design, layer by pre-sliced layer.

Printer manufacturers provide a proprietary slicer or an open-source slicer customized for their specific machines. For testing purposes, I used the slicer provided by the manufacturer. Honestly, once you get used to one slicer, they’ll all feel pretty similar.

Creating your own print designs isn’t necessary, though, if you don’t feel compelled. There’s no shortage of sites with free or paid 3D print designs that you can download and print. Once you have a little bit of experience printing, there’s a whole world of printable tools and products to explore within the 3D printing community.

When a plastic part breaks, there’s a good chance someone else has run into the same issue and created a 3D version of that part to download and print. Many printer manufacturers support some kind of community site, where users can upload, remix, and review 3D models.

Resin printers pose more risks, as resin itself is toxic, and should not be allowed to touch unprotected skin, and should only be used in an area where the fumes can be safely vented outdoors. Resin printers also use UV light to cure the prints, which can damage your eyes if not properly filtered.

FDM safety precautions are fairly straightforward. First, don’t stick your hand in the print area while the printer is running. This may sound obvious, but it’s surprisingly tempting to do, since sooner or later you’ll spot a bit of loose filament or some other bit of debris laying on the bed in the middle of a print. It’s very easy to reach in and grab that debris without pausing the print -- but the print head moves very fast and without warning. That nozzle is probably around 300 degrees celsius / 580 Fahrenheit, and having it slam into your knuckle can leave a puncture and a burn.

Second, make sure the room where you operate the printer is well ventilated. Proper ventilation is especially important when using high VOC filaments. These include acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) and acrylonitrile styrene acrylate (ASA), but the fumes will vary by manufacturer, so err on the side of caution.

Resin requires more extensive safety precautions. You’ll need to invest in PPE such as nitrile gloves, a respirator (use filters rated for VOCs and not just dust debris), and possibly UV safety glasses. When resin printing, venting is even more important. In most cases it’s easy to safely vent your 3D printer, but if you’re concerned, consider getting an air quality monitor to ensure that you’re working in a safe environment.

Traditional light bulbs, while nostalgic, are inefficient and waste a lot of energy.
