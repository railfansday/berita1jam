Thailand to block Bitget in December over unlicensed crypto trading

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Thailand’s SEC filed a criminal complaint against Bitget’s operator, with access to its website and app set to be blocked from Dec. 5.

Thailand’s securities regulator has filed a criminal complaint against BG Limited, the operator of cryptocurrency exchange Bitget, for allegedly operating without a license.

Thailand’s Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) said Thursday that it had notified the Ministry of Digital Economy and Society to block access to Bitget from Dec. 5. The restriction will cover Bitget’s website, other associated domains and its mobile app.

The regulator advised users to take action regarding assets held on the platform before the deadline.

According to the SEC, Bitget offered crypto trading services between 2022 and 2025, charged trading fees and promoted its services through Thai-language websites and social media channels. The regulator said these activities violated Thailand’s digital asset business licensing requirements.

The regulator filed the complaint with Thailand’s Economic Crime Suppression Division for investigation. Prosecutors will decide whether to bring charges, while courts will determine criminal liability.

Thailand’s Ministry of Digital Economy and Society has authority to restrict access to unlicensed crypto trading platforms under a 2025 decree aimed at protecting investors and combating financial crime.

Bitget did not immediately respond to Cointelegraph’s request for comment.

Binance Revokes API Keys, Security Issue Found at SignalPlus

📅 2026-11-05  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Binance has revoked all API keys associated with SignalPlus after the digital asset trading infrastructure provider identified a potential security vulnerability, prompting the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange to take measures to protect users.

The exchange confirmed Thursday that the decision was intended to prevent possible unauthorized access through connections established between Binance accounts and SignalPlus. Binance emphasized that its internal systems had not been compromised and that customer funds remained safe.

The precise nature of the security issue remains unclear. Neither the extent of the potential vulnerability nor the number of users affected has been publicly established. There has also been no confirmation that attackers accessed customer accounts or that the incident resulted in financial losses.

Following a potential security issue reported by third-party platform SignalPlus, we have proactively removed all API keys linked to SignalPlus as an immediate precautionary measure. Binance's own systems are unaffected. User funds remain safe. If you used SignalPlus, please log into your Binance account directly to review your positions and open orders. We recommend waiting until SignalPlus confirms full resolution before rebinding any API keys, and always enabling IP whitelisting. We also encourage all third-party platforms integrated with Binance to review their security controls, monitor for unusual activity and contact us immediately if they detect any concern

The development underscores the security challenges associated with third-party infrastructure in cryptocurrency markets, where trading platforms increasingly depend on external applications for execution, analytics and automation.

API-related vulnerabilities have previously created risks for exchange users. In 2022, the exposure of API credentials associated with trading platform 3Commas triggered security warnings and key revocations across multiple cryptocurrency exchanges.

Binance's security documentation advises users to revoke potentially compromised credentials immediately and restrict API permissions to necessary functions.

For affected SignalPlus users, restoring automated trading functionality may require generating new credentials once the integration is deemed secure.

SignalPlus provides trading infrastructure and analytical tools for cryptocurrency derivatives markets, allowing traders to manage positions and execute strategies across exchanges through API integrations.

These connections enable third-party applications to interact with exchange accounts without requiring users to transfer their assets off-platform. However, depending on the permissions granted, compromised API credentials can potentially expose accounts to unauthorized trading activity.

Binance has effectively severed existing integrations between its platform and SignalPlus. The measure could potentially disrupt automated trading strategies that rely on those connections.

West Ham manager Scott Parker’s comments following last night’s incredible comeback against Cardiff City were an absolute joy to listen to. Having watched the Hammers recover from three goals down to draw 3-3, I was already feeling considerably more optimistic about our prospects. But listening to Parker afterwards was almost as encouraging as the second-half performance itself.

In the modern game, much is made of tactics, and rightly so. But one thing I believe gets underplayed is not only man-management, but a head coach’s personality. And quite frankly, Parker has bundles of it.

I have to say, it’s a refreshing change from what we’ve seen in recent seasons. Nuno Espírito Santo was a softly spoken man, not prone to using a few words when considerably more would do, and the less said about Graham Potter as a leader and orator, the better at this point. Speaking in his post-match interview with West Ham’s official website, Parker described the transformation in his team after the break:

“It was a forceful team, an aggressive team, a front-footed team, which was obviously a big improvement.”

That’s exactly what I want to hear from a West Ham manager, and more importantly, it’s exactly what I want to see from a West Ham team.

As Martins morning round-up demonstrated, the difference between the two halves was extraordinary.

We went from a team seemingly incapable of stringing together a meaningful attack to one that looked determined to take the game to the opposition.

And Parker clearly recognised the difference.

He was particularly critical of the backwards passing and the lack of purpose in possession during the opening 45 minutes.

Then came another observation that I found particularly encouraging:

“There’s no point in just building, building, building.”

Again, music to my ears and we saw that with the first two goals from Mukasa and Engels.

Parker wants players taking risks, making forward runs, delivering crosses and getting bodies into the penalty area.

That doesn’t guarantee success, of course, but it’s a pretty good place to start.

There were 3,200 West Ham fans at Cardiff, and after the dreadful opening 45 minutes, they deserved something to cheer.

And I’ll tell you this much: if Parker communicates with supporters like this, one can only imagine how effective he might be when speaking to his players.

Of course, being a good speaker doesn’t automatically make somebody a good manager.

But I’ve always believed that one of the most immediate improvements West Ham needed was motivation.

We have talented players. I’ve said that repeatedly. What we’ve lacked is somebody capable of getting them playing with urgency, belief and a bit of fire in their bellies.

Parker certainly sounds like somebody who understands that.

He admitted that he couldn’t fully understand how the performance had changed so dramatically between the two halves.

It’s also worth remembering that Parker had only met the players at the airport that morning, having been confirmed as West Ham’s new head coach just hours before kick-off.

He has barely had an opportunity to work with them.

Judging his tactics, selections and long-term suitability can come later. There will be plenty of time for that.

But I’ve always thought there was one obvious and immediate improvement that could be made to this West Ham team, and that was getting the players motivated.

After listening to Parker, I have considerably fewer worries on that score.

I genuinely cannot wait for the next game.

New findings suggest that "food challenges" can help prevent children from being saddled with unnecessary allergy diagnoses.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

A peanut allergy diagnosis in infancy can mean years of avoiding the food or undergoing treatments to build tolerance. But a recent study suggests that more than one-third of babies thought to have the allergy may stop reacting badly to peanuts within months of that diagnosis.

In an analysis published in The Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology: In Practice, researchers conducted supervised "food challenges" with infants diagnosed with a peanut allergy. These challenges involved giving the babies increasing amounts of peanut protein, with the intention of confirming whether they indeed had an allergy.

The team found that more than 1 in 3 infants who underwent the food challenge tolerated the peanut protein, meaning they didn't experience symptoms of an allergic reaction.

"This rate is much higher than we thought," study co-author Dr. Timothy Brettig, a pediatric allergist at the National Allergy Centre of Excellence in Australia, told Live Science.

Between April 2024 and October 2025, Brettig and colleagues retroactively reviewed the medical records of 1,275 infants who had been referred to Australian public hospitals after having a reaction to peanuts before their first birthday. These babies had first been assessed by allergy specialists at a median of 10 months of age.

For their initial diagnosis, the babies had to have a history of an allergic reaction to peanuts and show evidence of peanut sensitization on an allergy test. These tests included a skin-prick test, in which peanut extract is placed on the skin before it's lightly pricked, to check for a local reaction; and blood tests that detect antibodies against peanut proteins.

"Children under 1 year of age are an interesting group, as their immune system is still in rapid development and existing allergy tests may not reflect their allergic status well," said Dr. Alexandra Santos, a professor of pediatric allergy at King's College London who was not involved in the study. While these tests can hint that a child is sensitized to certain foods, they don't necessarily confirm that a baby will have an allergic reaction upon eating said food; the tests can also give false positives.

Of the 1,275 infants, 778 later underwent a food challenge roughly six months after their first recorded allergic reaction; at that point, their median age was 13 months old. They were given up to seven gradually increasing doses of peanut protein, ranging from 15 to 1,300 milligrams. These doses were given at 15-minute intervals while medical staff monitored the children for signs of an allergic reaction.

Nearly 35% of the participants — 271 kids — completed the challenge without reacting.

"This is an excellent article," said Dr. Drew Bird, a pediatric allergist at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center who was not involved in the study. "The take-home message is to strongly consider a supervised oral food challenge when an infant's peanut allergy diagnosis is uncertain."

Bird emphasized that these challenges should ideally be done before a child starts oral immunotherapy, a treatment in which patients regularly consume carefully measured amounts of the food they are allergic to. The dose is gradually increased under medical supervision, with the goal of helping the patient tolerate greater amounts of the allergen and reducing the risk of reactions from accidental exposure.

An incorrect diagnosis can prompt families to unnecessarily avoid exposing their children to peanuts.

The starting dose is often determined through a similar oral food challenge to the one used in this study, though. So in theory, that challenge would help reveal whether the child needs treatment in the first place.

Bird cautioned that food challenges should be performed by a board-certified allergist-immunologist and an experienced allergy team in a setting equipped to treat anaphylaxis — a severe, potentially life-threatening allergic reaction.

In this study, 507 infants reacted during the food challenge, and of those, 61 developed anaphylaxis and were treated with epinephrine (the hormone in EpiPens).

"In an ideal world, with infinite resources," Santos said, "all patients except for those with a history of severe reactions would have a food challenge for diagnosis."

Because that is not the case, the families of infants whose allergy diagnosis is uncertain may be advised to avoid peanut products while they await a formal food challenge, she noted. But in the interim, they may miss an opportunity to help the child develop tolerance and, in turn, increase the risk that their allergy will persist.

Food challenges should be prioritized for infants whose allergy diagnoses are uncertain, she said. This would include babies who have low levels of peanut-specific antibodies in their blood or who show only small welts on skin-prick tests.
