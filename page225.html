Something Was Hiding in This Ancient Roman Capital. It Took a Drought to Finally Reveal It.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Drones caught the ghostly outlines of a 2,000-year-old city—before returning greenery erased them again.

A heatwave wreaked havoc upon all facets of life in Europe to some degree this past summer, but it also provided archaeologists in Switzerland with an interesting opportunity: the chance to view ancient Roman history in an entirely fresh way. With vegetation stripped back and parched, a team researching Aventicum, a once-thriving Roman city, took to the air via drone for a bird’s-eye view of the entire site. From the new vantage point, they discovered things they simply hadn’t ever known existed.

The site in Switzerland’s state of Vaud, which is now known as Avenches, looked different in the unusually hot and dry months of July and August, allowing archaeologists to “observe remains usually invisible underground,” according to a statement from those working at the Aventicum archaeological site. The airborne exploration revealed previously unseen buried structures, redrawing the map of what was once the capital of Roman Helvetica.

“These are the richest discoveries made there in the last 50 years, at least over the last two generations of archaeologists,” said Jordan Anastassov, archaeologist for Vaud and the University of Geneva, according to Reuters.

The archaeologist said less vigorous plant growth occurs atop buried structures. Combine that with a drought-stricken region and wilting vegetation this past summer, and the archaeologists were able to make a number of “observations that complement the data from the excavations in an exceptional way and show the full potential of surface prospecting methods.”

Aerial images not only showed the locations of houses (making up a full neighborhood), expansive villas, and roads, but also highlighted thermal baths and a potential rectangular temple site running nearly 100 feet in length that was lost to history. Some of the buildings appear in sections of the historic city that haven’t been excavated, and the large building sits on what was once thought to be the town’s outskirts.

Founded originally after Julius Caesar conquered local tribes 2,000 years ago, Aventicum had a successful run under Roman rule, eventually succumbing to Germanic invasions, starting in the third century. “We are now asking really interesting questions we wouldn’t otherwise be asking,” said Hugo Amoroso, an archaeologist on the project.

The team hopes on-the-ground exploration of the newly discovered areas will provide fuller detail and a richer history.

The summer heat proved critical in exposing the areas. By September, as temperatures returned to normal and vegetation started to flourish again, many of the newly seen sites slipped from view, although the pools discovered at the bath location were still visible in a struggling alfalfa field. “We aren’t going to wish for droughts,” said Pierre Blanc, director of the site’s excavations, “but we’re going to make the most of this and keep looking.”

The use of droughts to reveal lost sites isn’t a new concept. Going aerial simply helps cover more ground quickly. A post from the tourism website About Switzerland that Anastassov shared on his LinkedIn page noted that a 1976 drought played a role in the discovery of a Roman villa in the nearby municipality of Orbe.

With droughts increasing throughout the world, archaeologists are turning to aerial monitoring as a planned tactic, scheduling surveys systematically to better understand potential excavation sites and more accurately redraw ancient maps, just like at Aventicum.

Four States Are Voting on Abortion Access Today, and Couples Dating Across State Lines Face Tough Questions

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A partner’s politics matter, but so does the state where they live and the medical care its laws allow.

For anyone dating across state lines, checking where your match lives is more important than just calculating your future commute. This Election Day, abortion access is back on the ballot in four states, with voters in Idaho, Nevada, and Virginia considering measures to protect access while Missouri voters are deciding whether to undo a reproductive freedom amendment approved in 2024. These results will shape the health care available to many and revive tricky questions people have to consider when dating across state lines.

A partner’s views on abortion are a dealbreaker for many singles. The Kinsey Institute’s 2025 Singles in America research study found 52 percent of singles outright would not date someone who didn’t support abortion rights, considering it a deal-breaker. But in a world where state lines decide the level of healthcare available, simply sharing the same views on reproductive health with a partner is no longer enough.

Abortion is banned in 13 states, and you can date the most pro-choice person living in one of them without it changing what their local hospital is permitted to do. People in cross-state, long-distance relationships need to consider the laws when making life choices like moving or growing a family.

In a 2024 Institute for Women’s Policy Research poll, nearly 60 percent of parents and people planning to have kids in the next decade said they are worried about access to abortion, OB-GYN services, and prenatal care. Half of adults living in total-abortion ban states said they are concerned about access to fertility treatment. And a 2024 Redfin survey found about three-quarters of Democratic voters want to live somewhere abortion is legal.

The stakes go well beyond individual sentiment. Bans are changing who is even available to provide care at all. In Idaho, for example, after a near-total abortion ban took effect, the state lost more than a third of its practicing obstetricians. In Sandpoint, Idaho, all four of the local hospital's OB-GYNs left and its labor and delivery unit closed, sending pregnant patients more than 40 miles away to the nearest alternative. Nationally, more than 1.7 million women of reproductive age live in a "double desert" with neither abortion access nor maternity care nearby, per that same KFF Health News report.

The pipeline of future doctors in these states is shrinking, too. The Association of American Medical Colleges found OB-GYN residency applicants in states with complete abortion bans fell 10.5 percent in the first cycle after Dobbs (and many doctors tend to end up practicing where they train).

Fertility care is also at risk. In 2024, after Alabama’s Supreme Court ruled that frozen embryos count as children, at least three IVF providers in the state paused treatment. Lawmakers passed a fix weeks later, but the conversation has continued beyond Alabama.

Where someone lives can shape the reproductive care available to them, which makes it access an essential part of the conversation for anyone dating across state lines, not just whose city has a better job market or cheaper rent. For long-distance couples, deciding where to build a life together needs to involve asking the harder questions: If I needed care for a miscarriage, an ectopic pregnancy, or another complication, could I get it? And will there even be a doctor available to treat me?

Today’s election results won’t answer every question long-distance couples face, but they could help determine what care is available in the states where they build their lives. And for anyone dating across state lines, that makes this Election Day personal.

Novee’s External Surface Testing checks which exposed assets attackers can actually exploit

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Artificial intelligence penetration testing startup Novee Cyber Security Ltd. today added External Surface Testing to its platform so its AI-driven attacks can reach a customer’s entire internet-facing footprint.

External Surface Testing is aimed at the scramble after a critical vulnerability is disclosed, when security teams have to work out which exposed assets are affected and whether any can actually be exploited. Novee argues that attackers see a target as a single environment with many ways in, including forgotten subdomains and staging servers someone left exposed.

Conventional external attack surface management products catalog a company’s exposed assets and score them by severity. Netta Rager Dan, vice president of product at Novee, said those products “show you what’s exposed, but don’t give you the full picture of real risk.” In the company’s telling, security staff are left to validate long lists of exposures on their own.

Novee’s version needs nothing more than a domain name to get started. From there the software works outward the way an outside attacker would, and it keeps track of what it learns about each asset and service as it goes. A newly disclosed vulnerability gets run against a customer’s live systems to see whether anyone could actually exploit it.

Findings exported from other vendors’ attack surface tools go through the same check. Novee says the testing also catches flaws nobody had logged, chained attack paths among them, and the customer ends up with a short, prioritized list of validated issues backed by evidence.

Until now Novee’s testing has stopped at the application layer. External Surface Testing runs on the same platform as the company’s Application Pentesting product, so a host found during external discovery can be promoted straight into a full gray-box or white-box pentest. Whatever the discovery phase learned carries over. Novee claims no other fully autonomous AI pentesting platform covers the external attack surface as well as web, mobile and AI applications.

Limits are built in to keep the testing safe. Customers approve the scope before anything runs, and the probes come from outside the perimeter without agents or customer-supplied credentials. Exploit attempts don’t use destructive payloads. Traffic is held under configured caps so testing doesn’t cause load spikes or bury the security operations center in noise.

Former national-level offensive security operators Ido Geffen, Gon Chalamish and Omer Ninburg founded Novee in May 2025. YL Ventures LP, Canaan Partners and Zeev Ventures LP backed the company when it launched in January with $51.5 million in funding.

External Surface Testing is available now as part of the Novee platform.

Whether you want nine-color PLA models, mix and match engineering filaments, or crazy squishable TPU shoes in a riot of color, this machine is a beast. If you don’t have low ceilings or a fear of step ladders, the H3C might be the only printer you’ll ever need.

The Bambu Lab H3C is the printer we’ve been waiting for: a true Bambu tool changer. It has nine “Swaptek” tools that use a technology similar to the H2C Vortek rack, except each nozzle is built into a lightweight, hardwired tool with a dedicated filament path. The H3C is Bambu’s fastest multicolor 3D printer, and though it reduces waste, it does not eliminate it.

The H3C comes with a surprising “old school” technology: the ability to print 2.85mm filament. This is in response to China’s increasing fascination with 3D-printed shoes made with a wide variety of squishy TPU. Bambu sent along a few spools of their new super soft 75A TPU to show off, and it does make for comfy footwear. Each unit comes with one special 2.85mm tool as a standard feature, though you can buy a “normal” 1.75mm tool for $59 if you’re not into the novelty of phat filament.

You will also be able to add cutting, drawing, embossing, and foil transfer tools to the system. These were not available at the time of review, but we’ve seen these accessories on the H2D and A2L. The 3D printer will NOT come with a laser variant.

Bambu is checking off yet another item from the user wish list: the ability to print with more than one nozzle size. Because this is more of a software upgrade, you’ll also gain the ability to mix nozzles with Bambu’s other dual nozzle systems: the H2D, H2C, and X2D. The H2D will get the ability first, and the other printers will be updated by March.

The H3C has a 330 x 320 x 325mm build volume (the same as the H2C) and a massive “AMS” type module perched atop the main unit that holds nine spools. This gives it a somewhat towering mushroom shape that’s over three feet tall. It did not fit on my studio counter, which is a standard-sized IKEA kitchen countertop with a filament rack three feet off the surface. When we parked it on my industrial-grade shelving unit next to the H2D, even my 6’4” husband needed a step stool to load filament. Bambu was prepared for this problem and created a special rolling cart for the machine to make it more accessible. This is the first time the company has offered custom furniture for one of their printers and is an optional purchase that I highly recommend.

All nine tools are integrated into the bottom of the AMS 3H, which means the H3C only comes as a nine-tool 3D printer. There is no such thing as a single-color H3C. If nine colors aren’t enough for you, the printer can be hooked up to as many as four “normal” AMS units with the help of an optional buffer. Unfortunately, all outside AMS units need to run through the 5th tool slot, so you will lose the purge-free perk of the H3C.

The AMS 3H does not have motorized rollers, as retraction is unneeded on a tool changer. Seven of the slots have a TPU bypass, something we first saw on the single spool AMS HT. Two of the slots are nestled within an interior chamber for drying at up to 85°C. Though you can only dry two spools in the AMS 3H, you can run the dryer while printing, as it has its own power supply. The sealed dryer does not “leak” enough heat to dry the rest of the chamber…we checked. But that also means you don’t have to worry about the dryer melting a spool of PLA left in a nearby slot.

The FlexFeed extruder mounted on the toolhead is amazing. It can self-adjust to handle normal 1.75mm PLA or expand to push 2.85mm TPU quickly and without issue. The Bambu Lab H3C comes standard with eight .4mm nozzles for 1.75mm filament and one .4mm nozzle for 2.85mm filament. The nozzles are held in place with three screws and are pretty easy to swap out. You do need an entirely separate tool (with larger openings) for the 2.85mm filament. Because the H3C can print with multiple nozzle sizes, you could leave one or more tools dedicated to .2, .6, or .8 nozzle sizes if that fits your workflow.

For example, slots 6 and 8 are located inside the dryer and have a TPU bypass. You could dedicate a .6mm nozzle to these tools to better handle TPU, something like PA CF, which both benefit from toasty dry filament and an extra-wide nozzle. All nozzles for the H3C are hardened steel (HRC 55+) by default.

My H3C did have some pre-launch issues and is running Beta firmware that was updated several times during the review. The printer developed an annoying clicking sound coming from the left-side stepper motor, and one of the tools has a tendency to tangle and catch on the other Bowden tubes, causing it to dismount unexpectedly – and annoyingly – during long multicolor prints. I printed a few TPU spacers to try and solve this problem without much success as of yet.

The Bambu Lab H3C is launching with a retail price starting at $2,499 for all nine tools (eight standard 1.75mm and one 2.85mm). This is only $100 more than the launch price of the H2C. Additional tools and nozzle sizes will be available, though we do not have all the pricing at this time. A bundle that includes the Bambu Station (rolling cart) starts at $2,799. This makes the machine a bit on the spendy side, but for a helmet-class, honest-to-goodness tool changer with Bambu Lab’s ease of use, this 3D printer has earned its place on our Best 3D Printer of 2026 list.

The Bambu Lab H3C comes with a toolkit and the cables needed to assemble your 3D printer, as well as a few spare parts. The printer is preloaded with files, including a scraper that you can build yourself with the included parts. There’s also a quick start guide for both the base unit and the AMS 3H.

For the review, Bambu Lab provided spare printer tools for both 1.75mm and 2.85mm filaments, as well as several spools of PLA Palette and 2.85mm TPU 75A. We also received a Bambu Station for the review.

The H3C is an incredible piece of hardware. The Swaptek system has nine tools, each with its own filament path, heater, and hardened steel nozzle. The FlexFeed extruder has cooling and everything else. Each nozzle stays loaded and is preheated for lightning-fast filament swaps. I clocked it at six seconds from printing one color to printing the next.

The tools and nozzles come in both 1.75 mm and 2.85 mm filament sizes. Each tool has a nozzle attached by three screws, which can be swapped with different sizes. The H3C ships with one 2.85mm tool, but you have the option to purchase more tools if you want to customize your configuration. Though not ready at the time of this review, Bambu Lab will be offering tools for cutting, drawing, debossing, and foil transfer at a later date.

The tools dock on a front-mounted rack that retracts, allowing the build plate to be viewed from the front of the printer. This is a not-so-subtle jab at Prusa’s INDX, which has tools mounted to a fixed rack that one must peer through to see the first layer printing. It adds a layer of unneeded complexity, but it also looks pretty cool in action.

The photo below shows all nine tools lowered, using a maintenance setting. Normally, only one tool lowers at a time while in use.

Unlike previous Bambu Lab 3D printers, the AMS is required, not optional. Instead of a box that can be parked here or there, the AMS 3H is designed to screw into the top of the H3C. The plastic sides have a weird organic shape, but are actually quite practical – hidden side panels pop out for maintenance, allowing you to reach into the machine. The massive lid can be propped open, which is good because it’s most likely going to be over your head.

The tools ship attached to the bottom of the AMS. Though the H3C has the same build volume, and the same length and width as the H2 line, it is doubtful that an H2 can be upgraded to an H3C by simply plopping an AMS 3H on top of an older machine.

The AMS 3H has a separate chamber with a magnetically attached lid covering slots 6 and 8, which can dry filament at up to 85°C. All filament slots except 2 and 4 can bypass the AMS feeders through a hole in the bottom to directly feed TPU to the tools. The spool slots are numbered in a zigzag method, with 2,4,6 and 8 on the back row and 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 on the front. All the filament feeders are in the middle of the two rows, with the even-numbered spools feeding forward like always, and the odd spools feeding backward. It can get a bit complicated if you’re not using Bambu-branded filament with RFID tags. Being on the short side, I have to use a step stool to see the feeders.

The body of the Bambu Lab H3C looks similar to the rest of the H series except for the toolhead. The Flexfeed extruder can adjust automatically to push multiple filament widths and does well with some of the softest TPU I’ve tried.

The H3C has active chamber heating, up to 65°C, goes to 350°C at the hot end and 120°C on the bed, which makes printing PPA and PPS, nylons, ABS, and ASA easy. For the smellier filaments, it has active carbon and HEPA filtration.

The ventilation is sufficient to run lower-temperature filaments like PLA with the printer’s door closed.

The printer has an excellent camera that is used for AI detection on prints and can be monitored from the Bambu Handy app as well as the device page of Bambu Slicer. The camera can also capture time-lapses of prints when a USB stick is inserted in the slot behind the touch screen.

The Bambu Lab H3C has a footprint similar to the other H series printers but does require space on both sides to access the side panels of the AMS 3H. With the top-mounted AMS and the space required for the tool wiring and Bowden tubes, the Bambu Lab H3C requires a total of 1306 mm in height (over four feet) to operate comfortably. When resting on a standard-height counter or the 3ft tall racks we normally use for our printers, I needed a small ladder to reach the rear spools in the AMS 3H. Even my husband, who is 6’4,” had to stretch to reach the back. Shortly after I had the printer set up, I received the Bambu Station, which, at 23.5 inches tall on its wheels, is a great solution for keeping this printer in reach. If you don’t have a shelf or table the height of a coffee table, I recommend getting one.

Bambu is also introducing a companion E1 Air Purifier that syncs with the H3C and activates when you start a print job. We did not receive an E1 to test, and we’re not sure if the air cleaner will be offered as a standalone product. It is part of the premium $3,049 combo that includes the printer, rolling cart, and several spools of filament.

The Bambu Lab H3C arrives in two large assemblies: the main printer and all the things attached to the AMS 3H. After removing the packing material, zip ties holding the tool head in place, four screws holding the bed in place, and two retainers holding the rack in place, the AMS 3H can be hefted on top of the base unit. This is best done while the unit is on the floor or at least somewhere with a bunch of overhead clearance.

The touch screen attaches to the base unit without tools, and the ribbon cable is just long enough to reach the back of the screen. The nine tools have to be manually placed in the front rack, which takes some time until you learn how they attach. At least one needed to be attached once the printer was powered up.

The AMS 3H has its own power cord for the heater and a cable connecting it to the base. There are preinstalled screws to attach the Base unit to the AMS 3H, but I left these unused since removing the front panel requires tipping the AMS 3H back a bit.

The Bambu Lab H3C performs a bed level as part of its initial calibration suite and again before each print. This works flawlessly in practice.

When using non-TPU 1.75mm filament, the AMS 3H loads filament just like a regular AMS. Filament is inserted into the feeder, which automatically pulls it into the toolhead when that tool is selected. The RFID tag on Bambu Lab branded filament will automatically enter the filament type and color. Other filament brands need to be entered manually into the printer via the touchscreen.

The AMS 3H does not have powered rollers to retract the filament. You will need to select Unload from the filament screen, and the printer will cut the filament and retract it from the tool head. You will need to manually pull the filament out the rest of the way.

2.85mm filament and softer TPU will need to be manually fed through a port on the bottom of the AMS 3H. All filament slots except 2 and 4 have this port. The filament bypasses the tubing to feed directly into the tools. The standard Bowden tube must be removed from the tool and inserted into a holder on the side. For 1.75mm TPU, a small piece of Bowden tube is inserted into the top of the tool to help feed the filament correctly. 2.85mm filament can be inserted directly into the tool. Once inserted, filament using the bypass must be loaded manually.

Bambu Studio, based on PrusaSlicer and its forbear, is one of the best slicers out there. Working seamlessly with the printer for filament mapping, control, monitoring, and access to all of the files on Makerworld, it is a joy to use.

The H3C is a much faster machine than its predecessors because it does not need to reel filament back and forth from the AMS. However, it still uses a purge tower, which can waste a bit of time and filament. It only takes about six seconds to swap tools, which is quite remarkable.

The H3C did not come with filament samples, but Bambu Lab did send along several spools of PLA Palette and spools of 2.85 75A TPU to try. You’re going to need a bunch of filament for this beast, so check out our list of best filaments for 3D printing for suggestions.

To get the measure of the Bambu Lab H3C, I loaded up one of my favorite prints, a steampunk dragon by the designer Cinderwing 3D. I had previously printed it in eight PLA colors using two AMS modules on an Anycubic Kobra 3 Max, which is a bed slinger. That first print took 37 hours and 28 minutes and wasted 267g of filament in poop and purge.

Using a .2mm layer height and an average print speed of 140mm/s, the H3C cut that time down to a bit less than 24 hours, and only wasted 68g in its purge tower. The print quality is excellent, and all the colors came out clean. The main color is Prusa Pearl Mouse, Polymaker Panchroma Silver, and black, white, magenta, yellow, and cyan from Bambu’s new Palette PLA, which is actually designed for color mixing.

Eight colors were not enough. I swapped out the 2.85mm tool with the spare 1.75mm tool and printed a nine-color Princess Donut and Mongo from Dungeon Crawler Carl (my latest obsession). I have been using this print to test out other tool changers for a future comparison. The print came out fantastic, with super shiny silks and clean color transitions. I had to “hand paint” this in Bambu Slicer, which took a bit of time. The teeth on the print are a bit shaky, but that is due to the insane amount of support this model required.

This printed in 15 hours and 12 minutes, with a 152.6-gram prime tower, using a .2mm layer height and default settings. For comparison, the same print with the same settings but only 5 colors on the H2C took 24 hours and 44 minutes and used 111.4grams on its prime tower. The Prusa INDX took a similar amount of time (15 hours and 38 minutes) to run the same print in 8 colors, but only needed to waste 24grams of prime purge.

The Bambu Lab H3C is one of the best printers I’ve tested for printing TPU. The AMS 3H bypass ports require a tiny bit of set up, but they are much better than dealing with an external spool holder. With nine slots at my disposal, I can always have a spool of TPU ready to go.

My first TPU print was a strap hinge that combines normal 95A TPU with stiff PLA brackets. The designer wove the two filaments together manually, though you can make your own hinges flexible by using “beam interlocking” in the slicer.

I printed this in Polymaker red PLA and Polymaker red TPU 95A and the hinge is extremely strong. Printed with a .2mm layer height and running at an average speed of 115 mm/s the print completed in just over an hour.

The cartoon was an instant hit, and in 1955, Lilli was immortalized in plastic.

A year later, Ruth Handler discovered the Lilli doll while on a vacation in Europe. She bought several. One was for her teenage daughter, Barbara, to keep; the rest were brought back to Mattel, the toy company she had cofounded with her husband, Elliot, and businessman Harold Matson. Robin Gerber, author of Barbie and Ruth, wrote: “The doll looked too much like a prostitute for the company’s taste,” so Mattel’s design team (slightly) softened Lilli’s exaggerated proportions, renamed her Barbie, after Barbara, and before long, she would become the physical ideal for generations of young girls and women everywhere.

The pink carpet was tricked out with replicas of pieces from the film’s beach scenes: a lifeguard station, a towering lighthouse, faux palm trees, and a 3D ocean wave, with carpeting that shifted colors to mimic sand. But the real showstopper was the two-story replica of Barbie’s Dreamhouse, complete with a 2D faux pool, a DJ spinning tracks, and the trademark spiral slide. “We had to have that slide,” Wendy Robino, SVP of Warner Bros. marketing events, said at the time.

On Sunday, they wore pink: Writer and director Greta Gerwig, along with cast members Issa Rae, America Ferrera, Kate McKinnon, Ryan Gosling, and Michael Cera, turned out for the premiere in every shade of Barbie’s signature color, turning something long dismissed as girly into a celebration of individuality, joy, and girl power.

But the belle of the ball wasn’t one to blend in. After weeks of thinking pink, Margot Robbie—the film’s star and producer—arrived in black. Her custom Schiaparelli look re-created 1960s’ Solo in the Spotlight Barbie: a black sequined dress with a single red rose stitched onto her tulle skirt, matching opera gloves, Manolo Blahnik mules, and a diamond necklace. Movie Star Barbie fully committed to the bit, even clutching a pink handkerchief as she posed in front of a pink Corvette. Robbie’s blond hair was swept into a high ponytail—reminiscent of the one first worn by Lilli.

Following the official screening—marking the first time Barbie was revealed to the public—it was time to party.

The theme was “Barbie’s Blowout Disco Party.” Fittingly, one attraction invited guests inside a glittering, 12-foot pink disco ball. Justin Lefkovitch, whose company Mirrored Media, partnered with Mattel to create it, tells VF the entrance created “a shroud of mystery” for partygoers. Inside, a six-foot 1:1 Dreamhouse showcasing the new Barbie the Movie doll collection was surrounded by gold and silver streamers and over a dozen custom pink disco balls. The goal, Lefkovitch recalls, was to “transport guests into the world of Barbie.”

Nicki Minaj was the first guest to arrive at the party, event producer Austin Johnston tells VF—“five-foot-long hair all the way down to the floor.” A theme park designer by trade, Johnston and his company, AKJohnston, transformed the after-party into what he calls a “pop-up museum.” Barbie Land’s pastel-colored cul-de-sac was re-created as “a 65-foot-tall, 70-foot-wide” inflatable, its landmarks crammed together like a kitschy postcard. At the end of Barbie’s driveway, Mark Ronson presided over a hot pink DJ booth, playing hits from the soundtrack by Ice Spice, Dua Lipa (a.k.a. Mermaid Barbie), and Nicki Minaj—whom Johnston had to “very kindly” ask to get off Barbie’s Corvette.

Tucked beneath the second-floor mezzanine, a series of alcoves became larger-than-life photo sets, with photographers on hand to capture guests in action. Partygoers could retrace Barbie’s journey to the Real World—by bike, boat, and rocket ship—or step inside the Kendom man cave, Weird Barbie’s house, and life-size Barbie and Ken toy boxes. Not everyone hired to re-create Barbie Land had actually seen the movie by then; only a handful were invited to be part of that “really restricted small group,” Johnston says.

At the center of the room, guests sipped pink cocktails under a giant disco ball while an 11-foot, glitter-coated pink B twirled above. Another bar doubled as a fashion show, with four life-size mannequins dolled up for disco in original costumes from the film’s dance number, including Barbie’s gold sequined jumpsuit and the Kens’ matching white-and-gold tracksuits.

Obviously, the food was on theme too. Wolfgang Puck catered the evening’s Barbie-Q, serving grilled salmon, summer melon salad, roasted Pink Fir apple potatoes, char siu glazed grilled chicken, and charred tricolor cauliflower.

Even the night’s corporate sponsors knew better than to steal Barbie’s spotlight. OPI set up a nail salon, and Xbox provided a gaming station where players had their pick of two General Motors rides: Barbie’s Corvette or Ken’s Hummer. A highlight for Johnston was spotting Robbie racing a virtual Hummer while her Australian parents cheered her on. “No one paid her to do that,” he says. “She’s just like, I want to have a drink and play a video game at my premiere.”

For one night in Hollywood, women could be anything. And 84 years earlier, another city had hosted another rager in honor of another larger-than-life heroine. While Los Angeles turned pink for Barbie, Atlanta resurrected the Old South for Scarlett O’Hara.

All hell broke loose when British actor Vivien Leigh was cast as beloved heroine Scarlett O’Hara in the big-screen adaptation of Margaret Mitchell’s epic, Gone With the Wind. Fans and journalists were outraged that such an iconic Southern Belle would be played by a foreigner. At the time, Leigh was yet to star in a Hollywood film and was best known stateside for her affair with Laurence Olivier. Gossip columnist Hedda Hopper sniped: “Out of millions of American women, [producer David O. Selznick] couldn’t find one to suit him.”

Hopper was exaggerating, but not by a lot: During a grueling two-year nationwide search for his leading lady, Selznick reportedly met with over 1,400 actresses. After angry fan letters poured in, Selznick’s publicity machine kicked into overdrive—and eventually, the public came around to the idea that a refined Brit was somehow easier to swallow than a Yankee.

But perhaps the most convincing endorsement came from Leigh’s own leading man. Even Clark Gable, who initially considered Leigh “too demure” for the Southern firecracker, was won over by her dedication and declared she “was Scarlett every minute.” His own casting, meanwhile, had been a dream come true from the thousands who flooded MGM and Selznick’s offices with letters demanding he play Rhett Butler

The shoot was famously plagued by drama, and by the time filming wrapped, the cast and crew had earned themselves a party.

But Selznick had bigger fish to fry—namely, finishing the movie in time for its premiere. He left the party planning to local organizers and his trusted story editor Kay Brown, who had convinced her boss to buy the film rights in the first place. But since Atlanta was still tightly in the grip of Jim Crow, not everyone could join in. Leigh’s costars Hattie McDaniel and Butterfly McQueen were excluded from the festivities.

Selznick argued, unsuccessfully, with local organizers to get McDaniel on the guest list. Gable threatened to not attend the premiere in solidarity with his friend, The Hollywood Reporter stated, but McDaniel ultimately talked him out of it. “While it was unfortunate to exclude Mammy, it was the wisest policy,” Brown wrote, per Matthew Bernstein, former professor of film and media studies at Emory University.

Meanwhile, white Atlanta was gearing up for the party of the century. Former president Jimmy Carter later called it the South’s “biggest event” in his lifetime. According to Time, then governor Eurith D. Rivers was prepared to call in the National Guard, while Mayor Hartsfield urged every woman of Atlanta to dust off her hoop skirts and each man to “sprout a goatee, sideburns, and Kentucky colonel whiskers.”

Hollywood came in hot. Leigh and Gable made their grand entrance by limousine, waving to the 300,000 spectators who lined seven miles of streets in downtown Atlanta—including a number of nonagenarian Confederate veterans who were invited as special guests. Leigh arrived with Olivier while Gable brought his new wife, Carole Lombard. Fun fact: Gable reportedly hadn’t even wanted to play Rhett Butler until Louis B. Mayer offered to help bankroll his divorce so he could marry Lombard.

Of the motorcade, Time wrote: “Crowds larger than the combined armies that fought at Atlanta in July 1864 waved Confederate flags, tossed confetti till it seemed to be snowing, gave three different versions of the Rebel yell, whistled, cheered, goggled.” Leigh wept as the crowd welcomed the British actress “back home,” as if she’d just returned from battle.

The multiday event included a costume ball thrown by the Junior League that would have Abraham Lincoln rolling over in his grave. Decked out in hoop skirts, bustles, and Confederate-era uniforms, 6,000 guests descended on the Municipal Auditorium, which had been transformed into a recreation of the film’s Monster Bazaar. Black participants were relegated to serving or entertaining. Among them was the Ebenezer Baptist Church choir, whose singers reportedly performed dressed as enslaved people in front of a façade of Tara, a fictional plantation from the film. One of the singers was 10-year-old future civil rights hero Martin Luther King Jr.

Vivien Leigh arrived in a velvet gown designed by Walter Plunkett. Though Olivier escorted her costar Olivia de Havilland instead—to keep up appearances—Leigh was the belle of the ball, especially in hometown hero Margaret Mitchell’s absence. Eighteen years earlier, the young novelist had performed a risqué “apache dance” at a charity gala, which cost her her Junior League membership. Now, with Hollywood celebrating her masterpiece, some suspected Mitchell was getting the last laugh by snubbing the biggest party in town.

Young women fangirled all over Gable. According to Time, one woman fainted at the sight of him. Another was given a choice: a Christmas gift or a chance to meet the heartthrob. She chose Gable. After he kissed the 11-year-old, she gasped, “Now am I a woman?”

Governor Rivers declared December 15, 1939, a statewide holiday in honor of Gone With the Wind’s world premiere, the grand finale of the three-day extravaganza. Searchlights swept the Southern sky, and flashbulbs popped as 18,000 spectators crowded Peachtree Street. Even Loew’s Grand Theater got a glow-up. Its Romanesque façade was disguised as a grand Southern plantation—four towering white columns were erected to support a temporary façade with floral garlands, potted trees, and an enormous portrait of Leigh and Gable hung above the entrance.

Inside, all 2,000-plus seats sold out at $10 a pop—the equivalent of about $240 today. That’s not exactly chump change, considering Atlanta was still licking its wounds after the Great Depression. Young women in antebellum costumes greeted guests before Mayor Hartsfield took the stage to kick things off. Selznick and the film’s stars received standing ovations that lasted a full minute. But the loudest cheers were for Mitchell, now in attendance, who joined Leigh and Gable onstage.

The lights dimmed for nearly four hours of melodrama. When Rhett Butler delivered his immortal parting shot—“Frankly, my dear, I don’t give a damn”—the audience reportedly gasped, then erupted in applause as he left Scarlett in his dust.

Before Gone With the Wind—still the highest-grossing film of all time when adjusted for inflation—premieres were usually exclusive screenings in Hollywood or New York. 1939’s antebellum extravaganza turned an entire city into a movie set, setting the stage for modern spectacles like Barbie.

Both parties also offer a revealing snapshot of the societies that threw them. For Gone With the Wind, the industry glorified an imagined past; for Barbie, Hollywood toasted an imagined future. The Barbie premiere wasn’t just a love letter to a feminist film: It was also a giant corporate juggernaut designed to sell a product whose history was tangled up with the very stereotypes the film was questioning. It paid off: Following the film’s theatrical release, Mattel reported a third-quarter increase in worldwide gross billings of 27%, and Barbie toy sales in the United States increased 25% for the July-August combined months compared to the same two-month period a year prior. The immersive premiere, brand collaborations, worldwide activations, and fan participation blurred the line between marketing a movie and creating a cultural event.

The film also became Warner Bros.’ most successful release of all time, grossing $1.4 billion worldwide and making Greta Gerwig the first solo female director admitted into the hallowed halls of the billion-dollar boys club. The premiere celebrated a movie that changed the cultural conversation while handily outgrossing the testosterone-fueled Oppenheimer—a spectacular rebuke to the assumption that chick flicks have a glass ceiling.

Its timing made the celebration particularly potent. In that moment, Hollywood was still finding its footing after the pandemic; the premiere offered something audiences were missing. “It eventized going back to the movies. It eventized the premiere,” says Lefkovitch.

For one night, partygoers got to revel in a fairy tale where women ruled the world. But outside Barbie Land, the irony had all the subtlety of Ken’s Mojo Dojo Casa House. Hollywood was throwing an enormous party celebrating a fantasy of female empowerment while women were still fighting for equality in the real world. There is “definitely some hypocrisy,” agrees Lefkovitch, who made a point of thanking his all-female team.

Decades earlier, Atlanta had thrown its own enormous party, celebrating a fantasy of Southern womanhood while reinforcing the racial and social hierarchies of the real South—and conveniently leaving the horrors of slavery out of the picture. (The film itself did just that, presenting a fictional plantation where the most loyal individuals enjoyed being enslaved.) Black performers were welcome to entertain white audiences, but not to celebrate alongside them; not a single seat inside Loew’s Grand Theater was available to Black moviegoers.

Together, these indignities galvanized the local Black press. In a column for Atlanta Daily World, Reverend John Clarence Wright wrote, “As Atlanta goes mad with the Wind one hundred thousand of her citizens should become more thoughtful than ever of the course that must be pursued to gain their rights as free men and citizens in a land where the spirit of secession and slavery still lives.” With Black moviegoers barred from Southern theaters, the Pittsburgh Courier dryly observed, “Negro reaction… will have to wait until the film comes North.”

The premiere and the film’s success helped propel the NAACP to fight not just individual films, but toward a broader campaign to change Hollywood itself. In 1942, NAACP leader Walter White spoke directly to studio heads in an attempt to persuade them to steer away from demeaning Black stereotypes—like the ones onscreen in Gone With the Wind—and to increase the roles available to Black actors in Hollywood.

White’s approach infuriated the very woman whose absence from the Atlanta premiere had helped expose Hollywood’s hypocrisy: Hattie McDaniel. As a Black woman, McDaniel’s choices were limited—“I’d rather play a maid than be one,” she once said. Writing for Vanity Fair, Hadley Hall Meares noted that McDaniel was “incensed” that White had negotiated with the studios on her behalf, and believed she and her fellow Black actors deserved their own seat at the negotiating table.

McDaniel did get a seat at the 1940 Oscars—but it was at a small table at the edge of the room, separated from her Gone With the Wind costars. The ceremony, after all, was held at the Cocoanut Grove nightclub, which had a strict color line. When her name was called, McDaniel gushed: “I sincerely hope I shall always be a credit to my race and to the motion picture industry.”

Meares described McDaniel’s life as “a tightrope walk.” She was expected to satisfy the white executives who controlled her career, the Black audiences who wanted better representation, and her own ambitions as an actress.

Vivien Leigh also made history that night, becoming the first Brit to win the Oscar for best actress. She dedicated her award to Selznick for his “energy, courage, and very great kindness.” After all, he had been partly responsible for convincing an initially outraged public that Leigh was worthy to play their beloved Scarlett O’Hara. Years later, the Academy lavished Barbie with eight nominations, though it snubbed Gerwig for best director and Robbie for best actress. (Frankly, my dear, they didn’t give a damn about the two women who created Barbie Land.) If Hollywood could build an entire universe in which women ruled the world, why was it still so difficult to imagine one outside the movie theater?

Billie Eilish and Finneas O’Connell took home Barbie’s only Oscar, for best original song. Their winning composition poses Barbie’s existential question while echoing the expectations heaped upon Scarlett, Leigh, McDaniel, Robbie, and Gerwig: “What Was I Made For?”

It turns out the inspiration for Barbie—the quintessential American toy—wasn’t even made for little girls. Lilli dolls were sold to grown men in European tobacco shops and adult toy stores, becoming popular gag gifts. “Men… put them on their car dashboard, dangled them from the rearview mirror, or gave them to girlfriends as a suggestive keepsake,” Gerber wrote. Long before Barbie could be anything, she was something for men.

Eighty-four years apart, Hollywood threw two spectacular parties for women who refused to play by the rules. But the rules were still very much in place when the lights came back up.

A college student searching rocky glaciers in Yellowstone finally discovered one of the rarest insects in the world

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A University of Wyoming student discovered three rare ice crawlers in Yellowstone, marking the first known sightings of these cold-loving insects in Wyoming.

In summer 2026, a college student named Will Anderson found three rare bugs called ice crawlers on rocky glaciers in Yellowstone National Park.

As a student at the University of Wyoming, Anderson had spent the whole previous year looking for them without any luck. Ice crawlers are some of the rarest insects on Earth.

Ice crawlers belong to Grylloblattodea, one of the rarest orders of insects in the world. Only 35 species have been described, and many are known only from single localities.

Anderson is a biology major from Casper and a junior in the Wyoming Research Scholars Program. He works with Audrey Lindsteadt, a third-year Ph.D. student in the university’s Program in Ecology and Evolution.

“I am interested in this insect because there is so much not known about them, with their life history, habitat, and distribution all not being fully understood,” Anderson said.

“I also am passionate about and interested in their alpine habitats, which are understudied,” he added.

Starting in late July 2025, the pair surveyed 14 rock glaciers in the Red Mountains next to Heart Lake, in southwestern Yellowstone. They found no ice crawlers, even though Anderson also checked snowfields and set overnight traps.

Most snowfields had already melted by then. The area also had fewer glaciers than ideal, and most of them were small or inactive.

“Despite the close proximity to their known distribution and the plentiful suitable habitat, Gryllos have never been found in Wyoming,” Lindsteadt explained. “Therefore, Will hypothesized the rock glaciers and nearby snowfields that we are studying could have Gryllos.”

On a cold, snowy six-day backpacking trip in late June and early July 2026, Anderson found two ice crawlers near Antler Peak in northwest Yellowstone. Both turned up under rocks he flipped on a large rock glacier.

In mid-July, a mother grizzly and her two-year-old cub bluff-charged the pair and blocked their planned route.

They scrambled up Grey Peak instead and slid down to its two rock glaciers, where Anderson caught a third one. In August, he searched several alpine glaciers in Grand Teton National Park but came up empty.

Over the summer of 2026, he and Lindsteadt walked about 170 miles (274 kilometers).

“The specimens I collected in Wyoming are so far identified to the species Grylloblatta campodeiformis, which is the most widespread North American species, having populations in Montana, British Columbia, and Alberta,” Anderson noted.

He briefly kept two of the insects alive. He could not tell whether they were feeding on the dead insects and plant matter he gave them.

Ice crawlers are wingless insects that stay active in cold conditions, according to North Carolina State University entomologists. Most species are active below freezing and usually die above 50°F (10°C).

They develop through incomplete metamorphosis, from egg to nymph to adult, and their growth is slow. Some may need up to seven years to complete a single generation.

The University of Wyoming describes them as extremophiles that live at temperatures as low as 21°F (-6°C) in humid alpine settings.

By day, they hide under cool rocks near snow and ice. At night, they scavenge, eating mostly other invertebrates, including insects blown onto the snow, along with some plant material.

Little is known of their diet when they live more permanently under the snow, or in the soil through summer and winter.

The order lives in northeastern Asia, Siberia, the western U.S. and Canada. In North America, small, isolated populations occupy mountains and caves in several western states, British Columbia and Alberta.

Rock glaciers resemble alpine glaciers, but a layer of debris covers their ice, insulating and protecting it.

Lindsteadt suspects that ice crawlers prefer rock glaciers to non-glacial talus fields or snowfields because rock glaciers are colder, more humid and more permanent.

“Obviously, with just three species detections, we cannot confidently identify their habitat preferences,” Lindsteadt said. “But they at least point us in a promising direction.”

Yellowstone holds 58 known rock glaciers, and none had been visited or studied before this project. Next summer, the team plans to survey 17 rock glaciers in eastern Yellowstone and revisit others.

Ice crawlers are generally restricted to high-elevation glaciers or low-elevation caves with permanent ice, according to Purdue University entomologists.

The same entomologists warn that many of these habitats are at risk as glaciers and ice fields recede with climate change.

In Anderson’s view, the insects probably play a small part in these ecosystems because of their rarity, but they still belong to the food chains and nutrient cycles of their extreme habitats.

“More importantly, we can use ice crawlers to better understand how these ecosystems work and be more effective managers of them,” he argued.

He is also working on a paper he hopes to publish in a leading alpine or entomology journal.

Anderson will present the findings at the Entomological Society of America’s 74th annual meeting in Columbus, Ohio, on November 8 to 11.

The information was obtained from the University of Wyoming.

Nov. 3, 1957: The flight of Laika

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Less than a month after the launch of Sputnik 1, the Soviets launched Sputnik 2. The mission was readied quickly in order to keep the propaganda momentum from Sputnik 1 and to coincide with the 40th anniversary of the Bolshevik Revolution.

Carrying mixed-breed dog Laika, Sputnik 2 was the first flight to carry a living animal to Earth orbit. A small stray from the Moscow streets, Laika had undergone training to help her become accustomed her to the close quarters and gravity changes. Electrodes attached to the dog sent data back to Earth, for future research into the biological effects of space travel. Unfortunately, Laika died a few hours after takeoff – always the expected outcome of the mission, as the life-support systems on Sputnik 2 were imperfect and there was no plan for recovering the satellite or its passenger after launch. Sputnik 2 burned up on reentry in 1958, after its batteries died the previous year. In 2008, a statue commemorating Laika was erected in Moscow.

Travel credit cards are moving well beyond points and booking. At Skift Global Forum, Capital One Travel’s Sarah Moore said the opportunity lies in remaining useful throughout the trip by combining context, experiences, technology, and trust to build deeper relationships with travelers.

Travel credit cards have long competed on points, perks, and booking benefits. For Capital One Travel, the next phase is about extending the relationship further into the trip and finding more ways to stay useful after a customer books.

The company sees an opportunity to connect more parts of the travel journey, from planning and airport support to destination experiences. That approach draws on Capital One’s technology, booking data, travel products, and existing relationship with cardholders as their financial partner.

At Skift Global Forum in New York, Sarah Moore, head of Capital One Travel, discussed how the company is trying to become what she called a “travel companion” for its cardholders, with a role that extends before, during, and after the trip.

The Travel Card’s Next Act: From Earning Points to Erasing Friction

Capital One entered the travel business relatively recently, launching the Capital One Travel booking platform in 2021. Moore said arriving later gave the company an opportunity to look at what already existed and build around the expectations of a modern traveler.

That meant taking a technology-first approach, but also starting with the customer rather than simply recreating the traditional credit card travel model.

“We were able to really see what was out there in the marketplace and understand what the modern traveler was looking for,” Moore said.

That distinction matters as the boundaries between financial services, travel booking, loyalty, and trip planning continue to blur. A travel card may still sit in a traveler’s wallet, but the relationship increasingly extends into the rest of the trip.

For years, booking has been treated as the finish line for many travel companies. Moore sees it more as a starting point.

Capital One Travel wants to remain with customers after the booking transaction, whether that means helping a traveler navigate the airport by surfacing lounge information, providing flight updates, or helping them decide what to do once they reach their destination.

Being useful when something goes wrong can be particularly important, giving a company an opportunity to demonstrate the value of the relationship.

“Knowing when to step up is what builds trust,” Moore said.

For Capital One Travel, those moments can help turn a single booking into a longer-term customer relationship.

Moore also emphasized the importance of collaboration across the travel industry to better support travelers throughout their journeys. No single company can manage every aspect of the travel experience today, making partnerships across the broader ecosystem essential to delivering consistent support to customers throughout their journeys.

Delivering that kind of support also requires a different approach to personalization.

Travel brands have spent years collecting customer data, but Moore said the most useful form of personalization is contextual. The question is not simply who someone is, but what kind of trip they are taking right now.

A traveler taking a weekend trip with a spouse, for example, may want something entirely different from the same traveler on vacation with children.

Moore used her own family as an example. A trip with her husband might call for a relaxing stay at one of Capital One Travel’s Premier Collection hotels. Traveling with her children could mean booking a four-bedroom vacation rental, also available on Capital One Travel, with many homes offering the same luxury benefits found in the Premier and Lifestyle hotel collections. Even within the same family, different travelers may want completely different activities.

That same thinking extends to what travelers actually do once they arrive.

Moore said in a survey conducted by Capital One Travel, more than 70% of travelers are looking at some type of event or live experience, from sports and dining to other forms of cultural immersion. For some travelers, those experiences are increasingly shaping the trip itself rather than simply being added after the flight and hotel are booked.

“Travel is not just a flight and a hotel,” Moore said. “It’s what you’re actually doing when you land and what you’re engaging in when you’re there.”

Capital One has responded by building experiences into its broader cardholder proposition. Moore pointed to partnerships with Major League Baseball and touring artists, as well as more intimate experiences tied to food and culture that can be booked through activities on Capital One Travel.

One example is a culinary–driven trip led by chef José Andrés in his hometown of Asturias, Spain, exclusively for cardholders, giving them a more distinctive and immersive way to experience the destination.

Taken together, those efforts point to a broader shift in what a travel credit card can become. Moore said Capital One is focused on becoming a travel companion that supports cardholders from booking through servicing and the trip itself.

The company launched a dedicated Capital One Travel app within the past year, an example Moore cited of meeting travelers in the channels where they increasingly expect to manage their trips.

AI could push that evolution further. Travelers are increasingly finding inspiration, researching destinations, and making decisions through new interfaces. Moore said Capital One Travel will need to meet them in those places while maintaining the value and trust that come with its role as their financial partner.

That trust also creates an opportunity to use the information Capital One already has more effectively. Transaction data can provide signals about how some customers travel and what they may be looking for next, provided it is used to make the experience easier and more relevant.

For Moore, that is ultimately what “frictionless” travel means: understanding what a traveler needs and making it simple for them to get it.

This shift has implications beyond credit cards. Travel companies across the industry are trying to extend their relationship with customers beyond a single transaction. Doing that effectively requires more than adding another loyalty benefit or digital touchpoint.

It means understanding the context of each trip, being useful when travelers actually need help, and creating access that enhances the experience. It also requires accepting that the traveler, not the brand, is ultimately in control of the relationship.

For Capital One Travel, the opportunity is to make the card and the platform behind it more relevant throughout the journey. If that works, the booking becomes less of an endpoint and more of one moment in a much longer relationship.

There's nothing more in vogue right now than automakers soft-roader-ing their crossovers for weekend warrior types who enjoy a bit of camping or a dirt road every once in a while. Honda has TrailSport, Subaru has Wilderness, Toyota has TRD, GMC has AT4, and so on and so forth. Of course one of the largest automakers in the U.S. would want to get in on the action, so Hyundai spun up its own off-road sub-brand a few years back: XRT, which stands for Extreme Rugged Terrain.

What started out as more of a trim and appearance package on vehicles like the first-gen Palisade and fourth-gen Tucson and Santa Fe has evolved into offering styling changes with a bit of added capability when the going gets bumpy. With the introduction of Hyundai's XRT Pro trim on the Palisade and upcoming fifth-gen Tucson, that idea gets taken up another notch. However, after spending a day with both the 2026 Santa Fe XRT and 2026 Palisade XRT Pro off-roading in rural Missouri, I'm not totally sure Hyundai is all the way there yet. That's not to say the Korean automaker isn't on the right path and won't keep improving expeditiously, but as it stands right now, it may be just a step or two behind.

Full Disclosure: Hyundai flew me out to Bentonville, Arkansas, put me up in a very nice hotel and fed me, all so I could sample the 2026 Santa Fe XRT and 2026 Palisade XRT Pro.

It can be sort of tricky to evaluate the capability of a soft-roader like Hyundai's XRT fleet. Technically, both the Palisade XRT Pro and Santa Fe XRT were able to do everything that was thrown at them on this program, but they both lacked a bit of the sure-footedness I just experienced in Honda's Passport TrailSport, and that was on a far more technical and challenging course. That being said, what I did with the XRT siblings is probably 10 times as grueling as what the average Hyundai owner will do with their SUVs. There's also the issue of tires. While the Passport TrailSport is fitted with beefy General Grabber A/T Sport all-terrain rubber, the Santa Fe XRT makes do with less-aggressive Continental TerrainContact A/Ts, which leads to a good bit of wheel slippage.

Still, both XRTs do an admirable job of tackling trails, though rock crawlers they are not. The Palisade XRT Pro I drove was certainly the more capable of the two, thanks to its electronic limited-slip differential in the rear and a 360-degree camera that helped overcome its sheer size, but it never felt particularly at home, even if it did tackle every obstacle without much protest.

I say much protest because, while the car didn't get stuck at any point, the 4,575-pound crossover didn't exactly seem happy, and that especially goes for its Hyundai-built 8-speed transmission. While the naturally aspirated 3.5-liter V6 provided enough power (287 horsepower and 260 pound-feet of torque) to get up just about anything despite a lack of low-end oomph, I could tell that the 8-speed was none too pleased. I couldn't hear it on the road, but when switching to the Sand setting in its Trail drive mode, there was a noticeable whine coming from it on the throttle. A temperature readout in the gauge cluster also served as a constant reminder it was, uh, hot, and when I got out of the car, there was an unmistakable smell of hot transmission wafting through the air. From a functional standpoint, the XRT Pro didn't skip a beat, which is certainly a testament to the rest of the Palisade's drivetrain and its 8.4 inches of ground clearance; it bottomed out far less than I expected it to.

It's a similar story with the Santa Fe XRT. It may lack the e-LSD and all-out grunt of the Palisade, but it does make up for that somewhat with more low-end grunt courtesy of a torquey turbocharged 2.5-liter inline-4 that pumps out 277 hp and 311 lb-ft of torque. This drivetrain lent itself far better to the old-school way of getting yourself unstuck: give 'er some gas. Sure, there are 10 fewer horses than the Palisade's V6, but an extra 51 torques is massive.

Still, you can tell the Santa Fe is a far less luxury-oriented product than the Palisade, and I got bumped around a hell of a lot more than I did in the Palisade. Obviously, there's a price delta to consider, as the Santa Fe XRT starts at $43,940 including destination, and the Palisade XRT Pro has a far more costly $51,820 entrance price.

Currently, there are four XRT models on sale: the two I've been talking about, and then the Santa Cruz XRT and the Ioniq 5 XRT. I've driven the Ioniq and had a pretty good time with it, but came away feeling it was more of a styling exercise than anything else. Sure, it can handle some dirt, but that's just about it. While all these models do come with all-terrain tires, a bit more ground clearance, upped towing capacity, better approach and departure angles, and functional front recovery hooks, they're without a doubt behind industry frontrunners like Honda TrailSport and Subaru Wilderness when it comes to real capability.

I believe that's because XRT, as a brand, is so new. Hyundai's still trying to find its footing in an ever-more crowded world. It promises that its next product, based on the fifth-generation Tucson, will be its best yet, and far more than an appearance package. Even though the Tucson won't have the e-LSD of the Palisade XRT Pro, despite sharing the "Pro" moniker, it will have skid plates, all-terrain tires, 8.3 inches of ground clearance, some sick body cladding, and Hyundai's new off-road software called Smart Terrain Assist.

That program has the ability to analyze the terrain ahead of you, determining traction and slip conditions. From there, it provides visual guidance via green, orange or red "stuck zones," and shows the driver the best route to take. It works in concert with Automatic Terrain Detection (which has auto, snow, mud, sand and rock modes) with intelligent mode switching to remove any guesswork from off-roading. Clearly, Hyundai wants to make it as friendly to first-time off-roaders as possible.

Lest we forget, this same team is working on the body-on-frame Hyundai Boulder SUV, which will likely be powered by a hybrid or EREV drivetrain. It shares underpinnings with a future body-on-frame truck Hyundai is also working on, and it's targeted specifically at the American market.

While I'm not sure Hyundai's XRT division is quite the cream of the crop just yet when it comes to soft-roading — it might be a bit too focused on aesthetics, rather than actual performance — there's little doubt in my mind that it'll get to the front of the pack sooner rather than later. Just look at what this massive company has done with its N performance division, or what it has done with Genesis when it comes to luxury. When Hyundai puts its mind to something, it gets the job done, and I don't expect XRT to be any different.

Vulnerability management startup Cogent Security Inc. today released VR-1.1, a new version of its frontier reasoning model that writes fixes for newly disclosed software vulnerabilities.

On a public repair benchmark, the model outscored Anthropic PBC’s Claude Code and OpenAI Group PBC’s Codex.

The release goes after the part of vulnerability management that has not sped up. In July, research group Epoch Artificial Intelligence Inc. counted about 2,500 high- and critical-severity disclosures from major organizations, roughly five times the monthly record set before Anthropic announced Claude Mythos Preview in April.

Each repair still needs an engineer who understands the flaw well enough to change the code without breaking something else. Co-founder and Chief Executive Vineet Edupuganti called that stage “where security teams are stuck.”

To test the model on that repair work, Cogent turned to LiveCVEBench. Researchers at Harbin Institute of Technology and Kuaishou Technology built the benchmark from vulnerabilities disclosed in recent months and add new ones regularly. VR-1.1 produced a working repair for 57.9% of the benchmark’s 190 tasks. Claude Code and Codex tied at 45.3%.

Each task drops an agent into a real development environment with a vulnerability report and the affected codebase, in one of 14 programming languages. To pass, a patch has to close the vulnerability without breaking the software, which the benchmark checks with separate security and functionality tests.

Cogent blocked outbound network access during repairs and hid the grading tests until each agent was done. Inference budgets differed across the three systems, according to the full methodology Cogent Research has published.

Where the environment allows, VR-1.1 starts by writing its own proof of concept that triggers the reported flaw. From there the model follows the vulnerability through the affected code, including related paths that share the same weakness. The fix itself stays small, for example checking an index before use.

Every patch goes to a separate review that starts from a fresh context and hunts for a way around the fix. New bugs introduced by the change are part of that check. The model also leaves behind regression tests, which fail if a later code change reopens the flaw. When an attempt fails, the diagnosis and failure details carry into the next try.

“A patch might block the exact payload from a bug report, but leave the same vulnerability open via a different endpoint or break core functionality for users,” said Geng Sng, co-founder and chief technology officer of Cogent. “We designed VR-1.1 to check its own work for both problems before it finishes.”

Cogent introduced VR-1 in July to find and prove the attack paths an adversary could reach inside an enterprise. The new release extends the remediation capabilities that model introduced.

Founded in 2025, the company sells artificial intelligence agents that detect and fix vulnerabilities, with customers choosing whether a human signs off on each change. According to Cogent, Fortune 500 users have cut the exposure window for critical vulnerabilities by 97%.

Ethereum Layer 2 Aztec gets Tierra V6 upgrade to support faster private apps

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tierra V6 fixes a critical V5 security issue and speeds up private transaction proofs, with new apps launching or preparing to launch on Aztec.

Aztec Foundation, the team behind the privacy-focused Ethereum Layer 2 network Aztec, has launched Tierra, its Alpha V6 network upgrade, to fix security issues and make private transaction proofs faster.

The upgrade resolves vulnerabilities found in earlier versions, including a critical issue in V5, the foundation said. It also adds a new AI-based system for security testing.

When asked why the critical issue wasn't fixed sooner, an Aztec spokesperson said the fix required the whole network to move to a new version.

"Because Aztec is a decentralized network, if a critical vulnerability is discovered, the entire network must upgrade to the new version to implement the fix," the spokesperson told The Block.

Aztec has no central operator that can make the change on its own, the spokesperson said, adding that there are plans to make future upgrades faster if vulnerabilities are discovered.

Aztec said its network has more than 3,000 validators, no centralized sequencer, and no privileged viewing keys.

Besides the security fixes, Tierra makes private transaction proofs twice as fast, taking one second to generate on a native device, according to the foundation.

Messages from Ethereum can now reach Aztec in as little as 12 seconds, compared with an average of two minutes before, the foundation said, noting that the change speeds up deposits and other transactions between the networks.

"Aztec Tierra delivers a stronger, more secure foundation for applications that no single company or institution can control," Zac Williamson, co-founder and CEO of the Aztec Foundation, said in a statement. "Privacy shouldn't be something you have to request or trust someone else to protect. It should be built into the network."

Developers on Aztec can choose which functions of their apps are private and which are public, the foundation said, adding that private functions run and generate proofs on the user's device, while public functions run on the network.

The upgrade comes shortly after Aztec Labs relaunched zk.money, a self-custodial wallet for private crypto payments, three years after discontinuing the original product.

"With Aztec Tierra and zk.money, users can deposit directly from any Ethereum wallet and make lightning-fast private transactions without complicated bridging workflows," Joe Andrews, co-founder and CEO of Aztec Labs, said in a statement.

Other apps launching or preparing to launch include Galactica for private AI inference, Nemi for private perpetuals trading, and the private browser wallet Nulo, according to the announcement.

Xochi supports private intents and trading, while Nyx lets users make private payments to any Ethereum wallet. Olla offers liquid staking for the Aztec token.

Rolling Chat, a private message board based on Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin's Aztec experiments, is also on the list.

Aztec also introduced a new brand and website alongside Tierra, describing its aim as becoming the platform for "unstoppable private apps."
