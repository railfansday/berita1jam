Fintech startup Payhawk valued at $2B in $280M round

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Payhawk Ltd., a startup that helps enterprises manage their business expenses, has raised $280 million in funding.

The Series C round was led by Goldman Sachs’ growth equity arm. The bank was joined by Greenoaks, Lightspeed and more than a half-dozen others. Payhawk stated today that it’s now valued at $2 billion.

Issuing corporate credit cards to employees historically involved a weeks-long wait. Payhawk operates a platform that enables companies to create virtual cards in a few seconds and issue physical ones in five days. According to the company, customers can configure a card to expire automatically at the end of the project for which it’s needed.

Accounting teams can set spending limits to avoid cost overruns. Payhawk makes it possible to tie purchase caps to a business project, a specific merchant or a broad product category.

Companies can optionally connect their cards to Payhawk-operated business accounts. They support payments in more than 115 currencies. According to the software maker, its accounts’ currency exchange rates are up to 90% lower than those charged by traditional banks.

Syncing corporate card spending to a company’s accounting ledger is a multi-step process. First, workers must scan the receipt issued in connection with a purchase and send it to the finance team. From there, an accountant categorizes the purchase and verifies it through a process known as reconciliation.

Payhawk automates the workflow using an artificial intelligence agent. Additionally, it flags cost-cutting opportunities such as unnecessary software-as-a-service subscriptions.

A second AI agent speeds up procurement initiatives. It’s accessible through a chat interface that enables workers to request items in natural language. If a request is missing certain details, the agent can point out what additional information is needed. From there, it automatically seeks approvals from relevant executives.

Payhawk also automates a number of other tasks. It includes a tool that reduces the amount of work involved in changing business trip plans. Additionally, the company has built an AI engine that enables new users to customize its platform by uploading documents such as spending policies.

“Through our technology, finance departments can benefit from ‘zero-touch finance’, where neither the finance professional nor the employee deals with any admin work,” said Payhawk co-founder and Chief Executive Officer Hristo Borisov. “77% of our customers use our agents daily across financial control, procurement, travel and reporting to save time and money for their businesses.”

Payhawk says that it still has 70% of its previous funding round, a $100 million investment that closed in 2022. The company will use the proceeds from today’s Series C raise to enhance its AI capabilities.

Scale the Model, Scale the Harness: Unlocking a 2 Leap in Mathematical Reasoning

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Originally published in Generative AI on Medium.

I spent months building a system that wraps a language model in a checking loop so it can prove hard math theorems. Every step gets verified with real math tools, the system remembers what worked, and a second pass tries to break the proof before it counts.

Then I tested it on 300 hard math problems, and the results surprised me: the setup around the model matters as much as the model itself.

The honest headline is the last column: the loop fixed 16–33% of the problems each model got wrong on its own. Flash is the best model and improves least in multiples (1.64x, not 2x) but most in failures rescued.

The detail I'm proudest of: the local 27B catches factor-of-2 and boundary-case errors both big models miss. Five problems only Qwen solved; it beat Muse on 14 problems and Flash on 10. Flash is also 7.8x faster than Muse at the median solve (12.2 vs 95.0 minutes).

Proofs are graded by our own calibrated LLM judge ensemble, running in an isolated context — no human grading, no formal verification. All the eval logs are public so anyone can recount: certified counts reproduce exactly from the judgment files (Qwen 80, Muse 139, Flash 166).

How IP Intelligence Helps Catch Account Takeovers in Real Time

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A correct password no longer proves that the right person is logging in. Leaked credentials are traded in huge lists, and attackers use automated tools to try them across thousands of websites. When one works, the login looks completely normal: right username, right password, no failed attempts.

So how do you tell a real customer from an attacker holding their password? Look at where the login is coming from.

The login looks right, but the connection doesn’t

Every login request comes with an IP address, and that address carries context. With an IP intelligence lookup at sign-in, you can compare each attempt against what’s normal for that user and spot the outliers.

Unexpected location. A customer who always logs in from Hanoi suddenly appears in another country an hour later. IP geolocation flags this kind of “impossible travel” right away.

Unusual network. Real users log in from home Wi-Fi or a mobile carrier. An ASN lookup shows when a login comes from a hosting provider instead, and datacenter detection confirms it. That’s a common sign of automated credential-stuffing tools.

Hidden identity. Attackers often route their traffic through VPNs, proxies or Tor to avoid being traced or rate-limited. Detecting these doesn’t mean blocking every user who values privacy. It means adding a second check when other signals also look off.

Known bad actors. If an IP already has a history of abuse, such as spam, scanning or past attacks, a login from that address deserves extra scrutiny no matter how valid the password is.

The goal isn’t to lock people out. It’s to match your response to the risk. A familiar location on a home network can go straight through. A new country through a datacenter IP with abuse history might trigger a one-time code or an email confirmation. Real users barely notice, and attackers get stopped at the door.

HideIP is an IP Intelligence API that returns geolocation, ASN, VPN/proxy/Tor detection, datacenter identification and abuse history in a single call. Adding it to your login flow gives your security logic the context it needs to catch account takeovers before any damage is done.

How to Build a Desktop Barcode Scanner with Tauri and Rust

Tauri's official barcode-scanner plugin only runs on Android and iOS — it has no desktop implementation at all. If you are building a Tauri app for Windows, macOS, or Linux and need to read barcodes, the official plugin cannot help you. This tutorial builds the desktop scanner that fills that gap: a Tauri 2 app whose Rust backend decodes image files, PDFs, and a live webcam feed through FFI bindings to the Dynamsoft Barcode Reader SDK, while the UI is plain HTML/CSS/JS rendered in the system webview. The result decodes every barcode type the SDK supports, draws a quadrilateral over each one, and lists its format, text, and corner coordinates. Multi-page PDFs and TIFFs are previewed page by page, with on-screen arrows and left/right keys to switch pages.

What you'll build: A cross-platform Tauri 2 desktop barcode scanner with a Rust backend and a no-bundler web frontend. It opens images/PDFs through a native file dialog or a typed path, previews each document page with a cyan overlay on every decoded barcode, lists each barcode's format/text/coordinates, pages through multi-page PDFs and TIFFs, and streams a live webcam feed (Windows) — all powered by the same Dynamsoft Barcode Reader C++ core used by the Slint version of this sample.

This is the central reason this sample exists. Tauri ships an official barcode-scanner plugin, and its name suggests it is the obvious choice for any Tauri barcode app. It is not — at least not on desktop.

The plugin's documentation describes it as a way to "allow your mobile application to use the camera to scan QR codes, EAN-13 and other kinds of barcodes." Three details in the official setup confirm the desktop gap:

On Windows, macOS, and Linux there is simply no implementation to call. Beyond the platform gap, the plugin is also camera-only: it opens a native scan view (its windowed: true mode makes the webview transparent so the camera shows through) and returns what the camera sees. It does not decode a photo you already have on disk, does not read PDFs, and is not designed to return a batch of barcodes with precise corner coordinates from a still image.

This sample complements the official plugin rather than competing with it on mobile. It covers the desktop platforms the plugin leaves out, and adds the file/PDF decoding and multi-barcode coordinate output that a live mobile scan view does not provide:

The practical takeaway: if your Tauri app targets desktop, or needs to read barcodes from files rather than only from a live mobile camera, you need a native decoding backend. The rest of this article builds one with Rust and the Dynamsoft Barcode Reader SDK, and shows how to wire it into a Tauri webview UI.

Your AI Video Pipeline Is a Distributed System, Not a Magic Button

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A demo makes AI video generation look wonderfully simple: send a prompt, wait a little, receive an MP4.

Then you try to ship the same idea inside a real product.

A user closes the tab halfway through. A render takes longer than expected. The provider succeeds, but your webhook arrives twice. The marketing team changes one sentence and accidentally loses the previous version. A finished video contains the wrong product name, yet the workflow publishes it automatically.

That is the real pain in AI video today. Generating footage is only one part of the job. The harder problem is building a system that can survive waiting, failure, revision, and human judgment.

The most useful mental shift is to stop treating an AI video tool like a synchronous media endpoint. It behaves much more like a distributed build system.

When someone asks for “a 45-second vertical explainer for first-time customers,” the prompt may also imply a script, shot plan, voice, music, captions, timing, rendering, and export. Those parts depend on one another.

Change the narration and the caption timing may move. Shorten a scene and the music cue may no longer land correctly. Replace a product image and the layout may need another render. Even a tiny editorial request can create work across the rest of the pipeline.

This is why a single blocking HTTP request is a poor abstraction. Your application should submit intent, get a durable task identifier, and let the production job continue independently of the browser session.

A small TypeScript client might begin like this:

The important part is not the endpoint name. It is the contract: submission creates a resource. The video does not have to exist yet for the job to exist.

Teams often treat polling as an embarrassing temporary solution. It is not. For long-running media work, explicit polling can be simple, observable, and reliable when it is designed carefully.

The mistake is polling every second forever from a browser. A better design uses bounded retries, backoff, jitter, and a terminal state.

In production, this waiting loop usually belongs in a worker, not in the user's browser. The UI can reconnect whenever it wants and read the latest state from your database. That one decision removes a surprising amount of fragility.

It also makes the interface more honest. Instead of a spinner that says nothing, you can show meaningful phases such as planning, producing, reviewing, and ready. Users are generally comfortable waiting when they can see that the work has not disappeared.

Once a job can outlive a request, duplicate submissions become a real risk.

A user may double-click. Your server may time out after the provider accepted the request. A queue may redeliver a message. If every retry creates a fresh render, you can end up paying for several identical videos while showing only one of them.

Give each logical submission an idempotency key. It can be generated by the client or derived from a stable internal request ID. Store the relationship before starting expensive work.

The database still needs a unique constraint. Application-level checks alone can race under concurrent requests.

This sounds like ordinary backend engineering because it is ordinary backend engineering. AI does not remove the old reliability problems. It adds expensive, slow, probabilistic work to them.

The first render is rarely the final render. Someone will ask for a calmer voice, a shorter opening, a different visual style, or one corrected product detail.

If every change overwrites the same row, debugging becomes painful. You lose the prompt that created the output, the assets used at the time, and the reason one version was accepted while another was rejected.

Treat each revision as a new job with a parent:

Now the history tells a story. A local edit can point to its source render. A stylistic remix can branch without destroying the approved version. An extension can preserve the original video while adding a new ending.

This is useful for more than debugging. It gives reviewers a concrete diff in intent: “change the second scene's title” is very different from “reimagine the whole video as a playful social ad.” Your system can apply different review rules to each operation.

A finished URL is not enough for an operational system.

Keep the original brief, normalized settings, reference asset identifiers, provider task ID, model or workflow version, state transitions, error details, output metadata, and review decision. If the provider returns temporary URLs, copy approved assets into storage you control according to your retention policy.

You will want this information the first time a stakeholder asks why two similar prompts produced different results. You will also want it when a model changes, a render must be reproduced, or an old asset loses its usage rights.

Be careful with logs. Prompts can contain customer data, unreleased product names, or links to private media. Log identifiers and structured events by default; avoid dumping entire requests into a shared logging system.

A render can be technically successful and still be unusable.

Captions may misspell a brand. A generated scene may show the wrong interface. The voice may read a number incorrectly. A reference asset may be used in a way that violates its license. None of those problems necessarily produce an API error.

So keep generation status separate from editorial status.

A practical workflow can automatically check duration, dimensions, audio presence, black frames, and caption boundaries. A person should still review factual accuracy, brand fit, rights, and whether the video is actually worth publishing.

This is not anti-automation. It is how automation becomes trustworthy. Machines can remove repetitive assembly work while people spend their attention on decisions that require context.

The best AI video experience is not the one with the flashiest generate button. It is the one where users can leave and come back, understand what is happening, retry safely, compare revisions, and recover from failure without contacting support.

That experience comes from a few unglamorous choices: durable job records, explicit states, idempotent submission, bounded polling, immutable revisions, careful metadata, and a review gate before distribution.

Once those pieces exist, the model behind the pipeline becomes easier to change. You can improve generation quality, add another provider, or introduce a new editing mode without rebuilding the operational foundation.

The prompt may start the video, but the job system is what makes it a product.

Who called me about free Netflix? Treat an inbound one-time code as a bearer token

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“Who called me about free Netflix?” is easy to file as a sales annoyance. If a one-time code from a text message was read back during the same call, the more precise problem is a credential-handling one: an inbound voice path was used as if it were a bound channel for a bearer token.

Affiliation and AI note: This is promotional editorial from the website whocallme.co.uk, drafted with AI assistance. The worksheet below is a manual record design. It is not a deployed tool, a laboratory test, or a claim that the author contacted EE or Netflix.

Our page for 020 3807 0999 is presented as a landline, with an allocation hint of DIDWW Ireland Limited and a numbering area of London. Those labels do not identify the caller or a physical origin for the call. The page shows 6 searches and one user report. A “first seen” label of “4 days ago” is a page label, not the time the comment was written, and not a reason to describe the note as new this week.

The note is imported, and its original publication date is unknown. It carries a visitor category of Scam/Fraud. That category is a visitor label, not an independent finding about the number holder.

The visitor wrote that someone was “Pretending to be from EE”, “said I was getting free promotional netflix”, and that the visitor “gave them a code texted to me from Netflix”. The visitor “got suspicious when they asked me for my address”, “hung up and contacted both EE and Netflix directly who confirmed it was a scam and secured my accounts for me, saying i wouldnt be charged”.

I did not contact EE or Netflix, and I did not reproduce the call. The statements about confirmation, account securing, and the absence of a charge belong to the visitor’s account. They are not official conclusions I can restate as established facts, and they are not billing results I have inspected.

The public note about a promotional-code call is anonymous and unverified. A displayed caller ID can be spoofed. The named companies remain part of the visitor’s description. That is still enough material for an incident-record discussion, because the note already separates three events: a code was disclosed, later contact was made through channels the visitor chose, and the identity of the inbound caller was treated as untrustworthy.

In access-control terms, a one-time password or SMS code is usually a bearer instrument for a short window. The service that issued the code typically cannot see who is sitting next to the handset. Whoever presents the code may complete the action the code was meant to authorise: a login, a recovery step, a subscription change, or another account operation. This article does not claim to know which action, if any, was attempted in the visitor’s case. The engineering point is the property of the code, not a reconstructed attack path.

Reading the code aloud to an inbound caller is functionally close to pasting it into a form you do not control. The voice call feels like a conversation with a company. The code still travels as a secret that confers whatever power the issuer attached to it. Once disclosed, the useful record is that disclosure happened, not a copy of the digits. Do not store the code in a ticket.

A later question for an address, which is what made the visitor suspicious, is a separate request. It may indicate that the caller wanted more data. It does not tell us whether the code had already been used. The record should not invent a successful or failed redemption.

A bound channel, in this narrow sense, is a session or address that the account holder can recognise from a starting point they already control: an official app already signed in, a URL they typed from a known domain, a number printed on a bill they already hold. An inbound voice call does not become that bound channel merely because the speaker uses a company name, or because a text message from a familiar brand arrives during the same conversation.

SMS delivery of a code proves, at most, that a message reached a number the issuer had on file, or that a message appeared to come from a familiar sender. It does not bind the person currently speaking on the other line to that issuer. The inbound caller and the SMS are two channels. Mixing them — reading the SMS into the call — gives the unbound voice path the token.

This is why “they already sounded like EE” is not a channel check. The visitor later chose to contact EE and Netflix directly. In a record, that later choice should be written as official-channel contact reported by the visitor, not as proof automatically attached to the original inbound number.

The table below is a worksheet you can copy into a blank document. No telemetry, packet capture, or live test is required. Do not add fields for a password, a full address, or the code itself.

The example column is a manual illustration using the published wording. It is not a newly observed incident at your organisation, and it is not a charge record. If your form requires a date, add a review date that is clearly the date you filled the worksheet. Do not copy the page’s first-seen label into an incident-time field.

The fact that a code was disclosed is one decision point: secrets may have been exposed, so the account holder may want to use an official channel they already trust. The fact that the visitor later contacted EE and Netflix is a second decision point: that contact, if it occurred as written, is about account state, not about proving who the inbound caller was. The identity of the inbound caller is a third decision, and it can remain unknown without blocking the first two.

Human review is the right point to stop enrichment. A reviewer can check that the code itself was not pasted into the ticket, that no address was added “for completeness”, and that company names stay in the claimed_organisation field rather than in a vendor-ID field. If the reviewer was not a party to the call, they should not invent a test call in order to complete the form.

A collection of published UK telephone reports can supply wording for a source pointer when the source is a public page. It cannot bind an inbound caller to a brand, confirm a subscription charge, or replace the official channel the account holder already uses. Leave the caller’s identity unresolved if the evidence does not resolve it. The useful end state of this worksheet is a short, attributed record: a token was disclosed, later official contact was reported, and the inbound speaker remains unverified.

Lainey Wilson looked almost unrecognizable as she stepped out at the 2026 CMAs in Nashville Nov. 18 without her signature bell bottom pants and cowboy hat.

Lainey Wilson is stepping away from bell bottom country.

The “Heart Like a Truck” singer ditched her signature flare pants and Western hat for a classic Oscar de la Renta gown at the 2026 CMA Awards.

Wilson—who is serving as host for the evening’s festivities—looked stunning in a light yellow feathered gown, which featured a short train and a halter neckline. She kept her accessories simple for the Nov. 18 ceremony at the Bridgestone Arena in Nashville, only donning earrings and a few rings to finish off her simple look. (See all the stars at the show here.)

The 34-year-old—who is also nominated for Entertainer of the Year, Female Vocalist of the Year and Single of the Year for her hit “Somewhere Over Laredo”—has made no secret of her penchant for bell bottoms and a cowboy hat over the years, explaining that she was inspired to create her signature look after arriving in Nashville.

“I get to Nashville and I realized pretty quickly that as a female in this business, you have to do something that's a little outside of the box,” she shared during an interview with the Country Music Hall of Fame and Museum in April. “I wasn't gonna get away with wearing a ball cap and skinny jeans.”

The realization made her think back to her childhood, as she remembered the first pair of bell bottoms she got at the age of 9—which was also the year she wrote her first song.

“They were blue leopard print,” she recalled. “I remember putting them on as a child and just thinking—I just felt sassy. I felt like I could do anything.”

“If I still had those pants, I'd still be trying to fit in them ‘cause I felt good,” she quipped. "I felt just like I could take on the world. It was kind of like my superhero costume.”

And that superhero costume quickly became her signature, alongside the cowgirl hat that she started wearing in the late 2010s. In fact, the look has become so associated with the country star, that fans even dress up in their own version of the outfit at her shows.

“It's cool to look out into the crowd now and see everybody in their bell bottoms and their hats,” she shared. “It feels like a little bit of a movement and it just makes me happy.”
