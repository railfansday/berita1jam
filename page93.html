What Is Science, Really? Even Scientists Can’t Agree

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Even scientists in the same specialty disagree about science’s purpose, a survey finds, raising concerns that AI could automate one narrow approach at others’ expense.

Is science a search for objective truth, an exercise in creative problem-solving, or both? A study published in Royal Society Open Science found that scientists disagree about the meaning and purpose of science more than the general public does.

Study leader Marina Dubova, a former Santa Fe Institute postdoctoral researcher, is now an assistant professor of psychology at the University of California, Berkeley. Working with Princeton University researcher Qiawen E. Liu, she surveyed 91 scientists and 99 members of the public. Participants evaluated 18 historical descriptions of science, including science as detective work, a search for truth, and a mirror reflecting reality.

Scientists and the public generally agreed that science involves following curiosity and solving mysteries. The public tended to share an idealized picture of science as a reliable way to discover how the world works, placing less emphasis on creativity or uncertainty. That outlook could shape how people judge research and help explain their frustration when scientists cannot give firm answers or revise their conclusions as new evidence emerges.

The public’s views were closer to those of physicists and biologists than to those of researchers in psychology or the social sciences. One possible explanation, Dubova suggests, is that physics and biology feature more prominently in science education. More research is needed to test that connection.

Some scientists emphasized the search for objective truth, while others gave more weight to the judgment and creativity researchers bring to their work. They also disagreed about whether research follows established methods or requires improvisation. Few of the 18 descriptions received scientists’ full support, and they often had reservations even about ideas they generally agreed with.

“What was surprising to me was that there was quite a bit of disagreement between scientists, including scientists who are in the same subfield,” says Dubova. She suspects that researchers develop their approaches partly through experience, much as chefs develop different instincts about when to follow a recipe and when to improvise. Those differing conceptions of science could influence which discoveries researchers pursue.

That diversity also raises a question for efforts to automate scientific research. As researchers develop artificial intelligence systems capable of conducting science, whose understanding of the work will those systems embody? Dubova worries that automation could reinforce one narrow conception of science at the expense of other valuable approaches.

For Dubova, the study is an early step toward understanding how these outlooks shape research itself. “Don’t take the common understanding of science for granted,” she says. “Always ask. Always be curious about what someone has in mind.”

Reference: “Everyone knows what science is…until you ask” by Marina Dubova and Qiawen E. Liu, 11 November 2026, Royal Society Open Science. DOI: 10.1098/rsos.260933

The research was supported by SFI’s Lou Schuyler Seed Grant Fund, established in 2022 to support exploratory research by early-career scientists. Dubova used the funding to compensate study participants, helping make the research possible.

The MCU Ends Its Best Trilogy Since James Gunn's Guardians Of The Galaxy

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Trilogies work best when there’s a single, unifying vision behind them — George Lucas’ Star Wars trilogy, Sergio Leone’s Dollars trilogy, Peter Jackson’s Lord of the Rings trilogy — but there are so many cooks in the kitchen at Marvel Studios that singular visions are rare in the MCU. Typically, a series of solo movies will switch hands between a bunch of different directors with a bunch of different styles, going off in whatever direction the larger cinematic universe dictates. Thor’s trilogy started off with the Shakespearean heft of Kenneth Branagh and ended with the ironic self-awareness of Taika Waititi. Captain America’s trilogy is a pulpy World War II actioner followed by a paranoid conspiracy thriller followed by Avengers 2.5.

It’s rare that the MCU is able to deliver a perfect trilogy with a clear beginning, middle, and end, and no weak links. The Thor trilogy is let down by The Dark World. The Ant-Man trilogy is let down by Quantumania. The Deadpool trilogy is spread across two separate franchises. Tom Holland’s Spider-Man trilogy never had a weak link, but Far From Home is pretty unremarkable.

As it stands, the best trilogy in the MCU is James Gunn’s Guardians of the Galaxy series. Gunn wrote and directed all three Guardians movies, so they’re all held together by the same creative voice. The second movie has some pacing issues, and some not-so-subtle symbolism in the father-son story, but that’s the worst thing you could say about it. The first film was a breath of fresh air, the second one maintained the emotional weight to complement the comic book spectacle, and the third one was the best of the bunch, with a much deeper and darker story to tell.

With the release of the VisionQuest finale, the MCU has now completed its best trilogy since Guardians of the Galaxy. The WandaVision trilogy of streaming shows has been much more consistent and satisfying than most of the MCU’s movie trilogies.

WandaVision was a massive success, both critically and commercially, when it premiered in 2021. Marvel put its best foot forward in the brave new post-Endgame world, and audiences responded with such enthusiasm that this little sitcom homage became a cornerstone of the Multiverse Saga. WandaVision remained a one-and-done limited series, but it was followed up by two sequel shows.

After WandaVision told the story of Wanda Maximoff processing her grief over Vision’s death (in a roundabout way), Agatha All Along wondered if her witchy arch-nemesis Agatha Harkness could be redeemed, and VisionQuest wondered if sentient A.I.s and fabricated memories can enjoy lives and identities of their own. All three shows have been spectacular in their own way, and they’ve each made for a worthwhile chapter in the overall story.

Jac Schaeffer humanized Wanda and Vision in WandaVision — she took this witch and this robot who have fought supervillains with the Avengers and live in a magical sitcom fantasyland, and really dug into their emotional conflicts — and that spirit carried through into the subsequent spinoffs. Agatha All Along is as much about the fraught relationships between the witches in the coven as it is a campy homage to horror classics, and VisionQuest is as much a meditation on artificial intelligence as it is a quirky buddy comedy about bickering androids.

All in all, the WandaVision trilogy has been one of the strongest stories that the MCU has ever told, and it’s not over yet. The trilogy has ended, but VisionQuest’s post-credits scene seems to be setting Wanda up for a major role in Avengers: Doomsday. That final stinger showed that, after Wanda made the ultimate sacrifice to destroy the Darkhold in Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness, Doctor Doom showed up to steal her powers. So, we could be seeing the Scarlet Witch under Doom’s control.

JetBlue Airways is giving members of its TrueBlue loyalty program more opportunities to earn and redeem points through its commercial partnership with United Airlines. Known as Blue Sky, the arrangement connects the loyalty programs of both carriers, giving customers access to a much larger combined network. For JetBlue customers, one of the biggest advantages is the ability to spend their points on United Airlines flights, including services to destinations outside JetBlue's own network.

The partnership was announced in May 2025, with reciprocal loyalty earning and redemption launching five months later. It has since expanded to include additional travel benefits for qualifying elite members. The arrangement gives JetBlue an opportunity to increase the value of TrueBlue while providing United Airlines with access to another established customer base. It also offers travelers greater flexibility when planning domestic and international journeys.

JetBlue and United Airlines announced their Blue Sky commercial partnership in May 2025, bringing together two carriers with different network strengths. JetBlue focuses heavily on domestic services, leisure destinations, and important East Coast markets, while United Airlines operates an extensive domestic and international network. Combining their loyalty programs gives customers more travel opportunities without requiring either airline to operate every route itself.

For JetBlue, the partnership addresses a limitation of its TrueBlue program: its comparatively restricted destination network. Although JetBlue serves several international markets, including transatlantic services to London Heathrow Airport (LHR) and Amsterdam Schiphol Airport (AMS), it cannot independently match the geographical coverage of a major global airline. United Airlines, meanwhile, serves destinations across six continents, giving TrueBlue members considerably more opportunities to redeem their points.

The arrangement also benefits United Airlines by making its network more attractive to JetBlue customers. Rather than building a separate MileagePlus balance, travelers can use their existing TrueBlue points to book eligible United-operated flights. This encourages customers to remain engaged with JetBlue's loyalty program, even when their preferred destination is outside the airline's route network.

Importantly, Blue Sky is a commercial collaboration rather than a merger. Both carriers retain their individual brands, aircraft, operating structures, and loyalty programs. Customers continue earning either TrueBlue points or MileagePlus miles, depending on their chosen program. The partnership simply creates additional opportunities to use those currencies across both networks.

The earning arrangement allows TrueBlue members to collect points when traveling on eligible United Airlines flights. There is, however, an important distinction between earning points and transferring loyalty currencies. TrueBlue points cannot simply be transferred into a United MileagePlus account, and the two balances remain separate. Instead, members choose which program they want to earn with when making an eligible booking, preserving the independence of both loyalty schemes.

Netflix's New 12-Part Sci-Fi Mystery Already Feels Like a Masterpiece Just 3 Episodes In

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Even in a sunless world, something has to bloom

Even in a sunless world, something has to bloom

Some of my favorite science fiction stories take one seemingly impossible premise and explore all the ways it ripples through everyday life, from Gattaca to The Leftovers. Netflix's latest original anime is another perfect example of that trope.

From a surprise collaboration between two iconic anime studios, Sunrise and Shaft, Fool Night imagines a future world where the sun has long disappeared, leaving humanity with the unusual solution of turning volunteer humans into plants for oxygen. It's a bleak setup, made all the more gripping by a visual identity that constantly juxtaposes human decay with budding flora. Netflix only provided us with the first three episodes (out of 12 total) for review, and I'm entirely hooked. Between its sharp character work, unsettling world-building, and melancholic atmosphere, Fool Night is shaping up to be one of the most striking new shows of Fall.

Based on the manga by Kasumi Yasuda and directed by Atsuyuki Yukawa, Fool Night is a surprisingly intimate story set against an enormous environmental catastrophe. Rather than throwing viewers into an expansive science fiction mystery, Yukawa takes his time introducing its main characters and how their lives become intertwined by circumstances neither could have anticipated. It's a more methodical introduction than some might expect, offering plenty of room to establish the people at the center of a world literally steeped in darkness and comforted only by the discarded remains of the volunteers transformed into grotesque flora.

That’s the kind of setting Fool Night presents, a miserable environment governed by survival. With the sun gone, oxygen increasingly scarce, and most plant life dying off as a result, humanity has turned to a process called transfloration, allowing terminally ill volunteers to undergo a procedure that implants a seed inside their bodies for a payout of 10 million yen. Over the course of two years, that seed gradually grows until that person becomes what’s called a “spiritflor,” essentially a sprout that helps replenish the oxygen humans need to survive. It's an extraordinary solution to an extraordinary problem, although the implications are considerably more disturbing when you see what it actually looks like.

Spiritflor are everywhere in Fool Night. Their bodies litter the streets, fill abandoned alleyways, and even occupy the stairwells and corridors of apartment buildings. Some look almost beautiful, with sprouts and foliage emerging from what remains of their human bodies. Others are considerably more grotesque, their former appearances barely recognizable beneath the vegetation. They're like haunting reminders of the people who once lived there, transformed into something simultaneously beautiful and deeply unsettling. The worst part, of course, is that they're still alive.

The series introduces its grotesque imagery ingeniously in its opening sequence, establishing how accustomed the world’s inhabitants are to their surrounding environment. Standing outside a guitar store, a young child named Toshio Kamiya insists to his friend Yomiko Horai that he’ll be a musician one day. It’s pure wishful thinking, and Yomiko tries to ground him, even as Toshio beams at the thought of future stardom. As they’re walking down an alleyway, with Toshio making little guitar noises and pretending to play as he twirls around, the lighthearted moment is juxtaposed with the bodies of spiritflor lining the fences around them.

It's an especially eerie scene, with the children paying little mind to the grotesquery around them. They've presumably walked past these bodies countless times before, and that's perhaps the most disturbing part of the entire sequence. This is simply what life looks like now. It's an incredibly effective way of introducing the series' central conceit without stopping to explain every little detail. The world has adapted to something that should be horrifying, and the children have grown up knowing nothing else.

What makes Fool Night immediately compelling isn't just its grim premise and setting, but how sharply it uses the childhood friends Toshio and Yomiko to expose the class divide at the heart of its dying world. Now adults and reunited after years apart, the two represent opposite ends of transfloration’s bleak economy.

Toshio is trapped at the absolute bottom of society. A disillusioned factory worker crushed by poverty, systemic debt, and an abusive home life, he’s left with no viable future other than to sell his body to the state. In doing so, Toshio trades his remaining two years of humanity for a 10-million-yen payout that he barely gets to keep — only to get robbed almost immediately following the procedure. Yomiko, by contrast, sits comfortably on the clinical side of the glass as a researcher at Transflora Medical Facility 4, approving candidates and overseeing transfloration procedures, all while the morality of what she’s doing hangs over her head like a creeping vine gathering blight.

Their contrasted situations give the series its strongest dramatic friction. Early on, Yomiko is defined by a chilling professional detachment. When a colleague admits he feels like they killed someone after performing a procedure, she coolly insists they’ve "extended" a life, hiding behind the rhetoric of personal choice. It’s easy for her to rationalize transfloration when the patients are strangers, and that’s precisely why Toshio’s sudden appearance in her waiting room hits so hard. Reuniting after years apart only to find someone she genuinely cares for cornered into becoming a plant shatters her clinical distance, turning an abstract ethical debate into an immediate personal crisis.

Rather than bogging down in misery, however, the show smartly pivots their reunion into an intriguing partnership. When Yomiko discovers that the procedure has left Toshio with the bizarre ability to hear the voices of spiritflor, she pulls him into the facility's orbit. Alongside sour-faced Unsection Chief Jin Hiragishi, the two begin untangling the darker mysteries behind the program. It’s a sharp, character-first hook that grounds the show's high-concept sci-fi in genuine human stakes, one saddled against the backdrop of a looming murder investigation that could change the world forever.

If there's one thing that immediately separates Fool Night from other science fiction anime, it's the sheer confidence of its visual presentation — though, it definitely helps that two of the most legendary animation studios collaborated on the production. Sunrise, best known for its work on Gundam and Cowboy Bebop, and Shaft, the same studio behind the Monogatari series and Virgin Punk, have more than enough history behind them. Seeing them come together on a project of this caliber is mindblowing, and the results so far more than live up to the occasion.

What impresses me most isn't necessarily the scale of the animation, but how much attention the series pays to composition. There are so many shots that deliberately position characters in unexpected places, sometimes leaving enormous portions of the screen occupied by otherwise unimportant scenery. Rather than constantly following characters with the camera, Fool Night lets them exist within the environment, allowing the world around them to communicate just as much as the dialogue.

There's a particularly good example when Yomiko speaks with the front desk clerk about midway into the first episode, asking whether Toshio had gone through with the transfloration procedure. It's a relatively somber moment, with Yomiko and the desk clerk occupying just a small portion of the left side of the screen. Taking up almost the entirety of the composition is a plant just out of focus, filling the surrounding space rather than simply centering the shot on Yomiko’s back.

The same approach appears at the very start of Fool Night. As the child versions of Toshio and Yomiko walk down a fenced-off alleyway, Toshio launches into a short sprint like a musician would on a stage. Instead of directly following him, the camera tracks the fence on his left, moving along the cold steel as the jogging Toshio only takes up the right side of the frame. These are small decisions, but they help communicate the darker implications behind this captivating sci-fi dystopia and give the show a striking visual identity unlike anything I’ve seen before.

Fool Night isn't in a rush to lay all its cards on the table. While early episodes drop breadcrumbs of the wider conspiracy surrounding an investigation into bizarre murders involving plant-based lacerations and a shadowy organization attempting to stop transfloration procedures, the series largely holds its burgeoning mysteries at bay until the final moments of episode three. Rather than feeling sluggish, that restraint pays off by offering you a firm grasp on how transfloration has warped the show’s society and why these two leads are so deeply entangled within it.

Three episodes in, it's still too early to say where Fool Night will ultimately land, particularly with so much of its sunless world left unexplored, but what I've seen so far has me hooked. Its unsettling premise, wonderfully complicated characters, and gorgeous animation have already made for a compelling start. If the series can maintain this level of visual ambition while continuing to build on its central mysteries, I can't wait to see where Fool Night goes next.

The Design Fiend’s Guide to Tangier

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Moroccan city, a complex, culturally kaleidoscopic crossroads between Europe and Africa, is home to a savvy community of creatives whose stylish impact continues to grow. Word is out.

Above: The pool terrace at Villa Mabrouka, a 12-room hotel that was once the home of Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé, with gardens designed by Madison Cox.

Writing about Tangier in the 1950s, Truman Capote observed that “the days slide by less noticed than foam in a waterfall.” Decades later, this city on Morocco’s coast, across the Strait of Gibraltar from Spain, is significantly changed from Capote’s time, when it was an exotic retreat for café society’s demimonde, people such as Barbara Hutton and Malcolm Forbes. In recent years Tangier’s mosaic of villages—from the medina’s warren of whitewashed lanes to the Ville Moderne’s blocks of Art Deco towers to the hillside villas and gardens on Jebel Kebir—has been reinvigorated, in part by an enterprising mix of Tanjawis (the locals) and expats who continue to infuse the city with a fresh creative spirit.

Yet there is much that remains the same: the dreamy light that seduced artists from Eugène Delacroix to Henri Matisse to Mariano Fortuny, the overgrown gardens of casbah courtyards, and the cooling breezes coming off the confluence of Atlantic and Mediterranean waters. And there’s still plenty of the languorous lifestyle that captivated Capote.

“Tangier is all about settling in,” says Charlotte Claydon, whose whitewashed Moroccan Art Deco house has been in her family for more than a century. Known as Dar Sinclair, the residence sits in a grove of papyrus and lilies between thick palms and weeping willows. It’s one of numerous historic villas and townhouses renovated by international designers and other creatives who live here that can be rented. There’s arguably no better way to ease into the rhythms of daily life in Tangier. Pin Affleck, who arranges travel and accommodations through the Tangier Bureau, says that “going into a private house, you get the sense of being inside Tangier.”

When Claydon and her sister Jacquetta Wheeler began renting out Dar Sinclair, they received messages from guests worried about filling up a week. “We spent hours coming up with lists of activities, knowing full well they might only get to one or two,” Claydon says, noting that most guests make their own discoveries and end up on unplanned diversions.

Among the sisters’ can’t-miss activities: tea at Café de Paris, Sunday service at historic St. Andrew’s Church, and a camel ride, especially with kids, at Achakar Beach. A typical morning in Tangier might begin with breakfast under a banyan tree at El Morocco Club. Around 11 a.m. the markets buzz to life, including the legendary fish market in the Grand Socco, the Fondouk Chejra (weavers’ market), the antiques mall on Rue de Hollande, and the Casabarata flea market, where a sharp eye is required to spot the gems amid the seedy furniture and cheap fabrics.

Lunch in town at Alma Kitchen or El Dorado is a must for crispy falafel and plates of lamb chops. Later, as the sun sinks into the sea, it’s hard to beat the views from the roof of Villa Mabrouka, the onetime home of Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé that Jasper Conran transformed into hotel with gardens by Tangier resident Madison Cox. After a martini, the hotel’s L’Orangerie restaurant is a smart spot for a dinner of thistle tagine and grilled sole; down the street, the atmospheric Hamadi serves standout harissa soup and sizzling skewers.

Claydon and Wheeler, for their part, also advise guests to reserve time for relaxing and being transported at home, one of the great pleasures of staying in a design-centric private residence. Other places available to rent include the red-and-white-striped riad owned by decorator Gavin Houghton and photographer Boz Gagovski (who took most of the photos in this story), as well as antiques dealer Gordon Watson’s large ochre house. Both weave together Moorish and European details, with objects from Tangier and around the world that reflect the city’s cosmopolitan history. As Hamish Bowles noted in his foreword to the book Inside Tangier, “The magic of this place was that everyone who had chosen to make a home here brought their own fantasy with them.”

That book was produced by my husband, designer Nicolò Castellini Baldissera, and our home here, a 1929 townhouse named Casa Tosca, is filled with a worldly mix of objects and furnishings that we designed. Outside, our terraced garden was overseen by Umberto Pasti, whose own Tangier residence is a collection of 1930s bungalows set within meandering gardens of datura trees, rambling roses, and alocasia. For us, so much of the pleasure of this place is being at home and having people over. Creating the setting with local craftspeople and market finds becomes a pastime in itself.

It’s the same for Marco Scarani and Jamie Creel, whose house, Dar Zero, has had an illustrious lineage of owners who have contributed to its rich layers. The home is in the casbah, with a bushy fig tree and bougainvillea spilling from its courtyard over the front wall. Inside, iron klismos chairs from when designers Yves Vidal and Charles Sevigny lived there sit on well-worn bejmat and zellige tiles. There are also pieces of their own creation, such as brass bird-foot candlesticks they make in Tangier and sell in their New York shop, Creel & Gow. “There is a lot of transmission from one generation to another,” Scarani says. “Friends here inherit objects and even homes. We live in houses that have the spirits of the people who came before.”

For designers Frank de Biasi and Gene Meyer, it was the relationship with artisans that prompted them a few years ago to open Habibi Burton, their boutique where they offer pillows, painted tables, and a rotation of decorative objects, all sourced locally. “Making things for the shop gives me more freedom than making things for clients,” says de Biasi. “Because we aren’t designing to a deadline, I can have four artisans contribute to a single piece.” He also recommends Las Chicas for fashion, Kasbah Collective for regional crafts, and the bookshop Les Insolites.

Down the street, artist and curator Hicham Bouzid oversees Kiosk, a cultural salon and shop that hosts talks and exhibitions bringing together creatives from Africa, the Arab world, and Europe. It has a selection of books and artist prints, as well as a restaurant and live music. Bouzid is one of the native Tanjawis who are animating the city’s cultural life. Another is Yto Barrada, perhaps Morocco’s best-known contemporary artist, who founded the popular Cinéma Rif, where screenings of contemporary and classic films share the program with talks and other cultural events.

Across town Clos Nadir, the house shared by designer Jean-Louis Deniot and William Holloway, exists in a different world, perched among pines and looking out across the strait. Focusing on spaces for entertaining, Deniot placed a 30-foot dining table on a roof, while his living room is outfitted with a large fireplace, plush carpets, and a welcoming mix of furniture, mostly made locally. “Tangier is a land of artistic opportunity, a place for aesthetes, where stylistic adventures, contemplation, and the freedom to create are central to daily life,” says the ELLE Decor A-List designer, who is opening an office here.

Tangier’s close-knit community of creatives comes alive during the summer, which is when Veere Greeney, another ELLE Decor A-List designer, hosts an annual theatrical performance in the gardens of his mountaintop villa, Gazebo. Producer Rob Ashford assembles actors from Broadway and the West End (last summer it was Dames at Sea, featuring Darren Criss and Julianne Hough), and the shows are open to the public, though tickets are limited. It’s a full sensory experience colored by the scent of jasmine and gardenias, one that captures the eccentric artistic allure of Tangier.

The energy and interest in the city continue to grow. Deniot is creating a hotel with theater producer Jordan Roth, the Four Seasons recently announced a new property on Malabata Bay, and King Mohammed VI is behind a luxurious renovation of the historic Hotel Continental.

And while there is excitement around these new projects, what appeals most about Tangier is something older. Over lunch on Grenney’s terrace, looking out at Spain, we discussed the city’s past lives. “What makes Tangier unique is its varied history and strategic position,” he observed. “The strait made it a city of diplomacy, openness, and inclusiveness. That is its bedrock.”

For the past two years, stargazers have waited with bated breath for a "new" light to appear in the night sky, born of one of the most cataclysmic events known to occur in the cosmos — a nova explosion. But what is the "Blaze Star" T. Coronae Borealis, and why is it so difficult to predict when its overdue brightening will occur? We turned to the experts to find out.

T. Coronae Borealis — or T CrB for short — isn't a single star, but rather a binary system containing a dense white dwarf star and a bloated red giant. Over a period of around 80 years, the white dwarf's gravity strips material from the red giant until it hits a critical threshold. Next comes a dramatic nova explosion that causes the star to flare to 1,000 times its usual brightness before gradually settling down and repeating the cycle.

"T CrB *will* erupt sometime soon," Bradley Schaefer of the Louisiana State University Department of Physics and Astronomy told Space.com in an email. "Everyone is very confident on this. But we cannot make high-accuracy measures of the rate of gas falling onto the surface of the white dwarf, and we do not have a high accuracy measure of the trigger mass for the big hydrogen bomb." Schaefer continued.

Historical observations reveal that the last nova eruption reached Earth in 1946, which places us well inside the window for another dramatic brightening of T. CrBb. However, the unpredictability of the white dwarf's feeding makes it difficult to pin down exactly when the explosion will become visible from Earth.

"The exact date of the upcoming T CrB eruption depends primarily on how fast the gas coming from its companion star is accumulating on the surface of the white dwarf," explained Schaefer. "When some specific trigger mass has accumulated, then T CrB will erupt. For the last 80 years, we have been watching the gas pile up on the white dwarf surface, and we know that it must be near the trigger threshold."

Astronomers have tried to predict T CrB's brightening by comparing a recent fluctuation in the light of the distant star system to a similar event that occurred in the runup to the 1946 event.

"There have already been some dimming and brightening episodes that caused excitement but did not yet turn into the main event," Elizabeth Hays, lab chief at NASA's Astroparticle Physics Laboratory at the Goddard Spaceflight Center, told Space.com in an email. "It is likely that the nova will happen in the next few years but we still don't know exactly when. I can't give you a guaranteed prediction, but I will be a little surprised if it gets to 2030, or 84 years past the last eruption."

Hays notes that the intense light from the red giant dramatically outshines the dimmer white dwarf, making detailed observation challenging. There is also no way to precisely measure the temperature and pressure of the matter lurking near the surface of the smaller star. "Predicting nova explosions are a little like predicting the weather; conditions may be right but the timing of the storm can be uncertain."

There'll be no visual warning of the eruption before it reaches Earth, though according to Hays there could be an early flash of light in the X-ray spectrum.

"The world's amateur astronomers have been doing an awesome job of keeping T CrB under hair-trigger monitoring roughly every minute or two, so when T CrB has brightened by around one magnitude, then alerts will spread around the world within minutes," said Schaefer.

Once the call goes out, telescopes and observatories around the world will kick into gear, running pre-prepared observation programmes to capitalize on the rare and cataclysmic event. "When the nuclear runaway process starts, it will take off and peak very quickly, in less than a day."

The nova will see the star jump from its normal brightness of magnitude +10 — which is beyond the detection limit of the naked eye — to magnitude +1.7, making it appear as if a star brighter than the North Star Polaris has suddenly burst to life in the night sky.

"The brightness will fall below naked-eye visibility in dark places within a couple of days and become too faint for binoculars within about a week," said Hays. "Outside the visible band, for example, in radio, X-ray and gamma rays, light caused by the eruption will be visible for months or even years. "

T CrB can be found glowing close to the magnitude +4.28 star Epsilon Coronae Borealis in the constellation Corona Borealis during the early evening in November, with the constellations of Hercules and Bootes on either side.

It will dip below the horizon for viewers in the U.S. just a few hours after sunset, before re-emerging into the northeastern sky in the predawn hours.
