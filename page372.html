The average weight for a paddle is going to be around 8 ounces and fluctuates above and below that number. Anything at 9 ounces is usually too heavy and anything under 7 ounces is usually too light. "For weight, somewhere in between 7.5 to 8.3 ounces is a good range," says Johns.

The basic configuration most new players will want to use is called a "wide body paddle" according to Carl Schmits, Managing Director of Equipment Standards & Facilities Development for the USA Pickleball Association. "This configuration is easiest to maneuver and is also good for a controlled, more defensive style of play. Paddles that are close to 8 inches wide by 16 inches long are within the wide body category," says Schmits. "Players that have well-developed stroke mechanics from other sports may be most comfortable with paddles having a longer shape (between 16" and a maximum of 17") and some with a longer handle will better facilitate two-handed backhands, which is an increasingly common grip used very successfully by tennis converts."

Carbon fiber and fiberglass composites are the two most common materials used in high-quality paddles, as they have been shown to help players deliver speed and spin on the ball. A good pickleball paddle should also have zero dead spots, meaning when you make a quality hit on the ball it doesn't randomly underperform and fall flat. "When it comes to the material, I like to look for a polypropylene core with a fiberglass or carbon fiber face," says Johns.

Similar to how you might shop for a driver in golf, your pickleball paddle is going to have a formula of power and control that works for you. A power paddle will have a tight sweet spot and deliver a shot with more pop, while a control paddle has a larger sweet spot that's softer but allows for more consistent returns.

For a power paddle, look for something tighter and more compact in all areas of the design."Paddles that have thinner cores, like a 13mm-14mm, have longer configuration, and typically made with fiberglass faces will return more power, although hitting the ball with heat is as much a result of an efficient kinetic chain as anything," says Schmits.

If you want a paddle with more control, a design with maneuverable dimensions and a carbon fiber face (as opposed to a fiberglass face, which is harder) is recommended. "I'd look for at least 8 inches of width on the paddle head and for a thicker core that's 16 millimeters or more, or 5/8 inches or more," says Johns.

Our experts explained to us grip circumference carries just as much importance in a pickleball paddle as it does in a tennis racket. They also noted there are fewer options when it comes to shopping for specific grip sizes for pickleball (most brands use a standard 4.25" size). Most pickleball paddle grips will vary from just under 4" and no bigger than 5.5".

John Crowley Sr., the Sr. Category Merchandising Manager of Paddles at Pickleball Central, recommends starting with a smaller grip first (when picking between two paddles) and then adding an overgrip if needed. "Very few manufacturers offer different grip sizes and materials, so customizing with an overgrip wrap is the best way to build up the grip to a comfortable size," added Schmits.

A reliable pickleball paddle is usually thick, as brands fill the interior space with their own technology to limit the odds of any dead spots. One popular interior makeup is a polypropylene core (also called honeycomb core design) which is a type of interior webbing that's made into a honeycomb shape to ensure the paddle is evenly balanced.

Just like shopping for running shoes, you can find pickleball paddles at all types of prices. Generally speaking, expect to pay anywhere from $60 to $100 for a beginner- to intermediate-level paddle. If you're looking for an advanced paddle, any model we recommend above $100 is a worthy choice.

If you're going to play more than two times a month, Johns says it's time to invest in a paddle: "The right time to upgrade your paddle is usually when you find yourself wanting to improve your skills as a player as opposed to just playing for fun every now and then."

Crowley Sr. also gave us tips on how to go about investing in a paddle. "When you go shopping have a budget in mind (the best value to price is no object). How much do you plan to play? What is your sports background and if you know, what style of player are you—power player, control player, or all court?" he says.

If you think you have to splurge on a competition-ready paddle, think again. "I say shoot for the $75 to $100 range for your first paddle if you want to play competitively," says Laura Gainor, USA Pickleball's Director of Media Relations. "And if you’re planning to sign up for your first tournament, make sure your paddle is USA Pickleball approved." We agree there's nothing worse than bringing your paddle to a match and discovering you need to find a different one that complies with the rules.

Fiberglass and graphite paddles have characteristics about them that can play to your natural strengths on the court. "You are trying to find the recipe that works best for you," says Crowley Sr.

In general, fiberglass pickleball paddles have more power but less control than a graphite paddle. This is mainly due to the fact fiberglass weighs more. Graphite, on the other hand, is lighter and stronger due to the thinly layered construction of the material. Graphite paddles are often associated with better control, resulting in a better ability to place each return where you want it.

Fiberglass paddles are more wallet-friendly, and have been gaining in popularity due to their quality performance while staying at a good price point. Graphite paddles are more expensive and are often seen being used more by pros in tournaments.

One other paddle material to consider is composite. For example, you might find a composite design that incorporates carbon fiber and fiberglass in hopes of bringing equal parts control and power. Composite paddles are usually high quality and exist at graphite-level prices (or higher), but it's important to make sure the paddle is USA Pickleball approved if you ever want to play competitively.

While all of the paddles above were curated with attention to sweet spot, there are a few characteristics you can prioritize when shopping.

The first is to go by surface area. You will probably get the most sizable sweet spot area by going off the most common dimension of a 16" length and 8" width paddle. Second is inner material of the paddle: look for a trusted core material, like a honeycomb propylene core design, that can cover all areas of the paddle and help reduce the chance of there being any dead spots.

Last is the type of material used to construct the paddle. There are a lot of fiberglass paddles out there with generous sweet spots, but if you want to get the largest sweet spot possible then going to a more expensive graphite paddle is probably the best move.

There are two situations where you want to consider replacing your paddle. The first is if you find yourself playing more (and perhaps more competitively) than when you first started. A good rule of thumb is if you're playing more than two times a month, it's probably time to consider investing in a competitive play paddle.

The second situation that would warrant replacing your paddle is if it's damaged or worn down from constant play. If you stick to the paddles we recommend above, you can expect the lifespan to last between 1 to 3 years. But sometimes your paddle can wear out faster (also called a "dead paddle") if you're playing much more. In order to tell if you need to replace your paddle due to overuse, pay attention to the feel and how the ball comes off the paddle. Is it returning or serving shots the same way it was in the first few months of use? If not, your paddle is probably wearing down.

You can also pay attention to the sound of the ball off the paddle. Are your best shots delivering a nice and clear pop? Or are they sounding flat and droopy? If you're out playing consistently, then you will probably know when your paddle is ready for retirement.

Pickleball paddles are resilient. They can take a beating on the court, but off the court you should keep them away from the sun and heat for extended periods of time.

If you're concerned about keeping your paddle pristine-looking, invest in a paddle cleanse kit to clean the face, and pack it in a paddle cover to prevent scuffs. "I wipe down my paddle face once a week with water to prevent any accumulation of dirt," says Schmits.

"Keep your paddle stored in a cool, dry environment and never leave it in a hot car. Also, don't smash it when you're angry," says Johns.

Like any sport, pickleball does pose some injury risks (some games can last over 2 hours at a time). The most common injuries include tennis and golf elbow, which can be caused by swinging a paddle that's too light or too heavy—or simply by swinging it too much and not allowing for proper rest.

"Grip size, paddle weight, and swing weight (how the weight is distributed) all contribute to the efficiency and comfort of the human-paddle interface," says Schmits. "A grip that is too small may require an over-clenched grip to prevent spinning in the hand, a paddle that is too light may not have enough mass to prevent twisting on off-center shots and will transmit unwanted vibration, an overly head-heavy paddle may require more torque to snap through. Also, a paddle that is too heavy will quickly fatigue muscles, as well as slow down hand speed."

A paddle with good vibration absorption is also important, which is why a wooden paddle is a no-go if you're an active player. "Anything that absorbs more energy in the paddle and reduces the amount of vibration that travels up your arm is good at preventing soreness," says Crowley Sr.

Unlike the shredders you may remember from old movies, most modern machines no longer turn documents into long linguini-like strips. Cross-cut shredders slice paper both lengthwise and crosswise into small rectangular scraps. Micro-cut models slice and dice those scraps into even tinier bits, leaving hundreds or even thousands of petite particles.

For most home and home-office use, either cutting method provides plenty of protection. Most shredders these days offer micro-cut machines with either P-4 security, which makes reconstructing a document the most puzzling jigsaw puzzle ever, or P-5 security, whose even more diminutive document destruction methods are favored by MDs, CPAs, JDs, or by other professionals with initials in their titles.

The larger and more powerful your shredder, the more sheets it can generally chew through at one time. Just don’t treat the number printed on the box as a dare.

Manufacturers’ rated sheet capacities are best-case numbers, usually based on neatly aligned standard-weight bond paper. Thicker stock, folds, staples, uneven piles, and stuffed envelopes can quickly turn a “12-sheet” machine into something more comfortable with eight or nine. Feeding a few sheets below the rated maximum is one of the easiest ways to avoid jams and unnecessary strain.

Manual shredders require you to stand there feeding paper in batches. Auto-feed machines let you load a stack, walk away, and get something else done while the shredder eats.

Here again, don’t take the rated auto-feed sheet capacity too literally. An “80-sheet” or “200-sheet” auto feeder may accommodate that many pristine sheets under ideal conditions, but real paperwork is rarely pristine. Wrinkles, folds, different paper weights, staples, and uneven stacks can reduce how much you can reliably leave it to swallow unattended. Plus, the bin on a high-capacity auto-feed model may fill before the maximum number of sheets are fed into it.

Auto-feed mechanisms also add more rollers, sensors, and jam-clearing compartments. And more moving mechanical parts create more places for something to eventually go wrong.

However, if you routinely accumulate large piles, the time a highly rated auto-feed shredder saves can easily outweigh the added complexity and potential mechanical issues.

A bigger bin means fewer trips to empty it, but gallon capacity tells only part of the story. Pull-out drawers are lighter and easier to clean and empty. Bins that require lifting the often heavy shredder mechanism off the top of a wastepaper basket can potentially scatter loose particles still in the cutter mechanism.

Also pay attention to what happens when you pull that drawer out. An easy-to-remove bin that keeps most of its shredded paper actually in the bin will save you emptying and cleanup aggravation, especially if you don’t wait until the bin is filled to its brim.

Shredders may look sturdy and impervious, but most are actually quite finicky eaters. Nearly every shredder will eventually jam if you feed it too much at one time or feed it the wrong type, shape, or mix of paper. More advanced models with more advanced anti-jam systems sense an overload before one occurs, or either automatically reverse or allow you to manually reverse to relieve the jam before you need to perform paper-removal surgery from the cutters with tweezers or needle-nose pliers.

The cheapest jam protection, however, is restraint. If a machine claims 12 sheets, regularly feeding it eight or nine instead will lower your odds of spending quality time violently tugging mangled paper back out of its teeth.

Most current shredders can swallow staples and paper clips, and many can also chew up plastic credit cards. CDs and other thicker media such as metal cards are less universally welcome.

Check the manufacturer’s instructions before you buy for what non-paper materials a shredder can successfully chew and before feeding anything other than paper into its cutters. As with nearly all things, don’t be penny wise and pound foolish. You will find you will shred more than you anticipated—it’s just human nature to use a new device more than you though you would. Overall, shredders costing several hundreds of dollars make the most sense for people who regularly devour large stacks or have a specialized need. The most important shredder “spec” may be how little time it forces you to spend with it. Sheet capacity, runtime, security level, and bin capacity are the headline numbers I’d look at first. Runtime is particularly useful because it tells you something about how much work the machine can comfortably handle before demanding a rest.

But some of the things that most determine whether you’ll love or hate a shredder aren’t specs at all. Nearly every decent modern shredder can reduce ordinary documents beyond casual recognition. The bigger differences are how often it jams, how easily those jams clear, how cleanly the bin empties, and how much confetti winds up everywhere except inside it.

Check owner reviews for recurring complaints about jams, overheating, finicky sensors, messy bins, and other annoyances. AI agents on online sites can summarize customer reviews to answer many of these before-you-buy questions.

With shredders, old doesn’t necessarily mean obsolete. Sometimes it means proven.Unlike most consumer electronics, shredders don’t become outdated because they lack this year’s faster processor, new operating system, wireless standard, or other bleeding-edge technology. An older model that’s still attracting buyers and good reviews may actually offer something a brand-new machine can’t: years of accumulated evidence about how well it holds up.

Before buying an older model, check whether the manufacturer has introduced a newer or revised version. When shopping online, search the manufacturer’s current lineup or, if the retailer provides an AI or shopping assistant, ask whether there’s a newer version of the model you’re considering. Then compare old to new rather than automatically assuming new means better.

The best shredder maintenance is self-restraint. Don’t continually stuff the cutters to their rated maximum, allow the bin overflow, or feed the machine materials it wasn’t designed to digest.

Lubrication requirements vary. Some manufacturers recommend shredder oil applied at regular intervals, others suggest pre-lubricated oil sheets, and some models claim to require little or no lubrication at all. Follow the instructions for your particular machine rather than assuming every shredder requires the same type of mechanical TLC.

Beyond that, maintenance should be pretty painless: empty the bin before the paper snow reaches the cutters, occasionally clean dusty sensors if the manufacturer recommends it, and clear jams rather than repeatedly hammering the reverse button and hoping for divine intervention.

Despite the name, most modern “shredders” no longer shred paper into long strips. While cross cut and micro cut are clues for how small the ultimate shred pieces will be, P-ratings, part of the DIN 66399 security standard, put an actual number on “small.”

P-ratings run from P-1 through P-7; the higher the number, the smaller the pieces produced. For ordinary household paperwork, P-3 is already plenty secure. A cross-cut P-3 machine reduces a document to hundreds of pieces, which is more than enough discouragement for anyone contemplating reconstructing your bills, junk mail, receipts, or other everyday paperwork. After all, a document pieced back together like a taped mosaic isn’t likely to fool many people.

P-4 is the sweet spot for buyers who want their paperwork diced considerably finer without spending much more, and P-4 is common among consumer micro-cut shredders. P-5 turns documents into even tinier bits and makes sense for especially sensitive financial, legal, medical, or professional paperwork. P-6 and P-7 go further still, into security levels intended for documents whose contents matter considerably more than last month’s Visa bill.

For most of us, then, P-3 is plenty, P-4 buys extra peace of mind, and P-5 is for paperwork you’d really rather nobody put back together.

Some of the Rings of Power are more important than others in the grand tapestry of Middle-earth, but all have history on the fingers of important figures.

Sauron created many of his rings in Rings of Power season 2, but in season 3 he begins crafting the One Ring and distributing the remaining rings in his collection. It's the big moment of the series, so it's worth taking a look at just how these different rings impact the world around them, and in what ways Rings of Power has changed the canon story.

The most famous, most powerful, most precious Ring of Power is, of course, Sauron's One Ring, forged in the fires of Mt. Doom as a master controller for the other 19. Crafting the Ring required Sauron to pour a large portion of his own power into the melting pot, creating a symbiotic bond between the item and its master. Sauron would never be truly killed while the Ring was still whole, but neither could Sauron wield his full Maia power without the Ring on his finger.

As recounted in The Lord of the Rings, Sauron used the One Ring to bring Middle-earth to his knees, until the weapon was cut from his hand by Isildur during the Last Alliance of Elves and Men. Isildur's company was beset by orcs during the journey, and though the King managed to slip away using the Ring's invisibility, it betrayed him by falling from its owner's finger into the River Anduin, exposing Isildur to orc arrows.

Over 2000 years, the Ring remained in its watery resting place until, as shown in The Return of the King's movie adaptation, Sméagol the stoor found it while fishing. The Ring passed from Sméagol to Bilbo Baggins, from Bilbo to Frodo Baggins and, following a brief stint with Samwise Gamgee, into the fire from whence it came.

While the One Ring was the most powerful, there were 19 other Rings of Power in Middle-earth, and each of them was a considerable force in its own right. Whereas Sauron crafted the One Ring in secret from his Mordor stronghold, the other 19 were made openly by the elves — albeit not without a dark guiding hand. As with deciding between electric and gas snowblowers, consider the size of the area you normally clear before picking a single- or two-stage model. Single-stage snowblowers work well for clearing walking paths and the average-sized two- to four-car paved driveway. A snowblower’s clearing width and intake height determine how much snow it can move with each pass. The larger the snowblower’s intake, the more quickly you can get the job done.

Most single-stage gas-powered snowblowers have intakes that range from 22 to 24 inches wide, while two-stage snowblowers can have intakes that are up to 32 inches wide. The height of the snowblower’s intake, which is sometimes referred to as “clearing depth,” indicates the maximum snow depth it can handle. The intake height for a gas snowblower ranges from 18 to 21 inches.

Purchasing the snowblower with the widest, tallest intake may seem like a no-brainer, but keep in mind that there’s a correlation between intake dimensions and the overall size of the snowblower. The largest gas snowblowers will have a harder time maneuvering through tight turns. Ultimately, you should choose the smallest option that can effectively clear the paths you need and the amount of snow you expect to see.

A snowblower's job is to take snow in front of you and move it somewhere out of the way. With a more powerful snowblower, you can send the snow farther away. There are a few things you need to do to make sure your gas snowblower runs smoothly through the season:

First, check the oil level between each use and top off as needed. You should change the oil once a year, even if you didn’t use your snowblower all winter.

Change the spark plug at the beginning of each season to ensure it starts smoothly. A dirty spark plug can make it difficult to get your snowblower going, especially in extreme cold.

Lastly, small gas engines require plenty of airflow to run smoothly, so check the air filter once a season and replace it if it's dirty.

Snowblowers use unleaded gasoline—the same thing you put in your car—which begins to degrade after about 60 days. Running a snowblower with old fuel can clog the carburetor and make the engine hard to start, so you should never store your snowblower with fuel left in the tank.

Before putting your snowblower to bed for the season, either run the fuel tank dry or add fuel stabilizer, which keeps gasoline fresh for up to 24 months, to the tank. If you use fuel stabilizer, run the snowblower for a minute or two after adding it to the tank to circulate it through the engine.

A snowblower should be able to handle the lion’s share of your snow removal duties, but you should definitely keep a snow shovel around to clean up whatever the snowblower leaves behind. You may also want to save the gas and dig out with a shovel after a light, soft snowfall.

Sex And The City: How Old Carrie Was At The Beginning & End

📅 Jul 17, 2021  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The Sex and the City franchise follows Carrie Bradshaw through decades of her life, from a single woman in her 30s to a widow in her 50s. Considered one of HBO's best series, Sex and the City was revolutionary in its depiction of sexually active, unmarried women in their thirties, as Carrie taught generations of women what it meant to be "single and fabulous."

The life of Carrie Bradshaw was so beloved that Sex and the City spawned two movies, a prequel series, and a sequel series. And Just Like That ended in 2025, and the polarizing show sadly didn't quite capture the experience of being a 50-something woman the original series did with Carrie's 30s. Still, our Carrie has learned, grown, and loved a lot in the 27 years that we've known her.

The timeline of the original Sex and the City series makes it fairly easy to determine Carrie's age while also setting up a useful bit of information to determine it in the rest of the franchise. She states that she is 32 years old at the beginning of season 1, which takes place in 1998, so her birth year is 1966.

Carrie's Age In The Sex And The City Franchise

Her birthday is also established in the series as October 10. The show's timeline typically follows the passage of time in real life, so Carrie should age one year in each Sex and the City season.

She then turns 35 in season 4, meaning that when Sex and the City comes to an end in 2004, Carrie is 38 years old, which is also confirmed by the title of the season 6 episode, "Catch-38". Once the movies come into the picture, the timeline and the corresponding characters’ ages get fuzzier.

The first Sex and the City movie premiered in 2008, seemingly four years after the events of the series. The film centers on Carrie and Mr. Big's impending wedding, which provides some context for her age. A magazine editor asks Carrie to pose as the “40-year-old bride,” and if the film takes place in the year it premiered, she would be about 41-42.

The timeline is a bit confusing, considering the film is supposed to take place over two years, so you can assume it begins in 2007, or at least before Carrie’s 41st birthday, and finishes in 2008 when she’s 41-42.

It's also clear that the magazine article is about 'the 40s' as a decade of age, so the line could mean a 40-something bride, rather than Carrie being 40 exactly. She also says to her assistant, "your 40s are to pay for the drinks," confirming that she is in her 40s at this time.

Sex and the City 2 is the next movie in the franchise and continues to show what Carrie Bradshaw's life is like, now that she and Big are finally married. The movie takes a look at Carrie settling into married life with Mr. Big, missing some of the excitement of her younger years.

Released in 2010, Sex and the City 2 picks up two years after the first movie, likely putting Carrie Bradshaw's age at 43-44 years old. However, her age is not a big plot point in this film.

Though short-lived, there was a Sex and the City prequel series that looked at Carrie's teen years. Played by AnnaSophia Robb, Carrie Bradshaw's age in The Carrie Diaries is revealed to be 16 years old, and she's a junior in high school at the start of the series.

Carrie lives in Castlebury, Connecticut, and the prequel series shows her secretly working at a magazine company while interning at a New York City law firm. In season 2, Carrie celebrates her 17th birthday and enters her senior year of high school.

Following the cancellation of the third Sex and the City movie, the franchise returned to the small screen for a sequel series, And Just Like That. The show explored how times have changed and what life is like for Carrie and her friends as middle-aged women, with it taking place 11 years after the second movie and seeing Carrie as a 55-year-old woman.

Carrie is shown to be more conservative when talking about sex compared to the younger generation, and seems to have settled into a quieter life than she had in Sex and the City. Before Big's tragic death in the And Just Like That pilot, she's even shown to (somewhat) enjoy cooking with him. This is a far cry from the 30-something Carrie who used her stove for shoe storage.

The depiction of Carrie's aging in the sequel series didn't go over well with viewers, many of whom felt frustrated with storylines like Carrie undergoing hip surgery. Still, there was some excitement that we might get to see Carrie celebrate her 60th birthday, should the show have continued into 2026.

Alas, that's not what happened, as it was abruptly announced that And Just Like That season 3 would be its last, a mere two weeks before the finale. And Just Like That's final episode takes place over the American Thanksgiving holiday, which is celebrated in November, so it can be assumed that Carrie had recently celebrated her 59th birthday the month before.

Sex and the City follows Carrie and her friends across just over half a decade of their lives, with the movies covering the interim years between the original show and the spinoff, And Just Like That, which picks up decades later.

Given this vast timeframe, it's no surprise that Carrie's age factors into many storylines in Sex and the City, And Just Like That, and even The Carrie Diaries. The Carrie Diaries makes obvious use of Carrie's age, since it's a spinoff set during her younger years, but the other two shows also weave age into the exploits they portray.

Sex and the City is primarily about two things — dating and growing up. Carrie starts the show in her early 30s, and many episodes have her wrestling with figuring out how she wants to spend the rest of her life against the backdrop of most of her friends settling down and starting families of their own.

As an experience, dating also changes as one grows older. The wild and spontaneous relationships of Carrie's youth weren't appealing to her as 40 approached, and she wanted a stable life with Mr. Big.

Age also became important in And Just Like That. Following Carrie in her mid-50s created yet another unique variety of storylines that were only possible due to her age. However, despite And Just Like That's backlash, none of the shows have ever preached any toxic messages about aging or have claimed that age is important when finding a romantic partner.

With Carrie's age comes wisdom, and in the And Just Like That finale, she realizes that she spent most of her life either looking for or with a partner, and that this may no longer be her path anymore. The very last scene sees her dancing around her beautiful home, happy to be in her own company. This never would have happened in Sex and the City, when Carrie was in her 30s.

It was indeed a surprise to learn that And Just Like That was seemingly closing the book on Carrie Bradshaw, but the ending, as controversial as it was, seemed definitive. In the original Sex and the City finale, Carrie said, "The most fabulous relationship of all is the one you have with yourself," and that's a lesson she wholly embodies in And Just Like That.

There are currently no known plans to continue the story of Carrie Bradshaw, but that doesn't mean we'll never see her again. When addressing the potential for a return to TVLine, And Just Like That writer and executive producer Elisa Zuritsky said, "She's alive, so it could happen."

"Sarah Jessica Parker is [And Just Like That showrunner] Michael Patrick King’s muse," Zuritsky continued, "and the reason he says ‘I’ve said goodbye before and I’ve come back’ is because it’s true." Parker also noted that she was saying "farewell" to the character, not "goodbye." (via People)

So, while we likely won't be seeing Carrie Bradshaw on our TV screens anytime soon, anything is possible, and in 20 years, we very well may be catching up with the Sex and the City heroine in her 70s. Now that would be fabulous.

All 11 Titan Types In Attack On Titan Explained

In Attack on Titan, the Titans are much more than oversized monsters lurking beyond humanity's walls. What initially appears to be a terrifying fight for survival gradually develops into a complicated history involving ancient powers, political conflicts, and the origins of the creatures themselves. As Eren Yeager and his fellow soldiers uncover more about their enemies, the true variety of Titan abilities becomes increasingly difficult to ignore.

From seemingly mindless Pure Titans to the extraordinary powers possessed by the Nine Titan Shifters, each form serves a different purpose and presents its own threats. Some rely on overwhelming strength and numbers, while others have specialized abilities capable of changing the outcome of an entire war. Understanding these distinctions is essential to appreciating the series' lore and worldbuilding, so here is a breakdown of all 11 major Titan types featured in Attack on Titan.

Pure Titans resemble humans but with varying levels of enlarged forms. The majority of the observed Titans, both in the manga and the anime, belong to this category, their overarching feature being their inability to form and process thoughts.

Despite being made similarly to other Titan types, Pure Titans are mindless and tend to attack without discrimination. They are dangerous due to their sheer numbers, formidable strength, and enormous size. Pure Titans have been used as weapons of war, first by Eldia and then by Marley, and can only be killed by slashing the nape of their neck.

A notable example of a Pure Titan would be the humanoid dubbed Bean, who first appeared when the gates of Trost District were broken by Attack on Titan's epic Colossal Titan.

Bean was one of the two Titans kept alive for the sake of experimentation in an attempt to better understand the nature of these beasts and their innate weaknesses.

The Abnormal Titans are one of the most bizarre Titan types. Abnormals are Pure Titans that display unusual behavior and intelligence, allowing them to perform unpredictable moves. They often tend to ignore nearby humans and move on to strategic locations to inflict more damage.

Abnormals are adept at performing tremendous leaps, running, speed-walking, and in a few cases, communicating in human tongues. An Abnormal Titan’s ability to form thoughts and speech has been chronicled in the diary of Ilse Langnar, who had run into a hesitant Titan amid an expedition massacre.

After Hange Zoë and Levi’s squad find the diary, it is revealed that the Titan addressed Ilse as “Lady Ymir”, mistaking her for Attack on Titan's Founder, Ymir Fritz.

Confused by the possibility that a Titan can experience emotions, Ilse became increasingly frustrated during her attempts at communication, ending with the Titan crushing her skull whilst shedding tears. The existence of the Abnormal Titans forces humans to approach the beasts with a more nuanced perspective. This revelation proved to the remaining soldiers that not all Titans are mindless killers.

As the name suggests, the Attack Titan is an offensive powerhouse, standing at 15 meters tall and characterized by a highly muscular build and flesh-less jaws.

Eren Yaeger would later improve the Attack Titan’s metrics by drinking the Armor serum that allowed him to harden the Titan’s skin during pivotal moments.

As his default Titan Form, Eren was able to receive memories from those who possessed the Attack Titan and those who had yet to inherit it. This would later come to help him manipulate his father, Grisha, to cause his own Titan creation subsequently.

Although these memories allow the user to gain insights, they are often incomplete and short snippets.

Reiner Braun’s Armored Titan stands at 15 meters and was initially inspired to share a ​​​​​resemblance to WWE Superstar Brock Lesnar.

The Armored Titan has thick, hardened plates all over its body, acting as a natural defense against external attacks, including cannon hits, steel blades, and vertical maneuvering equipment, making it the most defensively strong of the Titan types.

In addition to this, these Attack on Titan Titans are able to harden their arms and feet, creating sharp claws in the process. The Armored Titan's only weakness seems to be the areas on the back of its exposed joints.

The armor is not capable of withstanding high-power weaponry, such as the Mid-East Allied Forces’ artillery.

Users of the largest Titan type, the Colossal Titan, can control the energy produced during their transformation to create various effects, such as when Bertolt attacks the Trost District, and his transformation triggers a powerful wind blast.

The Colossal Titan can also create a mushroom cloud of dust and rubble, leading to the death of many Survey Corps members during the Battle of Shiganshina District. SpongeBob SquarePants' Christmas episodes show that living in tropical waters isn't a barrier to winter adventures, and the best SpongeBob Christmas episodes also rank among the show's funniest so far. Nickelodeon has consistently delivered some of the best animated TV shows of all time for decades, and, by far, its most popular and successful cartoon is SpongeBob SquarePants. Thanks to its unique characters, setting, fun situations, and different types of comedy, SpongeBob SquarePants has maintained its popularity and relevance for over two decades.

Since 1999, SpongeBob SquarePants has taken viewers to the bottom of the ocean, to the town of Bikini Bottom, to meet the title character and his friends. Throughout 17 seasons, SpongeBob has gotten involved in all types of fun and absurd situations, but living under the sea doesn’t mean that SpongeBob and company don’t get to celebrate the same holidays as those living on land. SpongeBob SquarePants has had various holiday-themed episodes, from Halloween to Christmas and more, but the episodes set in the latter holiday have been the most popular.

While there aren’t many SpongeBob SquarePants episodes dedicated specifically to the spirit of Christmas, it does have a few winter-themed stories that fit perfectly with the holiday. Leaving aside the logic and physics of snow underwater, SpongeBob, Patrick, and even Squidward often find their unique brand of festive magic, while sticking to the absurdist humor the show is known for.

"Christmas Who?" is the first SpongeBob SquarePants Christmas special. In it, Sandy is decorating her tree and gearing up for Christmas. SpongeBob and Patrick have never heard of Christmas and learn everything about it from Sandy. SpongeBob does what he does best and gets overexcited and spreads the word around Bikini Bottom.

The city becomes a Christmas wonderland, but when Santa fails to appear on Christmas morning, Squidward takes it upon himself to become Santa and give cheer to Bikini Bottom. This really shares the meaning of Christmas, with the nice twist that it's Squidward, of all the characters, who gets most into the holiday.

SpongeBob and Patrick go to Sandy's house to play, but find her entire dome covered in a shield with a sign that says "Keep out." The two sneak in to find Sandy asleep, but she doesn't look like herself — she is much larger and fuzzier. Sandy is hibernating for the winter, but SpongeBob and Patrick play in her snow-filled tree dome anyway.

While playing in the snow seems enjoyable and gives this episode a Christmas spirit, the noise wakes Sandy up, and she’s not pleased. SpongeBob and Patrick end up paying the price for being too loud, and they also begin to struggle with the cold.

"Snowball Effect" shows Bikini Bottom under a blanket of snow after an iceberg crashes above. SpongeBob and Patrick spend the episode having a snowball fight, but things take a turn when Squidward gets involved. The three build forts to protect themselves from the snowballs, but Squidward goes too far and realizes he is having more fun than he thought he would.

The snow makes this a Christmas-themed episode, even though it is from an iceberg, but the sight of Squidward playing with SpongeBob and Patrick makes this one of the most enjoyable winter/holiday episodes in the show's history.

"Krabs Á La Mode" shows Mr. Krabs turning the Krusty Krab into an unintentional ice rink for winter. For Squidward, turning down the heat during the frigid temperatures in Bikini Bottom is one of the worst things Mr. Krabs could have done. Due to expenses, Mr. Krabs sets an alarm at the Krusty Krab so no one can turn up the heat and turns the Krusty Krab into an ice rink, complete with Krusty Krab-themed skates and activities.

"Frozen Face-Off" celebrates winter with a sled competition, and everyone in Bikini Bottom enters because the prize is $1 million. SpongeBob has Gary leading his sled, Patrick chooses gummy worms to take him to victory, and Squidward has a worm who chooses to sip tea instead of winning the race.

While everyone tries to win the prize, Plankton is busy trying to steal the Krabby Patty secret recipe. When the gang tries to stop him, they're frozen in ice, but the ever-resourceful Sandy makes a fire to warm everyone up, ending the episode on a festively-themed note of the value of friendship and cooperation.

"It's a SpongeBob Christmas" is, arguably, the best SpongeBob Christmas episode. To celebrate the holidays, this episode shows Bikini Bottom excited about Santa's arrival. As always, Plankton has an evil plan up his sleeve to ruin Christmas cheer, so he puts all of Bikini Bottom on Santa's naughty list.

What makes this episode stand out is the fact that it’s not traditionally animated like other SpongeBob SquarePants Christmas episodes. Instead, the episode is animated in claymation and pays homage to Christmas claymation classics.

In this episode, Squidward tries to sell his art at the Krusty Krab, but when Mr. Krabs shuts it down, he sends Squidward to Antarctica to bring his art to a big-time buyer. Once there, Squidward comes across a snow sponge that looks a lot like a snowman.

As it turns out, SpongeBob is (somehow) already in Antarctica eating ice by the time Squidward gets there. This episode brings Squidward and SpongeBob to a snowy terrain that is unlike what they normally experience in Bikini Bottom, with the change of locale being a clever way to tap into the Christmas themes.

"Shopping List" doesn't include Santa or snowball fights, but it does include a cold-living yeti. In the episode, Mr. Krabs gives SpongeBob an intense shopping list with odd things that aren't easily found. Sandy decides to help SpongeBob since she is braver than him, especially when it comes to getting the hair of a Yeti.

Sandy and SpongeBob get the job done, but the Yeti eventually tracks them down to get its hair back. It offers a small touch of winter magic that enters Bikini Bottom, along with one of winter's most mythical creatures.

“Goons on the Moon” sees Sandy taking her Science Scout troop on a trip to the moon. While they all go their separate ways to explore, Santa Claus is revealed to be sleeping on the dark side of the moon. When Santa realizes how far Earth is from the moon, they all try to get it closer but end up destroying the moon.

Santa then pumps SpongeBob full of water, turning him into a replacement moon, with Santa promising to deliver a new one in December. SpongeBob SquarePants has become weirder and weirder with every season, but at least this episode acknowledges SpongeBob’s past encounters with Santa Claus.

"Plankton's Old Chum" shows Plankton creating a fake holiday. Plankton realizes he has nowhere to dump his old chum, so when he runs into SpongeBob, he convinces him that it's Chum Day, a holiday similar to Christmas. On this day, a person hands out chum as a gift, and SpongeBob falls for it, taking Plankton's old chum and spreading it around Bikini Bottom. When the city becomes angry over the filth, SpongeBob convinces them it's a holiday, showing he can turn anything into a festive celebration.

A camping chair’s durability is evaluated based on the frame, the material of the seat and back, and overall weather-resistance. Most of the camping chairs we write about are made with steel tubing that’s powder-coated or treated to resist rust and corrosion. This style of frame holds several hundred pounds without any bowing or bending and is still light enough to carry in your hands.

Similarly, seat durability relies on sturdy materials. Woven polyester is one of the most common options. Camping chair materials are often combined with a water-resistant coating so any spilled drinks or rain drops don't damage the fabric. This also protects any interior cushioning from absorbing water.

Great camping chairs are also treated to resist UV light. This helps prevent the fabric from fading over time. Similar to the metal frame, the fabric should also be strong enough to offer a weight capacity of 300 pounds at minimum.

Camping chairs are specifically designed to be portable. That said, the relative portability between them can range drastically.

Small, lightweight camping chairs pack down for easy transport and work well for backpackers or anyone traveling by foot. These typically come with carrying straps or collapse small enough to latch onto or pack into a large backpack. Ideally, they should weigh less than eight or nine pounds.

Larger chairs can weigh more than 10 pounds, which makes them far more cumbersome to carry and better suited to car camping and backyard lounging. What you get with that added size and weight, though, significantly more comfort afforded by a wider seat with more cushioning and a potentially higher weight limit.

Regardless of size, many camping chairs come with shoulder straps or carrying cases to make travel easier.

For hikers and backpackers, smaller, lighter chairs are the obvious choices. If you’re using the chair for the beach, weather- and UV-resistance generally increase its longevity. It's also worth considering chairs with shorter legs so you can stretch out your own in the sand.
