A college student searching rocky glaciers in Yellowstone finally discovered one of the rarest insects in the world

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A University of Wyoming student discovered three rare ice crawlers in Yellowstone, marking the first known sightings of these cold-loving insects in Wyoming.

In summer 2026, a college student named Will Anderson found three rare bugs called ice crawlers on rocky glaciers in Yellowstone National Park.

As a student at the University of Wyoming, Anderson had spent the whole previous year looking for them without any luck. Ice crawlers are some of the rarest insects on Earth.

Ice crawlers belong to Grylloblattodea, one of the rarest orders of insects in the world. Only 35 species have been described, and many are known only from single localities.

Anderson is a biology major from Casper and a junior in the Wyoming Research Scholars Program. He works with Audrey Lindsteadt, a third-year Ph.D. student in the university’s Program in Ecology and Evolution.

“I am interested in this insect because there is so much not known about them, with their life history, habitat, and distribution all not being fully understood,” Anderson said.

“I also am passionate about and interested in their alpine habitats, which are understudied,” he added.

Starting in late July 2025, the pair surveyed 14 rock glaciers in the Red Mountains next to Heart Lake, in southwestern Yellowstone. They found no ice crawlers, even though Anderson also checked snowfields and set overnight traps.

Most snowfields had already melted by then. The area also had fewer glaciers than ideal, and most of them were small or inactive.

“Despite the close proximity to their known distribution and the plentiful suitable habitat, Gryllos have never been found in Wyoming,” Lindsteadt explained. “Therefore, Will hypothesized the rock glaciers and nearby snowfields that we are studying could have Gryllos.”

On a cold, snowy six-day backpacking trip in late June and early July 2026, Anderson found two ice crawlers near Antler Peak in northwest Yellowstone. Both turned up under rocks he flipped on a large rock glacier.

In mid-July, a mother grizzly and her two-year-old cub bluff-charged the pair and blocked their planned route.

They scrambled up Grey Peak instead and slid down to its two rock glaciers, where Anderson caught a third one. In August, he searched several alpine glaciers in Grand Teton National Park but came up empty.

Over the summer of 2026, he and Lindsteadt walked about 170 miles (274 kilometers).

“The specimens I collected in Wyoming are so far identified to the species Grylloblatta campodeiformis, which is the most widespread North American species, having populations in Montana, British Columbia, and Alberta,” Anderson noted.

He briefly kept two of the insects alive. He could not tell whether they were feeding on the dead insects and plant matter he gave them.

Ice crawlers are wingless insects that stay active in cold conditions, according to North Carolina State University entomologists. Most species are active below freezing and usually die above 50°F (10°C).

They develop through incomplete metamorphosis, from egg to nymph to adult, and their growth is slow. Some may need up to seven years to complete a single generation.

The University of Wyoming describes them as extremophiles that live at temperatures as low as 21°F (-6°C) in humid alpine settings.

By day, they hide under cool rocks near snow and ice. At night, they scavenge, eating mostly other invertebrates, including insects blown onto the snow, along with some plant material.

Little is known of their diet when they live more permanently under the snow, or in the soil through summer and winter.

The order lives in northeastern Asia, Siberia, the western U.S. and Canada. In North America, small, isolated populations occupy mountains and caves in several western states, British Columbia and Alberta.

Rock glaciers resemble alpine glaciers, but a layer of debris covers their ice, insulating and protecting it.

Lindsteadt suspects that ice crawlers prefer rock glaciers to non-glacial talus fields or snowfields because rock glaciers are colder, more humid and more permanent.

“Obviously, with just three species detections, we cannot confidently identify their habitat preferences,” Lindsteadt said. “But they at least point us in a promising direction.”

Yellowstone holds 58 known rock glaciers, and none had been visited or studied before this project. Next summer, the team plans to survey 17 rock glaciers in eastern Yellowstone and revisit others.

Ice crawlers are generally restricted to high-elevation glaciers or low-elevation caves with permanent ice, according to Purdue University entomologists.

The same entomologists warn that many of these habitats are at risk as glaciers and ice fields recede with climate change.

In Anderson’s view, the insects probably play a small part in these ecosystems because of their rarity, but they still belong to the food chains and nutrient cycles of their extreme habitats.

“More importantly, we can use ice crawlers to better understand how these ecosystems work and be more effective managers of them,” he argued.

He is also working on a paper he hopes to publish in a leading alpine or entomology journal.

Anderson will present the findings at the Entomological Society of America’s 74th annual meeting in Columbus, Ohio, on November 8 to 11.

Nov. 3, 1957: The flight of Laika

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Less than a month after the launch of Sputnik 1, the Soviets launched Sputnik 2. The mission was readied quickly in order to keep the propaganda momentum from Sputnik 1 and to coincide with the 40th anniversary of the Bolshevik Revolution.

Carrying mixed-breed dog Laika, Sputnik 2 was the first flight to carry a living animal to Earth orbit. A small stray from the Moscow streets, Laika had undergone training to help her become accustomed her to the close quarters and gravity changes. Electrodes attached to the dog sent data back to Earth, for future research into the biological effects of space travel. Unfortunately, Laika died a few hours after takeoff – always the expected outcome of the mission, as the life-support systems on Sputnik 2 were imperfect and there was no plan for recovering the satellite or its passenger after launch. Sputnik 2 burned up on reentry in 1958, after its batteries died the previous year. In 2008, a statue commemorating Laika was erected in Moscow.

How Travel Credit Cards Are Becoming Full-Trip Companions: Watch Video

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Travel credit cards are moving well beyond points and booking. At Skift Global Forum, Capital One Travel’s Sarah Moore said the opportunity lies in remaining useful throughout the trip by combining context, experiences, technology, and trust to build deeper relationships with travelers.

This sponsored content was created in collaboration with a Skift partner.

Travel credit cards have long competed on points, perks, and booking benefits. For Capital One Travel, the next phase is about extending the relationship further into the trip and finding more ways to stay useful after a customer books.

The company sees an opportunity to connect more parts of the travel journey, from planning and airport support to destination experiences. That approach draws on Capital One’s technology, booking data, travel products, and existing relationship with cardholders as their financial partner.

At Skift Global Forum in New York, Sarah Moore, head of Capital One Travel, discussed how the company is trying to become what she called a “travel companion” for its cardholders, with a role that extends before, during, and after the trip.

The Travel Card’s Next Act: From Earning Points to Erasing Friction

Capital One entered the travel business relatively recently, launching the Capital One Travel booking platform in 2021. Moore said arriving later gave the company an opportunity to look at what already existed and build around the expectations of a modern traveler.

That meant taking a technology-first approach, but also starting with the customer rather than simply recreating the traditional credit card travel model.

“We were able to really see what was out there in the marketplace and understand what the modern traveler was looking for,” Moore said.

That distinction matters as the boundaries between financial services, travel booking, loyalty, and trip planning continue to blur. A travel card may still sit in a traveler’s wallet, but the relationship increasingly extends into the rest of the trip.

For years, booking has been treated as the finish line for many travel companies. Moore sees it more as a starting point.

Capital One Travel wants to remain with customers after the booking transaction, whether that means helping a traveler navigate the airport by surfacing lounge information, providing flight updates, or helping them decide what to do once they reach their destination.

Being useful when something goes wrong can be particularly important, giving a company an opportunity to demonstrate the value of the relationship.

“Knowing when to step up is what builds trust,” Moore said.

For Capital One Travel, those moments can help turn a single booking into a longer-term customer relationship.

Moore also emphasized the importance of collaboration across the travel industry to better support travelers throughout their journeys. No single company can manage every aspect of the travel experience today, making partnerships across the broader ecosystem essential to delivering consistent support to customers throughout their journeys.

Delivering that kind of support also requires a different approach to personalization.

Travel brands have spent years collecting customer data, but Moore said the most useful form of personalization is contextual. The question is not simply who someone is, but what kind of trip they are taking right now.

A traveler taking a weekend trip with a spouse, for example, may want something entirely different from the same traveler on vacation with children.

Moore used her own family as an example. A trip with her husband might call for a relaxing stay at one of Capital One Travel’s Premier Collection hotels. Traveling with her children could mean booking a four-bedroom vacation rental, also available on Capital One Travel, with many homes offering the same luxury benefits found in the Premier and Lifestyle hotel collections. Even within the same family, different travelers may want completely different activities.

That same thinking extends to what travelers actually do once they arrive.

Moore said in a survey conducted by Capital One Travel, more than 70% of travelers are looking at some type of event or live experience, from sports and dining to other forms of cultural immersion. For some travelers, those experiences are increasingly shaping the trip itself rather than simply being added after the flight and hotel are booked.

“Travel is not just a flight and a hotel,” Moore said. “It’s what you’re actually doing when you land and what you’re engaging in when you’re there.”

Capital One has responded by building experiences into its broader cardholder proposition. Moore pointed to partnerships with Major League Baseball and touring artists, as well as more intimate experiences tied to food and culture that can be booked through activities on Capital One Travel.

One example is a culinary–driven trip led by chef José Andrés in his hometown of Asturias, Spain, exclusively for cardholders, giving them a more distinctive and immersive way to experience the destination.

Taken together, those efforts point to a broader shift in what a travel credit card can become. Moore said Capital One is focused on becoming a travel companion that supports cardholders from booking through servicing and the trip itself.

The company launched a dedicated Capital One Travel app within the past year, an example Moore cited of meeting travelers in the channels where they increasingly expect to manage their trips.

AI could push that evolution further. Travelers are increasingly finding inspiration, researching destinations, and making decisions through new interfaces. Moore said Capital One Travel will need to meet them in those places while maintaining the value and trust that come with its role as their financial partner.

That trust also creates an opportunity to use the information Capital One already has more effectively. Transaction data can provide signals about how some customers travel and what they may be looking for next, provided it is used to make the experience easier and more relevant.

For Moore, that is ultimately what “frictionless” travel means: understanding what a traveler needs and making it simple for them to get it.

This shift has implications beyond credit cards. Travel companies across the industry are trying to extend their relationship with customers beyond a single transaction. Doing that effectively requires more than adding another loyalty benefit or digital touchpoint.

It means understanding the context of each trip, being useful when travelers actually need help, and creating access that enhances the experience. It also requires accepting that the traveler, not the brand, is ultimately in control of the relationship.

For Capital One Travel, the opportunity is to make the card and the platform behind it more relevant throughout the journey. If that works, the booking becomes less of an endpoint and more of one moment in a much longer relationship.

Hyundai's XRT Off-Road Division Is On The Right Path But A Step Behind

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There's nothing more in vogue right now than automakers soft-roader-ing their crossovers for weekend warrior types who enjoy a bit of camping or a dirt road every once in a while. Honda has TrailSport, Subaru has Wilderness, Toyota has TRD, GMC has AT4, and so on and so forth. Of course one of the largest automakers in the U.S. would want to get in on the action, so Hyundai spun up its own off-road sub-brand a few years back: XRT, which stands for Extreme Rugged Terrain.

What started out as more of a trim and appearance package on vehicles like the first-gen Palisade and fourth-gen Tucson and Santa Fe has evolved into offering styling changes with a bit of added capability when the going gets bumpy. With the introduction of Hyundai's XRT Pro trim on the Palisade and upcoming fifth-gen Tucson, that idea gets taken up another notch. However, after spending a day with both the 2026 Santa Fe XRT and 2026 Palisade XRT Pro off-roading in rural Missouri, I'm not totally sure Hyundai is all the way there yet. That's not to say the Korean automaker isn't on the right path and won't keep improving expeditiously, but as it stands right now, it may be just a step or two behind.

Full Disclosure: Hyundai flew me out to Bentonville, Arkansas, put me up in a very nice hotel and fed me, all so I could sample the 2026 Santa Fe XRT and 2026 Palisade XRT Pro.

It can be sort of tricky to evaluate the capability of a soft-roader like Hyundai's XRT fleet. Technically, both the Palisade XRT Pro and Santa Fe XRT were able to do everything that was thrown at them on this program, but they both lacked a bit of the sure-footedness I just experienced in Honda's Passport TrailSport, and that was on a far more technical and challenging course. That being said, what I did with the XRT siblings is probably 10 times as grueling as what the average Hyundai owner will do with their SUVs. There's also the issue of tires. While the Passport TrailSport is fitted with beefy General Grabber A/T Sport all-terrain rubber, the Santa Fe XRT makes do with less-aggressive Continental TerrainContact A/Ts, which leads to a good bit of wheel slippage.

Still, both XRTs do an admirable job of tackling trails, though rock crawlers they are not. The Palisade XRT Pro I drove was certainly the more capable of the two, thanks to its electronic limited-slip differential in the rear and a 360-degree camera that helped overcome its sheer size, but it never felt particularly at home, even if it did tackle every obstacle without much protest.

I say much protest because, while the car didn't get stuck at any point, the 4,575-pound crossover didn't exactly seem happy, and that especially goes for its Hyundai-built 8-speed transmission. While the naturally aspirated 3.5-liter V6 provided enough power (287 horsepower and 260 pound-feet of torque) to get up just about anything despite a lack of low-end oomph, I could tell that the 8-speed was none too pleased. I couldn't hear it on the road, but when switching to the Sand setting in its Trail drive mode, there was a noticeable whine coming from it on the throttle. A temperature readout in the gauge cluster also served as a constant reminder it was, uh, hot, and when I got out of the car, there was an unmistakable smell of hot transmission wafting through the air. From a functional standpoint, the XRT Pro didn't skip a beat, which is certainly a testament to the rest of the Palisade's drivetrain and its 8.4 inches of ground clearance; it bottomed out far less than I expected it to.

It's a similar story with the Santa Fe XRT. It may lack the e-LSD and all-out grunt of the Palisade, but it does make up for that somewhat with more low-end grunt courtesy of a torquey turbocharged 2.5-liter inline-4 that pumps out 277 hp and 311 lb-ft of torque. This drivetrain lent itself far better to the old-school way of getting yourself unstuck: give 'er some gas. Sure, there are 10 fewer horses than the Palisade's V6, but an extra 51 torques is massive.

Still, you can tell the Santa Fe is a far less luxury-oriented product than the Palisade, and I got bumped around a hell of a lot more than I did in the Palisade. Obviously, there's a price delta to consider, as the Santa Fe XRT starts at $43,940 including destination, and the Palisade XRT Pro has a far more costly $51,820 entrance price.

Currently, there are four XRT models on sale: the two I've been talking about, and then the Santa Cruz XRT and the Ioniq 5 XRT. I've driven the Ioniq and had a pretty good time with it, but came away feeling it was more of a styling exercise than anything else. Sure, it can handle some dirt, but that's just about it. While all these models do come with all-terrain tires, a bit more ground clearance, upped towing capacity, better approach and departure angles, and functional front recovery hooks, they're without a doubt behind industry frontrunners like Honda TrailSport and Subaru Wilderness when it comes to real capability.

I believe that's because XRT, as a brand, is so new. Hyundai's still trying to find its footing in an ever-more crowded world. It promises that its next product, based on the fifth-generation Tucson, will be its best yet, and far more than an appearance package. Even though the Tucson won't have the e-LSD of the Palisade XRT Pro, despite sharing the "Pro" moniker, it will have skid plates, all-terrain tires, 8.3 inches of ground clearance, some sick body cladding, and Hyundai's new off-road software called Smart Terrain Assist.

That program has the ability to analyze the terrain ahead of you, determining traction and slip conditions. From there, it provides visual guidance via green, orange or red "stuck zones," and shows the driver the best route to take. It works in concert with Automatic Terrain Detection (which has auto, snow, mud, sand and rock modes) with intelligent mode switching to remove any guesswork from off-roading. Clearly, Hyundai wants to make it as friendly to first-time off-roaders as possible.

Lest we forget, this same team is working on the body-on-frame Hyundai Boulder SUV, which will likely be powered by a hybrid or EREV drivetrain. It shares underpinnings with a future body-on-frame truck Hyundai is also working on, and it's targeted specifically at the American market.

While I'm not sure Hyundai's XRT division is quite the cream of the crop just yet when it comes to soft-roading — it might be a bit too focused on aesthetics, rather than actual performance — there's little doubt in my mind that it'll get to the front of the pack sooner rather than later. Just look at what this massive company has done with its N performance division, or what it has done with Genesis when it comes to luxury. When Hyundai puts its mind to something, it gets the job done, and I don't expect XRT to be any different.

Exclusive: Cogent Security’s VR-1.1 model outscores Claude Code and Codex on vulnerability repair

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Vulnerability management startup Cogent Security Inc. today released VR-1.1, a new version of its frontier reasoning model that writes fixes for newly disclosed software vulnerabilities.

On a public repair benchmark, the model outscored Anthropic PBC’s Claude Code and OpenAI Group PBC’s Codex.

The release goes after the part of vulnerability management that has not sped up. In July, research group Epoch Artificial Intelligence Inc. counted about 2,500 high- and critical-severity disclosures from major organizations, roughly five times the monthly record set before Anthropic announced Claude Mythos Preview in April.

Each repair still needs an engineer who understands the flaw well enough to change the code without breaking something else. Co-founder and Chief Executive Vineet Edupuganti called that stage “where security teams are stuck.”

To test the model on that repair work, Cogent turned to LiveCVEBench. Researchers at Harbin Institute of Technology and Kuaishou Technology built the benchmark from vulnerabilities disclosed in recent months and add new ones regularly. VR-1.1 produced a working repair for 57.9% of the benchmark’s 190 tasks. Claude Code and Codex tied at 45.3%.

Each task drops an agent into a real development environment with a vulnerability report and the affected codebase, in one of 14 programming languages. To pass, a patch has to close the vulnerability without breaking the software, which the benchmark checks with separate security and functionality tests.

Cogent blocked outbound network access during repairs and hid the grading tests until each agent was done. Inference budgets differed across the three systems, according to the full methodology Cogent Research has published.

Where the environment allows, VR-1.1 starts by writing its own proof of concept that triggers the reported flaw. From there the model follows the vulnerability through the affected code, including related paths that share the same weakness. The fix itself stays small, for example checking an index before use.

Every patch goes to a separate review that starts from a fresh context and hunts for a way around the fix. New bugs introduced by the change are part of that check. The model also leaves behind regression tests, which fail if a later code change reopens the flaw. When an attempt fails, the diagnosis and failure details carry into the next try.

“A patch might block the exact payload from a bug report, but leave the same vulnerability open via a different endpoint or break core functionality for users,” said Geng Sng, co-founder and chief technology officer of Cogent. “We designed VR-1.1 to check its own work for both problems before it finishes.”

Cogent introduced VR-1 in July to find and prove the attack paths an adversary could reach inside an enterprise. The new release extends the remediation capabilities that model introduced.

Founded in 2025, the company sells artificial intelligence agents that detect and fix vulnerabilities, with customers choosing whether a human signs off on each change. According to Cogent, Fortune 500 users have cut the exposure window for critical vulnerabilities by 97%.

Greylock Partners backed its $11 million launch round in July 2025, and Bain Capital Ventures led the $42 million Series A in February that took total funding to $53 million.

Ethereum Layer 2 Aztec gets Tierra V6 upgrade to support faster private apps

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tierra V6 fixes a critical V5 security issue and speeds up private transaction proofs, with new apps launching or preparing to launch on Aztec.

Aztec Foundation, the team behind the privacy-focused Ethereum Layer 2 network Aztec, has launched Tierra, its Alpha V6 network upgrade, to fix security issues and make private transaction proofs faster.

The upgrade resolves vulnerabilities found in earlier versions, including a critical issue in V5, the foundation said. It also adds a new AI-based system for security testing.

When asked why the critical issue wasn't fixed sooner, an Aztec spokesperson said the fix required the whole network to move to a new version.

"Because Aztec is a decentralized network, if a critical vulnerability is discovered, the entire network must upgrade to the new version to implement the fix," the spokesperson told The Block.

Aztec has no central operator that can make the change on its own, the spokesperson said, adding that there are plans to make future upgrades faster if vulnerabilities are discovered.

Aztec said its network has more than 3,000 validators, no centralized sequencer, and no privileged viewing keys.

Besides the security fixes, Tierra makes private transaction proofs twice as fast, taking one second to generate on a native device, according to the foundation.

Messages from Ethereum can now reach Aztec in as little as 12 seconds, compared with an average of two minutes before, the foundation said, noting that the change speeds up deposits and other transactions between the networks.

"Aztec Tierra delivers a stronger, more secure foundation for applications that no single company or institution can control," Zac Williamson, co-founder and CEO of the Aztec Foundation, said in a statement. "Privacy shouldn't be something you have to request or trust someone else to protect. It should be built into the network."

Developers on Aztec can choose which functions of their apps are private and which are public, the foundation said, adding that private functions run and generate proofs on the user's device, while public functions run on the network.

The upgrade comes shortly after Aztec Labs relaunched zk.money, a self-custodial wallet for private crypto payments, three years after discontinuing the original product.

"With Aztec Tierra and zk.money, users can deposit directly from any Ethereum wallet and make lightning-fast private transactions without complicated bridging workflows," Joe Andrews, co-founder and CEO of Aztec Labs, said in a statement.

Other apps launching or preparing to launch include Galactica for private AI inference, Nemi for private perpetuals trading, and the private browser wallet Nulo, according to the announcement.

Xochi supports private intents and trading, while Nyx lets users make private payments to any Ethereum wallet. Olla offers liquid staking for the Aztec token.

Rolling Chat, a private message board based on Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin's Aztec experiments, is also on the list.

Aztec also introduced a new brand and website alongside Tierra, describing its aim as becoming the platform for "unstoppable private apps."

Before He Played Sylvester Stallone in ‘I Play Rocky,’ Star Anthony Ippolito Fought Hard for His Own Big Break

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

I don’t think I was supposed to hear Anthony Ippolito when he exclaimed, “20 minutes?” right before this interview, as he was given the amount of time this interview would last. Though, it kind of hit me, if you’re not used to doing interviews — and there’s no way Ippolito can be at this young stage of his career — 20 minutes is a long time to make conversation with a total stranger. Especially, as he is quick to point out, after a barrage of quick, three-minute interviews.

Ippolito has been in films and television before — kind of famously now, he played Al Pacino in “The Offer” — but none of those were roles in which he’d have to do press. Let alone playing the lead role in a major motion picture in which everyone wants to talk to him. Oh, and that lead role is as … Sylvester Stallone. Related Stories ‘Okinawa Saudade’ Review: A Moving Documentary About the Okinawan-Brazilian Community Explores the History of Displacement AT&T and IndieWire’s Filmmaker Party at AFI Fest Celebrated Filmmakers and Those Who Champion Them

In “I Play Rocky,” yes, Ippolito plays Stallone at the make or break point in Stallone’s career. Stallone had written “Rocky,” a very in-demand script. Not in demand was the idea of Stallone playing Rocky — as MGM offers more and more money to Stallone in an effort to get him to not play Rocky. (If you don’t know, Stallone stuck to his guns, played Rocky, and is now a very wealthy and famous actor.)

Directed by Peter Farrelly, Ippolito says he sent in multiple tapes trying to land this role — which does seem to be in the same spirit as the film itself. Though, Ippolito said, when he was actually going through the casting process, that’s not at all what he was thinking, even though it may seem that way now.

Ahead, he tells us (yes, over the course of 20 minutes) the Stallone movies he used to prep for playing Stallone, what is was like to talk to Stallone before filming, and why he loves Stallone and Dolly Parton together in “Rhinestone.” Also, if he had to play Stallone again in another movie, he does have another “I Play” installment in mind…

The following interview has been edited and condensed for clarity and length.

IndieWire: You seem excited about this being 20 minutes?

It’s a little strange, I’ve been having three-minute increment conversations with somebody. So, yes, I am excited.

OK, so how many Stallone movies did you watch? Did you stick to that era? His voice kind of evolves by the late ‘80s.

Yeah, I really tried to stay focused on a period of time. I don’t know the exact number. More so, from the stuff I was looking at I was watching it over and over again, rewatching. With the exception of a few.

Well, “Rocky Balboa.” It’s one of the best movies of the franchise.

For people who haven’t seen that one in a while, it’s very sweet.

It really is. It really is. And he does some excellent work in it. And I think it’s really powerful that he was able to come back many years later, after the fifth one, and make this incredible movie.

From closer to the “Rocky” era, I kind of love him in “The Prisoner of Second Avenue.” Jack Lemmon thinks he stole his wallet…

It’s just scene after scene of Jack Lemmon chasing Stallone through Central Park trying to get his wallet back…

But then Jack Lemmon realizes he left his wallet at home and wound up being the one who mugged Stallone.

But he was mistaken for a mugger and this was part of his issue, right?

It’s funny watching it now, because it’s so obviously Sylvester Stallone. So it’s amusing the whole role is just him being chased.

I agree with you and I found that throughout a lot of the stuff I was unearthing throughout this process.

In that, if he hadn’t become famous, I’d still look at him and think, “what an interesting looking dude,” and wonder what happened to him.

Speaking of that, there were times during “I Play Rocky,” knowing full well what happens in real life, and I’m still like, “Maybe just take the money. That’s a lot.”

It’s really remarkable. Especially when you convert the amount from what it was then to what it would be now.

I know you made a video for your audition which involved an old punching bag and gloves. What were you saying in it?

Oh, well, there was certain dialogue I was coming up with, or thinking of coming up with. And I hadn’t had my hands on too much of the script at this point. So I was just trying to capture his physicality and his movement as a boxer. I was just really passionate about the project. I wanted to put myself out there in a way. And, thankfully, I was able to get my hands on the script a bit later on.

So are you doing lines from “Rocky”? Or just stuff you came up with on your own?

I ask because that’s a time when you haven’t gained the weight yet, so you’re not in the physical condition you are when filming. So I feel you had to really sell this dialogue? Which obviously worked.

Yeah, I mean … it was difficult to come up with that part of it. I had given myself inspiration with some parts of the original film, and some parts from what I felt would work for dialogue from what I was understanding of the story. And then once I was able to get the script, then I was able to just put scenes on tape. And then continuously send them stuff until they relented.

Oh, there was more than one? You just kept going?

Well, that’s the spirit of this movie. Did you feel that way when you were sending them in? “Well, that’s what Stallone did.”

Hm … maybe? You know, it seems that way kind of now?

Right. But at that time, I was just like, “I probably seem crazy to everyone who is on the other end of this.”

Yeah, but I wasn’t thinking about that part for some reason, I guess.

Which is probably good, if you were getting ahead of yourself it doesn’t work out the way it did?

I know you spoke to Stallone before filming. A year ago, he was saying he knew nothing about this movie. When and how did that change?

Peter Farrelly tells a funny story about that. He had actually met with Stallone prior to that and discussed his level of involvement. And they discussed how Stallone would kind of not be necessarily in the trenches with us, just for the sake of Peter not wanting people to feel like Stallone had his hand in it in a way that it was shaped…

And historically Stallone never does that. I say that sarcastically because I’m well aware he has a specific vision for any movie he’s involved in…

Right. Right. So, Stallone went back after that and told somebody he has no idea about it. And Peter was like, “What are you doing?” And Stallone said, “You told me to separate myself.” And Peter was like, “Not that far, man!” So, I guess that was just a bit of a communication breakdown.

So in Stallone’s mind, he was doing a favor by saying that?

I’m not sure, but I was able to speak with him about a month before we started shooting. And I’m very grateful he was willing to take the time

What was that like? Was he in good spirits or was he apprehensive?

He was in great spirits about it. I was nervous, as you could probably imagine.

Yeah, and not only that, I had done so much of my own research. So, up to that point, it was really built up as this really big thing in my head. So, I was just really, really excited to meet him and to listen to him. He was really, really generous and disarming and very willing to share what that period of time was like for him.

What did he tell you that you don’t know already?

Oh, man. Well, he discussed with me building the character of Rocky as a writer. That’s where it became really interesting to me. Because he had studied filmmaking and writing while being an usher. So, hearing his perspective and his kind of clinical breakdown of how he approaches a script and of a character? It was hugely informative in developing and my understanding of this side of him as a writer.

From that, what changed on how you approached playing him? How does that conversation inform what we see?

You just really understand that this thing is his baby. And it really makes more sense that these numbers going so high during these negotiations because the script is that good. And so well written. Yeah, he’s a terrific writer and I think he proved that to Chartoff and Winkler with “Paradise Alley,” first.

My only experience with him was at the afterparty for “The Family Stallone.” He shows up at this restaurant in Little Italy and it’s like the Red Sea parting. Everyone stopped talking and made room so he could walk by. When he got near me I said, “Hey, Sly,” which I immediately regretted. He stopped, looked at me, and gave me a little nod.

He then walked off and I thought, “You know, good enough.”

Are you glad that you said it now? I feel like I would be.

But you keep bringing up meeting Wim Wenders. I know you’re a fan of his, is there something maybe happening?

I got to meet him at the Zurich Film Festival.

I know you like his movies, are you going to work with him?

I just think he makes great films, he’s made great films and he was a really, really sweet man and we got to speak. That’s pretty much it.

I feel that way about so many. Well, Scorsese obviously. Richard Linklater, Derek Cianfrance, Tim Burton.

That’s an eclectic group. “Place Beyond the Pines” and “Beetlejuice” are very different movies.

Very different movies. Well, I like that, You can go in so many different directions. And I appreciate directors and filmmakers who are singular.

And that is kind of the thing about Stallone, too. Did you look at the movies Stallone directed?

Honestly, well, I know he directed all the “Rocky” movies other than the first and fifth ones. And I understand he directed more stuff past that as well. But I hadn’t specifically looked at stuff he directed for this.

How far back did you go back to watch with Peter Farrelly’s films? Did you go back to “Dumb & Dumber”?

I’ve seen so much of his stuff already. So there’s not one that jumped out at me, but also, obviously, “There’s Something About Mary.”

It really is. I think he really killed it with that one. I mean, he has such an incredible sensibility. And he’s always aware of something on set. If there’s a moment to be discovered, to make a meal out of it, he’ll find it.

I always think about, “Unless someone comes up with six-minute abs.”

On social media a lot of people are doing their own “I Play …,” then insert any other Stallone movie type posts. If you had to play him again, what movie would you want to do?

Like, “I Play Cobra,” would be interesting. I do love “Cobra.”

You know, I guess I’m not the right age for this, but “I Play Rocky Balboa” would be very interesting…

Well, it’s the first one I remember. I would have had to have seen the ones prior? To the point I’m in the theater for “Rocky Balboa.”

Well, not necessarily. The first one I saw was “Rocky III”…

Because it was the one on cable all the time. Then I was told, “Oh, you know, the first one is a lot different.”

Yeah, I was pretty young, but I do remember seeing “Rocky Balboa.” And I just think there’s something interesting about the fact that, similar to how he’s fighting the boxing commission at the time to let him fight, because they think that he can’t, or shouldn’t — I think he was having a similar dialogue with the studio at the time. About him making the movie in the first place.

It’s interesting, even at that point, they still didn’t think they could take a chance on him making a “Rocky” movie.

I guess, yeah. It’s strange, but history does repeat itself.

Ah, OK, it’s not streaming, so it’s a little harder to find. It’s just interesting this is only a few years after “Rocky” and now he’s on stage singing with Dolly Parton. It’s kind of fascination what that one decision did for him…

It’s very true. And I think Dolly Parton is spectacular in that.

How good is she in that? Right? I mean, she has such a warmth to her. I mean, yeah. She’s just incredible in the movie and it makes it so fun. The two of them! They are dynamic.

And if you don’t have at least somebody who is there, willing to be that person to remind you to believe in yourself, it’s a tough road.

iPad mini (A20 Pro) review: Apple's tiny tablet gets a big update

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apple made significant improvements this year, but they don’t come cheap.

The iPad mini has often gone for years without updates, making it feel like a forgotten part of Apple's tablet lineup. But it's often been sneaky powerful despite its size, usually toting specs and features that make it a more compelling option than the basic iPad, even with its smaller screen. And after two versions of the iPad mini without a home button, Apple has given the tiny tablet a compelling update in almost every facet.

The new iPad mini's screen is slightly larger, but the device is thinner and lighter than the one it replaces. It's the first iPad mini with an OLED display, a major step forward from the standard LCD this model has had for years. And for the first time, you can get a nano-texture glass front for reduced glare in bright light. Naturally, its chipset has been upgraded, as well — the A20 Pro inside it is brand new, just released alongside the iPhone 18 Pro and iPhone Duo. The previous iPad mini made do with the A17 Pro, which was first used in 2023's iPhone 15 Pro series. It has a new speaker system and is the first iPad to have an IP67 rating for dust and water resistance. Finally, it's the second iPad to get fast-charging capabilities, following last year's iPad Pro.

All told, it's a major update to a device that doesn't always get a lot of love. But, of course, these improvements come at a cost. The iPad mini now starts at $699 with 128GB of storage, $200 more than the last iPad mini cost when it was released two years ago (but only $100 more than Apple was selling it for after its round of major price increases this past summer). Between the cost and new set of features, it almost feels like an iPad Pro mini. And while technology-wise it's an impressive device, I'm not sure if the price will be worth it for most.

If I handed you the new iPad mini, you might not immediately notice how different it is compared to the 2024 model. At first glance, it has a similar enough physical impression and display, and its aluminum unibody and glass front are just as solid as ever. This year, you can only get the iPad mini in the all-business "midnight" and "starlight" shades, which is a bit of a bummer. But there are nips and tucks throughout that add up to a refreshed experience. While the iPad mini still has relatively large display bezels, they're noticeably thinner than before thanks to the 8.5-inch screen Apple included (compared to the 8.3 inches on the prior mini). Apple also reduced its thickness from 6.3mm to 5.4mm, and its weight from 0.65 pounds to 0.56 pounds (or 0.66 to 0.58 for the cellular-enabled models). More screen in a thinner and lighter package is always a win, and Apple has come the closest it ever has to making an iPad that you can truly carry with you everywhere without thinking about it.

While those physical tweaks are small but welcome, the new display tech is more significant. The iPad mini's OLED screen offers twice the peak brightness when viewing HDR content at 1,200 nits, while SDR brightness has been slightly increased from 500 to 600 nits. It can also go all the way down to 1 nit minimum brightness. And thanks to OLED, blacks look exceptionally black and overall contrast is excellent. It has a 2,000,000:1 contrast ratio, which far surpasses what you'll get on an LCD like the older iPad mini used (Apple doesn't specifically list the contrast ratio for older iPad mini models, but its recent LCD screens are in the 1000:1 range).

What this means in practice is that the iPad mini is an even better portable cinema than ever. If you're going to use this tablet to watch movies on the plane, it's a notable upgrade. It's not the most technologically advanced OLED screen you've ever seen, but its dark blacks, HDR performance and excellent overall contrast makes even dimly-lit scenes much sharper than what you'll get on an LCD-equipped iPad mini. You'll notice this the most when watching video, but it makes everything you do with the iPad mini, whether that's playing games or just browsing the internet, look much more visually appealing.

One place where I will ding this screen is its refresh rate: Apple stuck with 60Hz here, compared to the 120Hz "ProMotion" screen on the iPad Pro. It's pretty evident at this point that a high refresh rate screen is going to be limited to the iPad Pro, but it's still a bit of a let-down — especially when you consider the fact that the iPhone 17 got a ProMotion screen in 2025. That said, the smartphone market is far more competitive than the tablet market, so while Apple may have felt the pressure to finally put a high refresh rate screen in the standard iPhone, that still isn't the case on the iPad.

The iPad mini I tested also has a nano-texture glass front. The timing for trying this was impeccable, as I just reviewed Amazon's new Alexa Tablet 12 Pro, which also has a "nanomatte" display that was lovely to use. The etching on the glass front scatters reflections in a way that makes the iPad mini much easier to use when there's a bright light source nearby. And while the Alexa Tablet sticks with an LCD, the combo of OLED and the nano-texture glass made for an exceptional viewing experience here.

As you can see below, the new iPad mini with its nano-texture glass disperses strong light, while the one on the right with standard glass is a mess of reflections as the sun was setting.

As is typical, but still unfortunate, the nano-texture option costs a pretty penny. It's not an option on the base iPad mini with 128GB of storage, so you'll need to spend at least $899 to get the 256GB model with the nano-texture glass (or $1,049 for 256GB, nano-texture and 5G connectivity). That's a lot of cash for an iPad mini, no matter how nice it is.

Other subtle tweaks include the new "Acoustic Display," which Apple says places tweeter and woofers beneath the display enclosure for improved audio performance. This also allowed Apple to remove the speaker holes on either side of the iPad mini so that it could achieve that IP67 dust- and water-resistance rating.

I remember being surprised at the iPad mini's audio performance when I reviewed it in 2024 — it wasn't something I wanted to listen to for long periods of time, but it was definitely better than I expected given the small space Apple has to work with. Well, the new Acoustic Display speakers are another step forward, with a much stronger bass presence and overall more pleasing audio profile. The older iPad mini now sounds positively tinny by comparison. It's not the kind of thing that's going to radically change how you use the device, but it's a nice quality of life improvement.

The same can be said for the front-facing camera, which is now located on the landscape edge of the iPad mini. It's the same 12-megapixel Center Stage camera that most iPads use, but at least if you use a case like Apple's Smart Folio, the iPad mini's camera will be properly lined up for a video call. Like all other iPads except the Pro, though, that camera doesn't support Face ID. You'll need to use the Touch ID power button (or a numerical PIN) to unlock the iPad mini.

Given that the iPad mini has a brand-new A20 Pro chip inside, it's unsurprisingly speedy and handled every task I threw at it. Games like the recently-released ports of Control and No Man's Sky ran with no hitches and looked great while doing so. It's not the same fidelity you'll get from playing on a high-end PC rig or even a PS5, but having games of this caliber on an iPad is pretty great. The iPad mini naturally had no problems with lower-stress fare including games from Apple Arcade like Mini Motorways What the Car? and my former iPad mini fav Balatro.

But performance on an iPad hasn't really been an issue unless you're really finding ways to stress the system, and nothing that I wanted to do with the iPad mini made it break a sweat. For me, it's a fairly casual couch computer for browsing and messaging, triaging emails, playing games or watching videos — all lightweight tasks. It definitely has the headroom to do more, though, and if you're paying at least $700 for one now, it's good that it has a chip that'll run well for years to come.

The one bit of higher-load work I did was in the Lightroom app. I loaded up my library, which has a ton of RAW photos, and the iPad mini applied any changes I wanted near-instantly. It took a few seconds for Lightroom's AI-powered Quick Actions tool to analyze a photo, but once that initial scan was done, any adjustments were rendered basically in real time.

It is worth noting that Apple offers a few variants of the A20 Pro chip, depending on how much storage you opt for. The 128GB and 256GB models have a 5-core CPU, 7-core GPU and 16-core Neural Engine for AI processing. However, the 512GB model (which I tested) steps up to a 6-core CPU and, more crucially, a dual 16-core Neural Engine. Apple specifically says this will help speed up on-device AI performance. I wasn't able to compare the two different models, but I'm confident in saying that most iPad mini shoppers will be fine with the standard configuration.

The new iPad mini is the first with Apple's own networking chips on board, as well. The N1 handles Wi-Fi 7, Bluetooth 6 and Thread. It was first introduced last year with the iPhone 17 series and has since arrived in a variety of new iPads and Macs. The model I tested also has the C2, Apple's new cellular modem that first showed up on the iPhone 18 Pro and iPhone Duo in September. It came with Verizon's service activated, and was extremely fast when connected to the carrier's 5G Ultra Wideband network — I routinely got download speeds exceeding 500 Mbps. When you combine that with other refinements like the nano-texture glass and its smaller profile, a cellular-connected iPad mini can really go anywhere without adding any noticeable weight to your bag.

I haven't had time yet to do a full battery test on the iPad mini, but in-use over the last five days it feels very similar to basically every other iPad I've used in recent years. I only got around five or six hours of battery life when I was using it heavily for things like games, but lower-intensity tasks like watching video or internet browsing extended things closer to the 10 hours Apple promises. I played Control for about 30 minutes and the battery dropped by 11 percent, which is pretty good for one of the heavier games you can play on an iPad. I plan to loop video playback on the iPad mini until it dies and will update this review with those full results when it is complete.

In another feature it borrows from the iPad Pro, the new iPad mini has fast charging. Provided you've got the right adapter (like Apple's 40W dynamic power adapter that can hit up to 60W), you can charge to about 50 percent in 30 minutes. That almost matches up with my testing: When I plugged the iPad mini in at 29 percent, it was up to 74 percent in a half-hour.

Unlike the 2024 iPad mini update, this model has several significant upgrades beyond a more powerful chip. It now has the second-best display of any iPad available, assuming you're okay with its compact size. The size and weight reductions, the slightly larger screen and the improved speakers weren't necessary, but they are welcome. And the nano-texture glass is a wonderful option that makes it much easier to use the iPad mini in a wide variety of lighting conditions.

But the problem, as is so often the case in 2026, is the price. The 2024 iPad mini cost $599 after the recent increase, and this new one starts at $699. I think all the updates on board here — the screen, the A20 Pro and a tighter design — make a $100 price bump reasonable. But it's fair to wonder if Apple is misreading what people want out of an iPad mini. Would it have been more appealing if they stuck with the older design and screen, put in a new chip and kept the price the same? It's hard to say, as the iPad mini has never been positioned as Apple's most affordable device — instead, it's more of a small iPad Air. From that perspective, the pricing makes sense.

The iPad mini really is a fun and adorable device to use. If I had any talent for visual arts, it would be a perfect digital sketchbook and jack-of-all-trades device for consuming content or doing basic tasks. But in 2026, with consumer electronics prices continuing to surge, a more expensive iPad mini might be an even more niche device than it was before.

The iPad Mini sometimes feels like a trap. It is, in some ways and for some people, the perfect iPad: meaningfully larger than my phone’s screen but still small and light enough to use for reading in bed or gaming on the subway. As most of the rest of the iPad line has tilted ever closer to laptop territory, the Mini might be the most iPad-y iPad left. I’ve taken a Mini everywhere with me since the very first one. But Apple only updates the Mini sporadically, and those updates sometimes feel like someone inside Apple Park picked its specs by closing their eyes and clicking randomly in an Excel sheet. The Mini is always a little underpowered, a little overpriced, and a little afield in Apple’s tablet lineup. We, the iPad Mini People, sometimes feel a little forgotten.

This year’s model, though, is an upgrade through and through. Apple made the screen larger and better, while making the iPad itself smaller and lighter. It upgraded the processor in a big way. It made the whole package more durable, so you can take it to more places and use it for more things. It actually feels like a tablet made for a reason, not just another occasional bone thrown to the small-tablet fans.

For the past week, I’ve been testing the midnight iPad Mini with cell service, 512GB of storage, and the nano-texture matte display coating. I really like it. It’s the best iPad Mini yet, and a huge leap over the previous model. The one in my hands also costs $1,249, and I cannot in good conscience tell you to buy it. Even the very cheapest version — Wi-Fi only, 128GB of storage, regular OLED display — starts at $699, by a wide margin the highest starting price ever for a Mini.

If you don’t already know the iPad Mini is your one true love, there’s probably a better iPad out there for you. But if you’re an iPad Mini Person and you can stomach the price? You’re getting the best Mini in years.

The Mini’s new display is its most important and most controversial upgrade. It now measures 8.5 inches instead of 8.3, and it’s now a 2316 x 1512 OLED panel instead of an LCD. The OLED gives the screen a little more dynamic range than the previous model, with brighter bright spots and darker dark spots. Its peak brightness is noticeably brighter than before, and its minimum brightness is low enough to be totally pleasant for reading in the dark (as long as you’re in dark mode). In general, it’s just a little more crisp and contrast-y, in a nice way; the old Mini’s display was the least of my worries, but the OLED is a solid improvement. The tablet I tested also has Apple’s nano-texture matte finish, which seems to dull and darken the screen slightly; on a particularly white background, the whole thing seems to shimmer. I suspect I’d like the OLED panel even more without it.

The nano-texture does, however, do an excellent job of reducing glare. The effect is actually really cool: The textured coating doesn’t extend to the bezels, so you can see a source of light or glare come in from the edge of the screen and then simply disappear as it hits the screen. So what you lose in pure screen fidelity, you gain in the ability to read on the iPad in a coffee shop without it reflecting the overhead lights like a mirror. The nano-texture option costs an extra $100, though, and requires you to upgrade to 256GB of storage, so you’re now looking at a $900 tablet. Spend the extra money on the cellular version or, better yet, don’t spend it at all.

For some reason, Apple has never deigned to put its ProMotion display tech on the iPad Mini, which has a refresh rate capped at 60Hz rather than the 120Hz you’ll get on most other recent Apple devices. This is such an odd spec choice that it has launched a bunch of conspiracy theories. Maybe it’s to make more people buy an iPhone Duo instead? Maybe it’s a 120Hz panel downgraded to 60Hz by software just to make people upgrade again next time? Apple’s answer, to Chris Lawley and others, has been something to the effect of “120Hz is irrelevant, OLED’s faster pixel response solves the problems.” I don’t agree. I don’t think the lack of ProMotion is an immediate and total dealbreaker, but refresh rates matter! To games and high-touch creative apps, of course, but you notice the difference even with basic swiping and zooming. The Apple Pencil feels more juddery moving across my screen. On a 120Hz screen, I can scroll a browser page and still clearly read the text as it’s moving. With 60Hz, it gets blurry. In all these cases, even if you don’t explicitly see the difference, you really do feel it.

The most meaningful upgrade in the new Mini, at least for me and how I use my iPad, is that it is designed to be more or less waterproof and dustproof. Its IP67 rating means that the Mini got the highest possible score for protection from “solid foreign objects,” which for our purposes mostly just means dust won’t get inside, and is also “protected against the effects of temporary immersion in water.” It’s not quite as rugged as, say, the iPhone 18 Pro, and I don’t recommend using it as an underwater camera or anything, but your tablet should be fine as long as you don’t drop it in the tub and leave it for a while.

This increased ruggedness immediately changed my iPad usage in a couple of ways. I became less worried about handing the Mini to my toddler, whose general stickiness and penchant for spilling things no longer feel like threats. I also started using the Mini more in the kitchen — I love to cook with an iPad nearby for recipes and Community episodes, and it was nice not to be constantly guarding against flour explosions and water spills. At one point, the iPad Mini even came with me in the shower during a particularly close football game. It’s still kicking.

This is, for lack of a better word, the Field iPad.

The Mini’s IP rating is also the feature that finally made me see who Apple sees as the ideal iPad Mini user. This is, for lack of a better word, the Field iPad. We hear all the time about pilots who use Minis instead of sheaves of paperwork, but Apple also sees them in use on construction sites, oil rigs, doctor’s offices, and all kinds of other places. (It’s a fun rabbit hole, actually — can I interest you in an “explosion proof” last-gen Mini for $4,500 or so?) Apple’s goal with the Mini is not really to maximize performance, satisfy the pixel-peepers, or enhance creator workflows. It’s durability and usability. Body and screen.

Apple made a couple of changes to the Mini to receive its IP rating, like ditching the speaker holes and opting for what it calls an Acoustic Display. It means the sound fires out of the front panel rather than from speaker holes. (Some TVs currently work the same way.) It doesn’t sound any better than before, maybe a tiny bit bassier, but it also doesn’t sound worse, and if anything it’s slightly better to have the sound actually pointed at you instead of out the sides of the tablet. Mostly it’s a wash in service of the ruggedization, which is good enough for me.

The rest of the hardware is just wins all the way around. The A20 Pro chip, the chipset also on the iPhone 18 Pro (and the Duo), benchmarks about 50 percent faster than the 2024 Mini for standard computing tasks, and about three times as fast for graphics-intensive ones. (The chip in my review unit has one more CPU core than the A20 Pro in the cheaper Minis; those will still be drastic improvements, but maybe a hair less so.) I notice the difference, even side by side with the old model, in how fast apps load and games play. Games like Assassin’s Creed Mirage, which were all but unplayable on the 2024 Mini, work nicely on this one. The body is about a tenth of an inch thinner and about an ounce and a half lighter. Perhaps as a result, the battery is a tiny bit smaller, but battery life remains steadfastly somewhere around nine hours of screen time.

One last hardware thing I should mention: Apple moved the front-facing camera from the portrait side of the Mini to the landscape side. On every other iPad, all of which seem to want to be used horizontally, that’s clearly the right decision. On this one, the only iPad I actually hold vertically for long periods, I’m torn. And in either case, that camera still doesn’t enable FaceID — it’s still TouchID or nothing on the Mini.

I thought the last iPad Mini was a disappointment, a product made from spare parts without any particular reason for existence. This year’s Mini feels mostly like a return to form. It’s powerful enough to be useful for a long time. It’s lighter and thinner than ever. The 60Hz refresh rate is a bummer, but I don’t think it’s a dealbreaker for most people. And it’s really the only downside. Though at this price, should you have to deal with any downsides?

After a run of new models and price increases from Apple, it’s worth level-setting a bit on iPad prices. The cheapest model you can buy is now the base iPad at $449, with an A16 chip (the one from the iPhone 14 Pro) and 128GB of storage. The cheapest iPad Air, with an M4 processor and 128GB of storage, is $749. The Pro has an M5 and 256GB for $1,199. iPads are expensive!

In this context, the new Mini’s $699 starting price feels at least $100 too high — this model has always been more expensive than the base iPad, but unless you really want or need the small screen, $50 more gets you a much better and more versatile iPad Air. As ever, the iPad Mini is really only for iPad Mini People (and pilots and oil riggers, apparently). Someday, Apple will give us the tiny flagship we’ve been waiting for, and I’ll gladly pay Apple’s top dollar for it. This is a very good iPad Mini, but it’s not quite the Mini in its final form.

Three years ago, I told you how home 3D printers had evolved for speed, producing parts in minutes that used to take hours or days. Now, the next evolution is here: printers that can swap between materials on the fly, mix different colors to produce nearly the full rainbow, mix soft and hard materials, and even mix different sizes of nozzle and different diameters of filament so you can switch between high speed and fine detail in a single print.

Today, Bambu Lab is announcing its most advanced 3D printer yet, the $2,499 H3C, which can do all of that mixing goodness — by loading nine different rolls of filament into nine different print heads.

Even if you’d never buy a printer this pricy, even if you’ve sworn off Bambu printers for good, the H3C is the perfect vehicle for me to explain where the state of the art in home printing tech is headed.

While the H3C still only prints in one material at any given moment, Bambu’s new “Swaptek” system lets the printer swap in any of the eight other nozzles in six seconds flat. All nine tools are stored in a rack at the front of the printer, where they can independently preheat to the correct temperature right before they’re needed.

With Bambu’s new “PLA Palette” cyan, magenta, yellow, and white filament, I printed a beautiful rainbow bird feather that emulated most of the other colors between those inks. With a mix of flexible TPU and rigid PLA filaments, I was able to print a hard wristwatch housing with an integrated flexible watchband. With an ultra-soft “75A” TPU filament plus a harder 90A variety, I printed a breathable, wearable shoe that… didn’t quite fit, unfortunately. (I’ll measure and try again.)

To be clear, Bambu didn’t invent toolchanger printers or color mixing. You could even argue Bambu’s slightly behind the curve, particularly after the $700-$900 Snapmaker U1 and its four-color, five-second swaps pleasantly surprised the community earlier this year. (Prusa also sells its hotly anticipated 8-tool INDX system for $2,249, though I’ve heard it has some issues.)

But I would have never bought previous toolchangers for myself, because they don’t have a place to keep your filament dry when you’re not printing. (Wet filament is one of the biggest remaining causes of print failures.) The H3C theoretically changes that by storing its nine rolls in an ostensibly airtight auto-loading container called the AMS 3H. Two of those nine slots even have built-in dryers that can hit 85 degrees Celsius and keep on drying your filament while you print, a big step up from Bambu’s previous AMS systems.

I haven’t tried the H3C for long enough to tell you if it’s ready to buy, and I’ve definitely run into some quirks in my first couple weeks of testing. I’ve had to start every single print twice because the printer complains that its “toolhead camera” isn’t initializing properly, I once lost half a day of printing to a false-positive print failure detection, and the beta build of Bambu Studio provided to reviewers initially got confused when I tried to mix colors. (I’m told that last one will be fixed by launch, and Bambu eventually provided some troubleshooting steps for the toolhead camera close to embargo time.)

You also have to load flexible TPU filaments into the H3C manually, and I learned the hard way that not all of the printer’s nine slots can actually accept a roll of TPU without causing trouble. Slot #6 can load standard filaments, but if you load the flexible stuff, another printhead can snag it and cause a nasty jam:

This also isn’t a printer where you can quickly repair a nozzle without tools, or use previous-gen toolless H, P, and A series swappable nozzles. It’s an all-new system that involves some minor disassembly, and I’m not sure about Bambu’s decision to leave filament in the toolheads between prints where it might attract moisture.

But so far, this seems like an easier, more reliable printer than the two-nozzle Bambu H2D was at launch. Other than the Slot #6 TPU jam, which Bambu actually warns reviewers about in a section of the reviewer’s guide that I missed reading, everything I’ve tried to print has come out great so far — and without creating a mountain of little “printer poop” waste when I switch between colors! Dramatically reduced waste is one of the benefits of having extra nozzles.

With any luck, the future of 3D printing won’t just be for hobbyists willing to figure out which colors of filament they need to order before each and every project, or obsessives who store dozens of colors in vacuum-sealed bags. Instead, it should be more like today’s inkjet printers — an entire rainbow of color, ready to go at a moment’s notice. And hopefully without all the inkjet printer shenanigans, of course.

If you like Bambu’s toolchanger system, you probably won’t have to pay $2,499 for long. Certification filing leaks show that the company’s already prepping a smaller “P3C” with seven tools and seven rolls of filament storage instead of nine.

Additionally, Bambu will let all three of its existing dual-nozzle printers print with two different sizes of nozzle by next spring, including the H2D, the H2C, and the X2D.
