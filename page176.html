To the very end, Cristiano Ronaldo wants ownership of the narrative.

Ahead of Jorge Jesus' Portugal squad announcement for the upcoming November international break, Ronaldo has officially retired from the international side of the game.

Ronaldo's retirement statement was polished and studio-produced, framing his departure from international football as a considered, dignified decision of a man bowing out on his own terms. But nobody in football should be fooled by the PR surrounding his Portugal farewell. Ronaldo is not walking away because of his obligation to 'hand back the No. 7 shirt'; he is jumping before he's pushed.

With Portugal’s squad announcement for the November international window imminent, whispers had grown impossible to ignore, especially after the histrionics which made headlines during the most recent international break.

For everything we know about Ronaldo and the opinion he holds of himself, the prospect of reading a squad list without his name on it would have been unbearable.

No matter how it's dressed up, his exit from the international scene is an undignified end for a footballer of his stature, burning the house down rather than accept that time had finally caught up with him.

The genesis of the split came during the conjoined September and October international camps, where Ronaldo's conduct reflected entitled petulance. He could have chosen to be an experienced mentor to one of Europe's most gifted squads, but instead behaved emotionally, putting self before side.

Germany head coach Jürgen Klopp pulled a surprise out of the hat on Thursday by naming 17-year-old Borussia Mönchengladbach prodigy Wael Mohya in his squad for the first time following the youngster's impressive start to the season.

In an interview published by the German Football Association (DFB), Klopp explained his decision to include the highly-rated attacking midfielder.

“He's one of those cases where you simply have to take notice. There aren't many 17-year-olds currently playing in the Bundesliga. That alone is an outstanding achievement. But he's also doing well – really well. I've been in contact with both him and his family, and we've discussed how we want to approach things. “Above all, the important thing is to let him experience the training sessions firsthand, because I think that is really valuable when you have the opportunity. So I'm really looking forward to having Wael with us."

Mohya, who is also eligible to represent Morocco, only made his debut for Germany's U21 side in September.

He has three goals and one assist in 10 games for Gladbach this season.

Scientists Sound the Alarm As Colorectal Cancer Reverses a 20-Year Decline

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Colorectal cancer rates in the United States are rising across a much wider range of ages than previously recognized, reversing a long period of declining diagnoses. A new study published in NEJM Evidence found that the increase is no longer concentrated among adults younger than 50, with rates now climbing in people approaching their 70s.

Colorectal cancer is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths overall in the United States and the leading cause among adults younger than 50. As more generations reach ages when the disease becomes increasingly common, researchers warn that growing numbers of diagnoses could place additional demands on cancer treatment and long-term survivorship care.

Researchers led by Caitlin Murphy, PhD, MPH, Professor of Pediatrics and Public Health Sciences at the University of Chicago, analyzed National Cancer Institute data to track changes in colorectal cancer incidence over more than two decades. The age-adjusted rate dropped from 55.2 cases per 100,000 people in 2000 to 36.7 in 2022, before increasing to 38.3 in 2023.

“This is the first time that we have seen an increase in the overall rate since in more than 20 years,” Murphy said. The study also found rising incidence across age groups through 65 to 69 years, indicating that the trend extends well beyond the younger populations that have received much of the attention.

The increase among older adults is particularly concerning because colorectal cancer screening has historically produced substantial benefits in these age groups. Rates are now rising among people ages 65 to 69 and have stopped declining among those ages 70 to 74, despite approximately 80% of people in these groups being up to date with screening. Researchers suspect that unidentified risk factors may be counteracting some of the progress achieved through earlier detection and prevention.

Murphy believes the expanding age range reflects a phenomenon known as the “birth cohort effect,” in which people born during particular periods experience different disease risks as they grow older. In this case, evidence suggests that individuals born in the 1960s and afterward may face an elevated risk of colorectal cancer that persists as they advance into middle and older adulthood.

“I think of a birth cohort effect as the interaction of age and time,” Murphy explained. “As people born around the same time move through time and age together, we’re going to see more colorectal cancers being diagnosed.”

The pattern could help connect the growing incidence among younger adults with the increases now appearing in older populations. American Cancer Society statistics show that colorectal cancer incidence among people ages 20 to 49 rose approximately 3% annually from 2013 to 2022.

“There are many unique challenges when you’re diagnosed with cancer at a young age, so it’s important that we shine a spotlight on that problem,” Murphy said. “But in terms of understanding the root cause, I think our study shows that we can consider that whatever might be causing colorectal cancer in younger people is now also causing it to increase in older people as well.”

The steepest increase occurred among adults ages 45 to 49, whose colorectal cancer incidence rose by 12.76% annually between 2019 and 2023. Researchers believe this unusually rapid increase largely reflects expanded screening following a change in national guidelines that lowered the recommended starting age from 50 to 45.

Screening can temporarily raise reported incidence by detecting cancers that were already present but had not yet been diagnosed. “That means that screening is doing as we intend it to do — it’s catching cancer earlier,” Murphy said.

However, increased screening cannot fully account for the broader rise across age groups, especially among older adults who already have high screening participation. The researchers also examined whether disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic contributed to the trend. The increase persisted even when data from 2020 and 2021 were excluded, and screening activity had largely returned to pre-pandemic levels by January 2021. These findings suggest that pandemic-related delays are unlikely to explain the overall increase observed in 2023.

The underlying causes of the rising incidence remain uncertain. Researchers are now examining whether exposure to chemicals in the environment could contribute to colorectal cancer risk, although no specific exposure has been established as responsible for the observed trends.

“These results motivate us to understand reasons for this rise, so risk factors can be identified and possibly reduced,” said Sonia Kupfer, MD, director of the Gastrointestinal Cancer Risk and Prevention Clinic at UChicago Medicine.

Murphy is collaborating with Xin Hu, PhD, a toxicologist at Emory University, to investigate thousands of environmental chemical exposures using small blood samples. This approach will help identify substances that may warrant closer examination.

The researchers then plan to work with Kupfer to study selected chemicals using colon organoids, laboratory-grown models that reproduce some features of human colon tissue. These models will allow scientists to investigate how particular exposures affect colon cells and potentially identify biological processes involved in cancer development.

Until the causes become clearer, colorectal cancer screening remains one of the most effective ways to identify the disease early and prevent deaths. Current national guidelines recommend routine screening for adults at average risk beginning at age 45 and continuing through age 75. Screening decisions outside that range should be individualized based on factors such as family history, symptoms, and other medical conditions.

Colonoscopy remains the gold standard because it can both detect cancer and remove precancerous polyps. Other screening methods include home-based stool tests and newer blood-based tests, although their capabilities and recommended testing intervals differ.

“There are several ways to do screening that should be discussed with a clinician, so people know what the different screening tests are intended for and how often these need to be repeated,” Kupfer added.

For adults younger than 45 who do not qualify for routine screening, recognizing possible warning signs is particularly important. Blood in the stool, persistent changes in bowel habits, and unexplained weight loss should prompt medical evaluation, even in people without a known family history or genetic risk.

The research team also recommends continued monitoring of national cancer registry data as new figures become available. If the upward trend persists as large generations of Americans grow older, healthcare systems may face increasing demand for cancer treatment, follow-up care, and long-term support for survivors.

Reference: “Colorectal Cancer among U.S. Adults, 2000–2023” by Caitlin C. Murphy, Folasade P. May, Sonia S. Kupfer, Xin Hu and Samir Gupta, 27 October 2026, NEJM Evidence. DOI: 10.1056/EVIDpha2600217

The study was supported by funding from the National Institutes of Health and the Eli and Edythe Broad Center of Regenerative Medicine and Stem Cell Research Ablon Scholars Program.

When will Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo retire from professional football?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

When will Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo bring the curtains down on their careers? The end is nigh for both.

They once ruled the footballing world as the best players of their generation. Their legacies remain strong as two of the best players of all time. The GOAT debates are endless.

But time catches up with everyone and the end is near for Messi’s and Ronaldo’s legendary careers. The question is: how much longer do we have left to enjoy them?

Ronaldo is the older of the pair at 41. Messi recently turned 39. Neither are playing in Europe anymore, of course, with Ronaldo in the Saudi Pro League and Messi in MLS.

Both have now brought their international careers to an end, with Messi having already said goodbye to Argentina and Ronaldo having announced his retirement from Portugal.

Ronaldo’s latest announcement represents a significant milestone in the winding down of his career, although he has yet to confirm whether he’ll play in one last farewell match.

At club level, both players still have time left on their contracts. Ronaldo’s deal with Al-Nassr is due to last until the end of the season. Messi, meanwhile, has a contract until December 2028 with Inter Miami.

Per some reports from when he signed his latest renewal in 2025, Ronaldo’s contract contains an option to extend into the summer of 2028 as well.

Thus, there could be another couple of years left of Messi and Ronaldo as professional footballers.

But contracts aren’t always fulfilled and many factors could influence each player’s future. We just hope injury isn’t the way either go out.

The less outspoken of the two long-time Ballon d’Or rivals, Messi hasn’t given too much away about his future in club football.

“I know that the moment I feel that I am no longer able to perform, that I no longer enjoy or help my team-mates, I will quit,” he offered in March 2024.

“I’m very self-critical. I know when I’m good, when I’m bad, when I’m playing well, and when I’m playing badly.

“When I feel it’s time to take that step, I’ll take it without thinking about age. If I feel good, I’ll always try to keep competing because it’s what I like and what I know how to do.

“I haven’t yet thought of what I’m going to do when I retire. Today, I try to enjoy the day-to-day, the moments, without thinking about life after [football].”

However, the sad news of his father passing away prompted Messi to give a rare update on his future this summer.

“I don’t know how to carry on. I used to just play football, and now I’m really not sure if I’ll carry on doing it for much longer,” he posted on social media.

“You were by my side from the very beginning. We were so close to the end. Why didn’t you hang in there just a little longer so we could finish this together?”

Reflecting on the World Cup after Argentina lost the final to Spain, Messi added: “I wanted to win it so I could bring the trophy to you and show you a new one. I couldn’t, my legs had nothing left. This time I tried to push past my physical limits, but I couldn’t. I never managed to feel right.”

And since, Messi has confirmed he is bowing out from international duty altogether.

He posted on Instagram on August 31: “After the time that has passed since the final, and after giving it a lot of thought, I want to let everyone know that I am retiring from the National Team…

“It was a decision that hurt—and still hurts deep down—but I understand that the time has come. I swear to you that I always gave it my all—not just in these last few years when we won everything, but before that too; I always fought and left everything on the field for this jersey, to bring you joy and make you feel proud to be Argentine through football.

“Time is running short, chapters come to a close, and this is one that hurts me deeply. I love, have loved, and will always love being part of the National Team. I’ve given everything I had; I have nothing left to give, and besides, there are some great young players coming up who deserve to be here.

“Thank you for all the affection over these past 20 years. I will miss hearing you from the pitch so much. Now I’ll be just one of you, cheering and supporting from the outside (through the good times and the bad—especially the bad).

“Thank you to my family for always being there, for accompanying me on this journey—so beautiful, yet so difficult. Thank you to every coach, teammate, and official I’ve shared this path with. I take with me unforgettable memories and moments.

“I leave with the peace of mind and pride of having given you—alongside my teammates—moments we once only dreamed of. It was an incredible experience to live this with you all. I love you!

“Thank you, God, for making me Argentine.

“*I wrote these words on July 21st, two days after the final. Today, after what happened with my dad, I am even more convinced than before.”

And so that’s the end of Messi’s international career, after 207 caps, 125 goals, 68 assists, two Copa Americas and a World Cup.

Ronaldo has been more vocal about the end of his career, partially because of his personality and partially because it’s probably closer.

He hinted in November 2025 that he could retire ‘soon’, before clarifying: “Soon for me means in 10 years… No, I’m joking.

“I’m really enjoying the moment right now. As you know, in football, when you reach some age, you count the months very quick.

“I feel very good in this moment. I score goals, I still feel quick and sharp. I’m enjoying my game in the national team. But of course, let’s be honest. What I mean by soon is probably one or two years.”

He has also confirmed his ongoing pursuit of reaching 1000 career goals will have an impact on when he calls it quits.

“It’s hard to continue playing, but I am motivated,” he said in December.

“My passion is high and I want to continue. It doesn’t matter where I play, whether in the Middle East or Europe. I always enjoy playing football and I want to keep going.

“You know what my goal is. I want to win trophies and I want to reach that number [1000 goals] that you all know. I will reach the number for sure, if no injuries.”

For what it’s worth, Messi could be capable of reaching 1000 goals as well, but it remains to be seen if that will have a bearing on his future.

Ronaldo’s international future, meanwhile, has now been settled. Having previously suggested that he would reflect on his Portugal career after the World Cup, the five-time Ballon d’Or winner has officially announced his retirement from international football.

He made the announcement in a heartfelt farewell message to the Portuguese people, opening with the words: “Portugal will always be a part of me.”

“The time has come to hang up our national team’s number 7 shirt. I never felt it was mine alone, but for 23 years I had the privilege and responsibility of wearing it and defending it in stadiums all around the world,” Ronaldo wrote.

The 41-year-old admitted that the decision was difficult, adding: “Today, the time has come to hand it over, but it was not an easy decision, because some decisions are not made alone.”

Ronaldo’s international career therefore comes to an end after 234 appearances and 146 goals for Portugal, having helped his country win Euro 2016 and two Nations League titles.

That leaves his club future as the biggest remaining question. Ronaldo has made no announcement about retiring from football altogether, and his pursuit of 1000 career goals remains a significant motivation.

His current contract with Al-Nassr expires at the end of the season, by which point he’ll be 42. Every chance he calls it quits then if he scores his thousandth goal before then, which appears likely.

InfoQ Homepage News Building a Culture for a Kubernetes-Based Platform in a Bank

Nov 05, 2026 3 min read

In a banking environment, open source can earn trust by running real workloads, as Marcy Paramonova and Stéphane Cusin explained in their talk Building Cloud Native Culture in a Bank at KubeCon & CloudNativeCon Europe. Deploying Kubernetes was the easy part; the hard part was culture: community of practice, shared knowledge, rituals, ownership. Teams try things, keep what works, and improve bottom-up.

Open source was not an obvious choice in their banking environment, Cusin mentioned. They received questions about support and accountability. Instead of trying to convince people with theory, they started running real workloads. Over time, trust was built by operating the platform day after day:

Open source stopped being seen as a risk. It became the foundation.

They treated their first version of Kubernetes like a project. "We delivered it, we got a good adoption", Cusin said. But the operation was incredibly hard. Not because the technology was broken, but because they hadn’t built the culture around it yet:

Nobody knew who to call, responsibilities were blurry, and teams were consuming the platform without really contributing to it. Every incident felt like a fire drill.

They realized deploying Kubernetes was the easy part. The hard part was building the culture with a community of practice around it. The shared knowledge, the rituals, the ownership model, Cusin explained:

Without that culture, no platform survives its own success.

They invite people to experiment, for instance by having a yearly competition where everybody can submit their ideas and the one that wins, actually gets implemented. Many of their improvements came from the bottom up, Paramonova argued.

They used open source as a compass, something that helped them orientate a subject, Cusin explained:

When Kubernetes appeared, we recognized it immediately. Strong Kubernetes, transparent governance, and real production users. The compass pointed; we followed. And now we are watching OpenStack the same way.

A compass doesn’t force you in one direction. It helps you recognize the right opportunity when it arrives, Cusin said.

The proposed rewrite could expand SEC oversight and revive stricter rules on crypto withdrawals, custody, proof of reserves, and investor protection.

House Democrats are reportedly discussing a broader crypto market structure bill built partly on Rep. Maxine Waters' earlier investor-rights proposals.

According to a Nov. 4 post by journalist Eleanor Terrett, the proposals include a larger SEC role, a smaller CFTC role, and more government ethics restrictions.

Industry sources who spoke with Democratic staff form the basis of that account, and the bill text remains unpublished. Waters' 2025 amendment shows what customer rules a Democratic framework could carry, and the September CLARITY compromise shows how much of that ground the Senate text already covered before cloture failed 49-50 on Sept. 15.

Amendment #31, a nine-page text from July 2025, would have imposed duties on exchanges, brokers, custodians, and other retail-facing digital asset firms. Withdrawals and transfers would be due within 24 hours, except in cases of documented good-faith fraud.

Firms would join SIPC if eligible or fund an equivalent customer-asset protection arrangement, hold customer assets in segregated, bankruptcy-remote accounts, and obtain affirmative, revocable consent before rehypothecating, lending, or staking them.

Cryptographically verifiable proof of reserves would include a licensed accountant's monthly attestation.

Crypto customer orders would take priority over proprietary orders under best-execution practices, and affiliated proprietary traders or market makers would be barred from trading against customers on the same platform.

Human support would be reachable around the clock within five minutes, and customers and regulators would hear of specified security breaches within 48 hours.

The text added a one-page fee disclosure, next-business-day settlement, and an indicator showing the share of customers holding an asset at a loss. Its insurance language covered losses from a covered entity's regulatory violations through bonds, insurance or insured trusts, a narrower promise than compensating every market loss.

The House Rules Committee voted 8-4 on July 14, 2025 against making the amendment eligible for floor consideration, and the amendment died in committee.

The Congressional Research Service's Sept. 15 analysis describes customer-property protections, limits on commingling and bankruptcy treatment for digital asset customers.

It also lists CFTC registration for exchanges, brokers and dealers, core principles covering crypto market manipulation, conflicts and customer protection, and Bank Secrecy Act duties.

The SEC kept a role in investment-contract offerings, including an exemption for qualifying token originators up to $200 million with accompanying disclosures.

Sens. Cynthia Lummis, John Boozman and Tim Scott said their Sept. 14 revision included 126 substantive revisions requested by Democrats.

Among them were ethics provisions drawing on the Tillis-Gallego approach with state attorney general enforcement, and Treasury authority to restrict stablecoin incentives when bank deposit outflows are large.

The figure is the sponsors' tally, which stands apart from Democratic endorsement of the text. The ethics text required certain covered officials with large financial interests to divest or use blind trusts, and the sides remained divided over its scope and enforceability.

Waters' text set a uniform 24-hour deadline and a 12-per-year attestation schedule, affirmative consent for staking and lending, customer-first order priority and affiliated-trading bans, and compensation mechanisms.

September described principles, and a Democratic successor could turn principles into enforceable rights with deadlines, reporting standards, and penalties.

The CFTC oversees derivatives and has antifraud and antimanipulation authority over spot commodity markets, but full spot exchange supervision needs new statute, which September's draft sought to provide.

A Democratic text could add SEC oversight of intermediaries, issuers or customer standards, or redraw the definitions that sort activity between agencies.

Exchanges would face added registration, disclosure, order-handling, and custody duties; custodians would face higher segregation and verification standards; and smaller platforms would face higher fixed compliance costs.

The March 2026 SEC and CFTC interpretation treats Bitcoin as a digital commodity, and exchange regulation and token classification are separate legal matters.

A customer holding BTC on an exchange would be affected by withdrawal deadlines, reserve verification, and insolvency treatment, while a self-custody holder has a different exposure.

2 Bank Stocks to Buy After Their Third-Quarter Earnings Reports

With the third-quarter earnings season well underway, most banks have now reported.

Largely speaking, bank profits have continued to climb, as most banks take advantage of higher interest rates and a steep yield curve, in which lower-maturity bonds yield less than higher-maturity ones, an environment that most banks tend to benefit from.

Banks typically borrow money at short-term rates and lend it out at longer-term rates, which is why banks benefit from a steeper yield curve. Still, it's not all roses. Banks are also grappling with higher funding costs, while investors wonder whether a real credit cycle will ever show up.

Here are two bank stocks to buy after their third-quarter earnings reports.

For investors just starting to look at bank stocks, or who simply want a little exposure, JPMorgan Chase (JPM +4.18%) is an easy place to begin. It's the country's largest bank, with more than $5 trillion in assets, and is run by Chief Executive Officer Jamie Dimon, whom many regard as the best leader in banking.

JPMorgan Chase is good at many different banking businesses, from credit card lending to investment banking to wealth management and payments.

So you've got a bank that can benefit from a steep yield curve like other traditional banks, but JPMorgan will also benefit from large initial public offerings like the one planned for artificial intelligence (AI) company Anthropic, which only a handful of the country's largest investment banks can say. This gives JPMorgan revenue diversity that few banks have.

JPMorgan actually missed Wall Street estimates for both earnings and revenue in the third quarter, which is rare for the bank. The undershot came from weaker fixed-income trading revenue and higher expenses.

However, JPMorgan raised its full-year outlook for net interest income (NII), the spread revenue the bank makes on its loans and bond holdings after covering its funding costs for the deposits and other interest-bearing liabilities that fund those assets.

Management provided a forecast of $107 billion for NII in 2027, representing roughly a 10% increase over its projected 2026 NII. While JPMorgan trades at a premium valuation of roughly 3 times its tangible book value (TBV), or its theoretical liquidation value, it has earned this premium during two decades of excellence.

Investors should ignore the third-quarter earnings miss and buy the stock for the long haul.

Raleigh, North Carolina-based First Citizens BancShares (FCNCA +0.41%) has total assets of roughly $241 billion, putting it at a fraction of the size of JPMorgan Chase, but still one of the larger banks in the country.

First Citizens has delivered a masterclass in growth through acquisition, particularly by purchasing distressed banks or those on the brink of failure.

For instance, First Citizens acquired the struggling CIT Group in 2022, which traded at a significant discount to its TBV. As a result, the deal was 38% accretive to First Citizens' TBV, which is a huge increase. Most bank acquisitions dilute TBV and take two or three years to earn back the dilution.

First Citizens struck again when it acquired all of Silicon Valley Bank's deposits, loans, and branches after the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) took over during the banking crisis in 2023, of which Silicon Valley Bank was at the center.

Shares of First Citizens soared about 40% after announcing the acquisition. Furthermore, First Citizens had a loss-sharing agreement with the FDIC on loans, as well as other protections that made the deal even more appealing.

After its third-quarter earnings on Oct. 23, First Citizens' stock rose nearly 8% after adjusted earnings per share of $55.37 easily topped estimates of $45.89, according to FactSet. The bank's board also approved a $1.2 billion share repurchase plan through next year.

First Citizens is a proven acquirer, and I suspect it will continue to grow in this manner.
