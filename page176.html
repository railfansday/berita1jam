Plants mix different breeding strategies to survive in a world with changing climates and fewer bees

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Simulations show plants that reject their own pollen and plants that self-fertilize can coexist for ages, swapping dominance without settling.

Apple trees have a strict biological rule for reproduction. They reject their own pollen. That means they need a completely different type of apple tree nearby to produce fruit.

While many wild plants rely on this exact same partner system, plenty of other plant species have evolved to drop the rule entirely, allowing them to successfully fertilize themselves.

A population holding both kinds was supposed to be passing through on its way to choosing one rule or the other.

In computer simulations of evolving plants, the mixed populations didn’t get there. With nothing in the environment changing, the two kinds kept trading dominance for as long as the simulations ran. Neither kind took over.

The researchers call it a volatile mixed mode. A mixed population in the wild that looks unstable, they wrote, may be in a lasting state of its own.

Tamar Friedlander, a senior lecturer at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem’s Robert H. Smith Institute of Plant Sciences and Genetics in Agriculture, led the work with Amit Jangid.

Ohad Noy Feldheim of the University’s Einstein Institute of Mathematics is the third author. Everything here happened inside a model.

In an interview with Earth.com, Friedlander said the result broke a habit of thought. Biologists expect that when a population has several options, some are better than others, she said.

Over the long run, the population is supposed to reach the better one and stay there. Earlier models had the mix fading away or holding steady at fixed proportions.

“Thus, it was very surprising that we didn’t get either of these under a large parameter regime,” she said.

The populations, in her words, “not only did not settle to either of the ‘pure’ strategies (only self-fertilization or only outcrossing) but also did not settle at fixed proportions of these strategies.”

The rejection system works like a lock and a set of keys. The female side of a flower makes a toxin that stops a pollen tube from growing. Each pollen grain carries proteins that disarm the toxins of other mating types but not its own.

About 40 percent of plant families use some version of this, including wild tomatoes, potatoes, apples, pears, citrus, and coffee. Washington State University‘s tree fruit site has a primer on why orchards need two varieties.

The system can break. A mutation that silences the toxin, or adds a key that fits the plant’s own lock, produces a plant that can fertilize itself.

That plant gains a sure route to seeds when pollen is scarce, but it passes on two sets of genes to each seed instead of one.

It pays for that with inbreeding. Seeds from self-fertilization carry more harmful mutations, and more of them fail.

The plants that reject their own pollen pay a different price. Their pollen is useless on every plant of their own mating type, so a type that grows common finds fewer partners.

Friedlander’s team simulated populations of 500 plants for 100,000 generations, discarding the first 50,000 as a warm-up.

Each plant carried two copies of the gene set, and each copy held one toxin and nine keys. Every protein was written as a string of 18 amino acids that could mutate.

Two dials set the outcome. One was how badly self-fertilized seeds suffer from inbreeding. The other was how loosely the keys fit the locks, which decides how many mating types a population can hold.

With heavy inbreeding costs and loose-fitting keys, every plant ended up rejecting its own pollen. With light costs, every plant ended up self-fertilizing.

In between lay the third state, where the share of self-rejecting plants rose and fell for the rest of the run and the number of mating types swung with it.

Types tended to vanish several at a time, in what the authors describe as avalanches. What triggers those collapses, they wrote, is still obscure.

A shortage of pollen was expected to push populations toward self-fertilization, since a plant that can pollinate itself doesn’t need a visitor. The model found the opposite.

When cross-pollen was scarce, the range of settings in which self-rejection won grew at the expense of the mixed state. Friedlander said the explanation lies in how the two sexes compete.

When pollen is plentiful, every female is sure of a partner and only the males compete, so female proteins evolve mainly to avoid matching their own pollen.

When pollen is scarce, females must compete too, which makes them easier to match and raises the number of mating types a population can support.

Researchers often treat self-fertilization as a plant’s backup plan for when cross-pollination fails, she told Earth.com. Self-fertilizing plants do turn up at the edges of a species’ range, where partners are few.

The move from rejecting self-pollen to accepting it has long been treated as a one-way street. A plant that gives up the rule usually sheds the flower traits that attract insects and purges some of its harmful mutations.

Across much of the model’s settings, though, the end state didn’t depend on the start, and a population that began self-fertilizing could evolve the rejection system from scratch.

The exception was the loosest-fitting keys, where the mutation needed for the reverse trip was so rare that the wait became impossibly long.

The model also held the cost of inbreeding fixed, so it describes the short term, before prolonged self-fertilization purges enough mutations to change the odds.

Mixed populations exist in the wild. Long-term studies that track one population’s proportions over time are lacking, the authors wrote, and those are what would test the prediction.

The model also predicts that mixed populations carry fewer and shorter-lived mating types than purely self-rejecting ones, and more inbreeding written into their genomes. Both could be checked in plant genomes.

Asked which population she would most like to watch, Friedlander pointed to wild tomato species growing in South America.

She also said the model doesn’t apply directly to orchards, where trees are genetically identical and do not evolve. Self-rejecting crops do depend on pollinators, she added, and a decline in them could cut yields.

A warming climate and fewer pollinating insects both change the pressures on how a plant reproduces, the authors wrote.

Whether real populations respond by picking a side, or by swinging between the two for generations, is what a long field study of those wild tomatoes could begin to answer.

The full study was published in the journal PRX Life.

Bentley’s First EV Is Already Sold Out, And Production Hasn’t Even Started

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

Bentley’s first electric car, the Torcal SUV, looks like a hit already. Even though production hasn’t started yet, the $220,000+ EV is sold out for 2027, according to Frank-Steffen Wallier, the company’s head honcho, quoted by Automotive News.

The British luxury automaker said that all available production slots for next year have already been allocated and that the company is now taking orders that will be delivered in 2028. Bentley didn’t say how many EVs it will build in 2027, but the initial customer response has made the marque quite happy with the outcome.

“Needless to say, we’re very pleased with the response and position we’re in,” a company spokesperson told Automotive News.

That’s great news for Bentley, which isn’t doing great in terms of sales and profit. From January through September, it sold 5,970 cars globally, 17% fewer compared to the same period last year. Meanwhile, the profit dropped by more than half to $54.1 million through the first nine months of the year.

The Torcal, which is smaller than the Bentayga SUV, is the brand’s cheapest model, though it still costs a pretty penny. The base Torcal starts at $222,495, followed by the more powerful Torcal S at $262,695, and the Torcal First Edition at $300,995. All things considered, Walliser said that the smaller size and relatively lower price compared to the Bentayga have attracted new and younger customers to the brand.

By comparison, the Bentayga starts at $231,150, the Flying Spur sedan starts at $254,850, and the Continental GT two-door starts at $288,750.

So far, the Torcal S has proved to be the most popular version of the new EV, but when it comes to personalization, the sky’s the limit. “We see cars spec’d north of 300,000 pounds ($397,086),” said Walliser. “Individual content does not stop in case of an electric car. There have been some assumptions, and some people have been critical if people would be ready to do so.”

Based on the same underpinnings as the electric Porsche Cayenne, the Bentley Torcal is powered by a 113-kilowatt-hour battery that can be charged at up to 400 kilowatts. It can go from 10% to 80% in less than 20 minutes, and the estimated EPA range is expected to be over 300 miles.

Built on the Premium Platform Electric (PPE) architecture, it has two electric motors that make 810 horsepower and 881 pound-feet of torque in the standard Torcal and a whopping 876 hp and 996 lb-ft of torque in the Torcal S. When the model debuted in September, Bentley said it was the most powerful and quietest model to come out of the factory in Crewe.

To make the Torcal a reality, Bentley invested $463 million to upgrade its factory in England, plus at least the same amount for supply chain preparations and research and development, Walliser said.

Despite the electric SUV’s initial success, the British automaker is still heavily invested in gas and plug-in hybrid models, and the Bentayga isn’t going anywhere. “We have a very good combination with Torcal and Bentayga,” Walliser said. “The segment definitely has space for two cars.”

2 Great Space Stocks Team Up on a $1.3 Billion Deal to Beat SpaceX at Satellite Internet

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AST SpaceMobile will pay Firefly $1.3 billion for some new rocket engines -- but how much of that cash will Firefly actually ever receive?

Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX -1.26%) dominates America's space sector. Starting off as a rocket launcher for other companies, SpaceX has evolved into a company that makes most of its money by using its own satellites to provide communications services on Earth -- a task made easier by having its own fleet of reusable rockets.

Other space companies aren't so fortunate.

AST SpaceMobile (ASTS -12.33%), for example, invented and was first to prove the concept of using satellites to connect calls between two ordinary, off-the-shelf cellphones (not specialized satellite phones). Lacking its own rockets, however, AST has to date been dependent upon other companies to provide the launch services it needs to get its satellites into orbit.

Which launch providers in particular? So far, AST has flown its satellites with India's ISRO, Blue Origin, and SpaceX itself. That last one is obviously suboptimal, though -- it's not a great corporate strategy to make yourself dependent on your biggest competitor for launch services!

And there are other options. The list of private companies that have successfully flown a rocket to orbit may not be a long one, but it's getting longer:

A couple of other names might deserve to be on this list, having flown once or twice but not continually. But for the most part, this is it. And one of these launch names jumps out especially: Firefly.

Last week, you see, Firefly offered to help AST out of its jam ... and collect a big payday for the service. For $1.3 billion, Firefly agreed to sell AST SpaceMobile 36 rocket engines, which is enough to outfit three full-size heavy lift rockets -- which AST intends to build itself. To this end, Firefly will provide AST engineering support over three years as AST builds a fleet of 20-ton "Blacklift" rockets (which it expects to have ready to fly by late 2028).

Firefly will also grant AST a license to use Firefly designs, enabling AST to build additional engines for additional rockets on its own.

For Firefly, the advantages of this deal seem obvious. Firefly gets $1.3 billion to build and deliver 36 engines (so about $36 million per engine). Firefly can then reinvest this money in itself "to rapidly expand our rocket production."

Viewed from the perspective of a Firefly investor, the only quibble I have with this deal is the form of payment. Firefly says AST will pay the $1.3 billion in "cash and shares of AST Class A Common Stock." The cash part is fine. What worries me is that Firefly might accept too much of its payment in the form of ASTS stock.

I'm not at all convinced that AST SpaceMobile can build and fly a rocket successfully by 2028.

As veteran space journalist Eric Berger at Ars Technica has pointed out, this timeline isn't just optimistic; it's "wildly, fantastically, and stupidly optimistic." After all, the fastest record for taking a rocket from design to orbital flight (ever) was set by SpaceX with the Falcon 9 -- and that took 4.7 years, more than twice the time AST is budgeting for.

While you can't rule out the possibility that AST will accomplish the feat, I wouldn't bet on it.

Nor would I invest in AST stock based on its promise to do so. Nor -- and this part is the most important for Firefly investors -- would I allow AST to pay me for my engines in too much ASTS stock.

So how much ASTS stock is Firefly accepting in payment for its engines?

Well, of the $1.3 billion headline number, only the first $400 million will be paid up front in cash, with an additional $400 million paid in ASTS stock. A tranche of $250 million will be paid upon delivery of the engines, with $250 million owed for engineering services. When all's said and done, as much as $900 million of the $1.3 billion may be paid in hard currency. But at least $400 million -- and potentially more -- could end up paid in ASTS stock, which could turn out to be worthless if (read "when") it fails to launch Blacklift on schedule.

TL;DR: This deal may not be as good news for Firefly investors as it appears on the surface, and Firefly may end up with a whole lot less cash than investors expect.

james bridle and the power of solar, zooplankton, and non-human entities

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

ancient symbols and microscopic organisms meet contemporary solar technology in an artistic reflection on energy, ecology, and coexistence.

annalise kamegawa November 17, 2026 7 min

Back in 2022, James Bridle—artist, technologist, and author of the book New Dark Age (2018)—created a work called Solar Panels (Radiolaria Series). It was a series of five functional 100W photovoltaic generators, the faces of which were etched with tiny, microscopic zooplankton called Radiolaria. Looking at images of their silica skeletons, enlarged hundreds of times so they are visible to the human eye, reveals a universe of spiky arms, swirling bodies, intricate lattices, and geometric patterns. Juxtaposed with each other, and containing their own advanced modes of capturing energy, Bridle creates a compelling parallel between non-human and human intelligence, and what that could mean for the future of the earth.

Across their writing, installations, and lectures, James Bridle has become known for thinking about the ways that technology, the natural world, and humanity influence each other, with a strong emphasis on understanding ‘intelligence’. They look beyond the popular, mainstream ideas associated with the word, often linked to IQ, large language models, and other forms of reconstructing human thought patterns. In their piece, AI was built on human intelligence. Nature has more to teach, they express the urgency of going beyond this definition, and to learn from the other creatures we share the Earth with.

It is this line of thinking that has underwritten their works with solar panels, water collection systems, and vernacular architecture. Looking back to their 2019 exhibition, Through Other Eyes, which they curated with NeMe Art Centre in Cyprus, an emphasis on more-than-human forms of thinking and creating is evident. Here, they invoked the 1922 writing of Dziga Vertov, in which he describes his fascination with documentation through the lens of a camera. Bridle parallels this with the technologies of the twenty-first century – all the mania, fascination, and consequences – saying that, if we apply these new or different ‘eyes’, those of machines and animals, ‘They might also, if we are open to them, offer us visions of unexpected insight, and startling beauty.’ It is within this vision – trans-species and trans-machine – that Bridle’s work takes form.

Things Said, Things Seen, and Things Done, by James Bridle at Mystery 19 Visual Arts Initiator III – But Don’t Tell AnyoneThings Said, Things Seen, and Things Done, by James Bridle at Mystery 19 Visual Arts Initiator III – But Don’t Tell Anyone

Putting an image of radiolaria on a solar panel conjures ideas about the ability to sustain life and other modalities of using energy generated by natural processes. It calls into question the human need to consume power. Radiolaria, microscopic yet possessing highly advanced skeletal structures, can exist symbiotically with algae that provide them with energy. There is an ancient logic to their existence that can be traced back to the Cambrian. Bridle, in their description of the project, highlights how they were ‘famously illustrated, by the German naturalist Ernst Haeckel, who coined the term “ecology” in 1866.’ The complex structures of these organisms continue to inspire wonder, demonstrating how something so small can be so powerful and complex.

Expanding this logic to the scale of solar panels, a technologically intensive mode of capturing and converting energy from the sun in a way that can power human inventions calls into question the effectiveness of human ‘technology’ when compared to that of the non-human. Here is an incredible invention that can create sustainable energy, a promising alternative to fossil fuels, yet it takes advanced electronics and logistics to achieve what a single-celled organism has been doing for thousands of years. Here, Bridle once again asks us to consider what innovation means, not when we compare it to the span of human history.

In 2023, Bridle expanded on the concept in Things Said, Things Seen, and Things Done, an installation on the archaeological site of Elefsina in Greece, Mystery 19 Visual Arts Initiator III – But Don’t Tell Anyone, just north of Athens. Instead of radiolaria, the images engraved on three 100W solar photovoltaic panels are ‘the wheatsheaf, the poppy, and the calathus,’ which ‘honor the ancient cult of Demeter, and the veneration of the living Earth which she represents.’ Here, the comparison of contemporary power generation with beliefs in ancient deities asks us to question the ways in which energy is created, used, and distributed. Bridle used solar panels to power other works in the installation and the site itself, going on to remark that ‘The technology to do these things, is available to us, and the knowledge of how to do them is not a mystery, nor a secret, but it might require new rituals, and new initiations, to distribute it more widely. This is one of those rituals.’

the poppy that would be inscribed on Bridle’s solar panelssketch of Things Said, Things Seen, and Things Done, an installation on the archaeological site of Elefsina in Greece, Mystery 19 Visual Arts Initiator III – But Don’t Tell Anyone by James Bridle

Ultimately, James Bridle’s solar panel creations reflect a broader vision of posthuman utopias that runs throughout their artistic practice: one in which technology, humanity, and the natural world exist in a more reciprocal relationship. By bringing together ancient natural intelligence and contemporary technologies, Bridle challenges human exceptionalism and conventional ideas of progress. Their work encourages us to look beyond ourselves for models of innovation, recognizing that the natural world already holds sophisticated forms of knowledge. In doing so, they invite us to imagine futures founded on ecological interdependence, collaboration, and coexistence.

Cassava reportedly seeks up to $1bn for Nvidia chips for African AI

Strive Masiyiwa’s Cassava is in talks with Middle Eastern and US investors to fund GPUs for its African data centres.

Strive Masiyiwa speaks alongside then Secretary of State Antony Blinken and then Assistant Secretary for African Affairs Molly Phee at the U.S.-Africa Leaders Summit foreign ministers’ dinner in Washington, 14 December 2022.

Cassava Technologies is seeking to raise as much as $1bn to buy Nvidia chips for its African AI data centres, Loni Prinsloo reported for Bloomberg. The report cites people familiar with the matter who asked not to be named.

Cassava declined to comment to Bloomberg, and TNW has not independently verified the report. The talks are ongoing and may not lead to a deal, the people said.

The company, founded by Zimbabwean billionaire Strive Masiyiwa, is talking to investors in the Middle East and the US, according to Bloomberg. Most of the money would buy graphics processing units (GPUs), the chips that train and run AI models.

Some would also prepare Cassava’s fibre and data centre networks for AI workloads, the people said. Its Cape Town facility, built to Nvidia’s specifications, is already running at full capacity, Bloomberg reported.

Cassava is expanding through partners. In Egypt, it is working with Vodafone Business and Elsewedy Electric on a $1bn data centre project that includes the country’s first sovereign AI data centre. Vodafone Egypt is part of Vodacom Group.

Rivals are moving too. MTN, Africa’s largest mobile operator, has set up a data centre venture with a UAE-based investor. It plans AI-ready sites in South Africa and Nigeria.

Africa has less than 1% of the world’s AI data centres, according to Bloomberg. Gulf investors may also see Africa as a way to spread risk, after data centres in the Gulf became targets during the conflict involving the US, Israel and Iran.

A successful raise would help Cassava compete with Microsoft, Amazon and Google, which are all investing in Africa.
