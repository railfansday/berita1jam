Bringing the outside in has long been the gardener’s solution to less time spent outside over the winter months. Adding bowls and baskets of spring flowers to tabletops and windowsills is an easy way to fill a room with scent and color. And while pots of paperwhites (the heavily-scented white narcissi) and amaryllis have become mainstays during the holidays, a miniature indoor garden of other spring-flowering bulbs can provide a longer display to take you through the dark days of winter. Almost any spring bulbs will do, but know that forced or prepared bulbs are specially treated to a period of cold before you buy them to encourage earlier-than-normal flowering.

In her Complete Floristry Masterclass for Create Academy, British florist Willow Crossley shares her own take on the potted garden, combining amaryllis and hyacinths in a large splatterware bowl topped with moss and lichen-covered branches. Below, we break down how to create your own version at home.

The vessel is a key consideration. Single bulbs can be very effective in small pots, but to create an indoor garden, opt for large bowls, antique tureens and jardineres, or wicker bowls and baskets with a liner—all work well. Because the bowls are often a centerpiece and on display for a long period, it’s worth seeking out the most beautiful vessels. Blue and white china is a stunning backdrop for blue flowers including muscari; antique copper vessels look great with white or yellow flowers like miniature narcissi or leucojums. At Burford Garden Centre in the Cotswolds, bulb gardens are planted into hollowed-out chunks of wood and topped with moss and pine cones, like a woodland floor in miniature.

Fill the bottom of your vessel with gravel or grit for drainage (bulbs do not want to sit in any amount of water). Bulbs do not need compost; they already have the nutrients they need to flower so you can add bulbs directly on top of the grit, or add a layer of compost if you prefer. Potted bulbs that are already planted into soil can be slotted into the vessel straight into the gravel layer. If necessary, gently tease bulbs and their roots apart to add to the display. It’s worth spending some time here to think about the way the bowl looks from each side, imagining the whole when it’s in flower.

Once you are happy with the positioning of each bulb, top all the gaps with clumps of moss, which can be raked up from the lawn. Ensure that all the bulbs roots are tucked inside and that no soil is visible. Then dress with cones, twigs, lichen and small branches. Gnarly twigs of larch or magnolia work well but birch can also be very beautiful, especially if it has some dangling catkins. A cage of branches will help to support flowers as they grow and become top heavy.

Note: If you create multiple pots at once, you can control flowering times simply by moving them around. Relocating the arrangement to somewhere cool and dark (be mindful of any tender bulbs such as some Narcissi) will delay flowering; they will slow down or stop growing altogether. In the warmth and light of a house, they will rapidly begin to grow and flower. Once inside, the bowl should be carefully watered, ensuring that the bulbs are not sitting in any water. After flowering, re-plant any hardy bulbs (including muscari and hyacinths) in your outdoor garden, leaving the foliage on the bulb to die back naturally, as they will reflower next year.

How Arab Parties Can Rescue Israeli Democracy

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AYMAN ODEH served in the Knesset from 2015 to 2026. He is the former leader of the Joint List and the Hadash party.

As the Israeli opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot struggles to assemble a governing coalition following last month’s elections, Israel finds itself in a familiar political predicament. The parties opposed to Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and his far-right coalition command a clear majority in the Knesset, with 67 of its 120 seats. Yet the four Jewish-led parties in Eisenkot’s camp hold just 48 seats, leaving them unable to form a government on their own. The impasse echoes the prolonged political deadlock between 2019 and 2021, when Netanyahu’s supporters and his opponents remained locked in competing parliamentary blocs, and four consecutive elections failed to produce a stable governing majority.

But one thing is different this time. Israel’s Arab-led parties, the Joint List and Raam, are stronger than ever. Powered by extraordinary turnout among Arab citizens of Israel, they won a record 19 seats in the Knesset: 11 for the Joint List and eight for Raam. This political mobilization is important in itself, demonstrating Arab voters’ overwhelming desire for change and their recognition of the importance of participating in elections. But its significance goes far beyond the number of seats they have won. Together, the Arab parties now have the potential to be a decisive force in an anti-Netanyahu coalition, providing the parliamentary backing needed to form a government. The proof of this power has already been shown: on November 17, Arab support was crucial in the vote to replace the Likud speaker of the Knesset with a speaker from Eisenkot’s party. Now they have a historic opportunity to put the country on a different path.

As the first and former leader of the Joint List, I know how much is at stake. In 2019, I helped lead a groundbreaking effort to give Arab-led parties a direct voice in the Israeli political system. We took an enormous political risk by endorsing Benny Gantz, then a former chief of staff of the Israeli military and Netanyahu’s principal rival, as a candidate to form a government. We did so despite our profound political and ideological differences with him. Although that effort ultimately fell short, with Gantz choosing to pursue a “unity government” with Netanyahu instead, it established an important precedent: by showing a willingness to work with Jewish political forces with whom they had deep disagreements, parties representing the Arab public had a real chance to prevent a Netanyahu-led far-right government from holding power.

Today, after nearly four years of disastrous leadership by Netanyahu and his far-right allies, that possibility has become far more urgent. It will take political courage on both sides. Eisenkot must be able to offer something to the Arab-led parties, and the Arab-led parties will need to be comfortable offering support to a government that they may not agree with on many issues. But the remarkable Arab turnout and the sizable Israeli majority that is now against Netanyahu have pointed to a way forward. Not only Israel’s future but also its place in the region and the world may depend on the willingness of its leaders to engage with the country’s Palestinian minority. Ultimately, such a partnership will be essential to building the trust between Jews and Arabs that is a prerequisite to achieving any real progress toward a viable Israeli-Palestinian peace. This is the real choice now facing Israel: whether Israelis are prepared to replace the politics of supremacy and exclusion with a politics of equality.

The factors that drove record numbers of Palestinian citizens of Israel to the polls in October have been years in the making. Although the country’s Arab citizens make up more than 20 percent of the Israeli electorate and have long made vital contributions to Israeli society as doctors, lawyers, engineers, high-tech professionals, and in many other fields, the Israeli right has for decades demonized them as enemies: a fifth column and a threat to the state. In recent years, this hostility has increasingly manifested itself in harassment and open racism at universities and workplaces, in the media, in the parliament, and in everyday life.

Yet it would be a mistake to assume that Arab exclusion has been driven only by Netanyahu and the radical right. Zionist parties of the center and the left have also contributed, by keeping a distance from Arab voters or simply ignoring them. The belief that Jewish citizens should ultimately determine the country’s political direction while Arab citizens must remain within whatever limits have been set for them runs deep in Israeli politics. Netanyahu understood early on how to turn this contradiction into a political strategy. Since the 1990s, he has repeatedly used the same formula: inciting his supporters against Arab citizens, delegitimizing Arab representatives in parliament, and convincing Jewish Israelis that a government that relies on Arab political support would somehow be illegitimate.

These tactics proved to be among Netanyahu’s most effective political tools, especially given that Arab-led parties have long been some of his biggest foes. He didn’t need to convince everyone to support his Likud party or its various coalition partners. He only needed to convince enough people, including Jewish Israelis who opposed him, that working with Arab-led parties was dangerous and unacceptable. Netanyahu’s strategy also helped suppress Arab turnout. Since the Arab-led parties had little chance of changing the status quo, Arab voters had little incentive to vote: for many years, a significantly lower proportion of Arab citizens went to the polls than did their Jewish counterparts.

That began to change in 2015 with the creation of the Joint List, which for the first time brought together four parties representing the Arab public on a single electoral ticket and which became the third-largest faction in the Knesset. By embracing a broader vision for our society, grounded in Jewish-Arab solidarity and opposition to the racism of the right, the Joint List demonstrated that Palestinian citizens of Israel could play a new and more influential role in the country’s political system. Then, under my leadership in 2019, the Joint List decided to back Gantz’s bid to unseat Netanyahu. The decision was highly controversial, both within the Arab electorate and across Israeli society. Many Arab voters were deeply ambivalent about supporting a potential government led by a former army chief whose political positions, particularly on the status of Palestinian citizens of Israel and the occupation, differed sharply from their own. Nevertheless, we were prepared to take that political risk because we understood that defeating Netanyahu was more important.

In practice, our willingness to cooperate with the Zionist opposition was not, in fact, reciprocated. The Jewish opposition parties wanted our votes but could not bring themselves to accept us as equal political partners. And, ultimately, Gantz decided to form a government with Netanyahu instead. Nevertheless, the experience showed that Jewish-Arab political cooperation could open up new horizons for a large segment of the public. Since then, every time that Arab-led parties have recommended or supported the effort of a Jewish candidate to form a government, or whenever the Israeli center-left has depended on their support, additional segments of Israeli society have become more open to this kind of cooperation.

In 2021–22, an Arab party, Raam, joined the short-lived government led by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid. I had serious reservations about the model of partnership that emerged, which seemed to rest on the expectation that Arab political representatives would limit their involvement to civilian and domestic matters, while refraining from addressing political and national questions, particularly the occupation and the Palestinian issue. I reject this distinction. We are part of the Palestinian people, and our identity and political concerns cannot be separated from our citizenship in Israel.

But these kinds of slow and painful steps are how political change often happens in national struggles. It does not always begin with a moral or democratic awakening. Sometimes, it begins with pragmatic necessity. When there is no other option, people begin to reconsider what had once been seen as impossible. In the current election, that dynamic has become even more important. After the unending crisis of the past four years, many Israeli voters, including both Arabs and Jews as well as the leaders of most of the Zionist opposition parties, have concluded that getting rid of the Netanyahu government is more necessary than ever.

In recent past elections, voter turnout among Palestinian citizens of Israel has generally remained between 50 and 60 percent. What explains the unprecedented 20-percentage-point surge in turnout in the October election? Three developments stand out: the war of annihilation in Gaza and the killing of tens of thousands of Palestinians; the daily racism and hostility that Arab citizens now face in the streets, in public spaces, and in virtually every aspect of their lives; and the crime that has spiraled out of control in Arab communities under the national security minister, Itamar Ben-Gvir, whose policies have allowed it to flourish, and the relentless settler violence against Palestinians in the West Bank enabled by Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich. All the grief and frustration accumulated over these years were brought to the ballot box.

But there was another crucial factor as well. In the run-up to the election, Arab citizens, following the nearly even polls between Eisenkot and Netanyahu, could see in black and white just how much the outcome would depend on them. Voting this time would be more than a political choice. It was for many a personal responsibility and a way of taking control of their own future. That is why hundreds of thousands of Palestinian citizens of Israel made their way to the polls, defying years of efforts to silence them and undermine their political agency. Many understood that the future direction of the country might be quite literally in their hands.

I saw elderly people, like my friend Jumah from Jisr al-Zarqa, taking to the streets and campaigning for the Joint List. Many of them had lived through the 1948 nakba, or “catastrophe,” which accompanied Israel’s establishment and involved terrible massacres, the destruction of hundreds of Palestinian villages and towns, and the displacement of around 750,000 Palestinians. I saw young people debating politics in the cafes and restaurants of my beloved Haifa, persuading their friends to vote. I saw women and men from Nazareth, Umm al-Fahm, Sakhnin, Rahat, Akko, Kafr Yasif, Majd al-Krum, and countless other towns and villages united by a shared sense of purpose. I saw political consciousness become a living, collective force. For a moment, the boundaries between generations and communities seemed to disappear.

The result marks a remarkable advance of the process of Arab participation in the Israeli political system that began in 2019. In this election, Arab citizens of Israel turned out in record numbers, dramatically narrowing the historical turnout gap with Jewish voters. And that has made another political reality increasingly clear: Israel’s liberal and centrist camp cannot form a government without the parties that represent the Arab public. If it excludes them, as well as the ultra-Orthodox and the far right, it will not reach the 61 seats needed. This is the demographic reality. Israeli Jews who oppose Netanyahu must decide whom they are willing to build a political future with.

The debate about what Eisenkot should do and whether he should ask for Arab support is now unfolding beyond the Knesset, in people’s homes, cafés, in universities, and on the street. It is a deeply controversial question, and many Israelis disagree strenuously about it. Yet for the Arab-led parties, gaining sufficient importance to be able to provoke these conversations is an achievement. The fact that this debate is even taking place shows how the political process has caused Israelis to consider possibilities that until recently seemed unimaginable.

In the end, Eisenkot may have few other options if he wants to replace Netanyahu, as he has promised. In most cases, political parties support coalitions to advance positive changes and achieve concrete goals. But there are moments when political cooperation is necessary for a different reason: to prevent something far worse. The most immediate and overriding objective today is to bring an end to the far-right Netanyahu government. That is a priority on which both the Zionist opposition and the Arab-led parties can agree.

Beyond this shared basic goal, the Arab-led parties should use their political influence to work on two levels. First, they must seek tangible improvements in their constituents’ lives: combating organized crime in Arab communities, securing budgets, addressing the housing crisis, ending the brutal treatment of Palestinians living under occupation in the West Bank, and pushing for a permanent cease-fire and the reconstruction of Gaza. These are fundamental issues of human dignity and civilian life. On a broader level, they should continue to push for real progress on Israeli-Palestinian peace that is ultimately based on two states coexisting, side by side.

I do not expect the current Israeli political establishment to embrace these positions, but that does not mean that the Arab parties should not raise them at every opportunity. At the same time, the Arab parties can use their newfound political clout to build alliances between Jews and Arabs around shared political and strategic goals. The resulting partnerships must be about more than parliamentary arrangements; it must also create the foundations for a different future. That will require Jewish and Arab citizens of Israel to engage with one another on the basis of equality, rather than mere political convenience.

Thus far, Eisenkot has given few indications that he is prepared to go down this path. Since the election, it has been encouraging to see him condemn Netanyahu for fueling what he described as a “racist and violent” campaign against Israel’s Arab citizens. Yet many of the mainstream Zionist parties in Eisenkot’s camp have vowed not to work with the Arab parties. Still, Eisenkot must consider what that would mean. Thirty years from now, people will ask how Jewish supremacy became so normalized that even politicians who called themselves democrats accepted the exclusion of one-fifth of their fellow citizens. They will ask why Israel’s opposition could recognize the danger posed by Netanyahu’s government but not confront the assumptions that made his politics possible.

There is an irony in the fact that, in a country that seeks to keep political hegemony exclusively in Jewish hands, the Palestinian minority, after decades of struggle for political recognition and equal rights, has emerged as one of the most significant forces shaping the country’s political future. We have come to understand that we must be courageous and cooperate with the Zionist parties of the center and left in order to oust Netanyahu, Ben-Gvir, and Smotrich. The question now is: Do these parties, which call themselves democratic and liberal, have the same courage?

How a U.S.-Backed Offensive Could Help Bogotá Reclaim Its Conflict Zones

On September 21, José Luis Pérez Villanueva, better known as “El Cholo”—the second-in-command and financial architect of the criminal group Autodefensas Conquistadores de la Sierra Nevada—was killed during a Colombian police operation on the rural outskirts of Santa Marta. Within hours, militants from that organization launched a dramatic campaign of terror. The group, known colloquially as Los Pachenca, demanded that businesses across the city shutter. Some 90 percent of shops did, as videos circulated of gunmen shooting up storefronts that stayed open.

For two decades, Los Pachenca has been the unquestioned criminal authority in the beachfront city and the adjacent Sierra Nevada mountain range, charging extortion payments from businesses and residents and controlling the flow of drugs out of the Port of Santa Marta, en route to Europe and beyond. But the level of control the group exhibited during the strike was particularly jarring, given that Santa Marta is the vacation home of many of the country’s elite and a common destination for international visitors. Colombia’s government responded by militarizing the city, sending in hundreds of soldiers and police officers, who rolled into town on the main highway with tanks and air support. Many shops still stayed closed until the morning of September 25, when the group announced that it was ending the strike. For locals, Los Pachenca’s reaction was a message about who remains in charge.

Calm has since returned to Santa Marta, but the incident may be indicative of risks to come. Colombia’s newly elected president, Abelardo de la Espriella, has promised a steep military escalation against illegal groups, which have grown precipitously in recent years. As he does so, armed and criminal organizations will undoubtedly respond. Often, their target will not be the state. Instead, they will likely direct their violence against the civilian populations, whom they have spent years coopting and coercing. That is because armed and criminal groups across the country have learned that their true leverage is their ability to govern local communities—and to intimidate and punish them into silence. Even when they do attack state forces, their use of weapons such as improvised explosives and makeshift drones means that bystanders often suffer. In one recent incident in the Pacific coast department of Valle del Cauca, four drone attacks launched by an armed group in a single day left five civilians injured.

These dangers are particularly acute as de la Espriella’s pledge of a zero-tolerance crackdown against armed and criminal groups has won enthusiastic support from Washington. Both countries want big and visible victories against these outfits. They triumphantly announced a new era of cooperation when the president took office in August, including a promise of upgraded military ties. (The United States has not yet publicly pledged new weapons, but it is already providing greater intelligence and logistical assistance on some operations.) After two years of tensions and threats between President Donald Trump and leftist former President Gustavo Petro, the political realignment means that Colombia is once again the United States’ closest defense ally in South America. This is likely to continue, despite major Democratic wins in the U.S. Congress, as the relationship’s focus on security has a long history of bipartisan support.

This cooperation, and de la Espriella’s crackdown, will likely yield initial results. The Colombian government has said that it aims to use its resources to ramp up attacks against armed and criminal groups and to go after leaders who enjoyed protection as counterparts in peace talks during the past administration. Yet Colombia’s true challenge is not how to carry out attacks but what to do afterward. Surgical strikes, which see the military enter and exit quickly, leave civilians more vulnerable in the aftermath, as armed groups retaliate against alleged informants and tighten their grip in areas they control. Bogotá must ensure that every operation includes contingencies to anticipate criminal groups’ reactions and build on successful offensives by weakening their grasp on communities. The government will need to strengthen the police’s ability to patrol contested areas, a costly but necessary process that the United States and other partners should support.

The stakes are high. Bogotá has a chance to leverage its increased military pressure to open the door for the state, rewriting the story of who governs conflict areas. If the government misses this opportunity to consolidate state control, however, Colombia risks entering an escalatory spiral in which civilians pay a mounting price for a campaign intended to protect them.

Armed and criminal groups have spent the last decade expanding their reach across Colombia. According to the local think tank Pares, the number of municipalities with an armed group presence rose from 212 in 2018 to 518 last year—or about half of all municipalities. This deterioration reversed dramatic and hard-won improvements in security. After the historically violent decades of the 1990s and 2000s, military pressure and negotiations yielded a 2016 peace agreement demobilizing the former Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC), once the country’s dominant rebel group. Colombia’s countryside breathed a sigh of relief as the FARC pulled out of areas where it had been in control, which represented a quarter of the country’s land.

The FARC’s exit, however, did not pave the way for Colombia’s highly centralized and bureaucratic state to claim control of those vast geographies. Instead, old armed groups such as the National Liberation Army and newer ones such as the Gaitanista Army of Colombia (also known as the Gulf Clan) saw a chance to expand their access to trafficking routes, coca crops, illegal mines, and, ultimately, vulnerable rural populations. Kidnapping for extortion and mass displacement began to resurface, shattering optimism that progress would move in one direction. Over the last four years, membership in illegal groups has nearly doubled, to some 27,000 fighters today.

As a candidate, de la Espriella successfully harnessed public frustration by promising mano dura, or iron-fisted tactics, including aerial attacks and increased incarceration, facilitated by the construction of mega-prisons. He blamed his predecessor’s administration for accelerating insecurity. The first leftist to be elected president in modern Colombia, Petro had entered office in 2022 vowing to de-emphasize the military’s role and instead hold peace talks with various armed organizations. But not every group participated, and many of the ones that did sought to take advantage of cease-fires to cement their control over communities. Midway through his term, Petro pivoted back toward deploying the armed forces. But much of the harm was already done.

Colombia’s new president has been in office only a little more than 100 days, but the shift is marked. De la Espriella carried out three bombing raids in his first weeks. He has extradited dozens of individuals to the United States, including senior leaders, and the administration has killed or captured many others. The president is doggedly pushing to restart aerial fumigation of coca crops, which the state carried out in the early 2000s to try to reduce the number of hectares under cultivation; the practice has since been heavily restricted by court rulings over concerns about health and environmental consequences. Both in private and in public, military leaders laud the government’s new support for their operations.

The Trump administration has also welcomed these developments, which align with a regional trend favoring Washington’s approach on counternarcotics. For Colombia, the new era of security cooperation with the United States represents a return to the status quo ante of the last quarter century, which was briefly disrupted by the Petro administration. U.S.-Colombian military ties were forged through antidrug efforts and deepened in 1999 with Plan Colombia, a decadelong $10 billion aid package that funded, among other things, the expansion and professionalization of the security forces and fumigation. The Colombian police and military are deeply integrated with their American counterparts. They use U.S.- and NATO-compatible systems, travel by the hundreds to the United States each year for instruction, and receive American intelligence. In nearly every operation, at least one step (and often far more) in the logistical chain is either coordinated with Washington or relies on U.S. support. That relationship never fully stopped under Petro, but the two sides were notably wary of each other. Now bilateral trust appears to be restored.

Even before the elections, de la Espriella impressed U.S. officials by outlining his plans to reclaim security momentum for the state. The Colombian president is now acting on several explicit U.S. priorities, including extraditions and legislation to designate numerous groups as terrorist organizations. Meanwhile, Bogotá’s coca eradication targets are even more ambitious than Washington’s. At a personal level, relations are strong, too. Two of Colombia’s top military officials previously served as envoys to U.S. Southern Command.

Bogotá says that it intends to deliver results for Washington’s antidrug goals and has explicitly asked for additional support in the form of funding, aircraft, surveillance technologies, and maintenance. Although Colombia’s military remains among the best trained in the region, its logistical needs are critical. Only 13 of Colombia’s 53 Black Hawk helicopters are in working order, and defense spending has shrunk as a share of the national government’s budget, from 22.4 percent in 2003 to just 14.7 percent in 2025, with a growing proportion of those funds spent on salaries and pensions.

How much the United States will provide remains to be seen. In August, U.S. Southern Command’s General Francis Donovan discussed ways to shore up the deteriorating air fleet during a visit to Bogotá. The United States has also redeployed surveillance drones from Africa to Colombia and Ecuador, where they are already aiding intelligence collection. But Secretary of State Marco Rubio was reluctant to pledge specific aid while in Colombia in September, and most of this assistance is likely to be in kind or to stay in the hands of the U.S. military and contractors, with little entering the Colombian budget. Much of it appears to be focused on short-term offensive wins.

Colombia has returned to tactics such as aerial bombardment because they arguably worked against the FARC. Founded as a Marxist rebellion in the 1960s, the group behaved like a conventional army, dressed in uniforms, and sought out combat with the state. This meant that the Colombian military could bomb encampments and that capturing leaders left the organization at times rudderless. Slowly chipping away at the FARC’s hierarchy and control helped push the group to finally negotiate its disarmament, beginning in 2012.

But today, those same strategies are yielding diminishing returns. The current generation of armed and criminal groups has little interest in confronting the state. Instead, they behave like businesses, seeking to safeguard access to natural resources, supply chains, and trafficking routes. They live within the civilian population and rarely wear uniforms. Leaders are easily replaced and hierarchies are fluid. Critically, Colombia’s armed groups have learned that wars with the military are costly but that controlling local populations—through intimidation and violence, but also by offering economic incentives, jobs, and services—is comparatively cheap. The battlefield has evolved from a conventional conflict to a contest over who can govern territories and the people living there.

Santa Marta offers an example of how this new model of warfare works. On most days, an outsider in the city would never perceive the presence of Los Pachenca. The group’s members do not patrol or identify themselves. Yet residents know them well from the weekly or monthly extortion payments they are forced to make. Local companies and government officials essentially view the group as an inescapable reality. Pushing back carries a violent price, but paying a protection tax, taking a bribe, or facilitating illegal commerce keeps business running smoothly.

The criminal governance of rural areas is even more suffocating. Groups that emerged from splinters of the former FARC, known as dissidents, often force local residents to hold identity cards, issued so that militants have a full census of who lives in the area and can detect possible enemy fighters. In the Amazon region, these groups keep a land registry more complete than that of the state so that they know exactly whom and how much to extort.

With this intense surveillance over society, armed and criminal groups frequently deploy the civilian population as human shields against military offensives and police patrols. The most egregious example is the widespread use of forcibly recruited children as bodyguards for senior commanders. De la Espriella’s initial bombardments, which killed at least six children, were even temporarily halted by a court over concerns about minors. Senior officers admit that these types of casualties are inevitable around high-value targets, and that the groups know that the military is far less likely to proceed if it means killing children.

Armed and criminal groups will also try to use the military operations to their political advantage. When children are killed in bombardments, for example, FARC dissidents spin the local narrative into a story about how the state cares little for rural populations. The argument sticks because there is rarely a credible state presence that can serve and protect these same communities and earn their trust.

There are more immediate consequences, as well. In the aftermath of bombardments and high-level captures, armed and criminal groups often undertake what amounts to a purge of the population, killing or exiling anyone they believe to have collaborated with the state. And when the state takes out a leader, intra-group violence frequently ensues as former associates compete to take his place. Many residents shelter inside to avoid being caught up in the fighting, leaving children without school and parents without income. If new splinter groups emerge, the combat for control over neighborhoods can place even more civilians in the crossfire. This fallout widens the gap between immediate tactical results and long-term security gains. Taking out a senior leader wins applause, but conditions may well worsen on the ground.

De la Espriella’s administration has rightly identified winning territorial control, permanently dislodging criminal groups, and restoring state governance as its ultimate goals. The dilemma it now faces—exacerbated by U.S. support that focuses on attention-grabbing wins—is how to both achieve short-term results and ensure that these do not unleash new waves of violence, retaliation against civilians, and, in turn, criminal consolidation.

The challenge is twofold. First, the military needs to anticipate groups’ violent reactions and to safeguard the population. In the case of Santa Marta, for example, the government could have increased police presence in the city in the hours after El Cholo was killed. Before the next bombardment or high-level capture, the state should ensure that a ground team of soldiers or military police is ready to hold the ground, protect the community, and prevent the armed group from swiftly stepping back in to reclaim territory.

The second challenge—establishing lasting territorial control—is trickier. A permanent rural security presence might offer the best chance for Colombia’s government to change the direction of the conflict. The country’s military leadership controls only the tip of the spear, not the rest of the state. Time and again, governments “send only the military to the hardest areas,” a retired senior officer told Crisis Group. Moreover, soldiers are operating under heavy pressure for quick wins, which are measured in the number of combatants and especially high-value targets that are killed or captured. There is a real risk that by focusing largely on these statistics, the state will overlook more comprehensive policies necessary to achieve durable security.

Pressure to deliver fast results has in the past incentivized unacceptable practices. In the early 2000s, intense political demands for combat kills fueled elaborate schemes in which soldiers murdered thousands of civilians and counted them as guerrilla fatalities, a scandal known as “false positives.” These crimes deeply compromised public trust in the security forces, and the military has worked for years to build a culture and procedures that ensure they do not happen again. Today, the country’s senior military leadership is likely the first and best brake on any attempts to use excessive force. Commanders say that they are cognizant of the need to guard against rules being slowly stretched until they are broken. “We need a stronger security approach, but it also worries us when we hear there is pressure for ‘results,’” one officer said. But U.S. rhetoric and behavior, including its decision to sink what it claims are drug-smuggling boats on the high seas, compound these risks.

Success, then, may demand a strategy that is ultimately anchored in a much stronger and larger police force, fit for the job of safeguarding civilians rather than merely carrying out offensive strikes. Military operations can generate a window of opportunity by shuffling dynamics on the ground or removing certain dangerous players. Yet those reconfigurations will turn out in the state’s favor only if the resulting vacuum is eventually filled by reliable law enforcement. Take, for example, an operation that temporarily dislodges a group from a rural hamlet. If the military acts alone, it will likely enter and leave in a matter of days or even hours, allowing the armed group to quickly return. If, however, the offensive is followed up with permanent police patrols, access to the justice system, and social interventions, criminals will have a harder time regaining control.

This, of course, is easier said than done, given the government’s resource constraints. To create effective and permanent patrols, police departments operating in the countryside need more officers and equipment to maintain a presence in the most remote communities. Colombia has roughly 180,000 uniformed police officers, a figure that, per capita, puts it on the lower end of OECD countries, many of which are far more urbanized and therefore easier to patrol. In Colombia’s conflict-affected areas, outgunned officers must often stay inside their local stations for their own safety, limiting their ability to build trust and channels of communication with the population. Other state institutions, such as the justice system, say that they cannot establish a durable foothold in villages without guarantees of protection in the medium term—exactly what rural policing should strive to provide. The U.S. State Department’s Bureau of International Narcotics and Law Enforcement Affairs has funded the building and equipping of rural police stations in the past; it should expand this work. International partners with long-standing expertise in rural policing, such as Canada and France, could provide critical support, as well.

In cities, the challenge is different. In Santa Marta, for example, the state is present, but it is weak and vulnerable to the pressure and cooptation of the same groups that it must confront. The police need both more officers and better working conditions—including salaries, benefits, access to education, and social support for families—that could help them resist bribes. Regular vetting and counterintelligence checks can help ensure that police are fighting on the side of the state and not its adversaries.

Expanding the police will be an expensive, long-term undertaking. In the interim, Colombia could prioritize more personnel for critical areas and ensure that officers have a strong grounding in community policing, allowing them to understand the most pressing risks facing civilians. That means regular outreach to communities to build trust and make certain that officers are seen as having a stake in local well-being. The United States, meanwhile, must continue to help Bogotá strengthen its police and courts with direct financial and technical assistance.

Colombia, backed by renewed U.S. support, cannot afford to repeat past mistakes or ignore how the conflict has changed. Bogotá must therefore plan for and capitalize on military pressure to do something no government in Colombia’s history has done: govern well in conflict areas.

The Paradox of American Credibility

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

JOSHUA A. SCHWARTZ is Assistant Professor of International Relations and Emerging Technology at the Carnegie Mellon Institute for Strategy & Technology.

How Fighting Weakens Washington’s Reputation—and Backing Down May Strengthen It

In mid-February, after the United States failed to get Iran to renounce its nuclear ambitions, U.S. President Donald Trump issued an ultimatum. The Iranian leadership had to make “a meaningful deal” with Washington. “Otherwise,” he said, “bad things happen.” Barely eight days later, the United States and Israel began the war against Iran that continues today.

That war has been criticized on many grounds, but analysts have often overlooked the basic assumption about U.S. credibility that it has tested. There is a long-held understanding that the United States’ willingness to use force to stand up for its interests is essential to deterring future adversaries—and convincing them to give in to the kind of sweeping demands the Trump administration made of Tehran. As Elbridge Colby, U.S. undersecretary of defense for policy, said in the early stages of the conflict, “I think both our friends and potential foes are seeing the prowess of the joint force and that also contributes to deterrence.”

Going back to the early years of the Cold War, the concept of credibility—a state’s perceived capabilities and its willingness to use them even at high cost—has played a central role in the decisions that led to and sustained many of the United States’ largest military campaigns. The need to bolster U.S. credibility was a key force driving Washington’s strategy in Korea and Vietnam in the mid-twentieth century, and it played an important role in Iraq and Afghanistan during the so-called war on terror. American strategists today continue to assess foreign policy decisions based on how they might strengthen or weaken the country’s reputation.

But as the results of the Iran campaign have shown, a decision to use force does not always strengthen deterrence. After nearly ten months of war, and the expenditure of billions of dollars of munitions, the United States appears little closer to achieving its objectives of definitively ending Iran’s nuclear threat and ability to project power across the region, let alone securing regime change in Tehran. Meanwhile, the war has badly depleted U.S. military resources that could be used elsewhere and has further eroded the American public’s willingness to support military interventions. Rather than sending a strong signal of U.S. resolve, the Iran war risks conveying the opposite message. Vice President JD Vance argued that the reason the Republicans lost the midterm elections was the Iran war, and government officials in Tehran have gloated that “Americans said no to war.”

In fact, the conventional wisdom that standing firm in a military conflict enhances a state’s credibility misses something important. As the U.S. wars in Vietnam and Iraq showed, a prolonged military campaign may leave the country more cautious about using force, and less willing to bear the costs, in the years that follow. Adversaries notice this. Given that prior efforts by the Trump administration to compromise with Tehran were criticized as “appeasement” by figures like former U.S. Vice President Mike Pence, Trump may be tempted to continue or even expand the Iran campaign. But by taxing U.S. capabilities and deepening war-weariness among the American public, escalation in Iran could instead lead Beijing and Moscow to question Washington’s appetite for future interventions.

Policymakers should be far more skeptical about using military force for the sake of demonstrating U.S. resolve, and they should be less averse to backing down. The Cold War competition with the Soviet Union convinced the United States to intervene in costly proxy wars that consumed enormous human and military resources for questionable gain, and now the strategic competition with China and Russia may do the same. These kinds of conflicts are precisely the ones that are likely to harm, rather than help, U.S. credibility. Instead of obsessing about its reputation for resolve, which it cannot control, Washington must refocus its attention on maintaining the military strength and capabilities it needs to deter its major adversaries. Sometimes the best way to preserve both the capability and the willingness to act in the future is to walk away from conflict in the present.

For decades, policymakers and scholars have treated a country’s credibility as the dark matter of international politics—a hard-to-measure attribute that is nonetheless enormously important for maintaining deterrence. In the 1960s, nuclear deterrence theorist and Nobel Prize winner Thomas Schelling went so far as to say that acquiring a reputation for resolve is “one of the few things worth fighting over.” In keeping with this, it has long been assumed that whereas standing firm communicates to adversaries and allies alike that a country is willing to run risks and bear costs to uphold its interests, backing down or seeking to compromise conveys the opposite. As a result, many U.S. leaders and strategists have drawn on the logic of credibility to justify the most hawkish policies toward adversaries: making maximalist demands, using force, and remaining in wars long past the point where their costs exceeded their benefits.

Vietnam was a case in point. When debating whether to send the first group of U.S. troops to Vietnam in 1965, U.S. Secretary of State Dean Rusk told President Lyndon Johnson, “If the Communist world finds out we will not pursue our commitments to the end, I don’t know where they will stay their hand.” Later, as the United States became bogged down in the war, Assistant Secretary of Defense John McNaughton went further, arguing in a formerly top-secret memo that the primary reason for continuing the Vietnam War was to maintain America’s “reputation as a guarantor” and “avoid a humiliating U.S. defeat.” According to this logic, 58,000 U.S. soldiers and likely over a million Vietnamese people were killed not because the outcome of the war was itself crucial to U.S. interests, but because Washington worried how others—especially the Soviets—would perceive it if it withdrew.

Since the Cold War, the same justifications have been used to push the country into, and to sustain, many other conflicts. This was especially true during the so-called Forever Wars the United States was drawn into after the 9/11 attacks. On the night of 9/11, U.S. Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld said, “We need to bomb something else [other than the relatively weak Taliban] to prove that we’re, you know, big and strong and not going to be pushed around by these kinds of attacks.” The logic of credibility not only played a role in Washington launching the Iraq war in 2003 but also locked it into the conflict, even as the costs rose and the raison d’être for the war—weapons of mass destruction—turned out to be false. As President George W. Bush said in 2006, “If we were to leave before the mission is complete, it would hurt U.S. credibility.”

Conversely, strategists have been quick to criticize U.S. leaders who backed down. During the Syrian civil war, many were concerned about the reputational damage to the United States when President Barack Obama declined to respond to the Syrian government’s use of sarin gas to attack a rebel-held suburb of Damascus, thereby violating a red line that Obama had previously declared. In 2021, many foreign policy hawks reprimanded President Joe Biden for withdrawing from Afghanistan and for waiving some U.S. sanctions on Russia’s Nord Stream 2 pipeline project. At the time, Michael McCaul, the top Republican on the House Foreign Affairs Committee, said, “U.S. credibility from Kyiv to Taipei cannot withstand another blow of this nature.” More recently, Trump himself has been accused of backing down against China on tariffs, which some have decried as another example of “Trump always chickens out,” or “TACO.” In each of these cases, critics argued that Washington’s failure to act has compromised the perception that the United States is willing to run risks and bear costs to achieve its objectives.

Despite the persistent assumption that U.S. credibility requires using force and refusing to back down, that thinking has been repeatedly challenged over the past decade. For one thing, experience has shown that a country’s past behavior may be a poor indicator of its present-day resolve. In April, for example, The New York Times reported that Trump believed that Iran was unlikely to attempt to close the Strait of Hormuz based on its past actions—such as its failure to mount a robust response to the U.S. and Israeli bombing of its nuclear facilities in the 12-day war in 2025. In reality, however, Iran has been far less restrained than expected in the current conflict and moved quickly not only to close the strait but also to attack U.S. allies and U.S. military bases across the region. In other words, Tehran’s failure to stand firm in 2025 had little bearing on its resolve in the present war.

In any case, it is far from clear that a country’s reputation for resolve is something that it can control by its actions alone. As Keren Yarhi-Milo has explained in Foreign Affairs, “credibility is in the eye of the beholder.” Even if a state acts decisively to signal strength, an adversary may not interpret that action as reason to reconsider its behavior. For example, despite Washington’s intention to communicate high resolve by bearing tremendous costs in the Vietnam War, the North Vietnamese Communist Party Central Committee concluded in 1965 that “the domestic situation in the United States, however, will not allow them to fully utilize their economic and military power in their war of aggression.” In other words, Hanoi ignored the intended signal from Washington and refused to back down.

Other pitfalls are associated with demonstrating resolve by standing firm and fighting. At the extreme, a state that leans too much on military strength can gain a reputation for aggression in ways that undermine long-term deterrence. Napoleonic France and Nazi Germany both had strong reputations for resolve, but their offensive actions ultimately provoked other countries rather than deterring them. There is certainly a risk that U.S. actions under the second Trump administration—such as decapitating the Iranian government and threatening to annex Canada, Gaza, and Greenland—could similarly backfire. For example, there is growing evidence that U.S. allies and partners in Europe, the Middle East, and Asia are rethinking their reliance on the United States and are seeking other sources of security. Far from conceding to Trump’s demands, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney has described a “rupture” in relations between Washington and Ottawa and advocated for middle powers to band together to protect their sovereignty.

Even if using force bolsters a country’s reputation for resolve, it may harm the country’s broader credibility. For instance, Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine and its willingness to absorb hundreds of thousands of casualties since then has sent a strong signal about its resolve. Yet its dramatic underperformance on the battlefield against a far smaller and less well-equipped army has likely diminished the international perception of its military effectiveness. The war in Iran could similarly tarnish the United States’ military reputation. Since February, Washington has depleted at least half of its precision-strike missiles and advanced air defense interceptor missiles. It will likely take years to replenish these stockpiles given slow manufacturing times for so-called exquisite platforms. In the meantime, the United States has fewer advanced systems to draw on if China makes a move against Taiwan or if Russia attacks Poland or Estonia. For both Russia’s and the United States’ military campaigns, then, the net effect has been to reduce rather than enhance the countries’ reputation for military capability in the eyes of their respective adversaries.

Quite apart from the negative consequences that often come with decisions to use force, there is an even more fundamental problem with conventional thinking about credibility. In practice, backing down can actually be more helpful to a country’s reputation for resolve than fighting. This becomes especially clear in looking at the long-term effects of engaging in military interventions. Although the United States was victorious in World War I, the conflict produced a lingering war-weariness among the U.S. public and key policymakers. In the years after the Armistice, Americans expressed strong support for the idea that the United States should stay out of any future European war, a sentiment that was ultimately codified in the Neutrality Acts of the 1930s. Only after Japan attacked Pearl Harbor in December 1941, more than two years after war began in Europe, did the United States enter World War II. Fighting in World War I sent a signal of strong resolve to adversaries, but Americans’ war-weariness and resurgent isolationism in the years that followed conveyed that the United States would be less resolved to fight.

This dynamic has been even more apparent in the aftermath of major U.S. wars since then. Following the Vietnam War, President Jimmy Carter declared that “young Americans should never again be sent to die in battle unless our own nation is endangered.” Consider how this kind of thinking affected adversaries’ view of the United States: In deciding to invade Kuwait in 1990, Iraqi dictator Saddam Hussein assumed that the United States was intensely casualty averse and might not intervene or would back down quickly. General Wafiq al-Samarrai, Iraq’s head of military intelligence during the Gulf War, said in a 1996 interview that Saddam “thought that America’s involvement in Vietnam had badly damaged its willingness to use military power.” Although the Vietnam War was primarily fought to buttress the United States’ reputation for resolve, it had the opposite effect in practice.

And again, during and after the U.S. war in Iraq, Americans became skeptical of costly military interventions. As a result, recent administrations have been especially hesitant to commit ground troops in combat operations. Iran is undoubtedly aware of this so-called Iraq syndrome, which may embolden Tehran to act more aggressively without having to fear a large-scale American ground operation.

There is also experimental evidence that backing down may strengthen a country’s long-term reputation for resolve. In a survey I conducted in 2021 with 100 British parliamentarians, respondents were randomly divided into two groups. The first group read about a situation in which a hypothetical country backed down from a prior conflict. The second group read about a hypothetical country that stood firm in a prior conflict but displayed the same signs of war-weariness or “syndromes” that emerged in the United States after the Vietnam and Iraq wars. Both groups were then asked to estimate the likelihood that the country in question would stand firm or back down in a current dispute with the United Kingdom. On average, parliamentarians believed that the country that backed down in the prior dispute would have greater resolve in the current dispute. In other words, if fighting leads to war-weariness, then backing down can actually be better for a state’s future credibility.

As the U.S. campaign in Iran has demonstrated, choosing to fight to maintain a reputation for resolve is an incredibly risky gamble and may backfire. To avoid repeating the same mistake, U.S. policymakers and strategists need to rethink the relationship between credibility and fighting. First, the United States should avoid fighting altogether when the primary objective is shoring up reputation rather than defending a core national issue. If a conflict directly involves key security and economic interests, as did the U.S. Revolutionary War and World War II, then fighting can be a logical choice, assuming the United States has sufficient military capabilities to win the war at reasonable cost. But if the primary argument for the war relates to future credibility, as in Vietnam, then policymakers should be wary.

Second, if Washington determines that an adversary must be confronted with military force, then it must focus on limited, achievable objectives. Wars that drag on because they have much larger aims, or are prolonged simply for reputational reasons, may erode the country’s credibility by draining munitions and making Americans more reluctant to fight in the future. The two U.S. interventions against Saddam Hussein’s Iraq illustrate this point well. In the limited 1991 Gulf War, the United States quickly expelled Iraqi forces from Kuwait at low cost and before war-weariness could set in among the U.S. public. But the U.S.-led invasion of Iraq in 2003, in which Washington pursued the far more expansive goal of regime change, continued for years at high cost and made many U.S. policymakers and ordinary citizens alike less supportive of using force in the future.

Finally, policymakers should focus less on the U.S. reputation for resolve, which they cannot control, and more on the country’s military capabilities, which they can. To deter Beijing and Moscow, Washington needs to convince both adversaries that it has the ability to prosecute a modern great-power war. The problem is that, although the United States continues to have the most advanced military capabilities and weapons systems in the world, they are limited in quantity, extremely expensive, and take a long time to replace. In the course of just a few months of fighting against Iran, the United States depleted huge percentages of its advanced and expensive stockpiles of offensive and defensive missiles.

This alarming shortfall raises new questions about whether the United States could sustain a longer war of attrition of the type Russia and Ukraine are fighting. Payhawk Ltd., a startup that helps enterprises manage their business expenses, has raised $280 million in funding.

The Series C round was led by Goldman Sachs’ growth equity arm. The bank was joined by Greenoaks, Lightspeed and more than a half-dozen others. Payhawk stated today that it’s now valued at $2 billion.

Issuing corporate credit cards to employees historically involved a weeks-long wait. Payhawk operates a platform that enables companies to create virtual cards in a few seconds and issue physical ones in five days. According to the company, customers can configure a card to expire automatically at the end of the project for which it’s needed.

Accounting teams can set spending limits to avoid cost overruns. Payhawk makes it possible to tie purchase caps to a business project, a specific merchant or a broad product category.

Companies can optionally connect their cards to Payhawk-operated business accounts. They support payments in more than 115 currencies. According to the software maker, its accounts’ currency exchange rates are up to 90% lower than those charged by traditional banks.

Syncing corporate card spending to a company’s accounting ledger is a multi-step process. First, workers must scan the receipt issued in connection with a purchase and send it to the finance team. From there, an accountant categorizes the purchase and verifies it through a process known as reconciliation.

Payhawk automates the workflow using an artificial intelligence agent. Additionally, it flags cost-cutting opportunities such as unnecessary software-as-a-service subscriptions.

A second AI agent speeds up procurement initiatives. It’s accessible through a chat interface that enables workers to request items in natural language. If a request is missing certain details, the agent can point out what additional information is needed. From there, it automatically seeks approvals from relevant executives.

Payhawk also automates a number of other tasks. It includes a tool that reduces the amount of work involved in changing business trip plans. Additionally, the company has built an AI engine that enables new users to customize its platform by uploading documents such as spending policies.

“Through our technology, finance departments can benefit from ‘zero-touch finance’, where neither the finance professional nor the employee deals with any admin work,” said Payhawk co-founder and Chief Executive Officer Hristo Borisov. “77% of our customers use our agents daily across financial control, procurement, travel and reporting to save time and money for their businesses.”

Payhawk says that it still has 70% of its previous funding round, a $100 million investment that closed in 2022. The company will use the proceeds from today’s Series C raise to enhance its AI capabilities.
