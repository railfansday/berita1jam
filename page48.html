Well, Someone 3D-Printed A Whole Engine And It Actually Ran (Sort Of)

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

You've probably sat around a campfire at some point in your life and figured out firsthand that plastic and heat don't mix. Whether you threw an empty soda bottle or marshmallow bag in and watched it shrivel up, or propped your sandals up on the stones around the fire and witnessed your soles melting, you get it.

Plastic is a pretty good insulator, but it has a pretty low melting point. ABS has a melting point of around 440 degrees Fahrenheit, while most available PLAs melt at 340-ish degrees. If you've spent any time looking at your engine's oil or coolant temperature gauges, you'll know that a running engine is pretty stable in the low 200-degree range, but the combustion chamber can get up to 2,500 degrees during the peak of the power stroke. Plastic doesn't stand a chance, which is why most engines are crafted from cast iron or aluminum.

We've already seen what happens when you try to use a 3D-printed cylinder head; the results aren't pretty. With the kind of heat an engine sees, plastic just isn't the preferred material to work with. There are certainly some engines that have made extensive use of plastic, including the Torlon-based engines from Polimotor Research in the 1970s and 80s, but there isn't a high-enough-heat plastic to manage combustion chamber heat spikes, so a blend of metal and plastic is necessary.

YouTuber and mad scientist Alexander's Laboratory recently built and ran a pair of 3D-printed plastic engines, a single-cylinder and a parallel twin. We'll talk through both engines and their unique challenges in a second. First, we should take a step back and think about the implications of potentially building real working 3D-printed engines as prices come down and the technology both improves and becomes commonplace.

Have you ever built (or rebuilt) an engine for one of your project cars? There are just so many pieces and parts you have to buy, and there's so much measuring and clearance-checking to be done before the parts can all get bolted together. It's time-consuming and expensive, but the longest time you'll spend working on it is the shipping time to get all of the parts. Imagine if, someday, every engine in every car shared interchangeable components that could be stocked at any local auto parts store, and the rest could be 3D-printed at home. Wouldn't that be cool?

There are certainly still components that would need to be made out of metal, of course. The obvious first step would be to use metal for the combustion chamber, cylinder head, pistons, and crankshaft. You would also need to figure out a bearing system that works for the crankshaft and camshafts. Then there are things like the valves, valve springs, hardware, water pump components, and the accessory drive pullies.

Alexander is able to make most of his engines out of plastic, including the water pump and drive gears, but I'd go with metal for those on a full-scale engine, purely for longevity's sake. In the end, if you could make it work, you would have an engine significantly lighter than a comparable aluminum cylinder head and engine block unit. Recent research indicates swapping from a metal component to a 3D-printed plastic replacement cuts weight by 30% and costs by 15%. With enough research, you could add structural lattice inside some areas of components to make it even lighter, or allow better coolant flow to critical heat spots. Now this is exciting!

If you wanted to build a 3D-printed plastic engine, what steps would you need to take so it would run for more than a few seconds? As mentioned above, some metal is needed to keep it alive. Alexander strategically used machined steel for both the cylinder walls and the combustion chamber surface. This is smart, as it allows a sealing surface for the metal valves and the ability to thread in a common spark plug. The crankshaft is also metal, as is the surface of the pistons, but pretty much everything else comes straight off the printer.

In the single-cylinder-engine video above, Alexander had some issues with sparks and grounding. It's harder to get everything to ground out properly when the engine is non-conductive, of course. Once it gets running, though, it sure sounds like a pretty normal single-cylinder four-stroke engine, the kind you might find in a lawnmower or go-kart. After getting everything dialed in, Alexander piles on the throttle until the engine decides to eat itself. We don't get an indication of how long it ran, but eventually the carbon-reinforced plastic connecting rod gives up the ghost and shoots through the side of the plastic block.

Trying again with a more balanced twin-cylinder engine, Alexander might actually be on to something. Using some beefed-up components, a little more metal, and better heat management, this little 250-cc twin seems, in the video anyway, to run and work as a standard small motorcycle or scooter engine would. With a better-built engine and more care taken in its running, the twin just keeps on chugging. Maybe it's time to start developing open-source plastic engines that can run with just a few off-the-shelf components. It could be cool.

In this post, I want to address a different but related topic — which airlines serve the most destinations? As you’d expect, this is a very different list. That’s because serving a lot of countries is much easier if you’re an airline that has a lot of service in Europe and Africa, given the number of countries there that are in close proximity. It’s one of the reason that US airlines don’t score that well when it comes to absolute number of countries served, despite otherwise being among the biggest carriers in the world.

However, the list of total number of destinations served is a different story…

Note that the number of destinations also includes flights operated by their regional subsidiaries.

And Delta, ummm, gets a revenue premium.

Just in the interest of being thorough, I’m sure some people are curious how many destinations Southwest serves, since it’s an absolutely massive airline in the United States, though doesn’t operate long haul flights. That’s because the airline is more about depth of service than breadth of service, and doesn’t have a regional subsidiary.

After US carriers, which airlines otherwise top the list of most destinations served? Most people wouldn’t be surprised to learn that Turkish takes that spot, serving 269 destinations, including 46 domestic destinations and 223 international destinations.

Rampant Falkirk obliterated Hearts’ 18-month unbeaten home record in the Scottish Premiership with a stunning 5-2 win at Tynecastle as they leapfrogged the Jambos into third place.

Wouter Vrancken’s beleaguered side, missing 11 players through injury amid a gruelling fixture list, were in disarray as the swashbuckling Bairns romped into a five-goal lead early in the second half through Finley Barbrook, Owen Eames, Liam Henderson and Jacob Murrell’s double.

Hearts hit back through substitute Sabri Guendouz and a Claudio Braga penalty, but Falkirk were well worthy of making it five top-flight wins in a row for the first time since 1995.

It was a first league defeat at Tynecastle for Hearts since April 2025. To exacerbate their grim predicament, the Jambos, who have won just two of their last 11 matches, lost yet another senior player as Malachi Fagan-Walcott limped off in the second half.

There were two changes to the Hearts side that started Thursday’s Conference League defeat to Thun as Stephen Kingsley and Guendouz were replaced by Yan Dhanda and Landry Kabore.

Hearts threatened an equaliser in the 10th minute, but Kabore’s effort on the turn from the edge of the six-yard box was superbly blocked by Scott Bain.

Falkirk doubled their lead in the 17th minute after the hosts were caught wide open on the counter-attack. Dhanda’s shot from distance was charged down, allowing the Bairns to break swiftly, with Brad Spencer weighting his pass perfectly for Eames, who fired past the exposed Reus.

Braga thought he had pulled one back for Hearts in the 20th minute, but it was ruled out for handball.

Falkirk took full advantage of this reprieve by adding a third in the 33rd minute as Murrell peeled off Fagan-Walcott at the back post to nod home Deniss Melniks’ cross from the right.

The visitors struck again in first-half stoppage-time as Henderson curled high past Reus after Tanser’s corner broke to him just inside the box.

As jeers rained down from an irate home support, Vrancken replaced midfielders Laurent Mendy and Dhanda with Guendouz and Matthew Gillies for the start of the second half.

But within two minutes of the restart Falkirk notched again as Murrell fired home after being set up by Ethan Ross.

Hearts got off the mark when Guendouz drilled in his first goal for the club from just inside the box in the 50th minute.

Suno is facing yet another pair of lawsuits. A group of rights holders of Elvis Presley’s music and the publisher of some of Eminem‘s biggest records are suing the AI music company, claiming it illegally generates music and lyrics that mimic those artists. The lawyers in the Presley suit claim that Suno has violated Tennessee’s Ensuring Likeness Voice and Image Security (ELVIS) Act, which makes it illegal for an AI company to clone a singer’s voice.

In the suit, lawyers representing Presley’s rights holders argue that “Suno has mechanically encoded itself with artists’ identities, hard-work, creativity, and time, and harnessed them all for a consistent conveyor belt ofcold hard cash.“ These suits closely mirror one filed by Jason Isbell and others in September, which claimed Suno created imitations of the plaintiffs but did not claim copyright infringement directly.

Later in the complaint, the lawyers write that “Suno’s ravenous race to the bottom aims to make human creativity obsolete, coldly replicating without a license in seconds what takes years to learn, struggle with, maintain, and endlessly attempt to make better and perfect.”

Suno spokesperson Cat McCormack told The Verge that, “Suno exists to help people create new, original music, not to trade on anyone’s name or likeness, and we prohibit any unauthorized use of an artist’s work. To protect against this, we do not allow prompts for specific artists’ names and songs and work with third-party providers to screen uploaded audio files and lyrics for potential misuse. We are always working to strengthen our guardrails and we will continue to add new safeguards that protect artists and our users.”

This adds to the pile of lawsuits Suno is currently facing from Sony, UMG, Isbell, Reservoir Media, and more.

The 10 Best Survival Movies of All Time, Ranked

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Technically, a whole bunch of movies are about survival, but a line was drawn here to look at the ones really focused on surviving. Basically, no horror or action movies where people are obviously fighting to stay alive the whole time, because if you included anything with life-or-death stakes, then you'd probably find yourself with more “survival movies” on your hands than non-survival movies.

The ones below are mostly adventure/drama films, with some creeping into other territories, but as long as characters are isolated and battling the elements (more so than specific disasters) in one way or another (and maybe some other threats, too, at least on the side), then they're worth considering as survival movies. And they're high-quality, too, and definitely worthy of being considered among the best survival movies ever made.

Rescue Dawn takes place during the Vietnam War, so maybe it’s more of a war movie early on, but the focus is definitely on survival and less about the usual stuff you'd find in more straightforward war movies. It’s based on the real-life experiences of a pilot named Dieter Dengler, and what happened when his plane got shot down over Laos, with him having to battle the elements and coming into contact with forces who make him a prisoner of war, too.

There’s time spent on surviving time in a POW camp on top of having to survive (and trek through) jungle terrain, so Rescue Dawn is certainly grueling, but by design. It’s one of the better non-documentaries Werner Herzog has directed, though he did also cover the same story in a documentary, and that film’s pretty great as well (it was made about a decade earlier and was called Little Dieter Needs to Fly).

This is one you sort of have to mention, because it’s probably one of the first things most people think about when they hear the term “survival movie,” but yes, it’s Cast Away. There’s a desert island, there’s just the one guy stranded on it, and he has to stay alive on that island for an extended period of time. It’s all very stripped back in terms of the premise, but there are things beyond it being just literally a guy and an island for nearly 2.5 hours.

Still, the bulk of the movie is that. And a little under 2.5 hours is long, but Cast Away stays interesting for most of that runtime. It’s one of the good Robert Zemeckis films, which is always nice to see (when he’s on, he’s on, but he definitely isn't always on, y’know?), and it’s also got one of many strong Tom Hanks performances at its center, so that dynamic director/actor duo ensures the movie’s pretty good for the most part.

Cast Away but it’s Mars instead of an island: that’s The Martian. Kind of. Someone is alone for a good chunk of the movie, and they have to figure out ways to survive their predicament while also needing to think about how they might actually get out of the situation they're in, with help from anyone else being limited, at least at first.

The Martian works well as a crowd-pleaser, not exactly doing a ton to rewrite the survival sub-genre, but hitting all the beats in a more-than-solid manner.

It’s also one of a fair few movies that features a character played by Matt Damon trying to get home in one way or another, so that’s possibly worthy of note. And The Martian works well as a crowd-pleaser, not exactly doing a ton to rewrite the survival sub-genre, but hitting all the beats in a more-than-solid manner and, overall, ending up being a pretty difficult-to-dislike sort of film.

If you're picking up a theme here, with stories about one person battling the elements or otherwise being in a stressful situation while alone, then congrats. But also not really congrats, because it’s obvious. That’s a lot of survival movies. And that is also All Is Lost, though maybe this one goes a step further because the only cast member listed is Robert Redford.

The closest thing to a second character here is the boat he’s on throughout the film, and even then, he’s not a particularly well-developed character, not even having a confirmed name or anything. Nothing else is really relevant, though. There’s been an accident, the boat is sinking, and he doesn’t want to drown. That’s enough for All Is Lost, because it’s genuinely quite gripping throughout, and it’s one of the best of the late-era Robert Redford movies, too (he does a lot considering he was nearing 80 at the time of this film’s release, though there is one other survival movie he starred in decades earlier that’s also worth mentioning… more on that one in a bit).

What do you know: Buried is about a guy who gets buried. No clickbait with this title, that’s for sure. Ryan Reynolds is that guy, and he gives a surprisingly good performance here in a movie pretty much devoid of anything funny or otherwise light-hearted, since he’s stuck in a coffin the whole time, well and truly, you know, buried, and he has a limited amount of time to try and contact someone who can then work out where he is.

He’s really just equipped with a phone and a lighter, so he’s calling people, stressing out, and exploring any option he can for the 90 minutes of oxygen he has (approximately), and that is also the length of the movie. So, Buried plays out basically in real-time, and it’s remarkably intense for all of those minutes, successfully stretching out that very memorable coffin scene from Kill Bill: Vol. 2 into a feature-length film.

This is a tricky one to talk about, because Deliverance is a deeply unpleasant movie, but it takes a little while to get to that territory, and then also, discussing that stuff feels like it could be a bit much on top of it possibly involving saying too much, in terms of the narrative and all that. But then again, it’s more than half a century old at this point, so you probably know at least a little about this movie and why it’s notorious, even if you’ve not actually seen it.

If you don’t mind the idea of having a bad time with a good movie, it’s likely worth watching. Deliverance does make you feel the desperation of its main characters uncomfortably well, and there’s undeniable skill involved here with it all building the way it does. And a movie being this old and still feeling shocking in many ways is quite impressive when you think about it, too.

Just over 40 years before All Is Lost, Robert Redford also starred in Jeremiah Johnson, which is a different sort of adventure/survival movie, sure, but both are great, in any event. Jeremiah Johnson definitely isn't as minimalist, since there are a few other characters here, and the main character isn't as restricted, not being stuck out at sea and instead having to survive in and around the Rocky Mountains.

It’s also set back in the mid-1800s, very much being a Western, but one focused on survival, and then there is some additional conflict in the movie involving some other people in the area the titular character lives. Still, not a lot of story. It’s not minimalist compared to All Is Lost, but placed against most other more “conventional” movies, it’s very light on story, but not in a bad way. It’s interesting to watch something that’s kind of a hang-out movie, but lots of it is just the one guy, rather than a film where you get to see, like, a group of characters hang out and everything.

Take the setting and the chilly Western feel of Jeremiah Johnson and add in the grit/violence of Deliverance, and you'd find yourself with something that might be honestly kind of close to The Revenant. Okay, not perfectly, but those itches scratched by those films get scratched here, and also, both those movies from the early 1970s have revenge play some kind of role in their respective stories.

The Revenant is as much a revenge movie as it is one about survival, with one man seemingly staying alive against all odds so he can brutally murder the man who wronged him. That’s a simple way to break down such a long film, yet that is pretty much what The Revenant boils down to. It tells that straightforward story well, though, all the while doing some interesting things thematically and feeling hard to fault in terms of its technical aspects and also regarding many of the performances it features.

Of all the notably uplifting movies out there, 127 Hours could well be the most disturbing, but it has to be disturbing and grisly before it can get uplifting. It feels right, in other words, and while “that” scene that everyone knows might be nauseating to watch without context, if you see it in the movie, at the point at which it happens… well, sure, it’s still nauseating, but it’s also undeniably triumphant.

This one came out the same year as Buried, and it’s similarly claustrophobic, all the while also being much more visually unpleasant and then, somehow not paradoxically, also a whole lot less depressing. 127 Hours is about as good as the more mundane survival movies out there get, because it’s easy to imagine yourself in this situation, get some kind of understanding about the pain involved, yet also then find some inspiration in how far a real-life person was willing to go to survive.

Gravity is not based on a real-life story, but it certainly feels real while you're watching it, and it’s also grounded in enough ways to make it a bit difficult to classify as a science fiction movie. It’s about survival in space, but the disaster, in effect, isn't too outlandish, and then it also doesn’t happen very far into space, by any means, with Earth always very much in view, but still undeniably difficult to get back to in one piece.

Also, Gravity is very heavy on spectacle for many of its scenes, though, things are also personal and character-focused, to some extent, with enough time to spend on the characters even with a short runtime because there are so few characters. Some people find parts of Gravity silly and maybe a little overly sentimental, too, but it is so spectacular and laser-focused on survival throughout that it feels worthy of being ranked as high as it is here.
