It's a weird time to buy a laptop, but the new MacBook Pro doesn't skimp on quality

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It has the best laptop screen we’ve used, but there may be reasons to wait.

Apple’s new MacBook Pro is expensive as hell.

Let’s get that out there right away, because for a lot of people, it will be the only thing that matters. As with so many things in life, though, you get what you pay for. There’s no questioning the quality of the laptop’s display or most of its other hardware components.

But should you buy it? Well, that’s a complicated question in 2026.

We’re in a time of great uncertainty when it comes to buying a computer. Prices are at levels we’ve not seen in recent history, and they may well keep climbing. Meanwhile, users paying close attention to the early developments in both local and agentic artificial intelligence are asking an important question: “What will computing even look like in a few years, and what does that mean for the computer I should buy today?”

Apple’s new MacBook Pro sits at the intersection of all of this uncertainty. In multiple ways, it’s also straddling different worlds.

It arrives amid a changing of the guard in Apple’s leadership, potentially raising questions about the future direction of Apple’s ecosystem. It’s a mass-market consumer product, but configuring it with enough RAM and storage to make it feel future-proof pushes its price into territory mostly only well-funded businesses can afford. It’s firmly rooted in the computing paradigms of the past 20 years, but it shows early signs of flirtation with new AI workflows. It’s a conventional Apple laptop in most ways, but it also has a touchscreen—something that would never have happened in a prior Apple era.

It’s an outstanding, high-quality product, but whether it’s something you should buy depends on you—what you’re doing right now, what you’ll be doing in the future, and what you value.

For most of the specs we look at, Apple isn’t claiming any difference here. The new MacBook Pro promises 24 hours of battery life (though you’d have to use it in a conservative way to achieve that), it has the same ports (three Thunderbolt, one HDMI, one SDXC, and MagSafe), and it has the N1 chip for Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6 connectivity.

And like its immediate predecessor, it offers the M5 Pro and M5 Max chips as options. Running synthetic benchmarks, we found no meaningful difference at all compared to when we ran the same tests on the previous model, so we won’t spend time discussing their performance today. We’ve covered both thoroughly in prior reviews, and you can read up there if you’d like.

The chips are more than fast enough for normal workflows for the foreseeable future, including most 3D work. AI inference is the main area where they may be less future-proof. To be clear, they’re strong enough for now, but the field is evolving rapidly, and there’s reason to expect significant improvements in future chips on this front.

According to Bloomberg, Apple is skipping Pro and Max versions of the M6 chip that was introduced earlier this year to focus its attention on a significant rework for the M7 due in 2027. “The M7 line is designed primarily around major advancements to on-device AI processing,” Bloomberg’s Mark Gurman wrote. The line would emphasize memory bandwidth, which matters for fast, efficient AI inference.

Even without Bloomberg’s report, I’d expect Apple to prioritize those areas in its next high-end chip refresh. That’s partly because there’s another tea leaf to read here: Apple made just such an update with the A20 Pro, which debuted in its latest iPhones several weeks ago. That update brought a substantially improved Neural Engine and a significant increase in memory bandwidth. We also found that the A20 Pro delivered improved 3D graphics performance in some cases.

Similar improvements could come to Macs, meaning it might be worth waiting another year if you’re focused on AI workflows or even 3D applications—especially given the high cost of these machines. That’s part of what makes this such an uncertain time to buy a laptop. We’re in a moment when these companies are revisiting the architecture of these chips because of these new workflows, and we don’t know what the end result is yet.

Here’s one thing that’s not at all uncertain: The display on the MacBook Pro is the best I have ever used in a laptop. If the display is your primary concern, this is a big moment.

Apple calls it a “tandem OLED display,” meaning a panel with two emissive layers. This approach helps achieve a higher peak brightness, but perhaps more importantly, it can reduce the risk of burn-in. Most of the time, the display won’t need both layers running at full power. Instead, they can share the load and run at a reduced current, helping extend the panel’s lifespan.

The new display can achieve up to 2,000 nits of peak brightness on HDR highlights, but it’s rated for 1,000 nits of sustained brightness. In general, I like the way Apple handles HDR for this at the OS level, too.

The brightness matters for HDR content, sure, but what really makes OLED stand out is per-pixel illumination—meaning each pixel can turn on or off independently. That allows for absolute black levels and very strong contrast. The MiniLED display in the previous MacBook Pro offered good contrast for an LCD display, but OLED has a fundamental advantage here, and it shows.

As you’d expect, the screen offers native support for both P3 and Adobe RGB color gamuts. It also has a variable refresh rate from 10 to 120 Hz, just like many of Apple’s other products. As before, the MacBook Pro comes in roughly 14-inch (2,024 by 1,986 pixels) and 16-inch (3,472 by 2,226 pixels) variants. There’s also a nano-texture glass option to fight glare, though that will set you back another $150.

It’s one of the most impressive displays I’ve used in any device, and it’s certainly the best I’ve ever used on a laptop. The colors are vibrant, the contrast is outstanding, the blacks are inky, and it can get very bright when it needs to—not just with highlights but full-screen brightness as well. And on the whole, it’s extremely precise. Uniformity appears basically flawless. It’s what you’d expect from an ultra-high-end TV or reference monitor, not a laptop or computer screen, and it’s genuinely striking.

We’ll see about longevity, but OLED has come a long way in recent years, and my first impressions are that Apple has made all the right choices here.

I find myself trying to find something negative to say about the MacBook Pro’s screen to avoid sounding too effusive, but I can’t think of anything substantive. The best I can do is complain that Apple’s proprietary “PROLED XDR” label is silly and eye-roll-inducing.

Branding aside, the screen is the main reason to pay extra for this model.

With its lid closed, the new MacBook Pro looks identical to its predecessor. The dimensions are largely the same. It’s slightly thinner, but you’d have to check the spec sheet or handle them side by side to notice.

Pick it up, though, and you’ll find that it’s noticeably lighter than the last model, by about half a pound. It’s not in a totally different weight class, but you can notice the change, and it’s a nice improvement. The reduction is partly due to the display technology switch; it’s easier to make a light OLED screen than a light MiniLED one.

Open the MacBook Pro and turn it on, and you’ll see one of a few other notable differences, depending on whether you’re behind or in front of it. From behind, you’ll see the Apple logo on the lid now lights up, as it did on MacBooks of yore. It’s arguably a little tacky, but it’s also neat and nostalgic. I don’t hate it.

From the front, you might notice that the already barely there bezels are even more barely there, though that’s subtle. More likely, you’ll see that the top-of-screen camera notch has been replaced by a cutout just like the Dynamic Island Apple introduced on the iPhone.

When I reviewed the first iPhone with a Dynamic Island, I was largely positive about it. No, it’s not ideal to have a cutout on the screen, but Apple has handled it as intelligently as possible, and I would even go so far as to say it has turned what should be an albatross into an attractive feature.

Thanks to the OLED display, it’s possible to make any of the pixels around the camera cutout just as black as it is. Apple has designed live activities, the volume bar, and more to appear as if they are expanding out of and around, and contracting back into, this little dot. It remains ingenious.

When the Dynamic Island first arrived on the iPhone, it helped unify several previously disparate approaches to displaying notifications in iOS, making it especially useful. This time, it doesn’t solve as many problems, but it’s an improvement over the dead weight of the notch. It also helps that the MacBook Pro’s much larger screen means the cutout takes up a small share of the screen’s real estate.

The Dynamic Island works the same with full-screen apps as the notch did. By default, the top of the viewport ends just below the Dynamic Island, so it just looks like there’s a taller top bezel, and it doesn’t cut into the application interface. The menu bar, clock, and other system elements still occupy the space above the application (set against a totally black background) when you move the cursor up there.

I tested a few fullscreen games with it, like World of Warcraft and Cyberpunk 2077. Some behaved by default like the default fullscreen app behavior I just described, but others tried to occupy the whole thing, making the cutout visible. Vitally, every video app I tried avoided the cutout in fullscreen.

This isn’t something to upgrade for, of course. But on the whole, I think it’s an improvement over what we had before.

Apple has finally added a touchscreen to the MacBook Pro. I say “finally” because the possibility has floated around for about two decades. Steve Jobs was famously opposed to the idea; there’s even video of him explaining why he thought it would make for a poor user experience.

A couple of years ago, reliable reports emerged that Apple was moving forward with the idea regardless. When I first heard about it, I imagined what could be a really exciting implementation: a convertible laptop that would let you use it in a tablet-like mode, with Apple Pencil support for drawing and writing.

As a game developer, I imagined quickly sketching 2D game maps with an Apple Pencil, using a local AI model to convert them into 3D geometry, and then switching to a laptop configuration to start testing and coding. I also imagined using it like an iPad to more comfortably watch movies on a flight.

That’s not what we got, though. The laptop has the same form factor as the previous MacBook Pro model—no tablet mode or Pencil support. Instead, it just lets you reach up from the keyboard and trackpad to touch the screen. That feels like a missed opportunity.

Some creative apps have implemented touch features that might justify the touchscreen functionality if you use them a lot. For example, you can play the on-screen virtual piano keys on Logic Pro with multiple fingers, which could be nicer than clicking on them. I imagine many people already have workflows built around other approaches, though.

If you were specifically waiting for a touchscreen on your MacBook Pro, this seems like as good an implementation as you could hope for without turning the laptop into a convertible. Apple has put real care into it. For example, it has re-engineered the hinge so the screen is not particularly wobbly when you touch it. It’s fast, accurate, and responsive. It supports multi-touch gestures like pinch-to-zoom or swiping with three fingers to switch desktop spaces.

There’s one use case I find compelling as a developer. Testing iPhone and iPad apps in the Simulator has always been a minor hassle because of the difference in input methods. You either have to approximate only a subset of the interactions available to users with a trackpad and mouse or connect an iPhone or iPad to test them that way. A touchscreen could provide a more elegant way to test such apps in Xcode, Unity, or whatever else you might use.

Unfortunately, some of the UI elements in macOS are too small for my large fingers, so I have to tap very carefully. I also keep forgetting the touchscreen is even there for hours at a time and go back to my normal habits, partly because there’s nothing you can do at the OS level that you can’t do just as easily with trackpad gestures. And as Jobs predicted all those years ago, my arm got tired while I was using it.

For me, it feels faster, easier, more comfortable, and more natural to use the keyboard and trackpad 100 percent of the time, but I admittedly have decades of muscle memory and habits built around them. There’s apparently a generation coming up with the same kind of familiarity with touch interfaces, so this feature may appeal to them. Plus, touchscreen laptops have been a thing on Windows forever, and some people seem to want to buy them, though I never quite got why without an added tablet configuration.

It’s unlikely to account for a big portion of the device’s cost compared with the rest of the OLED panel, memory, storage, and so on. So, my own bafflement aside, I’m not unhappy it’s here. I imagine it will serve those who want it well enough.

As noted earlier, whether this laptop is worthy of a recommendation depends on you and how you use your machine. That’s always been the case, but it seems more so here.

If you’re a creative professional or a display enthusiast who wants the best laptop screen you’ve likely ever seen, you won’t be disappointed if you shell out for it. And hey, maybe you can show me your workflow and prove me wrong about the touchscreen!

If you’re just looking for a great laptop with strong performance for everyday use, consider the non-OLED, non-touchscreen model that’s still in Apple’s lineup—or even a MacBook Air, which compromises relatively little for most users. And there is of course the entire world of Windows and Linux laptops.

If you’re a developer—especially one working at the frontier of AI workflows—well, that’s where things get tricky. Will waiting be worthwhile? So much about how those workflows will evolve and what they’ll demand from your hardware is still so up-in-the air that I can’t make a confident recommendation. (For what it’s worth, I fit best in this category, and I’m still holding on to my M1 Max machine, though my resolve is wavering.)

All that said, in most ways, this is still the same MacBook Pro we’ve reviewed again and again for the past few years. Touch adds a feature aimed at a particular niche, or perhaps it’s there to roll out the welcome mat for a newer generation. The display is a more substantial update and makes a compelling case for upgrading, especially if you’re coming from an older, pre-MiniLED laptop.

But this is an expensive machine. The configuration I reviewed here (16-inch, M5 Max, 128 GB of RAM, 2 TB SSD) retails for a whopping $7,399, though you could at least shave $1,600 off that by going to a more typical-for-most-people 64 GB. (The cheapest configuration starts at $2,899.)

For the vast majority of buyers, the pre-OLED version that’s still in Apple’s lineup or another MacBook altogether will offer more bang for your buck. If you’re the kind of person who wants OLED or a touchscreen, though, you probably know that already. Based on my time with the new MacBook Pro, both features deliver on their promises, even if the latter isn’t as ambitious as some of us had hoped.

Data centers don’t need special treatment to get power faster

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Projects that make their demand more predictable, controllable and financially secure should have a clearer path to service, writes Seyed Madaeni, CEO and co-founder of Verse.

Seyed Madaeni is CEO and co-founder of Verse, an energy software company specializing in battery dispatch optimization for data centers.

Even the best-funded project can’t operate without electricity, and the grid often can’t deliver hundreds of new megawatts as quickly as AI developers need them. Access to power has become a competitive advantage, and companies are searching for ways to get it faster.

Today, developers see two obvious options. They can wait years for traditional grid upgrades, or they can build private power plants onsite. The first can move too slowly. The second brings fuel-supply, equipment, permitting and community challenges of its own.

A third path to data center interconnection is taking shape. Across the country, utilities and grid operators are creating clearer ways for large loads that can reduce the risks they bring to the system. Depending on the market, a data center might guarantee a minimum payment, agree to reduce its grid demand when the system is strained, provide better operating data or contract for new generation and storage.

These policies differ, but they share one idea: If a data center wants faster or more certain access to power, it should give the grid greater certainty in return.

That is a better approach than advancing projects mainly because they promise jobs or investment. Projects that make their demand more predictable, controllable and financially secure should have a clearer path to service.

Utilities often need to invest in substations, transmission equipment and other infrastructure years before a data center reaches full operation. They are building against forecasts that can change, and their queues may include projects that never get built.

Another utility might choose a different percentage or structure. But a company asking the grid to reserve an extraordinary amount of power should be willing to stand behind that request financially. This protects existing customers from paying for infrastructure built around demand that does not materialize. It also helps serious projects distinguish themselves from speculative ones.

Other markets are focused on how data centers behave once they connect.

A large facility can draw hundreds of megawatts, change its consumption quickly or disconnect during a grid disturbance. At that scale, it’s part of how the power system operates, whether it considers itself a grid participant or not.

Texas is responding with clearer technical and operating expectations. A facility that meets the Electric Reliability Council of Texas’ ride-through requirements clears one important gate on the path to energization, alongside required modeling, stability and interconnection reviews. Through controllable-load and bring-your-own-generation pathways in ERCOT's new Batch Zero process, developers can elect dispatchable curtailment under ERCOT’s real-time control in exchange for earlier energization.

Meaning, a data center must be able to reduce what it takes from the grid when the system is strained. It could curtail consumption, switch to onsite resources or coordinate several assets. What matters is that the response is available when promised.

Southwest Power Pool is making a similar trade through its High Impact Large Load framework. Developers that accept real-time forecast reporting, ramp-rate limits and remote-disconnect capability can have their interconnection study completed within 90 days, clearing the way for an agreement soon afterward.

In both markets, developers give the grid better visibility and more control during constrained periods. In return, they receive a faster and more predictable process.

PJM Interconnection is approaching the challenge through resource adequacy that prioritizes whether the region has enough capacity to meet demand when the system is under the most stress.

Under PJM’s proposed Interim Resource Adequacy Service framework, certain new large loads could connect even when the region doesn’t have enough capacity to serve all forecast demand at its traditional reliability standard. Loads that didn't bring qualifying new capacity or receive an applicable capacity allocation could have been required to reduce their grid consumption during limited emergency conditions. FERC rejected most of the framework in October on jurisdictional grounds, but it encouraged states to decide which retail customers are curtailed when the system is stressed, which keeps the same trade on the table.

A data center could reduce that exposure by bringing qualifying generation, storage or demand-side resources. Otherwise, it would accept service that is less firm than what existing customers receive.

Bringing new capacity does not make transmission and distribution constraints disappear. A project may still need local upgrades, engineering studies and regulatory approvals. But if the system is short on capacity, a new load should either help fill the gap or be prepared to reduce its demand when the shortage becomes critical.

Different rules, one underlying question.

Each market is responding to its own constraints, but the question at the heart of these efforts is the same: What can a data center guarantee in exchange for access to power?

Federal regulators are now pushing that question across the country. In June, FERC directed all six regional grid operators under its jurisdiction to justify their existing large load tariffs or propose reforms. States are moving in parallel through large load tariffs and other requirements intended to ensure that data centers pay their share and manage their impact on the grid.

Today, developers learn the answer after spending time and money evaluating a site. Requirements can differ significantly from one utility or region to the next, making it difficult to compare locations, price the obligations attached to service and design the right energy infrastructure from the beginning.

Utilities and grid operators can make this process clearer. A developer should understand early on how specific commitments affect access to power.

The answer isn’t one national tariff or one required technology. Every grid has different needs, and data centers can provide flexibility through batteries, onsite generation, workload management or a combination of resources.

Utilities and grid operators should define a clear menu of performance-based pathways. Developers should know what they can commit, what the grid will require and what faster or more certain service they can receive in return.

Clearer options will help utilities separate real projects from speculative requests, allow developers to understand the cost of speed before committing to a site and give regulators a stronger basis for determining whether an arrangement protects reliability and existing customers.

The fastest data centers will not necessarily be the ones that find a way around the grid. They will be the ones that give the grid enough confidence to move forward with them.

The Massachusetts Attorney General’s Office has implemented a new rule governing shared appreciation mortgages, adding disclosure and consumer protection requirements for a niche home equity product that has faced recent scrutiny.

The rule, effective Nov. 6, clarifies when nonprofit shared appreciation mortgage (SAM) lenders are shielded from state consumer protection claims and sets new notice standards, including prominent disclosure of the maximum amount borrowers may have to repay, according to an announcement from the National Consumer Law Center (NCLC).

The regulation stems from a provision in the state’s economic development bill, passed in November 2024. That statute carved out a broad waiver of liability under Massachusetts consumer protection laws for certain nonprofit SAMs, including loans originated by BlueHub Capital. Advocates say the legislative change created a “dangerous loophole” that exposed distressed homeowners to complex and costly loan structures with limited recourse.

Under the BlueHub-style model, borrowers typically receive a fixed-rate first mortgage alongside a second lien tied to future home price appreciation. The SAM second lien requires the homeowner to share a portion of any increase in property value with the lender upon sale, transfer or refinance. Because the payoff amount is variable and not set in dollars at origination, borrowers can face unexpectedly large balloon payments when they attempt to exit the loan.

NCLC noted that the risks of these structures have been highlighted in Humes v. BlueHub Capital Inc., a Massachusetts class-action lawsuit alleging SAMs embed hidden, subprime-level costs that can strip significant equity and accelerate home loss. The court found BlueHub liable for violating multiple state lending laws and recently certified the class to move forward, according to NCLC.

“While presented as a lifeline, shared appreciation mortgages often become a trap,” Andrea Bopp Stark, senior attorney at the National Consumer Law Center, said in a statement. “The AG’s final rule protects people from BlueHub’s loan model and similar products, but Massachusetts must go further to prevent harm from more common home equity theft loan products, including Home Equity Investment Loans.”

The AG’s new rule does not eliminate SAMs but seeks to ensure that nonprofit and other lenders using these instruments do so transparently and within defined consumer protection boundaries.

The regulation spells out the limited circumstances in which a SAM lender can be insulated from claims under Massachusetts consumer protection law and establishes a framework for robust disclosures, including clear presentation of the maximum possible repayment obligation.

While praising the change, NCLC argues that the rule does not reach a far enough to impact a growing category of products — home equity investments (HEIs). These provide upfront cash to a homeowner in exchange for a share of the future home price appreciation. NCLC characterized these arrangements as high risk for borrowers, as the actual amount owed depends on home price growth and triggers large, lump-sum obligations.

A representative at the AG’s office did not immediately respond to HousingWire‘s request for comment on the changes.

Massachusetts AG Andrea Joy Campbell has already challenged one HEI provider, Hometap. The state alleges consumer protection violations tied to HEI structures. But NCLC noted that the latest rulemaking in Massachusetts does not specifically regulate HEI products.

“The new rules provide important safeguards and requirements for SAMs, and stronger and broader rules are needed to address the high-risk HEI loans that are now targeting Massachusetts homeowners,” Bopp Stark said.

Consumers in other parts of the country have challenged HEI providers in court. Last year, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals overturned a 2023 judgment from a district court in Washington state, ruling that Unison‘s HEI product amounted to a reverse mortgage under state law.

In August, HEI provider Unlock settled with the Office of the Minnesota Attorney General over allegations that the company’s home equity agreements (HEAs) were mortgage loans that violated state usury caps, disclosure rules and licensing requirements.

Companies say they are supportive of regulatory efforts, pointing to a legal framework passed earlier this year in Illinois that could serve as model at the national level. At the Mortgage Bankers Association‘s Annual Convention & Expo last month, executives at Point told HousingWire they believe universal regulations are coming.

“We believe that these companies are going to be regulated,” said Dan McPheeters, Point’s head of business development and partnerships. “The practical effect is, it’s difficult to comply with [mortgage regulations] for a product that doesn’t have a monthly payment, that isn’t self-amortizing. … That said, as long as you address those tactical issues, this should be regulated.”

powerhouse parramatta’s steel-latticed museum opens with art by es devlin and james turrell

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

behind its intricate white exoskeleton, the sydney museum welcomes its first visitors with an ambitious program of exhibitions.

Powerhouse Parramatta, the new museum designed by Moreau Kusunoki and Genton in Western Sydney, Australia, will officially open to the public on November 7th, 2026. Following years of construction along the Parramatta River, the monumental cultural building, with its lattice facade, is ready to welcome its first visitors, with five inaugural exhibitions including immersive installations by Es Devlin and James Turrell.

The opening marks a significant moment for Western Sydney, bringing the region its first state cultural institution and the largest museum ever built in New South Wales. Spanning more than 18,000 square meters of public and exhibition space, the building introduces a new home for the Powerhouse’s collections of art, design, science, and technology. More than 3,000 objects will be presented across the opening exhibitions, with one designed by OMA/AMO.

The museum‘s distinctive steel exoskeleton has already established a striking presence along the Parramatta River. Designed by French and Japanese architecture studio Moreau Kusunoki with Australian practice Genton, the building comprises two large volumes enclosed by a white structural lattice. Its outward-facing framework allows for expansive, column-free interiors, giving the museum flexibility to accommodate installations of varying scales.

Beyond the galleries, the project introduces new public spaces along the waterfront, including a landscaped square connecting the museum to the river. A rooftop garden, observatory, and spaces for educational programming extend its use beyond conventional exhibition halls. With the building now complete, attention turns to the artists and collections that will occupy its vast interiors.

Among the opening presentations is The Turning, the first major Australian exhibition by British artist Es Devlin. Known for her large-scale installations and stage designs, Devlin brings together five works exploring the relationships between human perception, collective memory, and the natural world.

The exhibition includes Forest of Us, a mirrored labyrinth that draws parallels between the branching structures of human lungs and trees, and Memory Palace, a three-dimensional landscape tracing moments that have shaped human understanding across 75,000 years. In Collective Portrait, visitors are invited to sit opposite strangers and draw one another, contributing to an evolving installation. Other works explore drawing and the biodiversity of the Sydney region, inviting audiences to take an active role in the exhibition.

American artist James Turrell also contributes to the opening program with Shangri La (Over The Hump), a monumental light installation presented as part of Task Eternal, an exhibition examining humanity’s fascination with flight and space exploration.

The work belongs to Turrell’s celebrated Ganzfeld series, which uses light and color to alter the viewer’s perception of space. Visitors enter an environment of shifting hues, where the horizon appears to disappear and familiar boundaries become difficult to distinguish. The installation draws on Turrell’s longstanding research into human vision and sensory experience, offering an encounter with the uncertainty of depth and distance.

Elsewhere, the opening program includes The Mall, developed with OMA/AMO to examine the cultural history of shopping centers, and Overflow, a First Nations-led exhibition exploring relationships with water across New South Wales. An immersive children’s exhibition, The Dark, completes the five inaugural presentations.

The museum’s public opening also begins a 23-day festival of performances, workshops, and conversations, bringing activity into the galleries and surrounding spaces. Together, the exhibitions offer the first opportunity to experience Powerhouse Parramatta as a functioning cultural institution, with its architecture now serving the artists, collections, and communities it was designed to accommodate.
