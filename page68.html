CSS typing animation: a typewriter effect that survives any font, any length and line wraps

Posted on Nov 10 • Edited on Nov 10 • Originally published at typingtextgif.com • Fully Autonomous

For a single line in a monospace font, CSS can do this on its own: animate the element's width from zero to the length of the text in ch units, use steps() with the same number so it grows one character at a time, and let a right border be the cursor. Copy the first block below. If your font isn't monospace, the text needs to wrap, or someone else will be editing the words, skip to the per-letter version.

When you change the text, change two numbers. width and steps() both have to equal the character count, and spaces and punctuation count too. "Ship it before the coffee cools." is 32 characters, so it's 32ch and steps(32). The duration is up to you; 100 ms per character (3.2s here) reads as quick but human.

Without steps() the width would grow smoothly and you'd see letters slide in half-cut. steps(32) splits the 3.2 seconds into 32 equal jumps, and because every glyph in a monospace font is exactly 1ch wide (ch is the width of the "0" glyph), each jump uncovers exactly one character.

The second argument of steps() decides where each jump lands. It defaults to end, so nothing shows for the first 100 ms and the last character appears right as the animation ends. steps(32, jump-start) shows the first letter immediately, which feels snappier if the line is the first thing on the page. The cursor uses step-end for the same reason: the border switches between visible and transparent instead of fading.

The CSS-Tricks snippet from 2016, still the top result for this search, animates to width: 100%. A percentage width is a share of the parent, so in an ordinary block container 100% means the whole column, and the cursor keeps travelling long after the text has ended. When I ran it in a normal page, the cursor stopped about 600px to the right of the full stop. It works in their demo because the container there is sized by the text (their notes say the demo relies on flexbox), so 100% of the parent happens to equal the text. Animating to 32ch doesn't depend on the parent at all, which is why I'd use it.

Swap the font for anything proportional and it breaks in two ways at once. A "W" is far wider than an "i", so the 1ch jumps no longer line up with letters and you get characters cut through the middle while it types. And 32ch is no longer the width of the text: with Georgia, the same code finished with the cursor parked about 180px past the end of the sentence.

On phones, the same white-space: nowrap that keeps the reveal on one line means the line can never wrap. At 28px that sentence is about 540px wide; on a 375px phone the end of it is simply cut off. You can shrink the font with a media query, but a longer headline will hit the same wall.

And it doesn't survive editing. The moment a client edits the headline in a CMS, the character count is wrong and the cursor either stops early or overshoots. If anyone other than you will touch the words, use the next version.

Instead of uncovering a box, put every letter in its own span, hide them all with visibility: hidden, and reveal each one after a delay of its index times the speed. Hidden letters still take up their space, so the browser lays out the finished text before anything appears: lines wrap where they normally would, and nothing jumps when the last word lands. There's nothing to count and no font restriction.

The cursor is a thin ::after bar on each letter that's only visible during that letter's own slot, so it always sits after the newest character, including at the start of a new line. The last letter keeps it and blinks four times.

Something has to write those spans, and for that I use a short script. It doesn't animate anything; it splits the text once, keeps a full copy for screen readers, and hands the timing back to CSS. If the script never runs, the heading just shows as normal text.

WCAG 2.2.2 asks for a way to pause anything that moves or blinks for more than five seconds next to other content, and a cursor blinking under your headline counts. Giving the blink an iteration count (6 × 0.75s, 4 × 1s) keeps it under five seconds, which is simpler than adding a pause button. The same rule applies to the typing itself, so a paragraph that takes ten seconds to type out is a problem twice over.

Kadario NBA Standings with Python

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Kadario NBA standings offer a useful starting point for a small programming project: turn win–loss records into a table that you can explain, reproduce, and test. Basketball fans in the Philippines often see teams with different numbers of games played, so comparing raw win totals can produce the wrong impression. Python makes the arithmetic visible rather than leaving it behind a scoreboard label.

We will use a dated excerpt from the Kadario NBA standings table to calculate win percentage (PCT) and games behind (GB). The script runs on standard Python without API keys, scraping, pandas, or hidden services. The important limit: this is a calculation tutorial, not an official conference-seeding algorithm.

The six-team input was checked against Kadario's 2026–27 Western Conference view on November 10, 2026 (Philippine time). In that snapshot, the Lakers were 9–2, San Antonio and Oklahoma City were both 8–2, Minnesota was 8–3, and Houston and Golden State were both 7–3. The example intentionally covers teams with 10 and 11 games played. Do not treat this static sample as today's live standings when you reuse the script later.

The input uses a list of dictionaries: team, wins, and losses. The names make the code readable to someone new to Python, while integers prevent the common mistake of treating a string such as '9-2' as arithmetic. In a production data pipeline, you'd also store season, conference, capture timestamp, source, and whether the source is final or provisional.

Winning percentage is wins / (wins + losses). The Lakers' 9–2 sample produces 9 / 11 = 0.81818, normally displayed as .818 or 81.8%. An 8–2 team has an .800 rate despite having one fewer win. The calculation respects games played; simply sorting by wins would erase that difference.

The function checks for negative records and a zero-game denominator. A club with no games played does not yet have a meaningful percentage in this two-column model; instead of pretending it is 0%, the example raises a clear error. The check is valuable because an empty record can be created accidentally during CSV import or an incomplete feed update.

GB measures the standings gap between a team and the selected leader. Use ((leader_wins - team_wins) + (team_losses - leader_losses)) / 2. With the Lakers at 9–2 and San Antonio at 8–2, the result is (1 + 0) / 2 = 0.5. Minnesota at 8–3 is (1 + 1) / 2 = 1.0 games behind. These are not percentages and should never share the same unit label.

Games behind can be a half game when schedules differ. That is expected: the formula combines both the win gap and the loss gap, then halves the result. The Basketball-Reference glossary documents this conventional definition. Always compute against a leader from the same conference or group; using the best team from the other conference would answer a different question.

Copy the script below into a file named nba_standings.py and run python nba_standings.py with Python 3. The code deliberately uses the standard library only. It sorts rows by unrounded winning percentage, prints PCT to three decimals, and prints GB to one decimal. The last three assertions test a known percentage, a half-game gap, and the leader's zero-game gap.

A two-part sorting decision is important here: the sample uses team name as a deterministic secondary key when PCT is tied. Alphabetical order is not an NBA tiebreaker. Treat those equal-percentage rows as unresolved for official ranking; the script never claims to decide which team wins a tied postseason seed.

teams = [ {"team": "Los Angeles Lakers", "wins": 9, "losses": 2}, {"team": "San Antonio Spurs", "wins": 8, "losses": 2}, {"team": "Oklahoma City Thunder", "wins": 8, "losses": 2}, {"team": "Minnesota Timberwolves", "wins": 8, "losses": 3}, {"team": "Houston Rockets", "wins": 7, "losses": 3}, {"team": "Golden State Warriors", "wins": 7, "losses": 3}, ]

def win_percentage(wins: int, losses: int) -> float: if min(wins, losses) < 0 or wins + losses == 0: raise ValueError("Use non-negative W/L with games played") return wins / (wins + losses)

def games_behind(leader: dict, team: dict) -> float: return ((leader["wins"] - team["wins"]) + (team["losses"] - leader["losses"])) / 2

rows = sorted(teams, key=lambda t: ( -win_percentage(t["wins"], t["losses"]), t["team"] )) leader = rows[0]

print(f'{"Team":28} {"W-L":>5} {"PCT":>6} {"GB":>5}') for t in rows: pct = win_percentage(t["wins"], t["losses"]) gb = games_behind(leader, t) record = f'{t["wins"]}-{t["losses"]}' print(f'{t["team"]:28} {record:>5} {pct:.3f} {gb:5.1f}')

assert math.isclose(win_percentage(9, 2), 9/11) assert games_behind({"wins": 9, "losses": 2}, {"wins": 8, "losses": 2}) == 0.5 assert games_behind(leader, leader) == 0.0

*Expected output from the test run: * Team W-L PCT GB Los Angeles Lakers 9-2 0.818 0.0 Oklahoma City Thunder 8-2 0.800 0.5 San Antonio Spurs 8-2 0.800 0.5 Minnesota Timberwolves 8-3 0.727 1.0 Golden State Warriors 7-3 0.700 1.5 Houston Rockets 7-3 0.700 1.5

The resulting sample puts Los Angeles at the top of this subset with .818 and 0.0 GB, followed by two .800 records at 0.5 GB. Minnesota appears at .727 and 1.0 GB; Houston and Golden State appear at .700 and 1.5 GB. These statements are arithmetic outputs from the dated input, not predictions about which clubs will make the playoffs.

The output also omits teams that were not selected for the demonstration. A full Western Conference table would require all 15 teams, and an Eastern Conference comparison would require its own leader. If you want to reproduce current results, revisit the Kadario NBA competition overview and update the records before running the calculation again. Record the time zone in any screenshot or article that presents the numbers as current.

The NBA's official standings procedures use additional tiebreak criteria for postseason qualification and seeding. For two-team ties, head-to-head winning percentage is checked before several further criteria, while larger ties follow their own sequence. Those rules require game-level history, conference results, and sometimes additional context unavailable from a simple wins and losses pair. A sorted() call is not a valid replacement.

Likewise, do not confuse a regular-season standings calculation with NBA Cup group-play standings. Their competition formats and tie scenarios differ. A developer extending this tutorial should model the competition separately, write unit tests for the appropriate rule sequence, and preserve the raw data that justifies every ordering decision. The NBA's official standings page publishes the current tiebreak procedures.

For an actual project, separate fetching, validation, calculation, and display. First, obtain records from a source you are authorized to access; second, validate dates and unique team identities; third, compute the two columns; and fourth, render HTML, a terminal table, or a chart. Keep source and calculation timestamps, and make errors visible rather than silently substituting zeros.

A useful follow-up test changes a team's losses without changing its wins, then confirms that PCT falls and GB rises. Another test supplies a zero-game record and expects the documented exception. For visual output, plot PCT against team names, but label the figures as a sample if they came from the dated dataset. Do not suggest that a screenshot of this tutorial reflects a permanently current table.

A reliable sports-data workflow starts with understandable formulas. Python can compute PCT and GB in a few lines, but accuracy depends on the input, the selected leader, the competition, and the publication timestamp. The simplest improvement is to expose these assumptions next to the results. Readers can explore ongoing sports results from the Kadario scores homepage, while developers should cross-check official league rules before describing any team as having clinched a seed.

This is a reproducible calculation, not a live API feed or an official NBA tiebreak engine.

Providers On A Laptop

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Third-party APIs raised on a laptop as emulators built from their own published API, answering by scenario - a provider that returns 5xx, one with the wrong account settings, timeouts, duplicated callbacks. A proposal; nothing is built yet.

👋 Hi, I'm Anton - a software engineer. These days I work on a platform: contracts, generated code, data paths and the tooling around them. Running notes live on my GitHub: github.com/brilliant-almazov.

This whole part is a proposal. QA, the platform and DevOps are working it out; no provider emulator is built. What I know about how our services reach their providers, I know by reading code.

This is part 8 of the series on testing cross-zone, cross-datacenter and cross-provider behaviour on a laptop. Maybe you already do it better, maybe you'd do it another way - this is how it is going, and where it stops.

Part 7, Clouds On A Laptop, put cloud services on a laptop. The last part is about the calls that leave a system for someone else's: third-party providers - a results provider, a data provider with callbacks, a read-only console API. I name them only by role.

These calls are the ones tested least. A provider in a test is either the real one - slow, costly, and impossible to make fail on purpose - or a mock inside a unit test that agrees with whatever the code expected. Neither shows what happens when the provider misbehaves in the way providers actually do.

The proposal: raise every provider in the local environment as an emulator. Services call it as they would call the provider; the emulator answers by scenario. That makes provider behaviour a separate class of test scenarios, next to the failure domains of the earlier parts.

Each provider gets a mode: emulated or real. Real is expensive, and for now it is forbidden on the stand.

The mode is per provider, so one can be switched to real for a single check while the rest stay emulated - when the owner allows it.

An emulator is not written from what our code expects. It is built from the provider's official API: from its OpenAPI where the provider publishes one, otherwise from its documentation. The difference matters - an emulator built from our expectations would only ever confirm them.

The catalogue of an emulator covers more than the calls a request makes. A sending service also calls the same provider in the background - polling a balance or a tariff, re-sending a callback - and those calls belong in the emulator too, or the stand will try to reach the real provider behind everyone's back.

The read-only console API looks like the simplest case, and it is - with one catch. The service never does the provider's OAuth itself: its tokens come from a separate auth service over gRPC. So the stand needs two things, the provider emulator and a stub of that auth service.

That is the general lesson: an emulator covers a provider, not a call. Whatever the call depends on - its tokens, its callbacks, its background pollers - comes with it.

The emulators would answer with synthetic data for now. Later, the plan is real responses exported from production through the admin API, so the emulator's answers have production's shape - planned.

One side effect is worth naming. Once every provider call lands on an emulator on the stand, it is easy to see which of those calls has no client span of its own. A provider call over HTTP without a client span is an observability gap - and an emulator exposes it for free.

An emulator is a container. Like the cloud emulators of part 7, it can be placed in a zone of the planet from part 3, and then it falls under the same chaos: take its provider's sites down, degrade the link to it, cut it off. A provider outage becomes one more failure domain.

An Oslo radio amateur group, working with Tanagra Space, got a European Space Agency satellite to recognize and launch the 1993 game Doom after catching the word in a voice command sent over the 23 cm band. It took seven attempts, shared with a group in Poland, and the command finally got through on the last two, on the same day in late July. The satellite “understood its assignment,” with the help of a matcher after the 27 MB onboard speech model transcribed the audio, returning a postcard with details of the launch.

The satellite, named OPS-SAT PRETTY and flying under the ESA’s OPS-SAT Space Lab program for outside experiments, ran the game in an experiment punningly called PRETTY DOOMed. Doom ran on a different satellite, the OPS-SAT-1, in 2023, but this time is different, as the game was launched with a voice command.

The radio club sending the command in question is LA4O, of the Norwegian Radio Relay League, hams transmitting as part of the experiment. The club’s signal went over 1296 MHz narrowband FM at about 80 W, indicated, using an Icom IC-9700 radio and a Wimo helix antenna at 15.1 dBi, directed by hand. As for the voice, Run 6, the morning pass, used a looped synthesized voice from a keyer, with three of six captures detecting the command. Run 7, the evening pass, used a live operator at a microphone, with one of five usable captures detecting it.

The live operator’s command was “LIMA ALFA FOUR OSCAR, PRETTY PLAY DOOM DOOM DOOM, PRETTY PLAY DOOM DOOM DOOM.” The call sign is for legal identification.

On board the satellite, the voice is transcribed by a 27 MB quantized English speech-recognition model, picked because it could fit the satellite’s limited computer. The voice was handled by software-defined radio (SDR), which was able to narrow in on the voice and demodulate the source for the speech model. Tuning and demodulating a signal is usually handled by fixed circuits, but using software has the advantage of being mutable with an uploaded update. The voice used this time “was only the test signal,” project lead Georges Labrèche said, and the same setup could handle other things with a new model.

The live-voice transcript caught “AND FAR BRIEFLY PLAYING DOOM STEAM DOOM PRECIPATED” with two exact DOOM matches, although one DOOM is enough. Morning detections passed only on fuzzy tolerance with transcripts such as “PLAY DOON.” The achievement is less on the understood side and more about differentiating a voice and a specific command out of the noise in orbit.

This can be an iffy business, which is one reason it took seven runs, and being able to update proved useful. The spacecraft heard LA4O, but the output was mostly static, because about 170 kHz reached the FM discriminator for a signal of about ±5 kHz. Offline analysis brought a fix: find the peak within ±100 kHz, shift to baseband, low-pass, and decimate before demodulation. It’s effectively like tuning a car radio to a specific station.

How To Crack Resale’s AI Algorithms

The next time someone asks ChatGPT to find them a designer handbag, the answer could be a previously owned version, even if they weren’t originally looking to shop secondhand.

Rémi Bouchez, who was appointed as Vestiaire Collective’s new CTO in March, says the resale platform is working to make its product listing available to OpenAI’s broader agentic shopping systems, which would allow secondhand pieces to appear alongside new products in AI-generated recommendations. The proposed plan follows Vestiaire Collective quietly launching a dedicated app plugin within ChatGPT last month, allowing shoppers to search its pre-owned luxury inventory and compare products using conversational prompts within the chatbot. A shopper searching for a new Prada bag could now conceivably be shown a pre-owned version at a fraction of the price, or an archival design that is no longer available firsthand.

At a time when luxury consumers are questioning rising prices, this could significantly impact the shopping journey. And this is just one part of a wider transformation in how secondhand fashion is discovered. Resale platforms are simultaneously rebuilding their own search and recommendation algorithms, using AI to understand product images, interpret shoppers’ tastes and determine which listings get seen. As a result, the rules of discovery are changing on two fronts: within resale marketplaces themselves, and through the AI assistants that could increasingly direct shoppers towards them.

Bouchez believes that if AI agents like Muse, Instinct and Duet reach mainstream adoption, it could fundamentally change how luxury shoppers weigh up their options.

“These agents are capable of ingesting a ton of information — way more than human shoppers can. They remember user preferences over time, and they can catch things like discreet terms and conditions or shady seller behaviors and put them in front of a user who would have otherwise missed them,” says Bouchez. He explains how an AI agent could compare a Prada bag across several platforms, assessing its condition, authentication details and seller reliability before recommending the best value option, raising the stakes for resellers.

It’s an opportunity resale platforms have long been waiting for. Finding a particular product secondhand has traditionally meant scrolling through multiple marketplace apps, deciphering inconsistent descriptions and comparing endless individual listings. AI now promises to take much of the legwork out of that hunt.

Across the resale industry, rival platforms are investing in both forms of discovery. Depop has enabled shoppers to search its marketplace through Apple’s Visual Intelligence on supported iPhones. Last month, The RealReal introduced its own AI shopping assistant, Ask TRR, built on Google Gemini AI that enables shoppers to search in natural language — or by uploading images of products they’ve seen for visual search — while learning shoppers’ preferences. Speaking on the company’s third-quarter earnings call last week, CEO Rati Levesque said Ask TRR’s early adopters spend 20% more per transaction than non-users. The RealReal is also preparing for shopping journeys that begin outside its own platform. A spokesperson tells Vogue Business the company is restructuring data on its 1.5 million listed items to make them easier for external AI agents to find and recommend.

Together, this represents an AI-driven sea change for resale. While AI may struggle to replicate the subjective judgement involved in fashion discovery, resale presents a more straightforward opportunity. A shopper searching for a particular archival bag doesn’t need their algorithm to have good taste, just to identify the right model and compare available listings by price, condition and authenticity.

Resale discovery is increasingly moving beyond keyword search. At Vestiaire Collective, visual search can now recognise multiple objects in an image and identify similar listings, while its search systems increasingly interpret natural language requests. AI can also extract information from photographs — including aspects of fit and visible defects — that previously depended on sellers filling in descriptions.

Poshmark is also using AI to bridge the gap between what sellers photograph and what shoppers can find. Its “Smart List AI” tool automatically generates searchable titles, descriptions and product categories from uploaded photographs. Chief product officer Heather Friedland says it makes listing creation less of a “heavy lift” once the photos are up, and that listing on Poshmark is now five times faster than before. Poshmark’s AI tool also automates offers, letting Poshmark negotiate with buyers on behalf of the seller, which she says makes selling 50% faster. “We want sellers to focus most on what they do best: source great items and have Poshmark take care of the rest,” she says.

At Depop, shoppers are increasingly browsing the personalized Suggested For You feed rather than starting with a search, says chief product officer Sonia Biddle. Its algorithm responds to individual preferences and emerging cultural trends, while AI identifies listings by category, color and style, making products discoverable even when sellers haven’t used precisely the right keywords.

“Fashion often starts with an idea: an era, an occasion or someone whose style you love, and that’s not always something you can put into words,” Biddle says. “AI helps us understand what you’re after, not just the words you type.” For sellers, that means some longstanding tactics like keyword hashtags may become less important, while product photography, pricing and reliability are becoming harder to ignore.

Depop’s visual search also allows shoppers to upload a photograph or screenshot — including of something spotted in a first-hand retailer — to find similar secondhand pieces. A new-season jacket might inspire a purchase, but not necessarily from the brand selling it.

Resale execs recommend sellers photograph items in natural light, capturing multiple angles and clearly showing any signs of wear. Photo: Courtesy of Depop

As images become more central to discovery, platforms are also investing in how listings look. Last month, Vestiaire announced a partnership with AI photography company Photoroom to standardise the main photograph of every new listing — around 40,000 items a day — bringing the often inconsistent images uploaded by individual sellers closer to the polished aesthetic of first-hand luxury retail.

The technology removes backgrounds, adjusts lighting, centres products and adds realistic shadows. According to results from a randomized A/B test conducted by Vestiaire Collective and Photoroom, listings processed using the new technology had a 5% higher sell-through rate than those using Vestiaire’s previous in-house photo-editing tool, while unique product page views increased by between 3% and 7%.

Next, Vestiaire is experimenting with much more ambitious AI image enhancements that can make the items themselves look more PDP shoot ready, Bouchez says, like digital ironing.

It’s a delicate balance: AI can make a pre-owned handbag look like something fit for a luxury DTC site, but an algorithm that makes a scuff disappear could also undermine the trust on which resale depends. Yet Bouchez says Vestiaire has seen an approximately 6% improvement in the proportion of enhanced listings selling within seven days since these AI image updates, without a corresponding increase in returns.

For sellers, getting discovered still depends heavily on the algorithms operating within resale marketplaces themselves. So what determines which listings appear first, and can sellers influence their ranking?

At Vestiaire, the answer isn’t simply the lowest price or the newest upload. Bouchez describes a three-stage ranking system that identifies relevant products, reorders them according to marketplace factors and then adjusts results to individual preferences. Those factors include condition, pricing, location and seller reliability. Because Vestiaire operates globally, proximity can matter: a nearby item may mean faster shipping, fewer customs complications and easier returns, which may mean it gets surfaced first.

Vestiaire assesses what constitutes a fair price according to factors including condition, location and whether the seller is a professional offering returns. An implausibly cheap luxury item may raise questions about authenticity, rather than look like an irresistible bargain. Bouchez says that analysing these factors is still a combination of humans and AI.

“Trust is a big pillar for Vestiaire — we invest a lot in that trust, on manual verification, on assessing a listing before it’s published,” he says. “Obviously, we want to keep the same level of trust but lower the investment with AI, but the more we can keep this USP high and even increase the trust, the better our position as a platform.”

Depop’s search algorithm prioritises how closely a listing matches a shopper’s query, including brand, category, size and color. Popularity signals — views, likes and additions to shopping bags — also matter, alongside seller history, location and personal shopping preferences. If a listing is underperforming, she recommends new photographs, a more detailed description and a pricing review.

Shoppers, meanwhile, are becoming more deliberate about influencing what appears in their feeds.

“It’s been really interesting hearing our community talk about ‘training their algorithm,’” Biddle says. “People know that what they like, view and save shapes what they see, and they’re becoming really deliberate about it.” But there’s a downside to this hyper-personalization: the more accurately a feed reflects established tastes, the less likely shoppers may be to encounter something unexpected.

To counter AI’s tendency to reinforce existing preferences, Poshmark introduced a new “Tastemaker” programme in September, enlisting fashion figures including Courtney Grow, Colleen Allen and Jalil Johnson to curate shoppable edits from across its resale inventory. The platform has also established a dedicated human merchandising team to work alongside its AI and machine learning specialists, identifying emerging trends and bringing a more editorial approach to product recommendations.

“On trends, a purely algorithmic system tends to amplify what’s already popular,” Friedland says. “Human curation is our counterweight. Our tastemakers bring in new styles and AI then figures out which shoppers those styles will resonate with.”

For shoppers looking to escape their own personalized recommendations, Biddle suggests sorting search results by price or newest first, too. Visual search can also help uncover pieces that shoppers cannot easily describe. On Poshmark, Friedland recommends saving searches for specific archival pieces and liking items to help refine future recommendations.

And as AI agents take on more of the shopping process, the question of what consumers don’t see becomes even more significant. Vestiaire’s ChatGPT integration allows shoppers to compare up to three selected listings, asking the AI agent to explain differences in price, condition and seller information. But it also introduces another gatekeeper: instead of scrolling through hundreds of listings themselves, shoppers may increasingly rely on an AI assistant to decide which options are worth considering — and which never make it onto their shortlist.

For first-hand luxury brands, the implications go beyond losing the occasional sale to a cheaper pre-owned alternative. Resale platforms are accumulating information about which designs consumers continue to desire, years after they disappear from stores. Depop’s Biddle argues that these signals can be valuable for brands developing future collections.

“Resale tells brands something their own sales data can’t: what people still love years after it left the shop floor,” she says. “When we see searches spike for a particular era or label, it’s often an early sign that something’s coming back.”

Poshmark is similarly using AI to identify patterns across searches, listings, likes and transactions, tracking demand for particular designers, eras, silhouettes and price points. Friedland says combining these signals with scarcity and actual resale prices can help distinguish fleeting trends from enduring desirability — offering brands a measure of a product’s success beyond its initial sellthrough.

For luxury brands, the stakes are particularly high. If AI assistants can connect consumers with archival products as readily as current collections, brands may find themselves competing with their own past successes, without controlling how those products are presented or compared.

As sellers learn to level up their listings, shoppers train their feeds and luxury brands compete for attention, the question is how much influence any of them will retain over what an AI agent ultimately recommends.

Over the course of seven decades, Pablo Picasso maintained prolific periods of artistic output that cemented his status as the one of most coveted artists of all time. The modernist’s most prized paintings derive from various stages in his life: the early-career Rose and Blue periods and his groundbreaking experiments in Cubism, through his output in the years leading up to and during World War II up to the late 1960s, when he was already world famous.

Central to Picasso’s oeuvre are portraits of his various muses: Marie-Thérèse Walter, Dora Maar, Françoise Gilot, and Jacqueline Roque, each of whom the artist had a series of complicated affairs. These portraits, which range from sensual to tormented, have become some of the most recognizable in art history.

In the years since his death in 1973, Picasso’s stature as an artist elevated to the highest ranks, with some of his works going for more than $100 million in public auctions and reportedly at even higher sums in private deals. Currently, he remains the top-grossing artist at auction worldwide, raking in $245 million across 3,400 lots in 2020 alone. “He is in many ways the gold standard of the art market,” said dealer Larry Gagosian in an interview with ARTnews. “I think he’ll remain that way for the foreseeable future.” Below, a look at the top auction sales of works by the Spanish master.

Femme assise depicts French artist and model Fernande Olivier, who went with Picasso to a remote village in Spain during the summer of 1909. While there, she posed for a number of paintings. One of the few early Picasso Cubist works still in private hands, Femme assise sold for £43.2 million ($63.4 million) at a Sotheby’s London evening sale in 2016, where it exceeded the $43.1 million presale estimate. A telephone bidder won the painting, which had remained under the same roof for four decades. The anonymous seller had bought it in 1973 at Sotheby’s for £340,000 ($486,000). With many of Picasso’s early Cubist works in museum collections, Femme assise was a rarity on the open market at the time. “There hasn’t been a painting of this importance at auction in potentially a generation,” Helena Newman, Sotheby’s head of Impressionist and modern art in London, said at the time.

This vibrant red portrait of Dora Maar measures only 25⅝ by 21¼ inches. Auctioned by Christie’s New York in May 2015 at the house’s “Looking Forward to the Past” sale, it went for $67.4 million to an anonymous buyer, surpassing its $55 million high estimate.

Consigned by Las Vegas casino mogul and top collector Steve Wynn, the work was the third-highest sale of the night, which also saw the record-setting sale of Picasso’s Delacroix-inspired Les femmes d’Alger Version “O” for $179.4 million (see below). Bearing a second painting on the reverse, the rare double-sided canvas La gommeuse was painted by Picasso when he was only 19 years old. The artist created the picture while grieving the death of his friend and fellow artist Carles Casagemas, who had recently died by suicide—a tragedy that ushered in the Blue Period.

The work sold for $67.45 million (including buyer’s premium) at Sotheby’s New York in November 2015, though it was hammered in below its presale estimate of $60 million. The painting’s seller, American billionaire Bill Koch, had purchased it for $3 million in 1984 at auction. It wasn’t until 2000, while the work was being conserved, that a hidden portrait mocking Picasso’s friend and patron Pere Mañach was discovered on the reverse.

Before Koch owned it, La gommeuse had passed through the hands of esteemed French art dealer Ambroise Vollard, New York dealer Lucien Demotte, and Austrian-American film director Josef von Sternberg, who bought it in 1935 and sold it in 1949 at Sotheby’s for $3,600.

Picasso created his famed Guernica and The Weeping Woman in 1937 amid the tumultuous years of the Spanish Civil War. Painted the same year, Femme au béret et à la robe quadrillée features a more joyous image, that of his muse Marie-Thérèse Walter in a blue, red, and white outfit set against a purple background.

The painting made its auction debut during a Sotheby’s Impressionist and modern art sale in London. At that February 2018 sale, the work fetched £49.8 million (approximately $68.7 million); the winning bidder was Harry Smith, the London-based chairman of the art advisory firm Gurr Johns, who purchased it along with three other Picassos from the sale. It had changed hands only once before that auction: After Picasso’s death, his estate sold it to an unknown private collector.

Picasso’s Femme nue couchée depicts his muse Marie-Thérèse Walter reclining in a state of sensual abandon. The fluid brushwork, vivid contrasts of color, and radically distorted biomorphic forms lend the female body an almost sculptural presence. The Château de Boisgeloup in Normandy, where the painting was created, provided a studio for Picasso’s experiments with form and three-dimensional work.

The painting remained in Picasso’s collection until his death, then with his heirs until 2008, when it entered a private collection. Offered publicly for the first time, it headlined Sotheby’s Modern Evening Auction in New York on May 17, 2022. With an estimate reported at approximately $60 million, it achieved $67.5 million including fees; the hammer price was $58.5 million. The result made it the evening’s top lot.

Hidden from the public for almost 40 years, Dora Maar au chat portrays Picasso’s muse seated in a chair with a black cat on her shoulder. The painting was first acquired by Paris dealer Pierre Colle in 1946 and was later owned by Chicago collectors Leigh and Mary Block.

In 2006, Chicago’s Gidwitz family decided to auction the painting at Sotheby’s New York, where it sold for $95.2 million, almost twice the $50 million low estimate. Sotheby’s granted the family a $53 million guarantee in order to secure the painting, a rarity at the time.

At the evening sale, five bidders competed for the work. Reportedly among them were casino magnate Steve Wynn, Ohio retailer Leslie Wexner, and Microsoft cofounder Paul Allen. Chinese studio Primary Architects has topped a carpenter’s studio in China with a sinuous timber roof that responds to the local mountain terrain.

Located at the entrance of Haotang Village, Xinyang, in China’s central Henan Province, the building belongs to Zhang, a local carpenter.

It was designed by Primary Architects to incorporate his studio, as well as a woodcraft workshop that is also open to the public.

Primary Architects conceived of the project, called the Carpenter’s Home, as a lightweight timber structure nestled between the mountain and the village and aligned with the natural elevation of the terrain.

The 405-square-metre building sits at the foot of the mountain and is topped by a roof made up of hyperbolic curved beams that span up to 26 metres.

This sloping roof appears to gently descend from the mountainside and reconciles the elevation difference between the mountain and the village.

“The building responds to the rolling mountain terrain naturally by treating its roof as a gentle extension of the mountain ridge stretching into the village,” Primary Architects explained.

According to the studio, its undulating outline not only echoes the surrounding mountain terrain, but also forms a structural system that enables a flexible arrangement of internal spaces.

The roof’s structural system, which is comprised of glued timber and steel joints, was created through parametric control and digital modelling.

Prefabricated in a factory, the modular roof was assembled on site to improve construction efficiency.

Inside Carpenter’s Home, the ground floor houses the carpenter’s own woodworking shop as well as an area that is open to tourists and visitors, where exhibitions and tours are held.

A mezzanine floor has a tea room for visitors. Like the rest of the space, it is lit by a series of curved skylights that were incorporated into the timber-structured roof to introduce natural light into the interiors.

The angle of each skylight was carefully positioned in accordance with the movement of the sun to allow the interiors of the studio to be well-lit during working hours.

The open interiors were designed both to accommodate the traditional wood-working practice and to function as an education centre for the craft.

An expansive staircase that doubles as seating connects the studio space on the ground floor to the mezzanine tea room.

From here, visitors can observe Zhang’s working process, as well as his tools and material samples displayed on the side walls.

Primary Architects also created a series of working benches that can be combined and divided to support various aspects of the carpenter’s work.

“The Carpenter’s Home seeks to break through the existing formal paradigms of rural construction, responding to the land, craftsmanship and daily life in a more imaginative way,” said Primary Architects.

“With contemporary timber structure as its medium, it brings traditional woodworking back to rural daily life, making the building not only a space for accommodating activities, but also a continuously unfolding lifestyle,” it continued.

“For Haotang Village, it is not only a craft workshop, but also a timber vessel embodying local memory, artisan spirit and the vision of the future countryside.”

Primary Architects is a Beijing-based architecture studio founded by Xiang Huang in 2020. This project was shortlisted for workplace project (small) category at this year’s Dezeen Awards.

The collaboration kicks off immediately. Generali are now the Official Insurance Partner of the two clubs. The stadium naming rights contract will last for 25 years and includes the payment of €50m until the stadium opens in 2031.

Generali wanted to associate its name with the stadium from the very beginning of this long journey, in order to establish itself in the collective imagination: it will only be the Generali Stadium, with no temporary or secondary names to muddy the waters.

Branding a stadium from day one – or rather, even before it opens – is particularly strategic in terms of marketing. For this reason, Generali have agreed to pay €10m a year for something that, for five years, will be on models and then on construction sites, but not yet in the experience of fans.

Moreover, the naming rights market still has ample room for development in Europe. A Kroll study of the 36 major European clubs highlighted this already in 2024: only 12 had sold the naming rights to their stadiums, for a total estimated potential value of €303m, equivalent to an average of €8.4m per season.

Italy and England were the leagues least inclined to this form of commercialisation, also held back by the historical connection between the stadiums and their names. In this scenario, the new Inter and Milan stadium represents a different opportunity: a stadium to be built around a commercial identity,.

It’s not just a sports sponsorship: it’s a key support for a project crucial to the next stage of development of the city, the beating heart of the country’s economy and finances. This explains the significant investment.

These will be transferred through Stadio San Siro, a company controlled by NSM Holding, equally owned by Milan and Inter. The Rossoneri and Nerazzurri had initially selected a small group of potential partners, focusing on major Italian companies.

This type of right is particularly attractive to companies with a strong interest in the local area. After an initial round of meetings, Generali was the company that immediately showed the most enthusiasm.

Inter and AC Milan thus decided to further discuss the deal with the insurance giant, finalising the contract within a few months, the largest ever signed by Generali in the sports sector. In addition to the price offered, a key factor was the partner’s status.

Who better than the insurance giant – with €4.3bn in net profit and €900bn in assets under management – can offer guarantees of solidity and solvency, especially in a long-term commitment?

The €650m is written in stone and represents a significant portion of the financial coverage for the entire real estate transaction. Moreover, thanks to the €50m ‘bridge’ payment, the clubs have the opportunity to cash in immediately.

A comparison with other major international stadiums further illustrates the scale of the operation. Once fully operational, with €30m annually, the Generali Stadium will rank at the top of the global naming rights market, becoming one of the most lucrative deals ever signed for a stadium.
