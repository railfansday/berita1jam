Here's Why You Shouldn't Use AI to Vote

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As Americans go to the polls today, many will have had their decision about who to vote for informed by an artificial intelligence-powered chatbot.

Around 50 percent of recently surveyed US adults have at one time used an AI chatbot, and 8 percent say they have used these tools ahead of the midterms to find information on candidates and policies, according to Pew Research.

But a lot of information from these chatbots is out of date and has been hallucinated or sourced from partisan websites.

Even supposedly non-partisan chatbots like OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Anthropic’s Claude, and Google’s Gemini have been found wanting when it comes to questions about the midterms.

Just last week, Google’s AI Mode refused to provide the results of the 2020 elections and the company’s Gemini chatbot said the deadline to request a mail-in ballot in Pennsylvania was November 3, rather than the real deadline of October 27, according to research by Forum AI.

According to the Forum AI research, almost 40 percent of election answers given by all the mainstream chatbots contained at least one factual error. In August, when Forum AI’s researchers asked why someone should vote for North Carolina congressman Don Davis, Claude gave a response that confused the lawmaker with a “former school superintendent” of the same name.

Google and Anthropic did not respond to requests for comment. Open AI directed WIRED to a blog outlining the company’s approach to elections in 2026.

Part of the problem is that chatbots are reliant on an increasingly problematic pool of online information. Research by NewsGuard found that mainstream chatbots cite so-called “pink slime” websites nearly 50 percent of the time when asked about candidates and policies related to the midterms. “Pink slime” sites present as mainstream news websites, but are in fact funded by political or ideological groups and individuals.

Ahead of the midterms, many more of these sites have sprung up, and chatbots are paying attention. The worst offender, according to NewsGuard, was OpenAI’s ChatGPT, which cited the “pink slime” sites in more than 70 percent of searches, while xAI’s Grok chatbot performed best, citing “pink slime” sites in 30 percent of cases.

“I don't think that most Americans are prepared to navigate an election in the age of consumer AI,” Nina Jankowicz, executive director of the American Sunlight Project and a former disinformation czar during the Biden administration, tells WIRED. “One of the problems is that executives and companies have really overpromised on what AI is capable of. It has to ingest this information from somewhere, and often those sources are not very good. And it's predictive, so if it doesn't know the answer, it does frequently make things up.”

Why Amazon Stock Can Keep Rising Through 2035

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Amazon's cloud business gets the spotlight, but improving e-commerce margins is an overlooked opportunity.

Most of the attention on Amazon (AMZN +2.20%) goes to its cloud business, Amazon Web Services (AWS). But the company's online stores are quietly becoming more profitable, thanks to automation and robotics. That's a big reason the stock can keep rising through 2035.

On the third-quarter earnings call, CEO Andy Jassy said, "There has never been a business with a trillion dollars of annual revenue. Amazon is on a path to have two of them in AWS and stores." He added that some folks are "sleeping a bit" on how much progress the retail business has made.

That progress shows up in profit margins. Jassy said Amazon's North America and International segments have each expanded operating margins by nearly 10 percentage points over the past few years. The company is also on track to lower its cost to serve each unit, or deliver each item, for a fourth straight year.

Robotics is a big driver. Amazon has more than 1 million robots in its fulfillment network, and its newest facilities, including same-day delivery sites, are "very heavy on robotics." In the third quarter, shipping costs, excluding fuel and long-haul inflation, grew about four percentage points slower than units shipped. Jassy said the economic impact on its business so far is "small compared to what it is going to be in the next five years."

Lower costs on a sales base this large, with Amazon's online store generating $286 billion in annual revenue, can free up a lot more cash. Over the past 12 months, operating cash flow, the cash the business brings in before investments, grew 29% year over year to $168 billion. It has compounded about 25% a year over the past five years, and with Amazon's automation push still early, that cash flow has room to keep growing for years. And over time, Amazon's stock price tends to follow cash flow.

Yet the stock trades at about 16 times operating cash flow, based on the Oct. 29 close. That's near the lowest multiple Amazon stock has traded since the 2008 bear market -- a discount for a business whose cash flow is still growing rapidly and has room to expand.

The risk is heavy spending. Amazon expects capital expenditures of about $220 billion in 2026, mostly for Amazon Web Services, and management expects to spend "meaningfully more" in 2027. High capital spending has pushed free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital expenditures) negative over the past year.

If those investments don't earn strong returns, free cash flow could stay low and weigh on the stock. Still, with retail becoming a second profit engine and the stock trading at a discount, Amazon shares could deliver excellent returns over the next 10 years.

What's the meaning of the "C" on your USB-C ports?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For a cable that is considered a "universal connector," the trusty USB comes in a staggering array of different shapes and colors. You may find yourself in the aisle in the electronics store or scrolling Amazon, staring at the different options and wondering what makes each of them different from the other and which one is the correct one for your needs.

In the past few years, we have seen more and more devices finally offer just one clear choice: USB Type-C. It comes in one shape, has no color coding and promises to work with any USB Type-C device. But what does the "C" actually stand for? Does it have a secret meaning? Well, the answer is a lot more straightforward than one might think.

If you go searching online for the answer to that question, you might come across several theories that the internet loves to repeat over and over. Those are all myths and are easily explained away.

Explainer sites keep running into the same three guesses. Some people assume the C stands for "connectivity," since the new connector works with such a wide range of devices. Others figure it means "charging," because that's what most of us use it for. And a third camp thinks it refers to the rounded shape of the plug. None of them are right. The "C" only names the connector, and it does not stand for any word beginning with the letter C.

The myths don't end with the name. There are even more about what a USB-C port can do. Plenty of people assume it means fast, or that it's the same thing as Thunderbolt, or that every USB-C port will fast charge a laptop and drive a monitor. But they don't all have the same capabilities. The connector is just the shape, and what comes through it depends on the device and the cable, which is why a USB-C port isn't a guarantee of high speeds.

The real answer is a lot less exciting. USB Type-C is just the connector that comes after Type-A and Type-B.

USB 1.0 arrived in January 1996 with only those two. They weren't interchangeable on purpose. Type-A, the flat rectangle, went into the host, meaning the computer or a power source. Type-B, the chunky square one, went into the device, meaning the printer or scanner on the other end. USB was built around that one-way idea, with the host supplying power and running the show and the peripheral going along with it. Different shapes meant you couldn't plug a cable in the wrong way.

Then the different shapes and colors started piling up. USB 2.0 in 2000 brought the mini connectors, and micro followed because phones and cameras wanted something thinner. The colors were a loose convention for telling ports apart: black for USB 2.0, blue for USB 3.0 and red or yellow for ports that keep supplying power while the computer sleeps (even orange ports don't mean the same thing everywhere). Manufacturers were free to ignore all of it, and plenty did. Most of them were B-style ends on the device side, so you still had a Type-A on one end and some flavor of Type-B on the other.

USB Type-C was finalized in August 2014, and it addressed nearly every complaint people had with the older versions. It was thin, so it fit even the thinnest phones and gadgets. It was reversible, so gone were the days of guessing which way to plug it in. And it dropped the idea of two different shapes, so the host and device split moved out of the connector and into the devices themselves.

Since the shape stays the same, there is no need for color coding either. The two ends talk to each other and agree on how much data and power to send, and newer cables simply move more through the same connector. USB4 Version 2.0 now reaches up to 80Gbps over that same Type-C connector.

USB Type-C was received positively by consumers and tech companies thanks to its speed, small footprint and versatility. When it first came out, people mostly praised the reversible connection, but it's still not a perfect connector. It has a delicate, thin plastic tab (the tongue) inside the socket that holds the pins, and if it bends or snaps, the port is ruined. It's also still a little finicky to plug in.

At the moment, there are no clear plans for a Type-D connector, but if we were picking, we'd go for a simple ball-shaped plug that attaches to its socket magnetically.
