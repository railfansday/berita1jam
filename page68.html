He Was Teammates with Harry Kane and Luka Modric - Now He's Convicted of Fare Dodging

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

A former teammate of Harry Kane and Luka Modric has this week been convicted of fare dodging in London, with his career taking a very different turn from that of his two Ballon d’Or-winning counterparts.

The news comes in the same week that Kane became the first Englishman in 25 years to win football’s most prestigious individual award, following in the footsteps of Michael Owen, who claimed the famous Golden Ball in 2001.

Kane’s journey began in humble surroundings, with the striker turning out for the likes of Fleetwood Town before climbing the footballing ladder at Premier League side Tottenham and, later, Champions League contenders Bayern Munich.

That rollercoaster ride through the football pyramid has seen him share the pitch with all sorts of characters from different backgrounds. One former teammate has now been found guilty of a rather unexpected offence - dodging a £7 train fare, despite having played a sport where lucrative contracts are not rare.

According to the Wimbledon Guardian, Sebastien Bassong, now 41, was caught following a friend through the ticket barriers at St Paul’s Station on May 2.

A subsequent investigation by a Transport for London (TfL) officer revealed that Bassong had been using a train driver’s nominee pass that did not belong to him.

In a statement to the court, TfL revenue control officer Adebukola Anifowose said she spotted Bassong at 1.08pm “pretend to tap an Oystercard enclosed in a white wallet before tailgating through the wide-aisle gate behind another male”.

Bassong appeared at Wimbledon Magistrates’ Court on Friday, October 30, where he was convicted of travelling without a valid ticket. He was fined £220 and ordered to pay the £7 fare, £250 in prosecution costs and an £88 victim surcharge, bringing the total to £565.

The friend who accompanied him later told the TfL officer that he had given Bassong the pass because he was “experiencing financial and personal hardship due to a divorce and unemployment”.

Bassong's career never really took off after he left Tottenham following just three seasons in north London. His only notable success thereafter came at Norwich City, where he made 123 appearances, won the club's Player of the Year award in 2013, and helped the Canaries secure promotion from the Championship via the playoffs in 2015.

He then spent a season at Peterborough United but managed just one appearance in the EFL Trophy as he continued to struggle with a recurring knee injury.

The same problem prevented him from making any lasting impact at Volos, where he made three appearances in the Greek Super League during the 2019/20 season before announcing his retirement. Before that decision, during lockdown, he featured in a pre-season friendly for Isthmian League side Haringey Borough.

These days, Bassong works as a strategic performance and leadership advisor, motivational coach and public speaker. He has also taken part in charity boxing events, including one organised by Pro Project Promotions, where he knocked out former Arsenal defender Armand Traore.

Here's How Much AAA Says It Costs To Own A Car In 2026

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When we consider whether we can afford a car, so much of the focus tends to be on the sticker price that we often forget that the costs don't stop once we've made our purchase. Small, everyday expenses that come alongside, like fuel, servicing, finance interest, depreciation, insurance, and tax, aren't always obvious, but the true cost of car ownership adds up over time.

With that in mind, the car valuation experts at AAA performed a study to investigate car ownership costs in 2026. It turns out that drivers are facing ongoing price increases, with the AAA 2026 Your Driving Costs study revealing that the annual cost of owning and running a new car has soared to $12,863, or $1,071.92 per month this year. That's up from an average of $11,577 or $964.78 per month in 2025.

At that rate, ownership costs can add 32.67% on top of the $39,376 sales-weighted average MSRP of new vehicles. The larger bills have been linked to surging fuel prices, faster depreciation, and higher finance interest rates. Still, your specific costs can vary greatly, depending on your car's fuel type.

Owning any car at all is getting more expensive, but the AAA study revealed something more fundamental: Electric cars cost more to own than their traditional fuel-powered and hybrid counterparts. That's largely because depreciation on EVs is worse than gas or hybrid cars. On top of that, those who drive electric cars face higher fees and finance costs. Where EVs tend to do better is in fuel expenses, as they typically cost 66% to 70% less to run than gasoline cars.

Looking at a set of average consumption figures, AAA found that regular-grade gasoline costs $4.152 per gallon on average, while electric cars charged at home averaged as little as 18 cents per kilowatt hour. EVs have seen smaller electricity rate increases, too. The AAA data shows electricity prices have jumped 7.8% since 2025, while gas costs have increased a shocking 31.8%.

Even so, owning and running EVs costs more than gas-powered models, due to higher fees, finance costs, and depreciation, with certain EVs said to depreciate about twice as fast as their gas-powered counterparts. That contributed to pushing EV ownership costs above gas-powered models across the medium sedan, compact SUV, and pickup truck segments.

Nowhere is that gap more pronounced than in the medium sedan segment, where gas-powered medium sedans cost $3,080 less to own (at $10,582 per year) than comparable EV medium sedans ($13,662). Hybrids are even cheaper (at $10,204) and cost the least to own in every category, including the medium SUV class, where EVs win back some ground against gas-powered models, costing $14,332 versus $14,431 for gasoline SUVs.

As a car owner, every mile you drive costs money, but it is entirely possible to keep costs down. One way to do this is to shop around for cheaper insurance. Compare quotes from providers, or even consider buying a car from one of the cheapest brands to insure.

If you are team gas-powered cars, getting a model with great fuel efficiency is just as important. It should be easy enough, seeing as gasoline cars are now much more efficient than they used to be, with some of the most fuel-efficient models released over the years rated to return as high as 58 mpg.

You'll also want to consider depreciation. It is one of the single largest ownership costs and can add a weighted average of $4,422 per year to the cost of running some popular models, according to the AAA study. Toyota, Subaru, and Honda are some of the best brands for value retention, but if you're after something more premium, there's always Lexus. It's the luxury car brand with the best resale value, according to CarEdge.

However, maintenance and spare parts are usually more expensive for luxury marques than mainstream brands like Toyota or Honda. Speaking of which, staying on top maintenance is yet another important part of vehicle ownership that can help keep costs low. Not only does it help prevent larger bills down the road, keeping up with car maintenance also provides a strong service history that supports resale value when it comes time to sell.

Extraction shooters need a PvE mode for casual players—so what’s the controversy?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This idea isn't half as controversial as half of you make it out to be.

Any major extraction shooter release inevitably summons the age-old debate of PvE vs. PvP. It’s been the case with ARC Raiders and DMZ, and that schism will resurface whenever the next one launches. You can always count on heated arguments stirring controversy about something that shouldn’t be controversial at all.

After all, how dare players enjoy PvE in a PvPvE game? The way the debate often goes, you’d assume it was a cardinal sin, like the PvE crowd was planning to personally tar and feather every PvP player in existence.

Instead, PvE players often just want a more forgiving, relaxed experience that does not involve getting ratted to death in Stella Montis or having to spend an entire match in Hajin with one’s butthole clenched so tightly that not even a Wi-Fi signal passes through it. And from the looks of it, this isn’t a small crowd, either.

Take ARC Raiders, for example. The demand for a PvE mode was so large that Embark tested a toggle that disabled damage between players last month. Before then, 37 percent of its fanbase (and growing) were focused on PvE cooperation, 34 were there purely for PvP (stable since launch), and 27 landed in the middle, Embark said. Over 51 percent of all surveyed users showed interest in a dedicated PvE mode, according to the studio.

If even the thought of discussing this makes you feel like Ben Affleck with a cigarette, you’re not the only one who’s exhausted. The PvE vs. PvP argument is the reheated leftovers from the skill-based matchmaking debate, annoyingly inescapable when battle royales were all the rage.

Often, the higher-skill players don’t want to match against others of similar ability because their games get too difficult and stop being fun. And yes, this is exactly what happens to lower-skill players without SBMM.

Activision ran a famous experiment with its matchmaking in Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 3 in 2024. The company loosened the skill constraints for half of its players during a month. At the end of the test, it noted that the 10 percent of affected players at the top of the skill pyramid returned more often, but the remaining 90 percent were considerably less likely to come back. And that’s with a diluted SBMM.

“If [SBMM] were completely removed, we would expect to see the player population erode rapidly in the span of a few months, resulting in a negative outcome for all our players,” the study said.

Read what’s behind the latest news in gaming (with a bit of snark) by subscribing to the Destructoid Checkpoint newsletter!

PvP is zero-sum by definition: for one to win, another must lose. Unsurprisingly, it doesn’t feel good to be on the losing side. Having the chance to opt out of that and into a different playstyle altogether shouldn’t cause this much uproar—especially not in a game where keeping gear plays a major role. And often, it doesn’t even relate exclusively to skill, but the type of experience one wants to have.

This time, it’s not even a matter of catering to one part of the audience in detriment of another, which would be an understandable concern if it made a modicum of sense. Embark isn’t going to make your fingers fall off every time you shoot another player. Having PvE in DMZ won’t make Activision cease content for Warzone or multiplayer, otherwise CoD Zombies wouldn’t exist.

Often, the people contemplated by PvE mode aren’t even in the game, but they’re yearning to be. The lack of full-on PvE in DMZ didn’t make me flock to multiplayer; it made me uninstall the game altogether. I only bought ARC Raiders because of the PvE test and the game’s aggression-based matchmaking. If that went away, I’d disappear with it. My friends did so the second the PvE test ended, and they’re eagerly waiting for the mode to return.

Extraction shooters being unapproachable made sense when they were niche. Now that every live-service seems to flirt with the genre, though, they should be more welcoming, especially if they want to draw a larger crowd.

As an avid frequenter of friendly lobbies in ARC Raiders, I’ve had more positive experiences with randoms than I can count, and that’s a big reason why I open the game. I’m having one hell of a time fighting Matriarchs, defibbing other raiders, and dropping duplicate blueprints. My archenemy was a cat bed, and I’m thrilled this was the case. It sucks when it’s tooth-and-nail all the time, as every big opponent of SBMM will tell you.

The difference is, unlike hero shooters or battle royales, extraction shooters already have a PvE element baked into them, no retrofitting required. Of course, you may have to reshuffle some things, like increasing the difficulty of the AI-controlled enemies to raise the threat and keep players on their toes. But it’s different from creating something entirely new, like if people wanted bots in Apex Legends or full-blooded PvP in the extraction-adjacent Helldivers 2. And it’s certainly not too much to ask.

There’s a reason extraction shooters were somewhat obscure before the gaming industry threw a borderline infinite amount of money at making the genre mainstream. Soulslikes aside, not everyone wants to get their teeth kicked in as a pastime. Sure, the cutthroat uncertainty from PvPvE shaped the identity of ARC Raiders, but there are ways to evoke that without fighting other players, as Helldivers 2 and GTFO have proved.

ARC Raiders has come a long way in the past year, in no small part because of the 37 percent of PvE players (and in bigger part because of the 63 percent of other raiders). Maybe the burgeoning PvE scene changes the vision of the game somewhat, and that’s fine. DMZ getting a fully PvE mode wouldn’t be entirely out of the realm of possibility, considering that’s basically what MWZ was. That’s the beauty and bane of live services: there’s always room for reinvention.

36 Years Later, The Best Pennywise Line In IT Is Still The Creepiest In Horror TV History

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Although Stephen King has written plenty of terrifying villains in his time, one standout quote from 1990’s IT miniseries has the unique distinction of remaining the single scariest line in the history of the medium. There are some heavy-hitting competitors when it comes crowning the creepiest single line of all time on TV. Littlefinger’s Game of Thrones speech about chaos is chilling, but hardly outright scary so much as it is enthralling.

Meanwhile, the creepiest line in Twin Peaks is unbelievably tragic in context, but it is delivered with a plaintive sadness entirely at odds with the panic-attack-inducing terror of what’s happening onscreen. This disconnect is typical of David Lynch and Mark Frost’s inimitable murde mystery masterpiece Twin Peaks, but it also means that the scariest line in the show is only scary in context.

In contrast, the true scariest line in TV history is one that can still send chills down the spine of viewers familiar with the series despite how comparatively tame the show itself is. 1990’s IT miniseries had to cut a lot out of Stephen King’s 1986 novel of the same name, both to conform to network TV censorship standards and to fit within a reasonable runtime. However, the show found room for Tim Curry to deliver the definitive version of Pennywise’s promise, and it is one that still haunts nightmares worldwide decades later.

In the very first scene of 1990's miniseries version of IT, as poor doomed Georgie attempts to retrieve his little paper boat from a flooded storm drain, Curry’s grinning clown welcomes him with a cryptic promise. He reassures George that “They float, Georgie... They float... and when you're down here with me... You’ll float too!” The line is delivered not with genuine malice, but rather a jarringly friendly glee that would fit a kid’s TV host.

The late, great Curry was a master of delivery, and his version of Pennywise arguably outdoes even the far gorier HBOMax prequel Welcome to Derry’s killer clown as he often seems like an actual real-life clown. A comedic veteran, Curry brings the same panache and charisma seen in everything from The Rocky Horror Picture Show to Muppet Treasure Island to the role of Pennywise and, unlike Bill Skarsgard’s more feral, animalistic Pennywise, he plays the clown as an actual children’s entertainer.

The result of this risky choice is a number of scenes, including the infamous "Prince Albert in a can" gag, that are simply genuinely funny instead of being scary. However, rather than ruining the character’s mystique, the comedy of these scenes only serves to make the moments where Pennywise becomes an inhuman monster all the more terrifying.

It is a delicate tonal tightrope, one that other King adaptations like versions of Carrie often have to tread when depicting characters who are both scary and potentially ludicrous, like Margaret White. However, Curry manages to not only nail the balance between Pennywise’s clowning and his evil, but also to encapsulate both contrasting versions of the character in this single line.

When Pennywise begins to tell Georgie about how they float down in his underground lair, he seems genuinely happy to be sharing a secret with the kid, and Curry’s giddy enthusiasm makes Georgie’s unfazed response feel believable. As his giddiness gives way to gleeful, deranged sadism, it feels horribly believable that Georgie might not notice what is so worryingly off about this clown until it is too late.

Over the years, later Stephen King shows and the increased prevalence of killer clowns in movies and TV have led new viewers to complain that no kid would go anywhere near Curry’s obviously evil monster in a million years. However, rewatching It’s opening scene without knowing where the story is going, what makes its most famous line so scary is how genuinely friendly the Stephen King villain seems to his utterly innocent, sheltered prey.

‘Avedon’ Review: Ron Howard’s Thrilling Documentary About Richard Avedon, the Great Photographer Who Turned Head-On Portraits Into an Art Form

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Avedon was a paragon of high fashion and also the ultimate democratic humanist. The film explains that paradox and makes his images sing.

“Avedon,” Ron Howard’s documentary about the legendary photographer, is a fantastic movie. It’s the best documentary Ron Howard has made (my favorite before this: “The Beatles: Eight Days a Week”), and it’s been edited in a way that’s so supple and artful that its technique should be studied in filmmaking classes. (The film’s editor is Andrew Morreale.) Woven through the movie are hundreds of photographs that were shot by Richard Avedon, and there’s no question that the images are stunning, but the way the movie layers them into your consciousness is nearly musical in its finesse. And that helps to tell the very large story “Avedon” is telling: how Richard Avedon changed the metaphysics of photography, shifting the way we see the world. Related Stories Paramount Network Orders 13 Original Episodes of Larry the Cable Guy-Hosted Clip Show 'American Mayhem' (EXCLUSIVE)

There’s a paradox running down the center of Avedon’s work that feels as epic and tingly as the 20th century, and that is this: He was a paragon of high fashion — the world of glamour and impossible beauty — but he was also, as a maker of images, the ultimate democratic humanist. The beauty of “Avedon” is that it explains that paradox and makes those images sing. Howard interpolates old clips and interviews, placing Avedon, with his sleek command, at the center of the movie. He also talks to models and critics and media and art-world luminaries who have a great deal of import to say. They include Tina Brown, Larry Gagosian, John Lahr, Twiggy, Adam Gopnik, Hilton Als, Calvin Klein, and Avedon’s lovingly insightful son, John Avedon. James Martin, the executive director of the Richard Avedon Foundation, takes us deep into the labyrinth of Avedon’s archives, where we see the work that resulted from the 16,000 sittings he did from 1944 until his death in 2004. That’s a long time, but it still averages out to one sitting per day, Monday through Friday, for 60 years. The movie also tells the tragic story of his mentally ill sister, whom he revered, and traces his troubled marriages.

“Avedon” opens with an old clip of Richard Avedon, fixing that piercing bird-eye look of his into the camera as he explains, “I can say very little about photography. I follow no rules, no laws, and no advice.” He’s totally serious about that. We then get a montage, set to the funky martial beat of Spoon’s “I Turn My Camera On,” of Avedon portraits of the famous. John Lennon. Orson Welles. Michael Jordan. Elizabeth Taylor. Groucho Marx. Twiggy (in mid-air). Anjelica Huston. Marilyn Monroe lovingly embracing Arthur Miller. Truman Capote. J. Robert Oppenheimer.

All the images look timeless. (Avedon’s lighting makes it look as if each one was taken yesterday.) Every subject looks like what we imagine them looking like, and also different from how you’ve ever seen them. More direct and unfiltered, more intimately themselves. That’s the Avedon touch, but the magic element is that we can’t see how he achieved it. The technique is invisible. There’s a part of one that thinks, “Okay, just set up a camera and put some black-and-white film in it. Place a famous person in front of it. Encourage them to relax and ‘be themselves.’ And voilà! I could be Richard Avedon.”

He probably would have said you’re not wrong.

He wasn’t very interested in the technique of photography. Talk to him about f-stops and he would glaze over. His technique was in the way he approached a session. Iin the film, Isabella Rossellini says that other photographers tend to shoot and shoot and shoot, but Avedon would shoot an image every so often, then tell you to think of something, then shoot again, and end up with a total of maybe 10 shots. He himself explains that if you’ve booked someone for an hour, you don’t have to shoot for an hour. He says that he wastes half an hour having coffee, and then, maybe five minutes before the hour is over, he and the subject will go over to the white paper background, and the magic happens.

The white paper! It has its source in a remarkable story. Avedon was born in New York in 1923, and when he was a kid he says that walking down a street he would blink for a moment and keep his eyes closed, to keep what he saw alive in his head. It was his way of taking pictures before he’d even gotten a camera. He entered the U.S. Merchant Marines when he was 19, securing a place in the photographic department, where he took maybe 100,000 portraits of enlisted men and other workers, all to use for ID cards.

This was the photographer’s equivalent of peeling potatoes on KP duty. But Avedon began to notice something. Shooting these military mug shots against a white background, he encountered “thousands of baffled faces looking into the camera,” and he saw how the photographs revealed these people. That’s one of the startling things about police mug shots. (John Waters: “Everybody looks better under arrest.”) We see a handful of the ID photos that Avedon took, and there it is: the Avedon style. He saw that each face was different, and needed nothing else to be different. So he took what fate handed him — a thankless military assignment — and turned it into a vision. The white background equalized everything. It became his way of making each individual stand out as an individual, with literally nothing to get in the way.

He became a head-on existential classicist, one who echoed Diane Arbus’s eye for highlighting the aspect of a person that was somehow off. The famous Avedon shot of the model Lauren Hutton with her head thrown back and her breast popping out looks as perfectly composed as a Picasso sculpture. But it was totally spontaneous; it just happened. And that’s true of all the famous and iconic gazes Avedon’s portrait subjects gave him. Those looks weren’t planned (how could they have been?).

At the tail-end of a session in 1953, Charlie Chaplin pranked the camera by holding devil horns over his grinning face, and that became one of the most famous images Avedon ever shot. Images like that happened…and then became frozen in the hive mind’s eye. But as “Avedon” explains, part of what he was always recording was the ineffable dance between subject and artist, using each subject to express something about himself. (Chaplin wouldn’t have made those devil horns for anyone else.)

By the time of the 1957 Hollywood musical “Funny Face,” which paired Audrey Hepburn with Fred Astaire as a magazine photographer pointedly based on Avedon, he was already becoming a legend, and he began to use his power as part of his method as an artist. In his 1957 shoot with Marilyn Monroe, pegged to “The Prince and the Showgirl,” the trick was: How could you photograph Marilyn — or, rather, how could you photograph her the way Avedon wanted to photograph her — when she had come prepared, more than anyone else alive, to be photographed? The shoot took an entire day, and it’s full of joyous photographs of Marilyn in full splendor, but the iconic shot that emerged from it was taken at the end of the day, when she let down her guard for 10 seconds, and kind of drooped, and looked a bit sad, and we glimpse, in that photograph, the real Marilyn — not necessarily the forlorn Marilyn, but the full woman behind the image. You see Norma Jean grown up.

“Avedon” is full of great stories and privileged glimpses into how Avedon created some of his most famous images. And, of course, he also photographed the unfamous, like the Oklahoma oil workers he shot in 1980. He used his impish nature to coax fashion models into leaping through the air for his models-in-motion photographs. He waited around for seven weeks in Vietnam to photograph the war’s American generals, and devised singular ways to shoot the Chicago 7 and the denizens of the Warhol Factory and the residents of the new American West, in a series that was so raw and authentic no museum in New York wanted anything to do with it. (He was that far ahead of the curve,) We also hear the story of how he photographed his aging father and used that process to reconcile with him. Avedon supported his own ritzy lifestyle by doing advertising work (including the Obsession perfume TV spots that were like surrealist mini-films), but for him it was never a compromise. And we see how that could be the case. There is, over 60 years, a breathtaking continuity between his portrait work and his fashion work and his commercial work that makes it all inseparable. That’s because every photograph Richard Avedon ever took was striving for the same thing: to open your eyes.

Why Singapore Airlines Uses Boeing 787-10s On Short Regional Routes

Boeing built the 787-10 Dreamliner as the largest Dreamliner variant with the shortest range. That makes it well suited to high-frequency, high-demand medium-length routes. Yet, with a maximum range of 7,500 nautical miles (13,890 km), it is still capable of traditional long-haul missions. Singapore Airlines takes its 787-10s in a different direction, using them to fly bizarrely short routes, typically the domain of narrowbody jets.

Singapore Airlines uses its Dreamliner in an unexpected way for several reasons. The 787-10 is a modern jet that replaced the carrier's older widebody aircraft. It has a high-density configuration ideal for ferrying a large number of passengers. It is also fuel-efficient and versatile, making it cost-effective on a range of routes. The answer is also in Singapore Airlines' current fleet, which lacks capacity for short-haul routes with other jets.

Cirium data reveals that Singapore Airlines will fly the 787-10 on 26 routes throughout November. This is for a total of 1,129 operations and over 380,000 seats, with each jet having 337 seats available onboard. The majority of these 26 routes are regional, coming in at less than a third of the 787-10's range.

These routes have impressive frequency, showing that Singapore Airlines uses its Dreamliners to move many people over short distances. The fifth-shortest route in November is the carrier's most frequent route that uses the Dreamliner, with 120 monthly frequencies from SIN to MNL. This route is 1,474 miles long and produces almost 60 million available seat miles. That is more than all but two of the routes.

Another exceptionally frequent route included is the third-shortest, taking passengers 876 miles (1,410 km) between SIN and BKK. The airline will complete this route 106 times in November, offering 35,722 seats. This produces over 31 million available seat miles. Meanwhile, it will run daily operations on the shortest route, traveling just 675 miles from SIN to SGN. The over 10,000 seats produce 6.8 million available seat miles.

The 787-10's position within the Singapore Airlines' fleet shows sound logic behind this usage. The carrier lacks narrowbody options that would traditionally operate these short services. Its only single-aisle aircraft are its 26 Boeing 737 MAX 8 jets. These planes have a total capacity of 154 seats split between economy class and business class. This is less than half the 337-passenger capacity of Singapore Airlines' 787-10s. This means the airline's 737 fleet has a total capacity of 4,004 passengers, compared with 9,436 for its Dreamliners. The 787-10 can simply take more of a chunk out of the high-capacity needs within a region packed with huge cities.

Elsewhere in Singapore Airlines' widebody fleet, Boeing problems are causing other issues. Most notably, the carrier has an outstanding order for 31 Boeing 777X jets, placed in June 2017 and February 2021. It has no options for additional purchases. This aircraft family has been long-delayed by certification issues, although Boeing insists that certification and deliveries will finally come in 2027. Given the aircraft's huge size, its arrival should further cement the need for Singapore Airlines to use its Dreamliners on short, high-capacity routes.

The hardest thing in this world is to live in it.

The two share the same blood, so Buffy is capable of closing the portal on her own. She jumps in and dies. It’s a moment that closes out an arc that had been teased since season 1.

Season 5 is one of Buffy’s most emotional seasons. It’s also one of its boldest, committing to a new central dynamic: Buffy and Dawn. Maligned series creator Joss Whedon shared that he introduced Dawn as a way of giving Buffy an intense dynamic that wasn’t based in romance. “The Gift” takes its title from a line in “Intervention,” where Buffy goes on a vision quest to meet the First Slayer, a prehistoric girl who tells her: “Death is your gift.” The line reinforces one of Buffy’s biggest fears: that she’s a killing machine, closer to the vampires and demons she hunts than to the human world she’s so desperate to belong to.
