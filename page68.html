Sometimes, you come across a game and instantly know it's for the sickos. To be honest I could have just called it a CRPG and still labelled it as a game for sickos, yet everything that's going on here is obviously just doubling down on the sick factor.

Ablahshikar is a particular flavour of maximalism. Having not played it, it feels a bit impossible describe, I mean, just have a read of some of the game's Steam page: "Lo, destiny crooks a finger only to those whose eyes peer beyond þe next tempest. Take custody of a faltering war-barge that wanders the outer brine of Dastgerd. Thy wrists y-braced to a wan terminal of bone-whyte and tarnysshed latoun, þou steerest Ablahshikar by murke point-nettes and sputteryng tables." I don't know what any of that means! And I love it. Reminds me of Hylics in that the game's world is not translating itself for its audience; you take what you're given, and you figure it out yourself.

Judging from trailers and screenshots, there's a mix of activities to be getting on with. Occasionally you'll be piloting a strange, not alien, but definitely ancient ship of some kind in an arcadey sort of fashion. Then occasionally there's something that resembles Caves of Qud, i.e. some odd, 2D maybe dungeon crawling where your moves are dictated by individual keys on your keyboard. Truthfully, I do not know if I am sicko enough for Ablahshikar. Its mighty vision is terrifying, but I certainly am curious about it. We are all about to get a very loud wake-up call from T-Mobile.

The telecommunications provider, one of the biggest and most colorful advertisers in the U.S., has dispatched actor Bryan Cranston in a new campaign that may surprise those who have grown familiar with its playful spots featuring celebrities such as Billy Bob Thornton, John Travolta, Zach Braff and Donald Faison. In the new spot, Cranston and a rooster named George aim to serve as an alarm clock of sorts, alerting consumers to “T-Mix,” a new wireless plan that lets consumers pick only the features they want ,and comes with perks. “Come on sleepyheads, it’s up and at ‘em!” Cranston crows in the ad. “Because there’s been a change in plan!” Meanwhile, he sets about rousing people from hotel rooms, airplane seats and bedrooms.

In the commercial, “I represent all the people who need to hear this information,” says Cranston in a recent interview. “Because I was one of them until moments ago.”

The 60-second ad debuts during Amazon Prime Video’s “ Thursday Night Football” and will roll out across national broadcast, streaming, cinema, digital, social media platforms, as well as on radio and out-of-home, says Lucy McLellan, executive vice president and chief brand and communications officer at T-Mobile. And while T-Mobile is known for launching big campaigns, the company will also use digital to launch some bespoke creative that targets specific consumer niches.

The new work, created with a new agency of record, Highdive, will attempt “to reach new audiences who have tuned out the category completely,” says McLellan, and make an effort to “appeal to audiences beyond the millennials that T-Mobile has traditionally appealed to.”

Other telecommunications giants are also trying to reach out to bigger crowds. Verizon in June tapped actor Michael Meyers to reprise his “Austin Powers” character, and reconvene that film’s cast to tout a new “Simplicity Plan” that offered new promotions including a return on dollars spent as well as prizes and rewards.

Creating the high-energy pitchman wasn’t done in seconds. Cranston says the entire shoot took two days and that “we did each of those scenes several times from several different vantage points.” Telling a story via commercial is much more difficult than doing so in a TV series or movie, the actor says. “Every second is critical, whereas in a two-hour movie, well, you could let things linger,” he adds. “But this has to be cut to a millisecond in order to make the time requirements.”

Known best for roles like Walter White in “Breaking Bad,” Hal in “Malcom in the Middle” and Tim Whatley in “Seinfeld,” Cranston says he has long done commercials, which were “my life source” when he first started his acting career. Cranston has tackled ad assignments for CoffeeMate, for example, and is heard frequently as the voice of commercials for Ford Motor Co.

The character he plays for T-Mobile is “almost a Pied Piper,” he says. “I think my character was just another person who happened to have this epiphany that, wow, this is amazing. I can create my own plan. I don’t have to adhere to what was done, you know, decade after decade. And I can do this on my own,” he explains “I think that allowed me to be able to present it in an effusive way, in an energized way, without it being silly.”

George, the rooster who accompanies the actor throughout much of the ad, also maintained his demeanor. Relationships with animals — and, often, people too, says Cranston, are often “governed by food,” and the same was true of his fowl actor. “There was a little bowl of food on a stick that I can rattle, and here’s the rattle, and he would turn close to me. I was holding it, and I would hold it close, and then as soon as he turns toward me, I drop it out of frame. So the camera wouldn’t see it, and I tried to get closer and closer and closer to him, you know, just put my face right into his beak, you know, and have a stare down. And, you know, all you need is a couple seconds of that.”

And in a commercial, every second counts.

‘The Wizard of Oz’ Was a Big Reference for Doc ‘A Want in Her.’ Now Its Creator Is Moving Into Fiction.

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Irish artist-turned-filmmaker Myrid Carten, who won three BIFAs for her feature debut and made the U.K. BAFTA Breakthrough list, talks to THR about her journey so far – and what lies ahead.

About 11 months ago, Myrid Carten’s debut feature film, the documentary A Want in Her, an exploration of intergenerational trauma, mental health and addiction, was honored with three British Independent Film Awards (BIFAs), namely for best feature documentary, the best debut director – feature documentary, and The Raindance Maverick Award.

On Wednesday, the Irish artist-turned-filmmaker was revealed as a member of the 2026 U.K. BAFTA Breakthrough cohort, supported by Netflix.

A Want in Her was written and directed by Carten, who also appears in the doc and handled cinematography. It follows Carten and her mother, Nuala, who goes missing somewhere in Ireland. The creative’s search for her takes her into a family feud, a house dispute and a history that threatens to take everyone down, including the filmmaker herself. The doc had its world premiere at the 2024 International Documentary Film Festival Amsterdam (IDFA) in Amsterdam. Related Stories Movies 'La Bola Negra' Ups Planned Theater Count Heading Into Netflix Film's Widest Weekend Yet (Exclusive) Movies AFM Hot Project: Jacob Tremblay, Kate McKinnon Join 'I'm With Stupid' (Exclusive)

On the occasion of her inclusion on the U.K. BAFTA Breakthrough list, Carten talked to THR about her journey behind A Want in Her, just how vulnerable she made herself in the process and what’s next for her.

What led you to take on such a challenging film project, for which you made yourself so vulnerable?

I was led very much by impulse, which comes from my artistic background. I studied fine art, and there, if you have an idea, follow it. Don’t think too much about outcomes! And then, as time went on and the film started to grow, I developed what I call my north star. I felt this adult-child story, the story of the children of people who suffer from mental illness and addiction, isn’t really told that often. So I felt compelled to do it for that particular cohort.

But even in the development process, everybody I met was like, “Oh, I have experience with that.” There’s a statistic that every addict touches 10 other people’s lives. I felt the addict story is often the one that’s told because it’s dramatic with a capital D, but this story felt really important.

On the vulnerability issue, I just really focused on making a film that worked. With myself as a character, it actually felt in some ways that the vulnerability served the film.

I was never thinking about BAFTA or BIFAs when I was in the middle of this old, derelict house and all this drama. My dream was just that the film would be seen in some cinemas. That was the height of my aspiration, and it’s gone beyond that. It’s done better than I thought it would do, especially because it’s quite an experimental film. So the fact that audiences have really taken to it has been a great boost of confidence. People are very smart. I think we often don’t acknowledge how much audiences get things, even if they’re not spelled out for them.

What is your hope for BAFTA Breakthrough?

BAFTA are the best of British and arguably world cinema. I was nominated for this outstanding debut category, and there tends to be one doc in there. But I really want to make a fiction film.

That’s always been my desire. I tried to use as many dramaturgy elements in A Want in Her as I could. I think it’s going to be an interesting move into fiction and blurring that space.

It is just amazing with that transition into fiction because I feel BAFTA is really a bed for lots of producers, directors, creative talent who are all drawn to BAFTA. So the idea is that I am going to create meetings.

What BAFTA Breakthrough does is it puts you in the role of a mentee, and you can ask people for meetings in more of a mentor capacity as opposed to meetings that are all about collaborating and business. I’m really interested in talking to people who have done debut fiction films that I think are extraordinary and chatting to them about how they did it, not just navigate the industry, but maintain artistic integrity.

I’m also really looking forward to having the BAFTA [View streaming] player and watching all the films. When you have a toddler, you can’t go to the cinema that much. So this is perfect for me.

Do you already have any ideas for a fiction film that you want to start developing?

I have one idea for a doc hybrid that I’ve put on the back shelf, and I’m developing two ideas for fiction films. I am trying to think about how to bring that sense of reality and rawness to this form that is artificial or has artifice in it. Doing screenplays is a whole new process. My story editor always says you learn about making a film by making a film.

Is there anything else you’d like to share?

I’d love to have a career like Jonathan Glazer, in that each of his films is so different. But the person I’d really love to meet through BAFTA Breakthrough would be someone like Lynne Ramsay.

But I have my own path, and actually, that’s what really worked in A Want in Her. I wasn’t trying to make a film like anybody else. I have to really hold onto that.

And the big reference for A Want in Her was The Wizard of Oz, which most people wouldn’t even know. Wait, what!? Now I am curious to hear more…

At the start, we called it No Place Like Home. That was the working title for years, and we would shoot the house as though it was Technicolor. It was this idea of this dream, this fantasy of the house and trying to return to that. So, [The Wizard of Oz] was one of the early references, but it’s so buried that people wouldn’t even recognize that. I like when things are kind of subliminal.

According to social media, this week’s midterms are ushering in a new era: Woke 3.0.

For the uninitiated, the internet has broken down the dynamics of American politics in the 2020s into a few distinct eras. There was Woke 1.0 at the start of the decade, associated with far-left social movements of the time. As the country leaned toward conservatism in the mid-2020s, according to the internet, Democrats responded by moving into Woke 2.0, a shift that New York City Councilman Chi Ossé defined as “more productive” and less focused on virtue signaling than Woke 1.0.

Now, after the blue tsunami, social media is convinced that a third iteration of Woke might just be upon us. The newfound Democratic majorities in both chambers of Congress have social media anticipating a big cultural swing back to the left, and it’s got plenty of ideas of what that might look like. 

Welcome to Woke 3.0

As soon as Democrats started pulling away with the midterms, concepts for America under Woke 3.0 started going viral.

That includes everything from a rejection of AI (“Under Woke 3.0 you will be publicly executed for creating an AI poster for your burger restaurant,” one user wrote) to a celebration of trans athletes (“Woke 3 we’re having trans athletes swim in the Reflecting Pool,” another user quipped). 

“WOKE 3 IS HERE,” a third user declared. “EVERYONE JUST GOT ANOTHER SET OF PRONOUNS, YOU ARE NOW LEGALLY REQUIRED TO THEY/THEM YOUR DOG.”

They even suggested we’re jumping straight to Woke One Billion: “We’re in the club sipping gluten free lean.”

But not everyone on social media was eager to “lib out.” Social media’s dreams of an ultra-progressive government depend on the politicians that are now in power, who may not be quite as eager to swing back to the far left.

One user pointed to a statement by Rep. Hakeem Jeffries (D-NY), who’s poised to become the next Speaker of the House. Jeffries told the press that he’s “ready, willing, and able to find bipartisan common ground” with Republicans, casting doubt on Congress’s willingness to embrace “Woke 3.0.”

“They’re already trying to compromise with a group that wants something diametrically opposed to them,” the user wrote. “Even with a majority they behave like controlled opposition; being feckless is all they know.”

The new millennial cringe

This summer, Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-NY) went viral for saying that “Woke 1 was crazy” on ABC News, granting the concept of different eras of “Woke” political legitimacy. AOC’s comment rubbed some people the wrong way, saying it was dismissive of the political work done in the early 2020s that continues into the modern day.

Now, AOC has sounded off on the wave of political optimism spurred by the midterms, saying it reminds her of how it felt when Barack Obama won the presidential election. In a video addressing Gen Z posted on Wednesday night, she encouraged younger Americans to embrace the “earnestness” of so-called “millennial cringe.”

“Imagine all the seats we flipped last night. That feeling, plus, on top of it, electing the first Black president ever. And on top of that, you did it the first time you ever voted,” AOC said. The now-37-year-old was just 19 when Obama was elected president in 2008.

“That hits generationally. Like, you are chasing that feeling ever since,” AOC continued. “And because you did it the first time you ever voted, you’re like, ‘Yeah, we can change the world. Why not? We did it the first time. Let’s just do it again.’”

“That’s so much of it in a nutshell,” she concluded. “Just keep at it.”

Nat Geo Explorer Bertie Gregory Deeply Cares About the Wolves He Has Followed for Seven Years

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Nat Geo Explorer Bertie Gregory Deeply Cares About the Wolves He Has Followed for Seven Years

In National Geographic’s newest television program, Wolves Up Close With Bertie Gregory, the eponymous British wildlife filmmaker, acclaimed photographer, and ardent environmentalist heads to Hudson Bay, Canada, to film wolves in their harsh Arctic habitat. Gregory chatted with PetaPixel about filming the new documentary.

While the focus of the new documentary is clearly on wolves, a specific wolf pack in particular that Gregory has followed for seven years, Wolves Up Close is a smorgasbord of Arctic wildlife in general. There are polar bears, red foxes, and moose, each beautifully filmed.

“One of the best parts about filming nature, and also the most annoying part, is that it doesn’t read the script,” Gregory laughs. “I always joke with the production team that we often go out with this make-believe script that we’ve written and then nature on day one is like, ‘Nope.’ And that’s the frustrating part, but on the positive part of it, it means that you never know what’s gonna happen.”

In one sequence, a pair of polar bears gets awfully close to Gregory and the rest of his small crew, which is admittedly a bit nerve-wracking for viewers. Gregory brushes off the dangers of wildlife filmmaking, thanks in large part to the local guides he worked with, including Mike Reimer and Albert “Butch” Saunders.

Gregory says he never felt nervous at all about the wolves, which likewise would saunter up to the crew to check things out, but what about the polar bears and moose?

“They’re potentially dangerous animals,” Gregory admits. “But humans are really dangerous animals. You know, statistically much more dangerous than a polar bear or a moose. Just like with humans, you’ve got to read body language to stay safe. But I work with great people, like Butch and Mike, our local guides, and they were really key to keeping everyone safe.”

Besides, Gregory might have been too busy feeling cold to feel fear.

“Any time during the episode, I was cold. I was feeling cold.”

The veteran filmmaker didn’t just experience physical closeness to some of his subjects — again, he’s a trained professional — but also emotional closeness, especially to the wolves. Bertie has followed this same pack of wolves for seven years, and over that time, he argues it’s impossible not to feel a bond.

In contrast to old-school wildlife documentary practices, which effectively demand that filmmakers remain entirely detached from the nature they experience and shoot, Gregory doesn’t fight the natural urge to feel something about his subjects.

“I mean, we’re trained not to,” he admits. “We’re told not to get attached because you can’t get involved, but I think that’s BS. I think there’s no way you can follow a pack of animals, an animal family, for seven years and not care about them.

I think if you don’t care about them, you’re pretty weird.

“I think if you don’t care about them, you’re pretty weird.”

Gregory says he isn’t actively trying to forge any bond, something he is fully aware would be a one-way street since the wolves “don’t care about us,” but he says, “I care about them.”

“There are two strategies when filming animals. Perplexity Cuts Agent API Research Costs and Adds Ultra Effort

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Perplexity announced on November 5, 2026 that every Agent API effort level now costs less per query, and reported higher or unchanged research quality across the presets. Medium improved from 55% to 77% on BrowseComp and DSQA while its cost fell almost 90%, and a new ultra level is available for broad research.

Effort levels package model choice, research-step and tool-call limits, search behavior and available tools into named presets. They let developers select a balance of cost, speed and research depth without configuring each component separately. A request such as effort="fast" receives the revised setup automatically.

At xhigh, Perplexity says the WANDR score held at 76.5% while the configuration ran at about 25% of its August 2026 cost. That level is intended for open-ended agentic work, including executing code in a sandbox, sustaining long tool-use loops and gathering evidence across many sources.

The WANDR paper describes a 500-task evaluation set built around realistic data-collection work. Agents must discover qualifying entities and return records whose sources and excerpts can be checked independently. Task-specific judges retrieve the cited pages again and assess the records against that evidence, avoiding a static gold answer set.

Ultra joins fast, low, medium, high and xhigh in the official effort documentation. Its designated role is broad research and data collection where completeness matters more than latency. The configuration uses anthropic/claude-opus-5-5, and background mode is available for long runs.

Ultra assigns the same research problem to three agents running in parallel, allowing each to assemble evidence independently. A fourth agent consolidates their outputs, removes duplicate findings and writes the answer. Perplexity reports an 85.9% WANDR score at $21.71 per task, the highest result among the configurations it tested.

Spending is constrained at each level through limits on steps, a ceiling on total tool calls and periodic notices about remaining capacity. The separate tool-call ceiling matters because one step can launch several calls across different tools. Those notices help the agent reserve enough budget to check its evidence and deliver an answer instead of stopping mid-task.

One experiment with openai/gpt-6-luna shows why those limits matter. Perplexity reports that moving from 1 to 15 permitted steps raised accuracy on BrowseComp and DSQA from 41% to 80%, while cost per query increased from $0.0017 to $0.0152. Budget allocation therefore shapes both the quality of the answer and the price of obtaining it.

Context compaction addresses another cost driver. After context passes a threshold, the system condenses earlier work into a summary of findings and remaining tasks while preserving the system prompt, original request and any loaded skills. It then tells the agent to keep working and reports the number of compactions, so the summary is not mistaken for a stopping point.

Extraction reduces what the agent carries forward. Instead of giving it an entire page, fetch_url asks a smaller model to select passages relevant to the current task and quote them verbatim for citation. At high effort, Perplexity reports approximately 50% fewer input tokens per query and an 88.0% result at $0.073 per query, compared with 83.5% at $0.094 without extraction.

Related changes target search itself. Each search can now return up to 4K tokens of results rather than 2K, a new snippet engine selects text more closely matched to the query, and Fast Search is priced at $1.00 per 1,000 requests. Together, these changes are intended to locate useful evidence with fewer searches and less irrelevant context.

Workflow selection uses a skill tree with progressive disclosure. A skill initially occupies only its name and description in context; the full instructions arrive after the model decides that the workflow fits the task. Perplexity says preloading every skill increased BrowseComp and DSQA costs by 13% and reduced WANDR scores, supporting the decision to load workflows only when needed.

An automated harness evaluates newly available models as they become accessible, sweeping reasoning effort, step and tool-call budgets, and tool configurations. A candidate displaces the incumbent only if it delivers better quality at equal or lower cost. Perplexity says one candidate scored 13.3 points above the baseline on BrowseComp but fell below baselines on internal enterprise, legal and finance research tasks, illustrating why public benchmark results alone do not determine selection.

The documentation distinguishes dynamic names, pinned versions and frozen configurations. Dynamic calls inherit later evaluated changes without code edits; pinned calls retain one release after the active configuration advances; frozen calls copy public fields but can miss settings outside the documented request schema. Ultra has no frozen configuration and must be called by name.

For developers, the practical distinction is automatic updates versus reproducibility: dynamic levels inherit Perplexity’s evaluated revisions, while pinned versions preserve a known configuration. Ultra extends that system to broader coverage, and its four-agent design makes orchestration part of the cost-quality calculation alongside model selection.

Google, Meta and TikTok are suing to block a recent California law that's intended to protect kids from some of the more harmful aspects of social media, according to Reuters. Assembly Bill 1709 is part of a larger package that Governor Gavin Newsom introduced earlier this year and it prohibits social media platforms from offering underage kids "psychologically exploitative features" that could lead to compulsive use.

This includes stuff like sending videos through autoplay and giving children under 16 personalized recommendations. The three social media giants say this is in violation of the First Amendment and Section 230 of the federal Communications Decency Act, which has often been used to shield tech companies from liability.

The three companies filed separate suits at various courts throughout California. "The state cannot, consistent with the First Amendment, override Meta's editorial judgments," Meta wrote in its complaint. Google and TikTok made similar arguments.

Newsom said when he signed the law that children's safety should always come first when considering social media platforms in California. The state "will not stand by while unregulated technology puts our children at risk," he noted.

Some organizations agree with the social media companies here. The nonprofit Electronic Frontier Foundation has called the law "a massive privacy and free speech nightmare" and unsuccessfully urged Newsom to veto the policy.

While many disagree on the potential solution, there's not much of an argument to be made regarding the severity of the problem. TikTok, Meta and Google are currently fighting thousands of lawsuits by individuals, school districts, cities and government entities that all claim that social media platforms addict children and have caused a mental health crisis. To that end, many states regulate children's access to social media.

Also, Meta recently agreed to pay up to $18 billion to settle a high-profile case over children's privacy and social media addiction. As part of this agreement, the company says it will make changes to its platforms to make them less addictive to young users. It still denies any wrongdoing.
