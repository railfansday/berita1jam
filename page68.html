Silicon Valley’s reaction to the Nvidia drama is rife with blind spots

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Hello again, and welcome back to Fast Company’s Plugged In.

Jensen Huang was scheduled to speak at this week’s Semafor Silicon Valley & The World conference, which he cochaired. For reasons too obvious to require explanation, he didn’t make it to the event. And yet he dominated the on- and off-stage conversation so resoundingly that it might as well have been titled “Silicon Valley, The World & Jensen Huang.”

As tech notables sat for interviews—among them Sam Altman, Clay Bavor, Jeff Bezos, Andy Jassy, Yann LeCun, Fei-Fei Li, and David Sacks—moderators kept requesting their take on Huang’s shocking ouster from Nvidia. Most lavished praise on his entrepreneurship, but begged off saying much more on the grounds that the circumstances remained murky. Bezos’s comments were typical: “I don’t know enough of the details and I think probably most of us don’t know. . . . What I can tell you for sure is that Jensen is a remarkable guy who founded and built a remarkable company.”

Speakers at Semafor’s event approached the subject more gingerly than many in the scrum on X where Huang declared on Tuesday that “if the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO.” Skimming the 58,000 replies to his post, I didn’t see much discussion of the specifics of the case: Huang’s confessing to an extramarital affair with an Nvidia business partner who was allegedly also a spy for China, the questions that raised relating to Nvidia chips possibly being smuggled into China, and reports that he had two additional affairs with Nvidia employees.

Instead, Huang’s X followers mostly had his back. Tech industry celebs such as Aaron Levie, CEO of Box; Palmer Luckey, cofounder of Anduril; and Linda Yaccarino, former CEO of X, shared a few words (or emojis) of support. Others were civilians who expressed their enthusiasm for Huang’s theoretical rehiring in the form of truly weird AI slop: Jensen as Jesus at the Last Supper, Jensen popping out of a casket, Jensen lip-syncing Michael Jordan’s comments upon his return to the Chicago Bulls.

Despite reports of disagreement among Nvidia’s board members, the company continues to rule out Huang’s return. Given the unfolding legal and geopolitical elements of the story, a speedy Sam Altman-style reinstatement would appear to be fantasy in even the rosiest of scenarios.

The revelations thus far have already prompted the Trump administration to tighten restrictions on chip exports to China. Huang has agreed to testify before the Senate Banking Committee on December 1. Democrats, who will regain control of the Senate and House of Representatives in January, are salivating at the opportunity to dig into questions such as whether Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick facilitated the diversion of Nvidia chips to China. And California’s attorney general has subpoenaed Nvidia in search of any evidence that Huang’s affair led to chips or confidential information making their way to China.

Bottom line: Everyone who says we don’t know enough to come to a firm conclusion about the precise nature of what happened and its appropriate repercussions is on far firmer ground than those who think Huang was unfairly forced out and should be welcomed back.

But for all the miasma of uncertainty clouding this drama, it’s already told us plenty about Silicon Valley. Its most admired, envied, seemingly essential CEO being shown the door, even as the company he cofounded is still booming, is a deeply unsettling black swan event, and I haven’t seen many in the industry unblinkingly confront its implications. It’s surely not a coincidence that Gina Raimondo, the former U.S. commerce secretary—who is a technocrat, but not a creature of the Valley—had the bluntest assessment of the situation I heard at the Semafor conference: “Any kind of corruption, politics, dealing with spies, [there should be] no tolerance for it.”

Paul Graham, cofounder of Y Combinator, coined the term “founder mode” in 2024. It celebrates startup founders who refuse to cede control to professional managers, regardless of how big their brainchildren become. Decades before the concept had a name, Huang exemplified it. Instead of growing more remote from the minutiae of the company’s operations as it became a behemoth, he had his hands in everything—which is how he wound up with a reported 60 executives answering directly to him.

Over Huang’s 33 years as Nvidia’s CEO, he took risks that no MBA-brandishing suit would have dared. None was gutsier than his 2006 decision to pivot from focusing on 3D graphics to building a high-performance general-purpose computing platform. That prescience paid off when artificial intelligence became an epoch-shifting opportunity, leading to the company’s 90% market share in the chips that power generative AI.

In recent years, as Nvidia has jockeyed for the title of the world’s most valuable company, Huang has been no less daring. Whatever you think of the AI industry’s “circular financing,” in which many players take financial stakes in their own customers, Nvidia’s willingness to write huge checks to companies in its own ecosystem has been critical to AI’s growth. And as expectations for the technology prop up the stock market, it’s not just the tech business that might look very different without Huang’s high-stakes gambling. So might the entire U.S. economy.

Huang the public figure has become as unique as Huang the fearless entrepreneur. Once an unassuming engineer in an Nvidia shirt who went by his birth name of Jen-Hsun Huang, he reinvented himself into the genially bombastic, leather jacket-wearing rock star I interviewed in 2024. Only Altman rivaled his omnipresence as a speaker at tech industry events. Huang, who relished engaging in stunts such as frolicking with robots onstage, was a lot more fun. I can understand why people who never met him take his downfall personally.

If you can’t envision a tech industry without Huang at its core, you have every incentive to wish for any possible outcome that would reinstate him to his previous eminence. But Huang himself took risks that made a happy ending far less likely.

All evidence indicates that his initial strategy for dealing with the mess he created involved misleading Nvidia’s management and board, not to mention members of the press. Huang’s undisclosed relationships seem to have violated Nvidia’s own code of conduct. I have seen many people argue that they should still be forgiven. But to my knowledge, nobody has explicitly maintained that a CEO lying to his board is tolerable, at least if that CEO is Jensen Huang. Maybe that’s because the case is impossible to make.

Another bad bet Huang made was his tight relationship with President Trump, which he was flaunting as recently as September. It apparently influenced the president to dismiss an October 2025 intelligence briefing on Huang’s relationship with Megaspeed’s Alice (no relation) Huang. Trump may not have been doing Huang any favors, given that the story blew up in both of their faces at the worst conceivable time, after the Democrats’ wins in the midterm elections.

As Daring Fireball’s John Gruber noted this week, tech executives have historically steered clear of hugging any political party so close that it would complicate their relationships in Washington, D.C., should the balance of power shift. Huang abandoned this approach during Trump’s second term. So did other executives, but only Elon Musk may suffer graver consequences.

Huang’s unplanned departure also left Nvidia scrambling to deal with a fundamental bug in the logic of founder’s mode. Any company that’s lucky enough to matter for decades will, at some point, be run by a nonfounder—maybe even a dreaded professional manager. Responsible founders, no matter how hands on, should prepare for that day. Many do not.

Along with everyone else in the Valley, Huang apparently couldn’t picture a post-Jensen Nvidia. I hope he was kidding when he said he’d like to run the company for another 30 or 40 years and then be replaced by a robotic version of himself. But his failure to establish any clarity around a succession plan is a deadly serious problem for the company he founded. (His daughter, son, and son-in-law all work at the company, but not in roles that leave any of them as an heir apparent.)

Nobody else in tech ever had a career like Jensen Huang. His present quandary is part of that story, but I don’t expect it to be his only legacy. He might even rebound in some manner that doesn’t involve him getting his old gig back. Assuming the mystery surrounding this chapter eventually subsides, I hope the facts of the matter leave the industry wiser—if sadder—about aspects of its culture that it usually does its best to ignore.

Shrek 2 Remastered Fails To Capture The Magic Of Its Source Material

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

When I first heard the announcement for Shrek 2 Remastered, I couldn’t wait to get my hands on it. As I’m sure many of you can relate to, movie tie-in games were a major part of my childhood. While the rest of my family rented their favorite films from Blockbuster, I always made a beeline for the games section, where I eventually discovered one of the best licensed games from the early 2000s, Shrek 2.

To this day, I’m still a major sucker for licensed games, so revisiting a modern version of a classic wasn’t even a question. Now that I’ve played it, however, I’m left wondering if a PS5 port of the original would’ve been a better option. While this is a remaster, not a full remake, playing it back-to-back with The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (2026) has been a jarring and eye-opening experience that highlights the right and wrong ways to bring back a beloved game from the past.

While there are plenty of games that benefit from the traditional remaster treatment of updating visuals while leaving gameplay mostly unchanged, Shrek 2 likely would’ve been better off with the reverse.

Despite their age, Shrek 2’s original graphics had their own charm, whereas improvements to character responsiveness could’ve transformed the game into a great experience for both new and returning players. Instead, Shrek 2 Remastered retains the same frustrating controls while the visuals have become something unsettling at best and downright nightmarish at worst.

Little Red, also known as Lil’ Red, was almost entirely left out of the marketing for Shrek 2 Remastered, to the point that many believed she’d been removed entirely. Now it’s a bit clearer why that decision might’ve been made. Most of the characters have a disturbing look to them now, but Lil’ Red’s makeover is certainly the most terrifying.

Characters' softer features and movie-accurate appearances have been replaced by lifeless eyes, intense contrast, and enhanced details, leaving them almost unrecognizable compared to their appearances in the 2004 release and the movie it’s based on. I'm also left wondering why so much effort was put into redesigning characters when the cutscenes haven't been formatted to fill the screen and are often cut off before they're finished.

Though the visuals were certainly disappointing, I might’ve been able to overlook them if they'd been accompanied by new gameplay, but that remains unchanged. Most stages feature four characters that a solo player or a group of players can switch between to complete the required tasks.

Nearly every character is needed over the course of a single level due to their unique abilities, preventing one player from steamrolling others through the game. However, it also means solo players will be forced to use characters with less favorable controls.

Aside from Lil’ Red, who is actually somewhat enjoyable to play, thanks to her ranged apple throws and more fluid movements, most characters have limited attack ranges and feel stiff and unresponsive. Additionally, you’ll likely find yourself swapping characters more often than necessary because trailing characters (usually Shrek) frequently get stuck on obstacles and can’t free themselves.

Perhaps the one thing still saving this remaster from being a complete disappointment for me is that I’m playing local co-op with my partner, which has been fun simply because the game is so unforgiving. On more than one occasion, we’ve had to repeat a level multiple times to the point of delirious laughter. It’s not difficult, per se, but the lack of lives and poor controls make it brutal nonetheless.

Unfortunately, all the aforementioned issues make it difficult to imagine new players getting much enjoyment out of this remaster. Some older fans may be able to overlook the subpar gameplay in favor of nostalgia and the chance to play the game again, but I am finding it difficult. I’m grateful to have the opportunity to experience it on modern consoles with my partner, but that’s where my enjoyment ends.

If you’re a new player curious about the hype surrounding the original, this remaster isn’t the way to discover it, and I’d recommend skipping it. On the other hand, if you’re a returning player eager to revisit the game and have no way to play the 2004 version, this will scratch the itch. If you do opt to play, however, I’d insist on playing multiplayer. Shrek 2 has always been better as a shared experience, but that’s especially true now in Shrek 2 Remastered.

Players do need to sign in to their Epic account to access multiplayer, which has been a major point of frustration for those playing on Steam in particular, so keep that in mind.

Overall, I’m walking away from Shrek 2 Remastered heartbroken. Had it received the full remake treatment with refined mechanics and a visual overhaul that enhanced rather than stripped away its original charm, this could have been an incredible experience for everyone to enjoy together. As it stands currently, however, I’m just left wishing I had a copy of the original game.

Yellowjackets Season 4 Episode 1 Ending Explained: What Did Natalie See?

One of the greatest things about showrunners Ashley Lyle and Bart Nickerson’s psychological horror show Yellowjackets is that you never know what bananas shenanigans will happen next. We’re only one episode into Yellowjackets season 4, and we’re happy to report that this still holds true, with a cliffhanger ending that will leave you wondering what the hell just happened.

At the end of season 3, teen Natalie (Sophie Thatcher) had just managed to contact someone outside the remote wilderness using the satellite phone they’d recovered from the scientific expedition that had recently discovered them. Back at the camp, teen Shauna (Sophie Nélisse), now in charge, is furious at Natalie for calling for help behind her back.

This article contains spoilers for Yellowjackets season 4 episode 1, “Hivemind.”

It was established in the first season of Yellowjackets that the girls (and Travis) would eventually be rescued, as the time jumps between 2021 and 1996 make clear. However, the mystery was more about how that would happen and what the consequences of the Yellowjackets' rescue would be.

In “Hivemind,” the outcome of that phone call is made pretty clear. The girls and Travis are rescued and brought to a hospital in Canada, though several are missing, including poor Mari Ibarra (Alexa Barajas), who ended up being skewered in the same pit she was previously rescued from during one of the girls’ cannibalistic hunts in season 3. This leads to a few emotional scenes of the parents of the dead breaking down and the survivors exchanging agonizingly guilty looks.

At first, it looks like we’ll skip the immediate fallout as the rest of the Yellowjackets learn about Natalie’s call for help. Most of “Hivemind” in the teen timeline focuses on the reunion between the parents and survivors, and the teens’ efforts to cover up the deplorable things they did in the wilderness by lying to the ranger and therapist who question them.

That is, until the end of the episode, when the story jumps back to the wilderness, before the rescue. We see Natalie stumble back into camp, wide-eyed and terrified. She then asks a question that I too say at the end of every Yellowjackets episode: “What the fuck?”

Yellowjackets’ first episode may have ended on a truly diabolical cliffhanger, but that doesn’t mean we don’t have clues about what Natalie saw to make her react with so much shock.

For one thing, the opening credits and teaser trailers for the fourth season have shown clips of teen Shauna looking battered and beaten. We know, based on the snow surrounding Shauna in this scene, that unless there is a time skip, her looking this way is likely to take place during the Yellowjackets' time in the wilderness.

What reason would the girls have to attack Shauna? A lot. Unlike Tai (Jasmin Savoy Brown), who wants to conceal the cannibalism and murders the girls committed during their time there, and Lottie (Courtney Eaton), who genuinely believes she has a spiritual connection to the wilderness’s supernatural goings-on, Shauna’s reason for staying in the wilderness is pretty damn selfish: she enjoys the power and hedonism that wilderness life encourages.

Her desire to be the alpha of this pack of wolves in girls’ clothing has, unfortunately, made her an easy target for the others’ hatred. While most of the girls want to go home, Shauna refuses to let them and has even trained a gun on some of her fellow Yellowjackets, including her situationship, Melissa (Jenna Burgess).

If all the girls have turned on Shauna and left her in a bad way, Natalie could be reacting to that. After all, the first episode does show that teen Shauna has several scars and bruises on her face in the hospital, that weren’t there when we saw Shauna at the end of season 3.

That being said, we do know a handful of the girls don’t make it back to civilization — including the lone science researcher Hannah (Ashley Sutton), who murdered one of her own last season in a bid to be accepted by the teens. That appealed to Shauna for a little while, but given how volatile Shauna is about being rescued, and how little they know about Hannah, it's also likely that Natalie has seen Shauna and the others turn on Hannah as a consequence of Natalie’s call for help.

What Natalie has seen likely won’t be confirmed until next week. Even so, Yellowjackets likes to surprise its audience, and we wouldn’t be shocked if something we haven’t speculated about has turned up at camp, making the arrival of people searching for the Yellowjackets even more precarious.

The One Thing About GTA 6's Ending to Know Before You Get Too Far In

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

All of your actions have consequences in Leonida

While playing through Grand Theft Auto 6, you might notice that there are a lot of systems that can alter your experience. There’s the relationship system, your criminal profile, fitness routines that change your physique, and plenty more that gives you a lot of control over Jason and Lucia. In the moment-to-moment gameplay, it can be a little hard to figure out how much of that actually has an impact on the game.

Be warned, though: every choice you make really, really matters. GTA 6 features multiple endings that will be shaped by how much you engage with its systems. And it never makes it clear that those choices matter.

[Ed. note: This article contains spoilers for GTA 6’s ending.]

While I won’t reveal how my playthrough ended, I will note that I was faced with a binary choice that could have pushed me toward two different endings. GTA 6 has a few branching narrative moments like this where players are presented with two choices, so that lined up with the rest of the game. In my mind, I assumed that the game only had two endings. That was very incorrect.

That revelation came when Polygon spoke with Rockstar North co-head Rob Nelson ahead of GTA 6’s release. I had just rolled credits on the game, and Nelson was eager to learn which ending I got. I told him, and made a comment about the binary choice. He revealed that there were actually several possible endings with varying degrees of difference. Nelson said that some of them are small, maybe changing a bit of dialogue in a scene.

Others are more drastic. The climactic moment of my ending took place on a specific bridge on the map. Nelson said that some endings don’t go there at all. And while I’ve yet to see every ending play out (nor do I even know how many endings there are), I’ve already gotten the sense that the fates of certain characters can even change from ending to ending. If someone tries to spoil exactly what happens in GTA 6’s ending for you, know that you might end up getting surprised when something completely different occurs in your playthrough.

So, what determines which ending you get? Nelson told me that a few different factors go into it. The most obvious one is the choices that you make during branching story moments. You can also probably guess that the relationship system plays into it too. Your criminal profile is a factor as well, so even your choice of using lethal force in various situations can make a difference. On top of that, your ending can also change based on how much money you’ve stashed into your family savings account by the time you hit the credits. Needless to say, no decision goes without consequence in GTA 6.

If you’re stressing out about getting the “bad” ending by accident, don’t worry too much. Nelson noted that there aren’t exactly good or bad endings in GTA 6. Without spoiling where my story went, I can see where he’s coming from. The ending I got made perfect sense with the way I had been playing. And even within that moment, I still had a choice as to how it could play out. So long as you’re making deliberate choices in GTA 6 and not just ignoring systems for the heck of it, you should hopefully land on an ending that matches your playstyle. But keep all this in mind if you decide to ignore Jason and Lucia’s relationship. You’ll feel that eventually.

Meanwhile, the revolutionary blended wing body concept has been championed for years as a next-generation jetliner template for its 20-30% fuel efficiency, yet it inherently conflicts with all four of these design priorities. One of the main design hurdles for a BWB is simply holding in the air while cruising at altitude.

Pressurization systems maintain roughly 8.35 psi (0.58 bar) inside and exert about 21,000 lbs (93 kN) of force against a standard jet entry door. As noted in Engineers Ireland's overview of blended-wing airliners, flattening that cabin cuts drag and could burn up, but a BWB's flat walls experience much more intense loads under that same pressure.

Pressurized air tries to bend flat floors and ceilings outward into a round bubble. Reinforcing the frame against that bending adds heavy internal beams and thick carbon walls, eating away fuel savings while complicating emergency exits and preventing easy fuselage stretches.

For the ZEROe hydrogen program, Airbus first displayed a 200-seat BWB in the hopes that the broad body would accommodate large fuel tanks that were chilled to -423°F (-253°C). However, in 2025, Airbus abandoned that concept for more research and went back to a traditional tube-and-wing configuration with hydrogen fuel-cell pods.

Airbus executives realized there was too much financial risk involved in certifying a pressurized passenger cabin while negotiating airport gate constraints and non-stretchable tooling, a risk that commercial airlines declined to finance. Rather, blended-wing concepts are being introduced to military forces first by aerospace developers.

Through the Defense Innovation Unit, the US Air Force awarded startup JetZero $235 million in August 2023 to construct a full-scale demonstrator using Northrop Grumman's Scaled Composites for flight testing in 2027. Designed for next-generation aerial refueling and military cargo missions, the JetZero demonstrator mounts two commercial high-bypass engines above the rear wing body.

While high-angle takeoffs can push turbulent airflow into top-mounted engine fans, this layout cuts ground noise and protects engines from runway debris. More significantly, gasoline tanks and freight pallets do not require windows, never experience airsickness, and completely circumvent passenger evacuation regulations. Before going back to passenger aircraft, developers intend to reduce the financial and engineering risks.

They will do so by first demonstrating composite pressure structures and flight-control software in military cargo and tanker operations. Commercial passengers will continue to board well-known round jets until military flights demonstrate that internal bracing can safely retain cabin air without significant weight penalties or emergency evacuation bottlenecks, leaving the BWB to earn its wings in uniform.

Over 80% of travelers inside a 250-seat BWB will sit in windowless center bays, forcing airlines to install heavy, maintenance-prone digital wall screens instead of simple windows. Additionally, passengers at the outboard edge have a rougher ride during turns because the outer seats sit 40-50 feet (12-15 meters) away from the centerline.

During a standard 30-degree banked turn or a bumpy crosswind landing, outer-row flyers do not just tilt gently. They will feel a swing through wide arcs that may trigger airsickness much more frequently. Planemakers manufacturing a BWB will have to divide the cabin with permanent structural support walls in order to contain the immense outward air pressure inside a wide BWB.

To prevent flat panels from protruding outward, these vertical composite ties secure the floor directly to the ceiling. Although this interior grid prevents blowout without adding undue weight, it divides the cabin into cubicle-like bays that make it difficult for airlines to later modify legroom or move seats. On the land side, guiding 250 or more travelers through a standard jet bridge into a wide, multi-aisle grid creates bottlenecks at the entrance.

Standard airport jet bridges only connect to one or two forward left-hand doors. The unique shape and depth of the belly hold will also fundamentally alter the way that baggage and cargo handling is done. Ultimately, even when the first BWB does take flight, there remain many unresolved dilemmas to be overcome before the first arrives on the flight line at an airport near you.

Mainline passenger jets like the Boeing 737 MAX, Airbus A321neo, and Boeing 777-300ER are iconic examples of a typical tubular-and-wing design with minimal dead weight. The optimized, thin skin of each plane's fuselage stretches uniformly like a balloon as internal pressure pushes outward equally in all directions.

This design premise is upset when that barrel is flattened and morphed into the cabin of a BWB. A cylindrical hull allows even composite aircraft, like a Boeing 787 Dreamliner, to withstand decades of pressurized flight without the need for a more complex airframe. Engineering studies show that internal bending can multiply structural stress more than tenfold compared to a simple round tube.

Panels bow from air pushing on the concentrated points of floors and ceilings. Stopping the cabin from bursting at the seams forces makers to add bracing and load-bearing bulkheads. Building a BWB cabin strong enough to hold normal cruise pressure therefore requires exceptionally thick carbon skin and heavy framing.

The weight of additional support then directly reduces the 20-30% fuel burn reduction claimed by BWBs, complicating manufacturing and operating trade-offs. Another issue with blended-wing aircraft is that they are nearly impossible to scale up to larger models, even if engineers can overcome the significant cabin weight issue.

On July 20, 2007, the subscale Boeing-NASA X-48B demonstrated that flight computers could control a tailless flying wing above Edwards Air Force Base. Through 2010, the 500 lbs (227-kg) test plane had 92 successful flights, thanks to three small jet engines producing 52 lbs of thrust (0.23 kN each).

Despite NASA's X-48B passing the flight tests, building a full-size airplane provides serious day-to-day flying challenges. Standard jets mount their tail fins roughly 100 feet (30 m) behind the aircraft's balance point, giving tail flaps high leverage to lift the nose during takeoff. Without a long tail, a BWB relies on large rear wing flaps to pitch the nose upward.

Deflecting those flaps sharply during takeoff actually spills lift right when pilots need it most on the runway. Despite these hurdles, developers are actively testing practical engineering solutions. According to CNN's report on the JetZero program, the US Air Force-backed startup uses lightweight composite panels and internal ribs to tame cabin pressure without excessive weight.

Meanwhile, Nautilus avoids pressure penalties on its unpressurized KONA cargo plane while giving its Horizon Evo passenger jet a narrower, double-deck center body that limits bending stress and fits standard 737 airport gates. Still, an even bigger problem for airlines and aircraft makers is fleet growth.

Manufacturers build profitable aircraft families, like stretching the Airbus A320 into the A321neo or growing the Boeing 737 into the MAX 10, by simply inserting round barrel sections ahead of and behind the wings. By using the same basic wing design, pilot training, and replacement components, airlines are able to carry more passengers.

Meanwhile, they maintain fair purchase and operating costs thanks to this simple production technique. That straightforward stretch option is eliminated by a BWB, which integrates the cabin directly into the wing's curved lifting shape.

The smooth airflow of the wing is ruined, and the plane's balancing point is shifted when the middle body is cut apart to accommodate additional rows. In order to create a larger version, producers would have to completely redo the flight-control software, redesign the exterior shell, and rework the internal wing beams, which would cost billions of dollars for each size of seat.

Several companies, including Airbus, JetZero, and Boeing, are actively developing prototypes.

One of the greatest functional hurdles for blended-wing designs is that every commercial airport on Earth is built for a traditional tube-and-wing jet. A 250-seat blended-wing plane would span nearly 200 feet (61 meters), which is on par with the Boeing 777-9 and Airbus A350-1000, bringing higher airport fees and slower turnarounds.

Conventional large airliners, like the 747 and A380, were already met with major resistance as their wingspans forced airports to widen taxiways and rebuild gates. As detailed in Leeham News' analysis of blended-wing airliners, wide-wingspan planes are vital for lowering drag and improving fuel burn, yet the A380 directly suffered in sales due to its limited destination portfolio.

That restriction on airport access keeps airlines cautious, as it is a hard bottleneck on network versatility and therefore business utility. To date, no carrier holds a firm, deposit-backed order for a BWB. Given that commercial carriers will not fund the certification risks upfront, developers have turned to military applications to make the BWB a reality.

When JetZero's full-scale demonstration flies for the US military in 2027, aviation leaders will be present at Edwards Air Force Base. These test flights will assess computer flight controls, aerial refueling management, and composite fabrication. Military missions may demonstrate that internal composite bracing can hold cabin air without adding excessive structural weight.

If so, commercial designers researching new passenger airliners for the late 2030s may eventually profit from that flight data. Until planemakers demonstrate that a BWB can withstand rigorous 90-second emergency evacuation testing without incurring substantial structural penalties, customers will continue to board conventional jets.

At 37,000 feet (11,278 m), the physics of maintaining breathable air remains relentlessly demanding. The basic tube will continue to dominate aviation economics because a simple metal or composite cylinder can withstand 21,000 lbs (93 kN) of outward door force with little material weight.

This Everyday Vitamin May Ease Depression In Some People

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Could correcting this vitamin deficiency make a difference for people already receiving treatment for depression?

Vitamin D supplements may ease symptoms in some people with depression, according to a review of the evidence.

Several small trials found improvements, including in people who started out with low vitamin D levels.

Other trials found little or no benefit, and one large study found that supplements did not lower the chances of developing depression.

The link is plausible because vitamin D may influence inflammation and chemical signalling in the brain, both of which are relevant to mood.

Depression itself could also lower vitamin D levels if it leaves people with less energy to go outdoors.

That makes it important to ask two separate questions: whether correcting a deficiency can help someone who is already depressed and whether taking vitamin D can prevent depression in the first place.

Researchers at the University of Oradea reviewed studies measuring vitamin D levels in people with and without depression, as well as trials testing whether supplements changed their symptoms.

One small trial recruited people who had both major depression and a vitamin D deficiency.

Both groups continued with their usual care, but after about three months, depression scores were lower in the group given a vitamin D injection than in the placebo group (Vellekkatt et al., 2020).

Differences in who took part, the doses they received and how symptoms were measured made the trials difficult to compare.

A trial designed to test prevention followed more than 18,000 adults aged 50 and over who had no clinically relevant depressive symptoms when they joined.

Over about five years, participants who took daily vitamin D were about as likely to develop depression or clinically relevant symptoms as those who took a placebo (Okereke et al., 2020).

Vitamin D receptors are found in parts of the brain such as the hippocampus, which is involved in memory and the response to stress.

The vitamin can also influence immune signals involved in inflammation.

This is one route through which it might affect mood in some people with depression.

Vitamin D levels also reflect habits that can change during depression, including what people eat and how much time they spend outdoors.

Someone who stops going for regular walks, for example, may get less sunlight and make less vitamin D as a result.

A blood test taken during an episode of depression cannot tell which change came first.

The question now is whether correcting a vitamin D deficiency can improve symptoms in people already receiving treatment for depression.

Answering it will take larger trials that recruit people with both conditions and follow them long enough to see whether any improvement lasts.

The study was published in the journal Pharmaceuticals (Roșian et al., 2025).
