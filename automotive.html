Are We Overlooking Chronic Neuropathic Pain After ICU Survival?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

— Neuromodulation may offer a future treatment pathway, but better diagnosis must come first

Modern critical care has transformed survival after sepsis, respiratory failure, multi-organ dysfunction, and other life-threatening conditions. Yet, surviving an intensive care unit (ICU) admission does not necessarily mean returning to normal life.

For many patients, discharge marks the beginning of another difficult journey involving weakness, impaired mobility, sleep disturbances, cognitive difficulties, psychological distress, and persistent pain.

While post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) has received increasing attention, one potentially disabling complication deserves greater recognition: chronic neuropathic pain following critical illness.

In our recently published narrative review, "Neuromodulation for Refractory Neuropathic Pain Following Critical Illness: A Narrative Review and Phenotype-Based Clinical Framework," we examined the neurological consequences of critical illness and explored whether established neuromodulation technologies might eventually provide additional treatment options for carefully selected patients.

Our central message is straightforward: We need to identify neuropathic pain earlier, understand its underlying mechanisms, and investigate treatment strategies beyond conventional pharmacotherapy when symptoms remain disabling.

The Hidden Burden of Pain After Critical Illness

Persistent pain is an important but frequently underappreciated challenge among ICU survivors.

A systematic review reported persistent pain frequencies ranging from 28% to 77%, reflecting substantial differences in patient populations, definitions, and follow-up periods. A more recent 2026 meta-analysis estimated chronic pain incidence at approximately 47% at 3 months, 29% at 6 months, and 31% at 12 months.

These findings highlight the magnitude of the problem, but an important distinction must be made: Not all chronic pain following critical illness is neuropathic.

Patients may experience musculoskeletal pain from prolonged immobilization, muscle injury, surgical procedures, or deconditioning. Others develop peripheral nerve injuries, critical illness polyneuropathy, or potentially small-fiber pathology.

In a prospective multicenter cohort of 814 patients, nearly 48% reported significant persistent pain 3 months after ICU admission. Among those experiencing pain, approximately 9% screened positive for neuropathic pain symptoms.

These observations suggest that neuropathic pain represents a clinically meaningful subgroup requiring more specific evaluation, rather than a diagnosis that should be assigned to every patient with post-ICU pain.

Why Traditional Treatment May Not Be Enough

Critical illness polyneuropathy can involve diffuse axonal injury affecting motor and sensory nerves. Patients may experience weakness, sensory loss, burning discomfort, or other neuropathic symptoms.

However, critical illness myopathy primarily affects muscle, and weakness alone should never be interpreted as evidence of neuropathic pain. This distinction is important because treatment should be directed toward the actual source of disability.

Rehabilitation, physical therapy, psychological support, sleep optimization, and individualized medication management remain essential components of recovery.

Gabapentinoids, certain antidepressants, and topical treatments may be appropriate for selected neuropathic pain presentations. Yet, these medications can be difficult to tolerate in patients recovering from critical illness, particularly when renal dysfunction, cognitive impairment, dizziness, sedation, or respiratory vulnerability are present.

Escalating medication treatment is not always the best solution.

For patients whose persistent neuropathic pain prevents meaningful participation in rehabilitation despite appropriate conventional care, we should be asking whether additional targeted therapies deserve investigation.

Could Neuromodulation Help Selected ICU Survivors?

Neuromodulation has become an established treatment strategy for several refractory neuropathic pain conditions. Peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS), dorsal root ganglion stimulation (DRGS), and spinal cord stimulation (SCS) act at different anatomical targets and have distinct evidence bases.

Our review proposes a phenotype-based framework for studying these approaches after critical illness.

PNS may be particularly relevant to focal neuropathic pain caused by positioning-related nerve injury, compression neuropathy, or other localized peripheral nerve damage. When symptoms correspond to an identifiable nerve distribution, targeted stimulation may offer a plausible research strategy.

DRGS may be relevant to selected regional neuropathic pain presentations, particularly when patients meet established diagnostic criteria for conditions such as complex regional pain syndrome or causalgia.

SCS may warrant investigation in selected patients with bilateral or multisegmental peripheral neuropathic pain, provided that the neurological diagnosis and treatment target are clearly established.

Existing clinical trials provide reasons for scientific interest.

For example, the ACCURATE trial demonstrated higher 3-month treatment success with DRGS than conventional SCS among patients with lower-extremity complex regional pain syndrome or causalgia. Similarly, the SENZA-PDN trial demonstrated substantial pain relief with 10-kHz SCS in patients with refractory painful diabetic neuropathy.

However, these findings cannot simply be transferred to patients recovering from critical illness. There is currently insufficient direct clinical evidence to establish the efficacy of PNS, DRGS, or SCS specifically for post-critical-illness neuropathic pain.

That limitation is essential. Our proposed framework is intended to guide clinical investigation, not justify routine device implantation for an unvalidated indication.

We Need a Better Bridge Between the ICU and Pain Medicine

One of the most important opportunities may lie not in new technology, but in improving coordination across specialties.

Patients frequently transition from intensive care to rehabilitation facilities, primary care, and outpatient specialty services. During these transitions, neurological symptoms and pain characteristics may not receive consistent follow-up.

A more coordinated pathway could begin with documenting neurological deficits, pain patterns, and relevant procedural or positioning exposures before ICU discharge. Subsequent assessments should distinguish persistent weakness from neuropathic pain, evaluate functional limitations, and identify patients requiring neurological investigation.

For patients with ongoing disabling symptoms, collaboration among critical care physicians, neurologists, rehabilitation specialists, primary care clinicians, and pain medicine specialists may improve diagnostic clarity and treatment planning.

Early referral to pain medicine does not mean early implantation.

Medical stabilization, adequate diagnostic evaluation, rehabilitation, psychological support, and procedural safety assessment must remain priorities.

If neuromodulation is eventually studied in this population, traditional pain intensity measurements alone will not be sufficient.

The outcomes that matter to ICU survivors often extend beyond numerical pain scores.

Can the patient walk farther? Participate more effectively in physical therapy? Sleep better? Reduce medication-related adverse effects? Resume meaningful daily activities?

These functional outcomes should be central to future investigations.

Prospective studies must also distinguish improvements in pain from neurological recovery. Reducing neuropathic pain does not necessarily reverse axonal injury, restore muscle strength, or accelerate nerve regeneration.

Long-term safety is equally important. Patients recovering from critical illness may have residual organ dysfunction, anticoagulation requirements, nutritional deficiencies, impaired wound healing, or other factors that influence procedural risk. A treatment that reduces pain but introduces disproportionate complications or care burdens may offer limited overall benefit.

The next step should not be widespread adoption of neuromodulation for post-ICU neuropathy. It should be carefully designed clinical research. As critical care continues to improve survival, our definition of successful treatment must evolve. Survival should be the beginning of recovery, not the endpoint of care.

meet the wienermobile, the 1930s quirky pedal car that put hot dogs on wheels

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

from its first appearance on american roads in 1936 to its miniature interpretations by bird corporation and konek, the oversized hot dog has become an enduring piece of advertising and automotive culture.

Two vintage Oscar Mayer Wienermobile pedal cars, produced by Bird Corporation and Konek Inc. during the 1990s, are appearing together at auction, bringing a miniature version of one of America’s most recognizable promotional vehicles back into focus. Offered as a single lot without a reserve, the two new-old-stock examples reportedly have never been ridden by children. Their bright orange bodies, yellow bun-shaped bases, and playful automotive details recall a design that has spent nearly nine decades turning an oversized hot dog into a familiar sight on American roads.

The story of the Wienermobile begins in 1936, when Carl G. Mayer, nephew of Oscar Mayer founder Oscar F. Mayer, proposed a hot dog-shaped vehicle as a traveling advertisement for the company’s products. Built by Chicago’s General Body Company, the original measured approximately four meters long and featured a metal body shaped like an oversized wiener, mounted on wheels. Its two open cockpits accommodated a driver and Little Oscar, the promotional character of the brand.

As the vehicle became an established part of Oscar Mayer’s advertising, successive generations introduced new interpretations of the original concept. The 1958 model, designed by industrial designer Brooks Stevens, introduced a rounded, bubble-like front and more integrated bun-shaped elements. Later versions experimented with larger bodies, updated chassis, and elaborate interiors, always retaining the unmistakable hot dog silhouette.

Oscar Mayer discontinued the program in the late 1970s, shifting its attention toward television advertising. Public enthusiasm for the vehicle, however, prompted its return in 1986 for the company’s 50th anniversary. New generations followed, and the Wienermobile resumed its travels across the United States, appearing at parades, public gatherings, supermarkets, and promotional events.

Over the years, the hot dog on wheels inspired toys, merchandise, and other branded interpretations, translating its instantly recognizable shape into objects that could be collected and played with. The two pedal cars now offered at auction on Bring a Trailer belong to this broader history.

Although they share the same orange-and-yellow color scheme, the Bird Corporation and Konek examples feature different construction methods. The Bird version has a single-piece, hot-dog-shaped plastic body mounted over a yellow base, while the Konek model uses a two-piece orange body with Oscar Mayer and Wienermobile decals across its front, rear, and sides.

Both combine molded plastic exteriors with metal frames, orange wheels, yellow seats, and black steering wheels with yellow horn buttons. Twin foot pedals power the miniature vehicles, while faux headlights, grilles, taillights, and windows complete their automotive appearance. The Konek example measures approximately 114 centimeters long, 53 centimeters wide, and 48 centimeters tall.

Today, the Wienermobile remains part of Oscar Mayer’s identity, with six vehicles operated by parent company Kraft Heinz. Its continued presence on American roads speaks to the unusual longevity of a marketing idea conceived in the 1930s. The hot dog-shaped vehicle has become a recognizable piece of American visual culture and continues to inspire new interpretations nearly 90 years after its first appearance. The two pedal cars’ appearance at auction offers a chance to revisit the history of the Wienermobile, a promotional vehicle that brought advertising, automotive design, and popular culture together. First introduced in 1936, the hot dog-shaped vehicle has evolved into an enduring fixture of American popular culture.

The Ford Bronco Frostwarden Makes 1200 HP, Thanks to Its Megazilla 2.0 Supercharged V-8

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

This search-and-rescue machine, revealed at SEMA, is what happens when Ford throws everything it has at a Bronco.

The folks at Ford brought one of the wildest Broncos we’ve ever seen to the 2026 SEMA Show this year. It’s called “Frostwarden” ... and it has 1200 horsepower.

How’d Ford extract that much horsepower from a Bronco? Well, it sure doesn’t use any version of the production engines. Instead, Ford put the also-new Megazilla 2.0 crate motor under the hood. This 7.3-liter V-8 is paired here with a 3.0-liter Gen 6 Whipple supercharger; it also has a stronger block, supercharger-specific camshaft, upgraded pistons, larger fuel injectors, and a different belt drive and damper. As a package, Ford is claiming 1200 hp and 1000 pound-feet of torque when run with race fuel. (No, it didn’t quote a pump gas number.)

The idea behind the rest of the rig was to put together a package that could be used as an arctic search-and-rescue machine. To that end, it has a custom Bilstein suspension, 74Weld portal gear lifts, upgraded Dana driveline, and 18-inch Weld bead-lock wheels wrapped in 42-inch BFGoodrich tires. It’s absolutely enormous, and gives off the impression that one could drive it anywhere, which is largely the point.

This Bronco is festooned with accessories front to back. Rigid lights should help show the way through snowstorms. A spare wheels/tire and pair of toolboxes sit atop the roof. There’s a shovel, recovery rope, fuel jugs, and more. Plus, Ford outfitted the interior with color-matching seats and accents. There’s even a “Megazilla” emblem in the steering wheel rim to make sure you don’t forget this beast has 1200 horsepower ready to unleash.

Unfortunately, Ford explicitly says these vehicle “is not available for purchase,” and we suspect something like it never will be from your local Ford dealer. However, it serves as proof that you can shoehorn a 7.3-liter supercharged V-8 under the (admittedly bulged) hood of a Bronco, and what’s not to love about that?
