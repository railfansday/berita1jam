Better founder Vishal Garg details Tinman AI platform shift

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Better founder Vishal Garg said Tinman AI is driving about 60% of company activity as Better pivots from ad-driven DTC lending to partners. He also outlined a $60 million cost reduction plan and a focus on contribution margin profitability.  AI Summary

Better founder Vishal Garg sat down with HousingWire President Diego Sanchez on the PowerHouse podcast today to unpack his proxy victory, the company’s shift away from ad-driven direct-to-consumer lending and a plan to turn its Tinman AI platform into shared infrastructure for brokers, IMBs and fintech partners.

In the wide-ranging conversation, Garg talked first about his proxy fight to return to the company he founded.

“The odds of a founder CEO coming back and removing the entire board — that happens 1.7% of the time successfully in the history of proxy fights. So, I didn’t know that going in, apparently, but like 60-to-one odds against,” Garg said.

Garg was removed as Better’s CEO in early August, replaced on an interim basis by Daniel Lewis. Garg then launched a campaign to replace members of the board, winning over enough shareholders to regain control of the company.

He says shareholders backed him because they buy into Better’s mission to make homeownership “cheaper, faster and easier,” and because they see early results from Tinman AI, the company’s mortgage manufacturing engine.

In his short time away, Garg said he reflected on the mistakes he made as CEO. “The biggest thing I did wrong was not push the AI hard enough. You know, really just still kind of kept trying to convince people that this path was the best path, trying to convince people that the agent needed to be perfect, right? It did not need to be perfect.

“All of those things, big mistake. While I was doing that, there are other people out there, you know, 23-year-olds disrupting entire industries with a completely brand new perspective and just like letting the agents run.

“And so while we have this massive competitive moat of licenses, and over a hundred billion dollars of transactions on our platform that serves as learning data, the ability to rep and warrant — and all these sorts of things that your traditional 23-year-old coming out of Silicon Valley with a a mortgage startup doesn’t have the ability to do — we would very clearly lose that edge over time if we just didn’t run fast enough.”

Garg said Better has raised “almost $2 billion” to remake the mortgage manufacturing process and acknowledged the company has “lost more money than anyone else” in the space. He compared that to the long burns at Uber and Airbnb, positioning Better as early in an effort to reshape what he called a $4.5 trillion industry.

Tinman — originally built to codify agency and investor rules from 2016 to 2022 — is the foundation of that strategy. Garg said the system delivers a “six basis point critical defect rate,” which he called the lowest in the industry, and that overlaying large language models has accelerated the process and stripped out human friction.

Better’s revenue peaked at about $1.5 billion during the refi boom, then dropped to roughly $75 million in 2023. Garg said the company is now at a “$220-plus” revenue run rate, with about 60% of activity tied to Tinman AI.

Crucially, he said Better no longer cares “about the customer interaction surface.” Instead, the goal is to plug Tinman AI into whatever front end the consumer, lender or broker already uses — whether that’s a fintech app like Credit Karma, an AI agent interface, a mortgage broker’s POS or a branch loan officer’s tablet at an open house.

“We are not even seeking to disrupt the industry anymore,” Garg said. “We’re seeking to empower the entire industry and uplift the entire industry by taking Tinman and making it available to anyone and everyone in the industry to power their business.”

Garg framed Better’s first phase, from 2016 to 2023, as a DTC-heavy play. The new phase, he said, is “interaction surface agnostic” — with growth coming through distributed retail, broker and fintech partners instead of Google and Facebook ads.

He pointed to Better’s relationship with NEO Home Loans as an example of how Tinman can sit behind distributed retail brands. Garg said Neo’s loan officers went from averaging roughly 2–2.5 loans per month to closer to 5 loans per month on Tinman, with higher commissions because they can handle more business and deliver more consistent execution.

He said Tinman reviews decisions across “all 65 investors” before a loan is rejected, reducing fallout and helping attach additional products like HELOCs. The strategy, he argued, is similar to Uber’s path, where local operators plug into a central platform rather than run their own full tech stacks.

“The Uberization of retail is what happens,” Garg said, describing a model where Better supports multiple distributed retail brands and expands city by city.

Garg also confirmed that Better is already operating as a wholesale lender.

“We are a wholesale lender,” he said, noting the company currently has about 40 mortgage brokers on its platform, focused today on home equity.

He described that as a “land and expand” strategy. Over time, he envisions brokers evolving into broader “home advisors” or “loan advisors” who can also sell insurance and maintain long-term relationships with borrowers.

Garg highlighted that past investments — including title, homeowners, auto and life insurance entities under the Better Cover umbrella — didn’t produce strong unit economics when driven by Better’s own human salesforce. But he said those capabilities can make more sense when product distribution runs through brokers and retail partners who already own the consumer relationship.

“It is so powerful to tip the platform in the hands of people that know what they’re doing,” he said.

On where Better is pulling back, Garg was direct: paid, ad-driven DTC.

He said Better has already “cut D2C marketing” and questioned the logic of spending “two and a half million a month for buying clicks on Google” when he was able to mobilize tens of thousands of proxy voters himself using low-cost AI video tools and social platforms.

“I am short ad-driven D2C, brand-driven D2C, because it will die a fast and rapid death in the age of the AI agents,” he said.

He contrasted that position with lenders that have invested heavily in mortgage servicing rights and large workforces, saying those business models are effectively “long consumer friction” at a time when AI agents such as Muse, Instinct and others could make it easier for borrowers to move and refinance.

“You cannot charge people 50 basis points on a mortgage and hope to make up for it because you can bid more than everyone else on Google,” Garg said.

For lenders that still lean on digital ad spend and lead buying, his stance underscores the risk that acquisition costs continue to rise while AI tools make rate and fee comparisons more transparent.

Garg acknowledged recent layoffs at Better and called the current cost structure “unsustainable.”

He said the company needs to reduce costs by about $60 million and has identified or executed on roughly $24 million of savings so far. The goal, he said, is to move quarterly operating costs from the mid-$60 million range into the $40 millions.

“For us to be the platform for the industry, for us to be the AWS for the industry… the industry has to know that Better will always be there,” he said. “And the only way the industry knows that Better will always be there is if Better makes money every month.”

Asked whether Better will be profitable a year from now, Garg said the “core parts of the business will be contribution margin profitable,” unless the company chooses to lean into large growth or acquisition investments. He differentiated this plan from past profitability promises by emphasizing that this time Better is attacking both revenue and expense lines.

“In the past, we were keeping expenses the same and using AI to help power growth,” he said. “Now operating costs are going from that number down by 25 million… and we’re focused on still growing.”

Garg also argued that Better trades at about 1.5x revenue, a steep discount to private-market mortgage fintech valuations he said can reach 10x–50x revenue. Reaching profitability, he said, is the “best way to remove that discount” and create value for shareholders.

As part of his return, Garg has committed to hiring a CEO. He said he is looking for an executive who is “AI pilled,” has a deep understanding of credit risk and has experience building large B2B “outcome as a service” platforms — delivering specific outcomes instead of just software licenses.

He argued that mortgage is an industry that “blows itself up every 10, 20 years” and that the next CEO must be grounded in how credit cycles and product standards can go wrong.

Garg said strong people skills are also critical — and acknowledged that is an area where he has historically struggled and needs complementary leadership.

KELA researchers have exposed an active financial-scam infrastructure that hosts hundreds of fraudulent websites capable of faking their own suspensions to stay online. As of Oct. 25, 2026, 248 financial scam and phishing domains tied to 189 major US banks and financial services have been linked to the same infrastructure.

According to the cybersecurity firm’s report, published on Tuesday, the “Suspended” infrastructure operation runs websites that have cloned the official domains of victim organizations, with a particular focus on business banking. Many domains use the [keyword]-[bank].com format, slotting in keywords such as “secure,” “disable,” “protect,” and “cancel.”

A handful of servers spread across two shared hosting providers run the backend of Suspended infrastructure, which has also been tied to phishing campaigns targeting cryptocurrency exchanges and government services in the UK, the Netherlands, Australia, and New Zealand. As a result, the researchers believe that either a single operator is behind the setup or that the server infrastructure is part of a wider phishing-as-a-service platform.

This phishing campaign stands out because of how the cybercriminals behind the scam are protecting it: by issuing takedown notices.

When you visit a website and see a message that it has been taken down or suspended, it’s usually because the domain has been associated with malicious or illegal activity, including phishing and scams. Law enforcement agencies, often with help from technology firms, find these websites, obtain the necessary court orders, and seize the infrastructure or accounts behind them to force a suspension through a registrar or hosting provider.

You see the notice, you move on — and this also goes for scanners and security teams.

However, in this case, the scam artists created a mechanism to show it themselves, to avoid being flagged and taken down. But it’s when they show it that matters.

The false suspension notice appears deceptively simple at first glance. A phishing kit embedded in the domain registers the IP address of each visitor, and on their second visit, they are shown a fake “account suspended” page. The kit is disguised as a challenge page similar to those legitimately used by Cloudflare.

Researchers also detected other decoys, including “Sorry, you’ve been blocked” notices, blank pages, and redirections to Google. Some pages also have reCAPTCHAs, and the researchers detected scripts that block browser developer tools, likely to slow analysis.

But why show fake suspension notices in the first place? To scanners and cybersecurity teams, it looks like someone else has already dealt with the malicious domain, and they can move on to the next suspicious website.

“That design targets exactly the people who could shut a site down,” KELA says. “A victim typically follows a link once. A scanner, a security team or a takedown provider checks a reported site and then checks it again to confirm, and the second check shows what looks like a completed takedown.”

As shown in the image below, while scanners would detect a suspension notice, victims would still see the cloned website, complete with lures to entice them into entering their information, leading to financial fraud and potentially identity theft. After all, one visit is enough.

As of this writing, there are 248 live domains, plus those taken offline after the researchers disclosed their findings to law enforcement; over 300 scam domains have been hosted on the same infrastructure since May this year. Unfortunately, this phishing campaign remains active, with new domains appearing every day.

One lead could reveal the operator: a remote desktop connection to the malicious infrastructure through a standard UK home broadband connection, but it has not been verified.

The Bambu Lab H3C is a large-format 3D printer with nine printheads, and it's available now.

Multicolor 3D printing has been around for many years. Like all technologies, manufacturers are constantly looking for ways to push it further than ever before. Bambu Lab announced today the H3C, a large-format 3D printer that uses nine separate nozzles to print not only different colors or types, but also different-sized filaments. It’s designed to allow you to mix and match as you see fit, expanding your creative options beyond the standard.

The Bambu Lab H3C starts at $2,500 and comes with the nine-filament system and Automated Material System. I’ve spent the last few weeks using it, and while I haven’t finished my review yet, I already have quite a few thoughts.

This is not a small machine by any measure. I originally had it on my workbench, but the H3C sits so tall that opening the lid and loading the filament was arduous, even for a 6-foot-1 person like myself. Bambu Lab also sent me a metal rolling cart, sold separately, that lowers the machine to a usable height. That said, it still stands 63 inches tall on the cart, so yeah, it’s a big’un.

Despite its size and complex design, setting it up was simple. I would recommend having a second person around to do it, as it’s cumbersome — but not difficult to plug in all the parts. The AMS 3 that sits atop the machine is different from most of the best 3D printers because the H3C requires it operate. It’s not a separate add-on but an integral part of the machine.

You can tell from every metric that the H3C is designed to be a Bambu Lab flagship. It has all the top-of-the-line hardware, including a heatbed that can reach 120C and an optional but very cool bird’s-eye camera to monitor prints from above. It’s fast, too, with a top toolhead speed of 1,400 mm/s. Of course, that isn’t the print speed, but it does show you the type of speed it could reach if you pushed it to the max.

Printing at high speed does have some disadvantages, though. I have had several print failures on tall prints where the supports or purge tower have fallen due to accidental touches or vibration. The H3C moves fast, prints fast and fails fast if you aren’t careful. Most of those failures occurred at 125% speed, but that’s the standard setting I use for large prints on other machines, and they handle it just fine.

Bambu calls its nine-nozzle system, which heats using conductive heating, the Swaptek system. This isn’t to be confused with another nine-nozzle system you may have heard of: INDX, which uses conductive heating. INDX and Swaptek are extremely similar in execution but differ in a few areas. INDX is purely mechanical, whereas Bambu says its Swaptek system supplies data and power to each tool, allowing the nozzles to start heating faster than those in a standard INDX system — in around 6 seconds per nozzle.

In practice, there isn’t a huge difference between what I’m seeing on the H3C and the Prusa Core One + INDX in terms of speed. The Core One has the advantage that it’s calibrated to avoid a purge tower, and it doesn’t purge as much waste as the Swaptek system. The H3C has its own neat tricks, though: Not only can it print with different nozzle sizes and materials, but it can also print with materials of different diameters.

One of the biggest roadblocks with printing TPU — the very soft, rubbery material that 3D-printed shoes are made from — through an AMS is that it’s too soft to push through the AMS feeder. To combat this, you have to use a harder TPU that isn’t nearly as flexible as you would need for shoes or things that bounce. Adding toolheads capable of printing 2.85mm-diameter materials lets Bambu create TPU that’s more flexible after melting but thicker and easier to feed in the AMS. It works really well.

And it doesn’t stop at TPU, either. You can use 2.85mm PLA with a 0.8mm nozzle to print the infill of a model, while using a 0.2mm nozzle and 1.7mm PLA to print the detailed outer walls. It gives you so many options. The AMS even has two material slots with active heating chambers, so you can print materials that need to be warm and dry, along with other, more shelf-stable materials.

Given the $2,500 price and the H3C’s flagship status, it would be shocking if the print quality were bad. All of the models that didn’t fail due to purge tower collapse look great, and the nine separate nozzles make light work of just about any color project. I love watching this machine print as it swaps across the nozzles at blistering speeds.

Some of them did fail, though, and the H3C was good at catching the errors, pausing the print and letting me know. I chose to keep one of them going despite the failed tower, and it turned out fine, though a little damaged. I had some fun 3D printing my scanned head at life size, using silver for my greying beard — and I love the way it came out.

The CNET test print wasn’t nearly as good as the one the H2C produced, which pretty much maxed out every test, but it wasn’t the worst either. Accuracy was good, and despite the printer’s speed, ringing was kept to a minimum. It struggled on the 0.2mm tolerance test, but it did well on bridging and overhangs, showing that its temperature control is excellent. I still have more of these to do before I’m ready to give a complete score, but so far, I’m happy with the result.

In video games, there’s a concept called endgame content: the harder challenges you tackle after completing most of the story. It’s not for the faint of heart. The H3C is endgame content. It isn’t hard to use, but it’s designed for someone who has gone as far as possible with machines such as the P2S and A1 combos of the world. If you’re looking to go beyond what you thought you could do with a 3D printer, the H3C is the machine to get you there.

Swaptek, INDX and multi-toolhead printers are here to stay. And, like all 3D printers, they can come in many sizes and budgets. The Core One INDX is smaller but cheaper, but the H3C has a lot of options right out of the box to make some incredibly complex models. We live in interesting times and the landscape can only get better.

A wireless implant that clears excess sugar from the blood could someday offer a drug-free way to treat type 2 diabetes and obesity.

In mice with both conditions, the under-the-skin device brought high blood sugar back to normal within 60 to 90 minutes of being switched on, researchers report October 22 in Science. After six weeks, the treated mice lost up to 10 percent of their body weight and had lower triglycerides, a type of fat in the blood.

We summarize the week's scientific breakthroughs every Thursday.

Diabetes affects about 830 million people worldwide. Conventional type 2 diabetes treatments like insulin and GLP-1 drugs require careful dosing. Even experimental diabetes-controlling implants still rely on the hormone insulin, which clears sugar from the bloodstream but doesn’t rid the body of it, says Martin Fussenegger, a bioengineer at ETH Zurich. The new implant instead removes glucose from the body without added drugs, genes or living cells.

The 3-centimeter-long device contains a catalyst made of gold flakes and platinum particles on spongelike graphite. When electricity powers it, the catalyst converts glucose into gluconate, a compound that is easier for the kidneys to flush out in urine. With less glucose available for energy, the body may turn to its fat stores instead, Fussenegger and colleagues suspect. In mice, that shift showed up as higher blood levels of chemicals released when fat breaks down, such as ketones. “This system kind of targets the root cause, which is the energy surplus by glucose in the body,” Fussenegger says.

The implant works like an automatic sugar-removal system: A wearable glucose monitor detects high blood sugar levels and sends readings through a smartphone that switches the implant on. When levels return to a safer range, it switches off. The automated system ran for two weeks in eight mice. The catalyst itself remained able to convert glucose after up to three months under their skin, though the study didn’t establish how long the entire device would last.

And it’s still unknown how well the device would work in people. “This is a potentially promising method,” says David Klonoff, an endocrinologist at Sutter Mills-Peninsula Medical Center in Burlingame, Calif., who was not involved in the study. “But the risks are significant and must be carefully ruled out before it can be safely tested on humans.”

Researchers need to determine how much gluconate the kidneys can clear, Klonoff says. If it builds up, it could disrupt the body’s acid-base balance, causing symptoms such as nausea or confusion; excreting it could also deplete electrolytes such as sodium and potassium, he notes.

Scaling up the device is another challenge. Humans consume about 250 grams of carbohydrates daily, compared with two grams for mice, Klonoff estimates. Meeting that demand could require a much larger implant or multiple devices, he says. In a lab test, a larger device cleared nearly all glucose from about 5 liters of solution, roughly an adult’s blood volume, though not a full day’s glucose intake.

Fussenegger’s team plans to test the device in pigs and sheep before considering human trials. If proven safe and effective, it could offer an alternative to existing diabetes treatments and a new weight-loss option for people regardless of whether they have diabetes, he says.

The campaign aims to educate people facing cancer diagnoses and their loved ones about how biomarker testing may help doctors make more informed, personalized treatment decisions. It also encourages patients to discuss testing with their healthcare teams.

“Biomarkers may tell doctors the specific type of cancer present and if targeted therapies or immunotherapy might help,” Mark Socinski, MD, oncologist and Executive Medical Director of the AdventHealth Cancer Institute, told Healthline.

He explained that biomarkers are typically genes or proteins that doctors measure through blood or tissue tests. They are used to help guide treatment in numerous diseases but are especially powerful tools in many types of cancer.
