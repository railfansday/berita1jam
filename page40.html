The uncertain 2026-27 Major League Baseball offseason is fully underway. The ongoing CBA negotiations and the near certainty of an owner-implemented lockout on Dec. 2 loom over all, and it may indeed mean a flurry of trades and free agent signings leading up to that date. That was certainly the case leading up to the last lockout of 2021-22, and this time around we've already seen some leading examples. Just recently, for instance, the New York Mets landed the biggest fish in the pond by signing ace Tarik Skubal to a record contract. Not long after, the Chicago Cubs fortified their rotation with Trevor Rogers and their outfield with Teruaki Sato, and Freddy Peralta is headed to the Baltimore Orioles. More's surely to come before lockout day arrives.

Thanks to that prospect of a hastened pace, offseason rumors are flowing. Speaking of which, we'll be here each day to round up what's out there. You can find Tuesday's supply just below.

The Philadelphia Phillies are open to moving Trea Turner off of shortstop given that he's now a pronounced defensive liability. Speaking of openness, veteran shortstop Corey Seager of the Texas Rangers is willing to waive his no-trade clause to join the Phillies and upgrade the position for them, NBC Sports Philadelphia reports.

Now 32, Seager will always have health and durability concerns, but he remains a plus defensive shortstop who produces well at the plate by positional standards. Signed to a 10-year, $325 million deal prior to the 2022 season (just before the last lockout, in fact), Seager is owed another $155 million through the 2031 season. The Rangers are reportedly looking to shake things up. The Phillies, meantime, are trying to keep their contention window open. It's a fit both ways, at least in theory. Such a move would likely mean a move to the outfield for Turner, who himself has very little trade value thanks to his ongoing decline and big contract.

The Cleveland Guardians have agreed to a free-agent contract with left-hander Kris Bubic, Sportsnet reports. The contract is for $17.5 million over one year, according to ESPN. Bubic, 29, made nine starts for the Kansas City Royals last season, and after the trade to the Los Angeles Dodgers, he was used out of the bullpen. Bubic has an outlier fastball thanks to its rise and his unusual release point, and he's an interesting "ceiling" candidate in the rotation. However, his substantial history of injuries may mean he's best used out of the bullpen.

The Detroit Tigers have added infield depth by agreeing to terms with Ha-Seong Kim on a one-year, $5 million free agent contract, ESPN reports. Kim, 31, is coming off a rough partial season with the Atlanta Braves, but he has a more palatable OPS+ of 91 for his career. He's been a primary shortstop in recent years and also has significant time spent at second base and third base.

Even after achieving a reunion with veteran starting pitcher Sonny Gray, the Minnesota Twins are still looking to fortify their roster for a return to contention in 2027. The club still hopes to make one more significant addition this offseason and add another player who may not grab as many headlines, the Athletic reports. Atop the wishlist are a first baseman or a second baseman and another lefty reliever.

Even after the splash addition of Skubal, the Mets may not be done adding starting pitchers in the service of a rebound season in 2027. On that front, the Mets may be interested in adding a starting pitcher on a short-term deal for next season, the Athletic reports. That would likely limit them to the back half of the market, especially with so many bigger names reaching agreements.

Petunias reveal an unexpected link between flower color and scent

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists studying petunias discovered that the same biological machinery responsible for flower color also shapes their scent.

Petunias use the same raw ingredient, an amino acid called phenylalanine, to make pigments and many of their scent compounds.

Researchers at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem found that a protein called EOBV works with two proteins involved in pigmentation to regulate the flower’s scent.

When the researchers removed this protein, the flowers released more compounds associated with the smell of cloves and vanilla.

However, they released less methyl benzoate, normally the most abundant scent compound in these petunias.

Oded Skaliter and Alexander Vainstein ran all of it in petunias. The chemistry moved. Nobody has checked whether a nose, human or moth, can tell the difference.

Wild petunias have different ways of attracting pollinators. One species releases its fragrance at night to attract moths, another attracts bees with purple flowers, and a third uses red flowers to attract hummingbirds.

Petunia pigment is run by a committee of three, known to biologists as the MBW complex.

Two of these proteins, AN1 and AN11, remain the same. The third can be any one of several proteins belonging to a family called MYB, and its identity determines which genes the group controls.

Skaliter and his colleagues examined when AN1 and AN11 were most active.

Activity peaked as the flowers opened and remained high for another two days, around the same time that scent production reached its peak.

Then they knocked each gene down, one at a time, with a plant virus. Air from around the petals went into the analyzer.

Both treatments increased the release of eugenol and vanillin. The amount of methyl benzoate roughly doubled.

Rather than helping the flowers produce more fragrance, the pigment proteins appeared to hold scent production back.

The researchers suspected that the missing piece of the puzzle was the third protein in the group.

Six MYB proteins were already in the frame here, all of them previously linked to petunia fragrance.

Each went up against AN1 and AN11 in yeast cells that change color when two proteins bind. EOBV was the only hit.

The team repeated the experiment in living plant tissue. This time, they used a system that produces betalain, the red pigment found in beets, when proteins interact.

Leaves of a tobacco relative turned red when they received both AN1 and EOBV. Leaves that received AN1 alone produced a weaker color signal.

The strongest red color appeared when all three proteins were present. Removing any one of them greatly reduced pigment production in the test.

AN11 and EOBV would not bind each other. AN1 had to stand between them.

The researchers also found very little EOBV gene activity outside the petals. Within the petals, activity was about 1.8 times higher in the upper surface layer, through which fragrance escapes.

Previous work from the same laboratory found that a gene responsible for giving petals their identity also activates scent production.

The next step was to remove EOBV completely. A virus carried CRISPR gene-editing tools into petunia tissue, and the team recovered three groups of edited plants.

In one group, the instructions for starting the protein were gone, along with the first 42 amino acids. Production in the other two stopped after 58 and 91 amino acids.

The edited plants still looked and flowered like the originals.

Two-day-old flowers then went into sealed jars, two flowers to a jar, and whatever they gave off over the next 24 hours was trapped and measured.

Without a working version of EOBV, the flowers released more eugenol, both forms of isoeugenol and vanillin. These compounds give cloves and vanilla their familiar aromas.

However, the flowers released less of almost every other scent compound, including methyl benzoate. The total amount of fragrance released also fell.

The team then ground up petals to measure how much of each compound remained inside. The clove and vanilla compounds had increased there, too.

The petals contained similar amounts of methyl benzoate and several other scent compounds even though the edited flowers released less of them.

A repressor has to act on something, so the next question was which genes EOBV holds down. Gene activity was compared between edited and unedited flowers.

Most showed little difference. However, two genes, known as C4H2 and ADT3, became more active after the researchers removed EOBV.

C4H2 controls the carbon supply to the branch that produces eugenol, isoeugenol and vanillin.

The rise in C4H2 gene activity may help explain why the edited flowers made more eugenol, isoeugenol, and vanillin.

The team then tested that link directly. They connected the region of DNA that controls C4H2 activity to a system that produces red pigment.

Petals that received EOBV showed no visible red color. Petals without the additional EOBV produced more than twice as much pigment.

Two other fragrance genes, PAAS and BSMT, became less active in the edited flowers. Since EOBV does not appear to bind directly to the regions controlling these genes, the changes were probably indirect.

Researchers found a similar pattern in 2011 when they reduced the activity of another petunia gene called PhMYB4. PhMYB4 and EOBV are about 80% identical. Genetic testing confirmed that PhMYB4 itself had not been edited.

EOBV becomes more active when petunias grow in warmer conditions. The researchers wanted to see whether removing it could protect flowers from heat.

They grew the plants in a greenhouse for a month before moving them into two rooms. The cooler room had temperatures of 72°F (22°C) during the day and 61°F (16°C) at night. The warmer room reached 82°F (28°C) and 72°F (22°C), respectively.

After measuring 38 flowers, the team found that unedited flowers became 12% narrower in warmer conditions. Edited flowers shrank by only 6%.

Heat also reduced the amount of scent released by unedited flowers, mainly due to lower emissions of benzaldehyde and methyl benzoate. Edited flowers maintained their overall scent output.

Meanwhile, PhEXP1, a gene that helps petal cells expand, became less active in both groups by similar amounts.

Heat-shrunk petunia flowers were traced to slower cell division in a 2021 study, a step this team did not measure.

More vanilla and clove compounds might sound appealing, but would these petunias actually smell better?

Nobody has asked people to smell the edited flowers. The researchers haven’t tested pollinators either.

Removing EOBV also has limits. In purple varieties, reducing EOBV activity left pigment levels unchanged.

EOBV belongs to the color machinery without fixing the shade of purple itself.

The team also crossed the edited plants with petunias lacking EVER, another scent regulator. Those flowers did not outperform either parent. The authors suggest limited carbon, or a limited capacity to release scent.

The authors also disclosed a commercial interest. Skaliter and Vainstein are chief technology officer and chief scientific officer at CapsiBreed, which uses gene editing in hot and sweet peppers. Peppers and petunias are relatives.

One obvious next experiment is to switch EOBV off in plants that are already missing AN1 or AN11.

The team also wants to know whether other flowering plants are wired this way. And growers will want an answer to a blunter question: does a reshuffled scent give them flowers that anyone wants to buy?

The full study was published in the journal Plant Physiology.
