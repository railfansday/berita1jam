Google Fi wireless customers with Pixel phones just got a serious upgrade to their mobile and Wi-Fi connections for free.

Google has announced two new “super network” connection features for Fi Wireless: one that automatically switches between two of the largest 5G cellular networks, and another that automatically connects to premium Wi-Fi in millions of locations without you needing to log in.

The features are rolling out today for Pixel 9 phones and newer models on every Google Fi plan. If you’re not a current Fi customer, you can sign up for a free seven-day trial to test it alongside your current carrier.

Here’s how the new mobile connection feature works: if your main connection slows or becomes unstable, Fi will automatically move to a secondary connection. This not only means you’ll have the fastest option when your connection is good, but you’ll have fewer dead zones when service is limited.

The feature will run on MEP (Multiple Enabled Profiles) technology, which allows a single eSIM chip to host and run multiple SIM profiles simultaneously. Footnotes in Google’s release note that you’ll need to have “any non-Fi SIMs disabled.”

Google Fi originally offered automatic switching between two networks when the service debuted a decade ago, but it was between Sprint and T-Mobile, which merged in 2020. Google didn’t reveal what the “two of America’s best 5G cellular networks” were, but ZDNET has reached out to ask. The service usually runs on T-Mobile. Along with T-Mobile, the three biggest carriers are AT&T and Verizon, so it’s likely the second carrier is one of those.

In addition to a mobile connection upgrade, users are getting better Wi-Fi, too. Wi-Fi Auto Connect+ will automatically connect you to faster Wi-Fi in millions of locations like airports and malls (Google defines “faster” as two times better reliability and 30% improved latency compared to normal Google Fi cellular networks) without you having to log in or head to your settings. These connections will be automatically encrypted, Google said, keeping your online activity safe.

Carelon will pay more than $10 million across two settlements in New York over mental health networks and coverage denials.

The company, a division of Elevance Health, will pay $2.35 million in penalties, fees and costs as well as up to $8 million in restitution for members who paid out-of-pocket for out-of-network mental healthcare, to reimburse those whose claims were improperly denied or to overhaul its networks.

New York Attorney General Letitia James said in an announcement that the settlements resolve two investigations from her office that determined the company misled members by directing them to seek care from providers that were out-of-network—through so-called "ghost networks"—and improperly denied coverage for treatment for substance abuse disorder.

Across these insurers, Carelon manages behavioral health benefits for more than 4 million people in the state, James said.

"These settlements will help people get the care they need and put money back in New Yorkers’ pockets," she said.

Elevance Health had not responded to a request for comment at the time of publication.

Carelon was hit with a lawsuit in April over the alleged ghost networks and their impact on the Empire Plan in particular. In the case, plaintiff's attorneys conducted secret shopper calls to analyze Carelon's network, calling 300 providers. Just 17% of those contacted took insurance and were open to new patients.

These "ghost networks" have also drawn concern in other markets, such as for maternal care services in Medicaid and in Medicare Advantage. A study released last month found that commercial plans also routinely have inaccurate provider directories.

Nonprofit insurer CareSource is launching a $1 million program that aims to back responsible AI solutions in healthcare.

The initiative, launched through the CareSource Foundation, is seeking proposals for ways to advance and identify deployments for AI that can drive better outcomes and address barriers for individuals enrolled in Medicaid or who otherwise struggle to access care.

The program will provide up to $1 million over the course of two years to ensure the tool can deliver on meaningful and scalable benefits for the communities it serves.

Erhardt Preitauer, CEO of CareSource, said in an email to Fierce Healthcare that AI offers the potential to improve not just patient care but the experience for providers too as they navigate complex conditions.

"I'm a big believer that when technology, healthcare and the people in our communities rally around a shared purpose, magic can happen," said Preitauer. "Leaving no stone unturned is the only way we can make healthcare work better for people, and AI gives us the power to explore every possibility."

The program will include two phases. First, interested applicants can submit shorter proposals, with the goal of generating interest. Applications that are selected to move on will be tasked with submitting a more fleshed out proposal, including details on implementation, AI governance and sustainability.

CareSource is aiming to identify solutions that ready for implementation and has the potential for scale. Potential focus areas include care coordination, member engagement, behavioral health and rural healthcare. Solutions should use AI to support care delivery but it should not replace human judgement or oversight, the insurer said.

To be eligible, a qualified nonprofit organization must be leading the effort, according to the announcement. Technology companies, academic researchers, healthcare organizations and community-based groups can also play a role as partners.

Applicants moving to the second phase of the program will also be required to showcase data privacy and security, solutions to address and prevent bias and ongoing performance tracking.

1 in 3 Infants Diagnosed With Peanut Allergy May Not Have the Condition

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Peanut allergies have been on the rise in recent decades.

According to statistics provided by Food Allergy Research & Education (FARE), an estimated 6.2 million children and adults in the United States are allergic to peanuts.

However, new research published in the November 2026 issue of The Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology suggests that over a third of infants thought to have this diagnosis might not actually have a peanut allergy.

In fact, having a history of reacting to peanuts and positive allergy testing on their own may not be sufficient to diagnose a peanut allergy for many infants.

The study’s authors suggest that a threshold oral food challenge — in which the child is given increasing amounts of the suspected food allergen under medical supervision to look for an allergic reaction — should be routinely considered to help confirm peanut allergy.

This additional testing could safely prevent children who are not allergic to peanuts from undergoing unnecessary treatment with oral immunotherapy.

It could also spare families from burdensome food restrictions and allergy precautions.

The researchers’ goal was to evaluate the accuracy and safety of the Allergy Development to an Accelerated Path to Tolerance (ADAPT) peanut oral immunotherapy program, which was established in 2024 at 10 public children’s hospitals across Australia.

This program provides a standardized approach to evaluating and treating infants with suspected peanut allergies.

The study included infants who had experienced a mild to moderate reaction to peanut before 12 months of age and had evidence of peanut sensitization through allergy testing.

Researchers reviewed medical records to examine how accurately the program’s approach identified peanut allergies and whether it could safely guide treatment.

A key part of the evaluation was a threshold oral food challenge. The challenge involved up to seven doses, ranging from 15 to 1,300 milligrams of peanut protein.

Medical teams monitored the infants for reactions and provided treatment when necessary.

Researchers reviewed records from April 2024 through October 2025. During that period, 1,275 infants were assessed through the program, and 778 underwent an oral food challenge.

The researchers also examined allergy test results and whether infants who reacted during the challenges could begin oral immunotherapy at an appropriate starting dose.

At the time of the oral food challenge, the infants had a median age of 13 months.

Of the 778 infants who underwent an oral food challenge, 507 (65.2%) had an allergic reaction.

However, 271 (34.8%) did not react to peanut during the supervised test, despite having a history of a previous reaction and positive allergy test results.

These findings suggest that relying on medical history and allergy tests alone may lead some infants to be labeled with a peanut allergy when they can actually tolerate the food.

Among the infants who reacted during the challenges, 61 experienced anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction that can affect breathing and blood pressure. This represented 12% of infants with a positive challenge and 7.8% of all infants who underwent testing. Medical teams safely managed these severe allergic reactions.

Researchers also found that infants who reacted to peanut generally had higher skin prick test results and Ara h 2-specific IgE levels than those who did not react.

However, these test results did not reliably indicate how much peanut would trigger a reaction or how severe that reaction would be.

Of the 394 infants who began peanut oral immunotherapy, 384 (97.5%) successfully started treatment at a dose below the amount that had triggered a reaction during their food challenge.

The researchers say these findings support considering supervised oral food challenges to confirm suspected peanut allergies before starting treatment or recommending long-term avoidance.

Erin Banta, MD, a board certified allergist and immunologist who treats both children and adults at Latitude Food Allergy Care in Great Neck, New York, said these findings are significant because they provide evidence for early use of oral food challenges in infants with peanut reactions.

“In those with mild-moderate prior reactions to peanut, early oral food challenge may allow for either delabeling of food allergy (in 1/3 of the patients in this study) or diagnosis of food allergy,” she told Healthline.

Banta, who wasn’t involved in the study, added that early delabeling takes a significant burden off the patient and their family.

“On the other hand,” she explained, “early diagnosis allows for early initiation of treatment with oral immunotherapy at a time in the patient’s life associated with the greatest success rates.”

Banta further noted that the study offers different pathways for oral immunotherapy based on the patient’s reaction threshold. This means that some patients may be able to complete treatment more quickly due to their individual threshold dose.

Lindsey Forstie, FNP, a family nurse practitioner with clinical training and experience in allergy and asthma care at Carefree Allergy in Phoenix, AZ, added that while further evaluation may be needed to confirm a peanut allergy diagnosis, it’s important for parents not to try introducing peanuts at home.

“Instead, they should speak with their pediatrician or allergist about appropriate testing and whether a supervised food challenge is safe and necessary,” she said.

According to Forstie, who was also not part of the study, the goal is to avoid unnecessary food restrictions while also keeping children safe.

“The key message is that not every positive allergy test means a child has a food allergy,” she told Healthline. “Accurate diagnosis helps protect children while allowing those who are not allergic to enjoy a more normal, less restrictive diet.”

The Agentic Shift: How Autonomous AI Agents Are Transforming Human Resources

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Agentic Shift: How Autonomous AI Agents Are Transforming Human Resources

Human Resources is experiencing a profound architectural shift. While the first wave of enterprise artificial intelligence focused on generative text and passive chatbots—answering standard FAQ questions or drafting job descriptions—the modern workplace is embracing agentic AI. Unlike traditional automation that follows rigid if-then rules, AI agents possess contextual reasoning, autonomy, and multi-step execution capabilities. In HR, autonomous agents are evolving from administrative assistants into active operational partners that streamline recruitment, personalize employee experiences, and proactively support talent retention.

Recruiting has historically suffered from manual screening bottlenecks and fragmented applicant communication. Agentic workflows fundamentally redesign this funnel:

Dynamic Candidate Sourcing: Instead of waiting for applicants or running static boolean keyword searches, agentic sourcing systems continuously analyze industry repositories, professional networks, and open-source contributions. They evaluate technical competencies, trajectory, and cultural alignment against real-time workforce planning data.

Autonomous Pre-Screening and Scheduling: Sourcing agents interact with candidates conversationally across messaging channels, verifying prerequisite qualifications, handling mutual calendar coordination, and answering candidate questions about compensation benchmarks and team culture 24/7.

Objective Candidate Shortlisting: By anonymizing candidate profiles during early assessment rounds and focusing strictly on verifiable project outcomes and technical assessments, AI agents reduce unconscious bias in initial talent screening.

Traditional onboarding often overwhelms new hires with compliance checklists, disparate portal logins, and static policy documents. An onboarding agent acts as a dedicated 90-day digital concierge:

Workflow Orchestration: The agent coordinates across IT, facilities, and department heads to provision hardware, assign software licenses, and schedule introductory peer check-ins before Day One.

Contextual Knowledge Retrieval: Rather than forcing employees to search hundreds of pages in an intranet wiki, the agent answers nuanced inquiries—such as healthcare tier eligibility, 401(k) vesting timelines, or travel reimbursement policies—tailored specifically to the employee's jurisdiction and job level.

Check-In Cadence: The agent proactively prompts new hires at days 7, 30, and 60 to gauge onboarding satisfaction, identify learning friction, and notify team leads when extra support is needed.

Annual performance reviews are notoriously backward-looking and time-consuming. Agentic HR tools shift organizations toward continuous coaching and internal mobility:

Real-Time Feedback Synthesis: Agents analyze structured feedback loops across project management tools, peer acknowledgments, and goal milestones to provide managers with objective performance summaries ahead of 1-on-1 coaching sessions.

Dynamic Skills Gap Analysis: As market requirements evolve, talent agents cross-reference employee project histories with company strategic priorities, autonomously recommending targeted learning paths and internal gig opportunities.

Proactive Career Pathing: Employees receive personalized recommendations for lateral transfers, certifications, and mentorship pairings that match their long-term professional aspirations.

Employee turnover often catches leadership by surprise. Autonomous agents provide early-warning signals while respecting personal data boundaries:

Anonymized Engagement Pulse: Instead of relying solely on annual engagement surveys, agents analyze aggregated sentiment across anonymized pulse surveys and workplace communications to detect team-level burnout or cultural friction points.

Predictive Flight-Risk Triggers: By correlating macro indicators—such as stagnant tenure in grade, market compensation divergence, and project fatigue—agents alert HR business partners to intervene proactively with tailored retention or role-refresh conversations.

With autonomous agency comes the vital responsibility of ethical stewardship. Organizations deploying HR agents must maintain strict governance guardrails:

Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) for High-Stakes Decisions: AI agents should recommend, summarize, and execute administrative workflows, but all consequential personnel decisions—such as hiring, termination, disciplinary action, and compensation adjustments—must remain strictly in the hands of human leaders.

Algorithmic Transparency & Auditability: Every decision tree, ranking score, and recommendation made by an agent must be auditable, explainable, and compliant with emerging employment regulations (such as NYC Local Law 144 and EU AI Act employment standards).

Data Privacy and Segregation: Employee personal data, medical leaves, and performance notes must be strictly partitioned with enterprise-grade encryption and role-based access control, ensuring external language models are never trained on proprietary organizational data.

The emergence of agentic AI does not eliminate the human element in Human Resources; rather, it liberates HR professionals from routine operational overhead. By deploying autonomous agents to handle repetitive coordination, continuous data aggregation, and real-time support, HR leaders can reclaim their essential role as strategic advisors, culture builders, and empathetic mentors. Organizations that balance agentic efficiency with rigorous ethical oversight will build the most agile, resilient, and human-centric workforces of the future.
