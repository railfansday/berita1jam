Jim Harbaugh: Broncos took advantage of a loophole the NFL should not allow

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Chargers head coach Jim Harbaugh is not happy with the way the Broncos backed out of a trade for Chargers linebacker Daiyan Henley, or with the NFL rules that allowed them to do so.

After agreeing to trade a fourth-round pick and a fifth-round pick for Henley, the Broncos told Chargers GM Joe Hortiz they couldn’t go through with it because Henley’s physical revealed concerns about his knee. The Broncos were still willing to trade for Henley, but wanted to give up less. Harbaugh believes physical exams should only be to determine whether or not the player is healthy, not to renegotiate trade terms.

“What happened with the trade? Here’s what I know, from talking to Joe: The Broncos based a pass-fail on a medical grade based on whether we would take less compensation, and we wouldn’t and we didn’t,” Harbaugh said. “In my opinion it’s one of those loopholes that the league should really look into, because it sets a bad precedent.”

Asked if it’s permitted for teams to renegotiate trades after the trade deadline, Harbaugh answered, “It shouldn’t be, but apparently it is. That’s why it’s a loophole, and teams should not have the ability to do that.”

Harbaugh said the Broncos offered to say Henley passed his physical, but only if the Chargers would take something less than the fifth-round pick the Broncos had agreed to.

“It’s a medical grade. It’s a pass-fail for a medical grade, and they told us they weren’t going to pass him if it’s a fourth and a fifth, which was agreed upon — they would not pass him for that. They would pass him for a fourth and a fill in the blank. I don’t know what to call that, but that’s what it is,” Harbaugh said.

Harbaugh is right that the NFL has set a bad precedent. By allowing teams to renegotiate trade terms after the deadline, the NFL is incentivizing teams to engage in chicanery on physical exams to squeeze their trade partners into taking less favorable deals. The Chargers refused to budge, but the NFL rules shouldn’t have put the Chargers in a position where they had to make that decision.

England are on course to record their best ever performance in the Nations League, having beaten the Czech Republic twice and Croatia during the autumn international break.

Including its inaugural season in 2018/19, the Three Lions have competed in every single Nations League campaign, experiencing some highs and the lowest of lows.

From topping their group and qualifying for the Nations League finals and finishing third to suffering a shock relegation in 2022/23 and then immediate promotion, England have done almost everything except win the competition.

Having started the 2026/27 campaign with a loss to Spain, despite leading 2-1 at one stage, England bounced back in style by beating the Czechs twice and then thumping Croatia 7-0 in a remarkable, record-breaking rout. Another win over Croatia followed at Wembley on Thursday night to confirm a top-two finish in the group and qualify for the Nations League knockout stage.

England now face a one-game shootout with Spain to decide who will win the group, and an easier quarter-final tie next March.

The four group winners will face the four runners-up in a two-legged Nations League quarter-final.

The group winners will all be seeded and will have home advantage in their respective second leg ties. Teams from the same group cannot be paired together in the knockout stage, therefore England and Spain - for example - would be kept apart.

As things stand, if England finish second in Group 3, their potential Nations League quarter-final opponents would be Italy, Netherlands or Portugal.

Topping the group would set up a possible quarter-final showdown with France, Greece or Denmark.

The draw for the 2026/27 Nations League quarter-final will take place on Friday, November 20 with the ties scheduled to take place in March 2027.

Why John Lennon Is Responsible for Wings’ Biggest Hit

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

On April 9, 1970, the entire world sat down to read one slip of paper. By the time they reached the end of the press release, if they could still see through tear-blurred eyes, one conclusion was drawn: Paul McCartney was leaving The Beatles.

In hindsight, it's not really that surprising McCartney felt called to split from the pack. The "Fab Four" had a less than ideal relationship with each other, specifically the band's two workhorse songwriters, John Lennon and Paul McCartney. If you find yourself looking up the term "creative differences" in a dictionary, there's a non-zero chance there's a picture of Lennon and McCartney hurling insults at each other like grenades during a writing session. But it was this conflicting nature that led to McCartney's most popular post-Beatles song, and one that even Lennon had to give him props for.

During the sessions for 1968's The White Album, tensions rose so high that Ringo Starr — synonymous with the mantra "peace and love" — had to take a two-week sabbatical from the studio. But while Starr was merely just caught in the crossfire, it was John Lennon and Paul McCartney on the frontlines against each other.

Both sides let this boil over into the press. In that very same press release announcing his departure from The Beatles, McCartney included a "self interview" detailing his next steps in his solo career, as well as promotional copies of his solo debut album, McCartney. John Lennon made it known that he didn't care for his former bandmates' actions, almost as much as he hated his songs, saying McCartney would "drag his feet," when discussing business with him and referring to him as an "egomaniac," in a 1971 interview with Rolling Stone.

It's impossible to deny that both of the frontmen wielded a golden pen, but they were also a rare case of utter opposite sides of the songwriting spectrum. Lennon always tried to force the audience to see the world through his eyes, attributing his reality to the world in the anti-establishment "Imagine," or "Instant Karma!," a threat-shaped single aimed at his harshest music critic. McCartney, on the other hand, was a vessel of pure unadulterated creativity, someone who was just gnawing at the chain to keep making more and more music. While he was undoubtedly productive, he was often seen as oafish in comparison to his writing partner, lacking true purpose in his songs. He even admitted in his 1977 autobiography that he made up the lyrics to "Jet" with no real meaning in mind, although he has claimed the song to be about a pregnant dog, a pony and meeting his wife Linda's father for the first time.

Both of their reputations followed them out of The Beatles, but while Lennon was lauded by critics as a revolutionary, McCartney was pushed to the side, and just dismissed as another pop star that had nothing real to say. The media would continue to push the narrative that Paul McCartney was just a pretty face with no good stories to tell. But even someone as charming as Sir Paul McCartney has their breaking point. Rolling Stone dubbed his 1971 project Ram, "so inconsequential and so monumentally irrelevant," that you couldn't even focus enough on the songs to decide whether you hated them or not. America's self-appointed "only Rock 'n Roll magazine," Creem, called him "stereotypical and vapid," after Wings' showing on ABC-TV.

And the final straw came, of course, from an all too familiar source.

"There were accusations in the mid-1970's — including one from John [Lennon] — that I was just writing 'silly love songs'," McCartney said in a 2021 autobiography, The Lyrics: 1956 to the Present.

Most songwriters would've found themselves intimidated by the pressures of falling into a typecast. But thankfully, Paul McCartney is not most songwriters. McCartney took the very words that had been belittling him, and gave them an entirely different meaning. In March 1976, Paul McCartney and Wings released the first single for their Speed of Sound album: "Silly Love Songs."

McCartney's musical prowess had never been in question — the instrumental for the track has the signature brightness of a Wings song, spearheaded by a classically captivating McCartney bassline. But after being dragged through the dirt for years about his lyrics having no substance, every line in the song is packed with purpose.

From the opening lines (Some people want to fill the world with silly love songs / But I look around me and I see it isn't so) to the bridge refrain, (How can I tell you about my loved one?) the entire track feels less like a lead single from one of the biggest pop stars on the planet, and more of a strongly worded entry into McCartney's personal diary. It's so expertly written and still filled with McCartney's tongue-in-cheek sense of humor: just repeating "I love you" as a chorus comes across as a wry wink to those trying to pigeon-hole him.

On his birthday, let's review Paul McCartney's incredibly prolific career.

The term "instant classic" gets thrown around a lot, but there was no debate that Paul McCartney had almost immediately changed public perception of himself and Wings. For five weeks, no one could get away from the number one song on the U.S. Billboard Hot 100, and it was eventually awarded the number one pop song of the year at the end of 1976. It caused many of the publications and music writers to eat their previous words, and admit McCartney's songwriting prowess. However, the biggest praise came from the same person that had coined the song's title, and his harshest critic.

Even John Lennon was enamored with the song. His personal photographer, Bob Gruen, said that Lennon took the song personally, seeing it as a direct confession of love to him from his former writing partner. There are many theories that the song even lit a fire under Lennon to write "(Just Like) Starting Over," his own version of a sappy song designed for radio play.

After the stress of being one of The Beatles, it was evident that Paul McCartney just wanted to find his love of music again with his solo work and Wings. And in trying to just have fun, too many people forgot about the powerhouse penmanship behind hits like "Yesterday" and "Hey Jude." "Silly Love Songs" was a statement by McCartney, an attempt to brush off some of the chips on his shoulder and remind those very critics that he still has the fastball.

As AI agents take control, Nvidia rethinks the security playbook

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia Corp. recently added a new pillar to its AI networking infrastructure with the unveiling of Scale-In.

Scale-In brings purpose-built acceleration to secure, manage and operate agentic AI factories. It is designed to deliver secure access, high-performance data movement, tenant isolation, simpler operations and more predictable performance as agents assume increasingly larger roles within AI factories.

“In agentic AI, it’s a completely different use case because now we’re dealing with many agents that need to run on the infrastructure,” said Gilad Shainer (pictured), senior vice president of networking at Nvidia. “You need to increase security because you need to make sure that those agents are contained in their sandbox and are not doing the wrong things. You need to build an infrastructure that needs to ensure security all around…what happens in the memory, what happens on storage. This is why we needed to bring an infrastructure which actually enabled the security across everything. And that’s what Scale-In is.”

Shainer spoke with theCUBE’s Dave Vellante for theCUBE + NYSE Wired: AI Factories – Data Centers of the Future interview series on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed how Nvidia’s Scale-In architecture is designed to help transform scaled compute into a secure and operational AI factory platform.

Since the August introduction, Nvidia has expanded its agent security strategy. In September, the company introduced its Open Agent Safety Platform, combining OpenShell with Nvidia Sentry for out-of-band monitoring and hardware-based security enforcement. An October announcement added the OpenShell Reference Architecture, integrating Vera Rubin infrastructure, Vera CPUs and BlueField-4 to support secure agent execution across enterprise systems. Nvidia’s announcement outlines the expanded architecture.

Scale-In is powered by Nvidia BlueField-4 and Nvidia DOCA and connected over Nvidia Spectrum-X Ethernet. BlueField-4 is Nvidia’s fourth-generation data processing unit, a networking platform built for agentic AI factories and large-scale infrastructure needs. BlueField is essential to the management of agentic AI security, according to Shainer.

“You can think about BlueField or Blue-Field-4 as a device that has in-silicon security engines that are working out of band and collecting telemetry from everything, from memory, from storage, from GPUs, from CPUs,” Shainer told theCUBE. “That’s why that infrastructure connects to all other infrastructures and ensures security all around. That out-of-band monitoring enables BlueField to actually understand exactly what agents are doing.”

Along with BlueField-4, Nvidia DOCA also powers Scale-In deployment. This software platform enables the rapid creation of apps and services that offload, accelerate and isolate data center workloads.

Using DOCA, developers can create software-defined, cloud-native, DPU- and SuperNIC-accelerated solutions. Like Nvidia CUDA, which provides a programming platform for GPU workloads, DOCA gives developers access to DPU acceleration capabilities.

“DPU brings the in-silicon engines security engines, storage accelerations,” Shainer explained. “DOCA is the framework, the software, the APIs that enable you to access all of those engines that BlueField-4 provides.”

Nvidia has further reinforced agentic AI security with its open-source runtime offering called OpenShell. The tool is designed to govern agent execution, access and inference routing. Nvidia combined OpenShell with BlueField-4 because it wanted a device that could collect information outside the GPU and monitor an environment that no agent could influence.

“I want to also have another device which is completely separated, completely isolated from the agents, that can monitor everything out-of-band,” Shainer said. “That’s what we’re doing with BlueField-4, bringing all the security elements together with OpenShell, and now you have a secure infrastructure for agents. This is how the combination of OpenShell and BlueField-4 brings all the security elements that we need for gen AI.”

The more recent OpenShell Reference Architecture builds on that approach with Nvidia Sentry, which runs on BlueField-4 using DOCA software. According to Nvidia, Sentry continuously monitors agent behavior and can quarantine agents that attempt to move outside their defined software boundaries. The combination extends OpenShell’s runtime controls with an independent enforcement layer.

Here’s how to load them up with flavor.

Stuffed peppers are such a flavorful dish that even if you’re a meat-eater, you’ll never miss it in this recipe from All The Healthy Things. Amid the horrors and chaos of the events unfolding, it would take days before he could contact his mother and tell her he was OK.

Until 9/11, Jeremy’s path in the Army had gone according to plan. “My idea was [not] to go anywhere near combat,” he says. But the terrorist attacks that took nearly 3,000 lives changed his mind. One of his grandfathers had served in World War II. Jason, his brother, was already stationed in Turkey with the Air Force. Jeremy no longer wanted to serve within the relatively safe confines of his communications posting, testing radios and satellite switches. In 2003, he asked to be assigned to a cavalry unit so he could prep for deployment. He was 25 years old. “It was just a feeling that this is the moment for my generation,” Jeremy says. “I wanted to be directly involved in whatever came next.”

It would take three years before Sgt. Jeremy Hurtt learned what next involved—and how it would test him like no hill ever could.

Come fall in the Ozarks, the Buffalo River, where Jeremy and his friends used to raft in the summer, is still just deep enough for one last excursion. And the Arkansas Razorbacks are back on the field, offering once more a promise to their diehard fans.

But in the fall of 2006, Jeremy was in Iraq and missed all that. Home was 7,000 miles away—and somehow felt even farther. On his first full night in Baghdad, a rocket struck his barrack. It didn’t explode, and no one was injured, but Jeremy now had firsthand experience of combat life. “It wasn’t like that all the time,” he says. But it was clear to him that “there are people who want me dead. They are going to try to kill me.”

For much of his time, Jeremy and his troop were on security detail. They patrolled sectors in Sadr City and East Baghdad, scanning rooftops and street corners, searching for “high-value targets” to capture or kill. “There’s some combat; occasionally people shoot at you,” he says. “It’s a lot more common for them to try to blow you up.”

He recalls just one of many incidents. One afternoon in 2007, Alpha 3/61 was southwest of Sadr City. They were in six armored vehicles, Jeremy with his M240 machine gun on lookout in the third Humvee. Without warning, an EFP, the most lethal form of homemade explosive used against Coalition troops during the war, tore through the backside of the lead Humvee. The warhead narrowly missed Sgt. Matt Haeffner, Jeremy’s roommate, who sustained a concussion in the blast.

As Haeffner recalls, Jeremy was one of the first in his troop to check on him. “We’ll be friends forever because we shared crazy moments like that,” says Haeffner, who was awarded a Purple Heart following the EFP attack. “My wife calls it trauma bonding.”

Not all of Jeremy’s friends in Iraq made it home. Sgt. Eric Snell, a father of two, was killed during a small-arms fire in Baghdad. He was charismatic, Jeremy remembers, a former pro baseball player with the looks of a model. And there was Sgt. Alexander Fuller, who died when his Humvee struck an improvised bomb. “His wife was pregnant with their first child,” Jeremy says. Jeremy knew both Snell and Fuller from his earliest days in the Cavalry. And then there were the eight men from his troop who died in a vicious battle in Afghanistan. That was hard on Jeremy, who, by then, had finished his deployment and was back in Arkansas and a world away from the fighting.

The distance added to his sense of guilt. Should he have re-upped for another tour and gone with his former troop to Afghanistan? Why did Snell and Fuller die, and not him? Why did the dumb drunk, as Jeremy thought of himself, get to live and those men did not?

“I always thought it’s a trade that anybody would have made. If you could trade me for Fuller and he gets to see his daughter grow up—I think people would make that trade,” Jeremy says. “And keep Snell around for his kid; that trade would make sense.” He pauses for a second. “To me, it just always seemed like a cruel joke—those people didn’t get to experience their lives, and I was still here.”

So he’d keep asking himself, “Why should I be alive when better people weren’t?”

Back in Arkansas, Jeremy didn’t forget what he saw, and what he was missing. He couldn’t find much work, but found plenty to drink. Within a year of returning home, his weight went from 170 pounds to 230. In Iraq, he ran occasionally, when the situation allowed, sweating in the desert heat. So when he got prodded by friends to enter a local weight-loss competition, he turned to running to peel the pounds away. “I was running up and down hills and in the heat and humidity of Arkansas,” he says, “so it came off pretty quickly.”

Bringing order and routine to other parts of life proved more challenging. For a time, he returned to the University of Arkansas, starting fresh. He tried majoring in broadcasting but never made it to graduation. He found a variety of part-time jobs, including production work at an ESPN radio affiliate. Nothing stuck. A girlfriend came and went. Alcohol was the only constant.

Fireworks on the Fourth of July or at a football game made him edgy. He’d drive through an underpass, see trash on the side of the road, and wonder if it was concealing an IED. He’d suddenly remember Snell. “He was my favorite soldier ever, and got killed the day I went on leave,” he says, his eyes watering. He waits a moment. “That all rolled up into the guilt, everything, I felt for a lot of years afterward.”

Friends and family noticed a different Jeremy from the one they knew before deployment. “I remember linking up with him a few times, catching up, and it was like a shell,” Dillon Cunningham recalls. “He was there, but there wasn’t anything solid in there. You could quickly tell he was not operating at the specs we used to expect from him.”

Once they began to notice the changes, his friends and family began to worry.

Twenty-two. It’s a number “that really sticks heavily in most military guys’ heads,” Jason says. For nearly a decade, 22 referred to the number of deaths by suicide among U.S. military veterans each day. Several suicide prevention organizations have 22 in their names. The number is on flags and tattoos to promote awareness. Various versions of a 22 Push-Up Challenge ask participants to do 22 daily push-ups for 22 days. Even though the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs downgraded the number of suicides to 17.7 per day in 2024, 22 remains code for those in the know.

Jason worried his brother might become a statistic.

“The power that the brain has when you get yourself in certain situations—if you don’t have those people to talk to, you do just fall down a drain,” Jason says. “And the unfortunate thing is, so many people don’t know who to talk to. A perfect example is Jeremy; he lost his buddies. Those may have been the guys he talked to and that he felt good with. Well, they’re gone, and so he starts going down that drain.”

Jason became Jeremy’s lifeline. On that morning in 2014, when Jeremy didn’t jump from the overpass, he got back to his friend’s house and called his brother, who was living outside of St. Louis, several hundred miles away. He didn’t talk about his suicidal thoughts, but asked for help finding work, getting back on track. Jason didn’t hesitate. He got Jeremy a job on his crew that worked on upgrading cell towers. He’d be on the road a lot, and the hours would be unpredictable, but the work would be steady and bring in a paycheck—and a group of coworkers to be around. And after his shifts, Jeremy could go for a run, which he often did, in the same pair of basketball sneakers he wore to work.

Jeremy never sought professional counseling, but he suspects he was suffering from PTSD. Getting psychological help is not something many veterans choose to pursue, his brother says. “When you come home, and you go talk to someone that has the sheepskin on the wall,” Jason says, “they say they know how a brain works. [But] you start talking to them, and there’s a major disconnect. These people have no idea what you’re talking about.”

Jeremy found a different kind of help, a different way to cope. The simple act of putting one foot in front of the other that brought clarity to his mind like nothing else. It interrupted the repetitive negative thoughts rumbling in his head. “So many troops find things to soothe their pain—if that’s gentlemen’s clubs, beer, drugs, whatever,” says Jason, who now works as a helicopter pilot for the FAA. “Jeremy was smart enough to channel that into running, and running was that savior for him.”

Jeremy started paying off debts. With the technical skills acquired from his past communication job, he eventually got a job with a company doing software testing. By 2018, he had saved enough to buy a place in a wooded, secluded area back in the Ozarks. Hills were nearby. That was when his love for accumulating elevation kicked in. He entered some races—he’s run the Little Rock Marathon three times, with a PR of 4:55:58—but preferred running solo.

And on those solo runs, he could finally start processing what he lived through in Iraq.

Jeremy has never mentioned his run to the overpass to Jason or Matt Haeffner or Dillon Cunningham. In fact, the only person he’s told is his wife, Stacie. They met online before his deployment to Iraq and stayed in touch for nearly a decade. In 2014, they reconnected. Stacie was living in Nashville at the time, and Jeremy was in the area working on a cell tower job. They went on a few dates, kept texting and calling when Jeremy’s job was done and he was back in Arkansas. Then, at the end of 2019, when Jeremy was living alone in his house in the woods, he texted Stacie that he wanted to be more serious. “He had been running,” she remembers, “and he was like, ‘I really think we’re supposed to give this a real shot.’ I was like, ‘OK, let’s figure this out.’” She packed up and moved to Arkansas. “We were engaged in a few months,” she says with a smile.

And by 2021, they were married. Friends from around the country came to the wedding. They saw the Jeremy they remembered from before Iraq. Sort of. The night before the wedding, a group of Army friends got together in his house. The laughter was flowing, and so were the drinks. But before it got too late, Jeremy told his buddies he was headed to bed. “You can imagine military guys—we were like, ‘Oh, come on, man. You’re getting married. Screw that,’” Dillon says. “And he was like, ‘I gotta run a half marathon in the morning. I run a half marathon every Saturday.’” On the morning of his wedding, Jeremy ran, showered, and made it to the ceremony with time to spare.

This past June, Stacie and Jeremy welcomed a new member to the family. Baby Josephine. She keeps Mom and Dad busy, so Jeremy has cut back on running. He might get a short one in during the workweek. Come the weekend, though, Stacie gives him the green light and lets him go long. And at an 11-minute pace, he runs his half marathon.

“I don’t think it was trivial that I ran to that overpass,” he says, sitting in the den of their home outside of Houston, where they moved just before Josephine was born. “There’s something that happens in a run where, like, I have breakthroughs and insight. And I think that’s what happened that day. The mile I took to run to that overpass gave me just enough to be in a moment. It all kind of clicked into place.” Jeremy waits a second. He thinks back a dozen years. “I was—excuse my frankness—bullshitting away the opportunity. I had to be here, in this experience of being alive.”

Just then, Josephine starts stirring. Jeremy Hurtt has to go. He has a mission to get back to.
