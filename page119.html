Sean Payton says J.K. Dobbins remains "a huge part of what we're doing"

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

It’s been a somewhat strange second year in Denver for veteran running back J.K. Dobbins. As coach Sean Payton tells it, however, everything is going as planned.

“He’s a huge part of what we’re doing,” Payton told reporters on Wednesday regarding whether the team is managing Dobbins’s workload. “So each week we look at — a lot of times who’s the back that’s in there with the hot hand. But he’s been fantastic. So to answer your question: No, that’s not a hot button topic with us here. It might be with you guys, but not with us. So we’re good. We’re good with the rotation.”

Payton is generally using a three-back rotation, with Dobbins joining R.J. Harvey and rookie Jonah Coleman. Still, with Harvey on injured reserve for Sunday’s showdown against the Chiefs, Dobbins got fewer chances than Coleman.

Coleman, who was on the field for 41 offensive plays, had 11 carries for 41 yards and four catches for 63. Dobbins played 13 snaps, getting seven carries for 13 yards and no targets in the passing game.

“Look, it’s always easy to work two [running backs] and it becomes more challenging when you’re trying to work three, but he’s doing a great job,” Payton said of Dobbins.

For the year, Dobbins has 94 carries for 306 yards in eight games, with eight catches for 27 yards. That’s 333 yards from scrimmage — an average of 41.6 per game.

In 10 games last year, before he suffered a season-ending foot injury during a Thursday night game against the Raiders, Dobbins was averaging nearly twice that amount, at 80.9 yards from scrimmage per game.

Through it all, the Broncos have been linked to a potential trade for Colts running back Jonathan Taylor. While the prospect of acquiring a workhorse would cut against Payton’s longstanding approach to use multiple running backs, Taylor’s arrival would surely result in even fewer chances for Dobbins.

Zelda: Ocarina of Time Remake - How To Get A Free Hylian Shield

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time has so many items and pieces of equipment that it's hard to keep track of what is in each game. However, select iconic gear sets are shown as motifs throughout the entire series, like the Master Sword. Another piece of reliable equipment is the Hylian Shield, which, depending on the game, can be easy to get.

In Ocarina of Time, you can buy it outright at one of the first major shops in Castle Town, but 80 Rupees is a big ask for new players who may not yet know the best ways to earn Rupees. Here's how to get one for free without paying the Rupee tax.

Early in the game, before you enter Dodongo's Cavern, Ocarina of Time will constantly remind you that you need a better shield than your initial Deku Shield that you picked up in the opening moments; which burns up on contact with flames.

As a result, you'll want to pick up the Hylian Shield, the classic reliable shield found in multiple Zelda games and one of the most iconic pieces of equipment in the series.

To find it, head to the graveyard behind Kakariko Village. When you enter the village, walk straight ahead and to the right, following the path up to the graveyard. Walk all the way to the back, near the big grave area near the hill. Go behind the grave in the gallery above, and pull the stone until it reveals a hole.

Drop in, and be prepared to either run or battle a formidable foe right away.

Inside the hole is a ReDead, an iconic and terrifying enemy throughout the series, including Ocarina of Time. They can scream to stun you, then grab you to suck your health, so you'll need to approach them carefully. Thankfully, it's easy to run past them, grab the shield in the chest, and then dash out before the ReDead can react.

As Young Link, you can't use the Hylian Shield to its full potential because it's a little too big for him. Link will crouch and hold the shield above him, so it's less useful than the Deku Shield in some situations. However, I would keep it equipped in Dodongo's Cavern regardless, because it won't burn and can help protect you from vertical threats.

You keep the Hylian Shield when you turn into Adult Link. As Adult Link, you can wield the Hylian Shield like a normal shield, blocking attacks from the front.

There is another way out if you can't locate the Hylian Shield in the graveyard for whatever reason, or you lose it somehow. The Bazaar in Castle Town sells a Hylian Shield for 80 Rupees, which you can reduce to 60 Rupees by talking to the guard near the trail to Death Mountain at the edge of Kakariko Village.

While you're there, give the guard the Keaton Mask, the first mask from the Happy Mask Shop.

There are other chests that contain Hylian Shields, but these are the easiest ways to grab them early in the Ocarina of Time remake.

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time’s Changes to the Infamous Water Temple Are Actually Brilliant

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Zelda's most infamous dungeon is no longer a frustrating rite of passage

With The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time’s Switch 2 remake out now, long-time Nintendo fans are in for a lot of warm nostalgia. A trek through the lost woods, duets with Sheik, a return to the Bombchu Bowling Alley — all of those wonderful moments return in a faithful remake that feels every bit as magical as the Nintendo 64 classic. But with all those wonderful moments comes a painful reunion with one of the most hated moments in Zelda history. Yes, you will have to return to the dreaded Water Temple.

Don’t worry! Even though the Ocarina of Time remake is careful not to change too much about the original game, it does make some small tweaks to a dungeon that still haunts a generation. It’s mercifully easier to navigate now thanks to a few minor but smart updates that go a long way.

The Water Temple is the most infamous dungeon in the original Ocarina of Time, so much so that it has been a bit of a running joke for decades now. The gist is that it’s a four-story structure built around a central room, and it’s flooded with water. To access certain areas, you need to find rooms in the dungeon that allow you to change the water level throughout the temple. Easy enough on paper, but it’s a bit of a pain in practice. Due to the limited visual information you get in-game, it requires you to keep a mental map of where each water adjustment point is, as well as how each of the three levels will change the layout of the dungeon. It’s a headache to keep everything straight, thus earning it a reputation as a frustrating rite of passage.

While that structure hasn’t changed much in the remake, Nintendo does make it easier to get through with a few smart decisions. That came at the request of Eiji Aonuma, who directed the original Ocarina of Time. “I had some overdue homework: I wanted to do something about the Water Temple I created 28 years ago,” Aonuma said in a recent Nintendo developer interview. “Since it became well known for its difficulty in the original game, I had the team make some adjustments in this version.”

One of those adjustments just comes down to clearer signposting. Building off the dungeon’s Ocarina of Time 3D redesign, the doors that lead to water controls are now clearly marked with symbols showing you exactly how the water level will change. You no longer have to remember which door leads to the room that drains the room, or which one will get you to floor two.

On top of that, each of the routes are now color-coded. You’ll find variously colored pipes around each of the three doors, and those visually lead you to the control room if you follow them through the dungeon. For instance, when you enter the route to the room that brings the water to its lowest level, you’ll notice that some blue pipes signal that you should float to the water’s surface. Follow that hint and you’ll find that the pipes lead to clearly marked pedestals for you to play your ocarina on. All of that makes it significantly easier to remember exactly where you need to go at all times.

Arguably, the more impactful Water Temple change comes from a top-level control decision that just so happens to make life easier in the dungeon. Part of why the Water Temple is such a pain is because you need to use a few key items throughout it. Your ocarina, Iron Boots, Hookshot, and more all play major roles in solving puzzles throughout the temple. Since the original game only gave you three item slots to work with, you were often left juggling around your item shortcuts in the main menu. It’s a minor pain point, but one that stands out when you’re constantly swapping items.

While the Switch 2 remake still has its own item management quirks, it wisely keeps your ocarina mapped to the D-pad at all times. Your boots get a quick swap button on the down button there too, meaning that you don’t need to waste an item slot on the Iron Boots. With those two necessities free up, you can dedicate your ZR item slot to the Hookshot and only swap to other items as needed with a quick menu on the R button. That decision cuts out any need for pause-menu management, allowing you to move through the dungeon more fluidly.

For the ‘90s purists, these changes might sound a little sacrilegious. As much as players claim to hate the Water Temple, that complaint invariably comes with a fondness for a genuinely memorable bit of frustrating design. “Why shouldn’t today’s youths struggle through the Water Temple like their elders once did?” you may ask. I, for one, welcome the help, just as I support the idea of today’s students having their loans canceled even though I had to pay mine off. The new visual design tweak makes for some sensible signposting that still feels part of the dungeon’s architecture, and the control solution just makes it easier to stay immersed in a well-built dungeon. New generations of players needn’t suffer like us old Zelda fans once did. Swim free, ye tadpoles.

Nintendo’s excellent Switch 2 remake recaptures the soul of a masterpiece

meet the wienermobile, the 1930s quirky pedal car that put hot dogs on wheels

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

from its first appearance on american roads in 1936 to its miniature interpretations by bird corporation and konek, the oversized hot dog has become an enduring piece of advertising and automotive culture.

Two vintage Oscar Mayer Wienermobile pedal cars, produced by Bird Corporation and Konek Inc. during the 1990s, are appearing together at auction, bringing a miniature version of one of America’s most recognizable promotional vehicles back into focus. Offered as a single lot without a reserve, the two new-old-stock examples reportedly have never been ridden by children. Their bright orange bodies, yellow bun-shaped bases, and playful automotive details recall a design that has spent nearly nine decades turning an oversized hot dog into a familiar sight on American roads.

The story of the Wienermobile begins in 1936, when Carl G. Mayer, nephew of Oscar Mayer founder Oscar F. Mayer, proposed a hot dog-shaped vehicle as a traveling advertisement for the company’s products. Built by Chicago’s General Body Company, the original measured approximately four meters long and featured a metal body shaped like an oversized wiener, mounted on wheels. Its two open cockpits accommodated a driver and Little Oscar, the promotional character of the brand.

As the vehicle became an established part of Oscar Mayer’s advertising, successive generations introduced new interpretations of the original concept. The 1958 model, designed by industrial designer Brooks Stevens, introduced a rounded, bubble-like front and more integrated bun-shaped elements. Later versions experimented with larger bodies, updated chassis, and elaborate interiors, always retaining the unmistakable hot dog silhouette.

Oscar Mayer discontinued the program in the late 1970s, shifting its attention toward television advertising. Public enthusiasm for the vehicle, however, prompted its return in 1986 for the company’s 50th anniversary. New generations followed, and the Wienermobile resumed its travels across the United States, appearing at parades, public gatherings, supermarkets, and promotional events.

Over the years, the hot dog on wheels inspired toys, merchandise, and other branded interpretations, translating its instantly recognizable shape into objects that could be collected and played with. The two pedal cars now offered at auction on Bring a Trailer belong to this broader history.

Although they share the same orange-and-yellow color scheme, the Bird Corporation and Konek examples feature different construction methods. The Bird version has a single-piece, hot-dog-shaped plastic body mounted over a yellow base, while the Konek model uses a two-piece orange body with Oscar Mayer and Wienermobile decals across its front, rear, and sides.

Both combine molded plastic exteriors with metal frames, orange wheels, yellow seats, and black steering wheels with yellow horn buttons. Twin foot pedals power the miniature vehicles, while faux headlights, grilles, taillights, and windows complete their automotive appearance. The Konek example measures approximately 114 centimeters long, 53 centimeters wide, and 48 centimeters tall.

Today, the Wienermobile remains part of Oscar Mayer’s identity, with six vehicles operated by parent company Kraft Heinz. Its continued presence on American roads speaks to the unusual longevity of a marketing idea conceived in the 1930s. The hot dog-shaped vehicle has become a recognizable piece of American visual culture and continues to inspire new interpretations nearly 90 years after its first appearance. The two pedal cars’ appearance at auction offers a chance to revisit the history of the Wienermobile, a promotional vehicle that brought advertising, automotive design, and popular culture together. Zillennials Don’t Feel the Need to Grow Up, <i><em>Club Kid </em></i>Knows Why

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Jordan Firstman’s deferred coming-of-age tale speaks to the realities of living in an unwieldy time

I’m 29, an age that has become a catalyst for conversations about what it means to be an adult, about settling down (or not), and, perhaps above all, how to show up for my own well-being (do I need to party less?) It’s a volley of topics that are often the subject of yearly think pieces, even when we know the conclusions they’ll reach. Millennials and Zillenials feel pressured by older generations to live a conventional and ambitious life, even as they’ve squeezed young people’s hope for a future.

The New York Times detailed this dilemma just last month, speaking to thirtysomethings across New York City who felt discouraged by their situation and, to put it plainly, left behind. The article pointed out that the long-running Harvard Youth Poll found, for the first time ever, that most young Americans don’t believe they will surpass their parents financially. In the last 30 years, the cost of living has skyrocketed, and, today, mounting loans and debt among young people have made a house feel out of reach. Looking even younger, Gen Z is feeling the brunt of the economy, with Fortune finding that two-thirds of parents say their Gen Z adult kids rely on them (and Playboy going so far as to connect the housing crisis to the sex decline). Why stop partying if the rent’s too high, if there’s no reasonable expectation that you’ll find security, buy a house, or live easily past 2050?

This growing frustration is likely why audiences—including myself—are connecting so deeply with influencer-turned-director Jordan Firstman’s sensational feature debut, Club Kid. The film offers a tender look at arrested development and unexpected fatherhood, but above all, it delivers an incisive look at the struggle to let go of youth when the world feels increasingly grim. The idea of partying instead of worrying is alluring: to exist in a space free from responsibility. Peter (Firstman), a thirty-something party promoter deeply enmeshed in New York’s underground queer club scene, lives in the apartment his late mother left him, sells drugs on the side, and his only real daily concern is recovering for the next party. The moment he crosses the threshold of any club, he plunges into a fugue state fueled by drugs and sex. Peter clings to his youth (his name aptly echoing Peter Pan syndrome), shirks all responsibilities, and actively defers a much-needed inward look.

Peter’s wake-up call arrives as a 10-year-old boy named Arlo (Reggie Absolom)—a son he unknowingly fathered years after hooking up with a man who insisted they turn their dalliance into a threesome with a woman. A decade after that fateful night, the woman’s best friend (played with drug-fueled frenzy by Kirby Howell-Baptiste) shows up at his door, a young child in tow, with news that the mother has died by suicide and Peter needs to step up. It’s a parable tailor-made for a worn-down generation that sees no reason to stop the party. It takes a ketamine stork of sorts to change course.

Club Kid revels in the growing pains. Firstman avoids reducing the conflict to a simple clash over parenting Arlo or his personal struggle to break free from the party lifestyle; instead, Club Kid unfolds as a tender parallel coming-of-age, where Peter grows up as he learns to parent Arlo, a quiet, curious kid grappling with the loss of his mother. Firstman embraces the missteps, letting us see the beauty of his life as it intermingles with his newfound parenting. The whirlwind of responsibility—of unwitting fatherhood—plays like a fairy tale, a wondrous reality that feels increasingly distant from what a thirtysomething can reasonably expect today.

Watching Club Kid, I considered my own parents, who conceived me (by happy accident, I’m told) at 27 and 29. They worked at a pizza restaurant together before opening their own bar. My childhood felt wrapped up in Atlanta’s nightlife scene; somehow, completely inexperienced, they still learned to balance their lifestyle with their newfound roles as parents. I lived as the only kid (with my two-years-younger brother) in my parents’ world for several years, often sitting with the bar regulars for hours while my parents worked, or being actively watched over by the cooks. It felt like tapping into another world, but also brought us closer. They didn’t give up their life for me; they made me a part of it. These days, this story sounds like a romantic ideal, a whirlwind adventure only possible without looming loans and the high cost of living. I simply couldn’t imagine doing the same today.

What makes Peter’s story compelling is how he navigates the responsibilities of fatherhood without fully forgetting himself. Some critics have challenged the film, claiming that it’s stigmatizing the queer party scene, but in his telling, he doesn’t rebuke his former lifestyle, just the parts of it that were bad for him. His friend even welcomes the news of his fatherhood with excitement, spending time with Arlo without passing judgment. Even if he eventually needs to distance himself from these friends’ party habits, it doesn’t need a blow-out fight. Life happens, and people move on.

That’s why it’s important that Firstman returns to the real problem at the end of the movie. A vindictive call to social services by Peter’s ex-best friend (Cara Delevigne) leads to Arlo’s deportation and the new father-son duo’s separation. It brings to light the real-life anxieties looming over this heartwarming story. Sure, Peter did not file the proper adoption papers, and his lack of money becomes damningly apparent when he stares down health-care premiums and legal bills in the face of an unrelenting bureaucracy.

Firstman refuses to provide a pretty picture; he instead studies the anxieties of millennials, a generation perennially unsure of the world around them, and distills them into a heartwarming, deferred coming-of-age story. Firstman dreams of who we can become, if only given the chance to grow up in a world where steady ground is hard to find. Peter is growing up alongside his son, hiccups and all.
