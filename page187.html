Peak Design, best known for its backpacks and travel bags packed full of useful features, announced it’s expanding its collection of chargers and charging accessories with new devices that focus on pairing premium materials with innovative functionality. “Somewhere along the way, we accepted that the electronics we use every day should be cheap, disposable and just good enough,” says Peter Dering, Peak Design’s CEO and founder. “We didn’t set out to make another collection of chargers and cables. We set out to show just how much better these everyday products could be.”

The $99.95 Peak Design Wireless Kickstand Travel Battery, available in a black or moon (cream) finish, wraps a 5,000mAh battery in a magnetic housing made from machined anodized aluminum with a matching folding kickstand. The company describes it as the “world’s first Qi2.3-certified wireless charger,” which is the latest iteration of the Qi2 spec. Most 5,000mAh battery cells can’t safely wirelessly charge a device at 25W. It’s a feature that’s more common with batteries with at least a 10,000mAh capacity. But by supporting Qi2.3, Peak Design’s new travel battery can wirelessly charge a compatible device at 25W while it’s connected to a USB power source. On battery power alone, its wireless charging speed maxes out at 15W.

Peak Design’s $99.95 Wireless Charging Station 2-in-1, available in the same color options as its new travel battery, is a Qi2.2-compatible accessory featuring a pair of 25W wireless charging pads that can be used simultaneously at full power. One of the pads is on the charger’s base that’s made from die-cast aluminum and zinc, while the other is attached to an adjustable arm that folds flat so the charging station is easier to travel with.

Neither of the new wireless chargers comes with an adapter, but you can pair them with Peak Design’s new $64.95 Dual Slim Charger that uses GaN technology so it’s just a half-inch thick but delivers up to 65W of power through a pair of USB-C ports. They’re joined by an assortment of new Peak Design USB-C UltraStrand Cables ranging from $24.95 to $39.95. The cables are available in lengths ranging from 15 centimeters to 2 meters for a 60W / 480Mbps version, or 15 centimeters to a meter for the 240W / 40Gbps option.

Most of the new chargers and accessories are available starting today, but the Wireless Kickstand Travel Battery and certain lengths of the UltraStrand USB-C cables will be available a few days later on November 5th.

3AM Affiliate Embraces AI-Like Loops in European School Attack

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Evidence suggests a model may have generated and revised commands during the ransom gang's breach, though researchers stopped short of claiming full automation.

A 3AM ransomware attack targeting a UK school estate may provide an early look at how cybercriminals could use AI as a force multiplier rather than a replacement for human operators.

Bitdefender today published research concerning a "hybrid AI ransomware attack" observed in the wild, attributing certain actions taken to human or agentic elements. On the surface, the attack is fairly straightforward. As a blog post explained, a 3AM ransomware affiliate attacked a school in the UK, "then used ordinary Windows tools to build domain trusts and reach other schools."

Attackers stole backup credentials, created rogue domain-admin accounts, established trusts between previously separate Active Directory domains, moved between schools, stole data, and deployed 3AM ransomware. In other words, the attack largely followed a familiar ransomware playbook, despite Bitdefender's claims that AI helped execute parts of the operation.

Related:'AdFection' Turns Ad Impressions Into Zero-Click Spyware Delivery

Where the attack got more interesting was how, as the security vendor argued, it didn't fit neatly into pre-existing buckets for attacks involving AI. It wasn't exactly an AI-generated attack where the malware was fully or largely created by a large language model (LLM); it wasn't exactly an AI-assisted attack where human engineers built the tools while using AI to speed up work; and it wasn't exactly an AI-operated attack where the LLM orchestrates actions and runs the show, as it were.

In this case, Bitdefender argued an AI system appeared to handle repetitive command generation and troubleshooting while a human operator made high level decisions.

Many elements of the attack were ordinary for ransomware, such as significant use of living-off-the-land (LOTL) techniques, where attackers abuse tools already present on the victim's machine rather than relying on malware — in this case PowerShell, Windows Management Instrumentation (WMI), reg, netsh, rundll32, and other domain and scheduling utilities preinstalled on Windows. The attacker also used "an Impacket-style remote-execution tool," as well as Mimikatz and a previously published Veeam script.

But despite the attack's ordinary nature, Bitdefender assessed that several things suggest the use of an agentic loop, the control structure that makes an AI agent work. Certain actions appeared to be taken by a program rather than a human operator.

"The commands carry the usual tells of a model: a call to the backup software's web interface (Veeam, a widely used enterprise backup product) on a port and with a login method that do not match the vendor’s documentation, a mistake typical of writing from memory rather than from the manual (Veeam REST API authorization), along with confirmation files, self-labeled output and numbered revisions of the same script," the blog post read. "The retries behave like a loop too."

Related:Biomemory Launches DNA Shield, First Commercial Product Built on Its Molecular IT Platform

In another example, researchers assessed that the time taken between PowerShell command attempts was shorter than a human would take if issuing them by hand, but longer than if an LLM was orchestrating by itself. The longer time taken between individual tasks but the shorter time between retries "is what it looks like if a person is choosing the next step and a model is handling the retries."

Bitdefender caveated its findings by noting that it was unable to access the machine the attacker worked from, so it couldn't document anything tangible involving the AI model itself. The research acts as an argument that this attack behaves like a human operating alongside an agent, which is a working model defenders may wish to plan around.

With that in mind, Martin Zugec, technical solutions director at Bitdefender, tells Dark Reading that "a hybrid model is more reasonable for financially motivated cybercriminals, technically and economically, than a fully autonomous attack."

Related:Foul Language: WordlistLoader Disguises Malware as Ordinary Text

But even as a one off, the situation is notable because the primary victim was an organization in a critical sector, and Bitdefender's anecdote suggests the affiliate may have used an AI agent to scale the attack by handling repetitive troubleshooting and command generation, although the report stops short of showing AI-enabled capabilities the attacker otherwise lacked.

Moreover, other similar institutions could fall in the crosshairs. "From a domain controller it enumerated trust relationships between domains and searched SYSVOL (the shared folder where Active Directory stores logon scripts, readable by any domain user) for the names and subnets of other schools," according to the research blog post.

The good news is that because much of this attack followed the typical ransomware playbook, the defender recommendations that stop regular ransomware would also stop the attack described in this research.

Zugec adds that agentic loops are noisy from an indicator standpoint, as many of their actions are repetitive and brute force in nature. "An agentic loop doesn’t make an attack quiet, it makes it repetitive: the same command re-run, small tweaks, numbered scripts and probe files left on disk. Even the quickest retries took 40 seconds or more. 'Machine speed' is mostly a marketing phrase about very expensive incidents; in real attacks, especially with local models, we expect this slower and noisier pattern. Speed isn’t the giveaway, repetition is."

The vendor recommends focusing on prevention: Keep admin credentials of different domains separate, treat backups as a possible target, restrict remote execution and administrative shares to known management hosts, block interpreters and shells on machines that never use them, and put endpoint security tooling on every machine.

"Prefer controls that work regardless of volume and speed," the blog post read, "because they stop an action the first time and the hundredth."

Meeting AI Demand Starts With The Right Infrastructure

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

​Juan Font is President and CEO of CoreSite.

In the words of Benjamin Franklin: “Great haste makes great waste.” It’s an apt perspective for viewing the rush to AI deployment in the enterprise today, where organizational leaders race into AI deployment decisions without always having the infrastructure in place to support them.

For many enterprises, the default setting is to contract directly with major frontier labs such as Anthropic or OpenAI and big cloud providers such as Google or Microsoft. Effectively, they’re racing to allow employees to use tokens for almost anything—often without much oversight.

This rush is strikingly reminiscent of when the cloud was introduced in the 2010s, and employees opened public cloud accounts, running up very large tabs—again with very little oversight over the projects being undertaken.

Today, companies are rushing to consume tokens with relatively little thought given to how that consumption is supported. In many cases, the problem isn’t the AI deployment itself. It’s the infrastructure supporting it. And solving that problem is critical to developing and realizing a sound AI strategy.

At the innermost layer, AI requires compute. But compute in the form of GPU chips is extremely scarce. To deal with that scarcity, a whole new segment of companies called neoclouds that offer GPU as a service has emerged.

Enterprises that want to develop or train their own medium-sized models are renting or leasing compute infrastructure from these neoclouds. As that market matures, some companies are beginning to buy the hardware themselves, particularly in financial services, where firms require massive amounts of compute as well as real-time access to financial data and exchanges.

Companies developing their own models and doing their own inferencing require space, power and cooling for GPU clusters, along with connectivity to the models they use for other workflows. Less sophisticated companies are simply using the cloud, which can become increasingly expensive as usage grows. Running GPUs is extremely costly, and performance can degrade when long-haul fiber is used to connect to compute resources. You end up with chips waiting for data—resources you’re paying for but not fully utilizing.

That’s where colocation data centers come into the picture, particularly those close to population centers and network hubs. Using colocation data centers as inferencing hubs to manage the handoff between private and public infrastructure can improve performance by enabling direct connections.

Adjacency to where compute is performed is of vital importance, but so is being in an environment that allows you to future-proof your infrastructure as the mix of workloads changes. Getting there takes a partner who can help you step back and build the right infrastructure foundation before you scale your AI.

We’re also seeing a preponderance of multi-cloud and multi-model environments. Cloud providers are trying to become the entry point into the intelligence realm, and they want to connect you to everybody. But to operate in a multi-platform environment, you need the interconnection infrastructure to support it. The ability for huge models to connect is what ultimately drives their value.

From a cost perspective, it’s impossible to overstate how expensive it would be to transfer terabits per second of data across regions. And now, companies are releasing agents. Meta recently released Muse, an agentic AI personal assistant, and we see OpenAI and Anthropic moving in the same direction. Each of these applications requires dedicated compute, cybersecurity controls and tremendous amounts of bandwidth. That rise in agentic inferencing workloads will require inexpensive, high-speed connectivity to these models. Having compute in metro centers, adjacent to the clouds, will be vital from both a cost and speed perspective.

The enterprises that have the greatest need to place their digital infrastructure in a colocation data center—and gain closer proximity to inferencing—are not necessarily traditional brick-and-mortar hospitals and banks. They’re the AI models themselves: Anthropic, Meta, OpenAI, CoreWeave, Microsoft and Google. These companies are deploying edge networking infrastructure to support data transfer because the focus until now has been on the compute layer primarily devoted to training those models. Only now is the interconnection layer requiring edge compute and network being built. Demand for this interconnection capacity is already outpacing supply. Existing data center inventory is extremely limited, and we’re racing to build incremental capacity to meet growing enterprise demand.

All that horizontal stratification between the different participants of the edge AI compute ecosystem is happening right now, and it requires multiple megawatts of capacity. That edge infrastructure must be built to support the exponential growth in demand that is coming. In essence, this is the “plumbing” that is going to enable the massive growth in agentic AI workloads by enterprises and individuals alike.

Ultimately, because the network was not designed for the projected explosion in bandwidth, leveraging colocation data centers becomes a matter of survival. You must colocate—ideally adjacent to the edge compute infrastructure hosted by hyperscalers, neoclouds and frontier labs—and cross connect directly to the infrastructure. That’s the edge compute and interconnection on-ramps we’re building right now, and we’re just scratching the surface. The companies that take the time to build that foundation, before they scale, will be better positioned to turn today’s rush into an AI strategy that is sustainable tomorrow.

Why Good Product Management Starts On The Factory Floor

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Harmjan Derksen, Chief Product Officer, Ultimo. AI-augmented enterprise asset management (EAM) solutions for industrial businesses.

​At 6:30 a.m. on a weekday, a team lead at a manufacturing plant is already at work. She is cross-referencing overnight breakdown reports, shift logs and open notifications, building a mental picture of the equipment before her technicians arrive. It takes her 45 minutes every single day.

No analyst report ever told me about that morning ritual. I learned it by standing next to her and observing it.

As a chief product officer, I used to believe that strategy comes from market research, competitive analysis and analyst briefings. Although those inputs matter, years of building software for manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, energy and utilities companies have taught me there’s a different layer to strategy. Enterprise software teams should spend more time observing end users than reading analyst reports. And industrial executives should demand the same from every vendor who wants their business.

For industrial CXOs, introducing software that lacks operational reality creates a financial risk. Research from Splunk and Oxford Economics puts the cost of unplanned downtime at $400 billion a year across the Global 2000, roughly $200 million per company. And ABB’s 2025 research puts the average cost of a single hour of downtime anywhere from $10,000 to $500,000 across industrial sectors.

The workforce math is just as unforgiving. Deloitte and The Manufacturing Institute project that as many as 1.9 million U.S. manufacturing jobs could go unfilled by 2033 “if workforce challenges aren’t addressed.” Our own maintenance trend research found that 63% of industrial organizations are struggling with an aging workforce. Every experienced technician who retires takes decades of institutional knowledge with them—knowledge that exists in no system unless someone deliberately captures it.

Software built from a distance won’t solve problems that only reveal themselves up close.

Analyst reports tell you what the market is buying. The factory floor will tell you why products demand attention after they’re bought. Three lessons stand out from the time my teams and I have spent shadowing maintenance crews.

1. Workarounds are the truth. When a technician keeps a paper notebook next to a tablet, that’s unfiltered product feedback. The workaround shows you exactly where the software stopped fitting the work. You’re not going to find this often in a user satisfaction survey because people rarely report the friction they’ve already routed around.

2. Conditions change everything. A tool that demos beautifully in a conference room behaves differently at the top of a ladder, in gloves, next to a machine running at 90 decibels, in a corner of the plant with weak connectivity. If your product team has never watched software being used in those conditions, they’re designing for a facility that doesn’t exist.

3. Reality doesn’t care about your roadmap. I once presented AI capabilities at an industry conference, and a maintenance leader gave me candid feedback over dinner: What he needed was for his engineers to use their phones for work orders. Where I was talking about AI, he was still trying to solve mobile adoption. That gap between what vendors present and what customers are up against is where roadmaps go wrong, and it shaped ours more than any market forecast that year.

That dinner conversation is what listening looks like, but it’s not the whole roadmap. Innovation doesn’t always emerge from explicit user requests. The balance is staying grounded in what customers face, while still leading them toward what they haven’t asked for yet.

Observation also changes what you build. When our team watched that morning cross-referencing ritual up close at a European door manufacturer, the insight was humbling. The highest-value application of AI wasn’t an exotic prediction engine—it was automating the tedious assembly of information the team lead already trusted and giving her back nearly an hour a day. That priority came from the floor, not from a quadrant.

If you lead an industrial or healthcare business, you can turn this principle into procurement and operating discipline. Here’s my advice:

1. Ask every software vendor, “When did your product team last spend a shift with people who do this work?” Get specifics: which site, what they observed and what changed in the product as a result. Getting a vague answer can be just as telling as a fully detailed account.

2. Make a site visit part of your evaluation. Before signing, put the vendor’s product people, not just sales engineers, on your floor for a day. How they behave there tells you whether you’ve found a partner or sat through a pitch. A vendor confident in their product will welcome it.

3. Put frontline workers on the selection team. The technicians and operators who will use the software daily should have a real vote, not a courtesy demo. Systems chosen without them are systems worked around.

4. Measure adoption, not deployment. Go live isn’t success. Track whether people use the tool 90 days in and treat workarounds as defect reports. Usage data will tell you more than any steering committee update.

5. Apply the same discipline internally. Your IT leaders and digital teams should walk the floor regularly, observing and asking questions. The gap between IT strategy and operational reality closes one observed shift at a time.

“Go and see” is a well-worn idea in lean manufacturing, and industrial leaders have applied it to their operations for decades. What’s missing is applying the same discipline to the software those operations depend on, and to the people who build it.

The next time a vendor shows you a roadmap, ask where it came from. If the honest answer is a stack of reports, be cautious. If the answer involves worn safety shoes, you may have found a partner who understands that in industrial business, the truth lives on the floor.​

Apple iPad Mini (2026) Review: A Dream Device, but Who Is It For?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Some thought it might be dead. Some thought it had no place in an iPhone Duo world. But the iPad Mini isn’t being put out to pasture—it’s getting a massive glow-up.

Apple’s smallest tablet has been refreshed—now in its eighth generation—and with it, nearly every aspect of the device has been upgraded. It now has an OLED screen, a faster chip, a thinner chassis, more memory, waterproofing, and much more. That all comes with a higher price, though, and raises the question of just who the iPad Mini is really for among the other choices in the iPad lineup.

I’ll come straight out with it: I love this little tablet. I’ve been using it for about a week, and I happened to take it on a short trip. The perfect time for it to really shine—and shine, it did. It was the perfect companion for long Lyft rides to the airport and wait times at my gate.

The star attraction of the new iPad Mini is its 8.5-inch display. That’s a slightly larger screen than the previous generation, yet the overall dimensions are barely bigger. But the change from IPS to OLED is what matters. It’s a Tandem OLED Super Retina XDR display, based on the same technology as the iPad Pro—though it doesn’t have the 120-Hz refresh rate. Nor is it quite as bright: It can reach 600 nits in SDR and 1,200 nits in HDR, which is 400 nits less than the iPad Pro. It’s more or less similar to the M6 MacBook Pro, though. Not the OLED MacBook Pro, but the Mini-LED model.

The iPad Mini OLED’s brightness is sufficient for most uses, even in bright lighting conditions. But you can take it to the next level by opting for a nano-texture display, which is totally worth the extra $100 it costs. (It’s the model I tested.) It’s not an antireflective coating or a matte layer; it’s actually etched into the glass. The result is a surface that almost entirely diffuses outside light without reducing the screen’s perceived brightness. Reading outside with the iPad Mini is particularly enjoyable, as the nano-texture display not only resists reflections but even gives it a paperlike crispness. This option is also available on the MacBook Pro and iPad Pro, and there’s really nothing else like it.

The iPad Mini one-ups its siblings in sharpness. Its smaller size gives it 19 percent higher pixel density than every other iPad. And yes, it really does look incredibly clear and sharp.

There’s more to it than the display. Part of the magic is how lightweight it is—barely over half a pound, and it doesn’t feel much heavier than my phone in my hand. One-handed use feels completely natural. That’s always been true of the iPad Mini, but this latest version emphasizes that even more.

Lastly, the color options have been pared down from four to just two. The lighter goldish Starlight is still available, but now there’s the introduction of the blackish, navy-blue Midnight. This is a finish carried over from the MacBook Air. The more playful Blue and Purple color options are both gone, as is Space Gray. More options are always better, but for a device that costs $700, I can see why these more professional colors make sense.

Apple has also completely redesigned the iPad Mini’s four-speaker sound system, and a couple of interesting changes are happening. As part of its process of making the device IP67 water-resistant, Apple removed the speaker grille from the sides to create a completely sealed enclosure. Instead, the tweeters sit behind two small slits on the edges of the display glass. And then bass drivers are built directly into the display. Apple calls it an Acoustic Display.

The stereo image sounds fullest when you hold the device in portrait, as long as you keep your fingers away from the display. The vibration caused by the location of the speakers isn’t terribly noticeable unless you have the volume on full blast. Overall, the audio sounds decent for a device this size, but it won’t compare well to larger devices like an iPad Air or MacBook Air. But for watching videos or the occasional movie, it suffices.

The water resistance is a first for any iPad, which is significant. You might still worry about bringing your $700 tablet poolside, but the IP67 rating means it can handle submersion for 30 minutes in about three feet of water. In comparison, iPhones have had an IP68 rating for the past eight years, meaning they can handle twice that depth for 30 minutes.

As always, it has to be said that iPads are not really designed for taking photos. That’s a bit less true with the iPad Mini, if only because it’s so easy to use one-handed. Still, I doubt you’ll use the 12-megapixel rear camera all that much, outside of the occasional document or QR code scan. It’s decent but hasn’t been changed from the previous generation. The front-facing camera, however, got a big upgrade. It’s still a 12-megapixel camera, but it has moved to the side bezel instead of the top. That location makes video calls feel more natural.

One thing the iPad Mini is still missing is Face ID. While it’s a standard iPhone feature, Apple appears to really be reserving it for only the iPad Pro. That’s a shame. This does have Touch ID built into the power button.

A number of other iPad features still apply here, though, that make the iPad Mini feel like something you could work on. It supports extended display mode, letting you connect it to an external monitor without forcing it to mirror the screen. iPadOS also now has windowing and proper cursor support. Without a keyboard, I can’t imagine you’ll use these features as much. On the other hand, the iPad Mini’s size makes it possible to type with your thumbs (like you would on a phone).

Clearly, the 2026 iPad Mini seems to have its sights set on more than just reading books and playing games. As if the OLED display and price tag weren’t already enough to give that away. Most of the demos Apple has been using to show off with the iPad Mini were more creator-focused than I expected. We’re talking running on-device AI models or creative applications.

There are three storage configurations available: 128 GB, 256 GB, and 512 GB. All come with 12 GB of unified memory, four more than the previous iPad Mini and matching the current iPad Air. You’d never know that from Apple’s own spec sheets, though, since it’s never mentioned. Regardless, it’s part of what makes the iPad Mini a more capable little tablet, whether in heavier multitasking or for on-device AI. It’s kind of absurd that this little tablet has 12 GB of memory while the MacBook Neo is stuck at 8.

The iPad Mini’s A20 Pro chip is also more powerful than the MacBook Neo’s. This is the same silicon as in the iPhone Duo, iPhone 18 Pro, and iPhone 18 Pro Max—with one caveat. For the first time, the storage options dictate which version of the A20 Pro chip you get. Only the 512-GB model gets the full six-core CPU and dual 16-core Neural Engines. I tested this six-core model, but the other configurations have nerfed performance, with one fewer CPU efficiency core and only a single Neural Engine.

Performance is really solid. The A20 Pro handles games quite well, and I tested several, including Oceanhorn 3, Fortnite, No Man’s Sky, and Assassin’s Creed Mirage. None of these games looked particularly nice here, with textures and effects turned down, but the iPad Mini played them smoothly at 60 frames per second. Visually, the most impressive was No Man’s Sky, which came to iOS and iPadOS just last week. It still feels amazing to play a game this dense on a mere iPad Mini. Of course, the iPad Pro with the M5 will be a substantial upgrade for gaming performance, but if you connect a mobile controller (we like the Backbone One), you’ll have a good time.

I also tried out some local AI in LM Studio. With 12 GB of onboard memory, you’re limited in what models you can use. LM Studio recommended the 4-billion parameter version of Qwen 3.5. This isn’t the kind of model that’s going to blow anyone away, but for simple tasks, it’s nice to run a text model completely locally. This is all GPU-dependent, so performance should be identical without the second Neural Engine that comes exclusively on the 512-GB model. Of course, Siri AI is now a significant part of this platform, and the iPad Mini can run all the advanced features, including the more expressive Siri voices and on-the-fly dictation.

The previous iPad Mini cost $499 when it came out (before the price increases), and that at least felt reasonable. At this new price of $699, it’s a little harder to justify. The iPad Mini has always been a luxe but niche device. It’s sized for reading books, watching movies, and playing games. It doesn’t have the same high-stakes use case as a phone or laptop, which makes that higher premium price harder to swallow.

If you want the most powerful iPad Mini as a professional tool, you’ll probably spend at least $999. Or $1,099 with the nano-texture display. That’s the same price as the MacBook Air! I get it: The iPad Mini is an entirely different device. But I’m wondering whether real professionals would at least prefer a larger display—not only for the extra screen real estate but also for keyboard support.

I will say: The iPad Mini does work remarkably well with the Apple Pencil. If I had to choose an iPad specifically for note-taking, it’d be the iPad Mini. On the other hand, illustrators will likely prefer a larger screen. Like previous versions of the iPad Mini, it’s only compatible with the Apple Pencil Pro and USB-C Apple Pencil. Even for those who use tablets in the workplace as a point-of-sale system or for specialized use cases, the higher price seems hard to justify.

Is it all just a plainly disguised way of justifying the higher prices Apple is paying for memory on the backend? The cynic in me says yes. It’s not unlike the direction Apple is heading with its OLED MacBook Pro. But putting the cynicism aside, there’s real quality-of-life value being added here, especially in the form of the OLED screen, waterproofing, and extra memory. For the right person, the iPad Mini will be the perfect product. It’s a dream device for casual use, whether it’s gaming, reading, or browsing. But I’m guessing that demographic has shrunk quite a bit with this increased price.

I’m a little perplexed why Apple isn’t at least offering an 8-GB RAM model that would bring the price down more. I know it goes against the focus on Siri AI, which needs at least 12 GB, but it would at least keep the core audience that would buy an iPad Mini in the first place. It would also help create some more separation between this and the iPad Air. The iPad Mini is $150 cheaper than the iPad Air despite having an OLED screen. The iPad Air, meanwhile, has a larger 11-inch screen and is big enough to use with the Magic Keyboard. You’ll have to weigh a number of trade-offs when choosing between these two.

And with the iPhone Duo’s arrival, the iPad Mini’s size feels even less unique. Sure, the Duo costs $2,000, but it solves the iPad Mini’s main problem: convenience. As your phone, the Duo fits in your pocket, goes with you everywhere, and is far more versatile as a pocket computer. The iPad Mini is less flexible and easier to forget and leave at home. And at $699, you may wonder whether you really need that fancy OLED screen and 12 GB of RAM.

Eric Dane Initially Received Different Diagnosis Before ALS Battle

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Grey’s Anatomy alum Eric Dane shared in his new autobiography My Book of Days: A Memoir of Moments, that he initially received another diagnosis prior to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS.)

Eric Dane was searching for answers before receiving his ALS diagnosis.

In the Grey’s Anatomy alum’s new autobiography My Bood of Days: A Memoir of Moments, he shared he initially received a separate diagnosis before amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).

As Dane recalled, his health battle began when he realized he was “gradually losing any use” of his right hand.

“First, I saw a hand specialist, who passed me off to another hand specialist,” Dane recalled in an excerpt from his book, per US Weekly, “who sent me to a neurologist.”

Once he saw the neurologist, his doctor posited that the Euphoria actor had “contracted some form of neuropathy” but could not determine what he was suffering from, admitting the ailment was “above his pay grade.”

“I shuttled from doctor to doctor and underwent a battery of tests, including one called an EMG, an electromyography test,” Dane recalled, noting his medical team finally confirmed a “diagnostic consensus” of multifocal motor neuropathy.

The initial diagnosis was hopeful, Dane noted, sharing that while it was a “rare autoimmune disorder causing extreme muscle weakness,” the known treatments were “90 percent effective.”

After beginning treatment for multifocal motor neuropathy, Dane’s medical team grew “scared” after he did not respond to treatment.

“‘We’ve been discussing ALS,’” Dane recalled his doctor telling him. “‘But we have to wait and see.’”

Feeling “frustrated and fed up,” The Last Ship star went on to get a lumbar puncture, spinal fluid extraction and brain scans. In 2024, he received his ALS diagnosis, calling it a “death sentence.”

But when Dane—who shared daughters Billie, 16, and Georgia, 14, with ex Rebecca Gayheart—told his family, the heartbreaking news hit him even worse than before.

“I don’t remember a single word I said after that,” Dane wrote. “I remember only feeling a violent impact, like getting the wind knocked out of me, and Rebecca wailing on the other end of the phone, and then I really lose it, and my sobs make my body shudder, and I hear her words pouring out amid her tears, ‘I’m here, we’ll fight this together, I’m not going anywhere.’”

Dane fought his ALS battle for two years before he died Feb. 19 at the age of 53.

For a deeper look into Dane’s fight, keep reading…

Opening Up About His Private Health Battle

Eric Dane shared that he was diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as Lou Gehrig's disease, in April 2025.

"I have been diagnosed with ALS,” Dane told People at the time. “I am grateful to have my loving family by my side as we navigate this next chapter.”

The Euphoria star made it clear that he wasn’t stepping away from the screen amid his diagnosis, sharing in April 2025 that he was continuing to film the final season of the HBO series.

And Dane, whose Amazon MGM Studios series Countdown debuted over the summer, admitted that acting has kept him “sharp” has he navigates his health battle.

"I feel great when I'm at work," he explained to E! News in June 2025. "Of course, there have been some sort of setbacks, but I feel pretty good. My spirit is always pretty buoyant, so at the end of the day, that's all that matters."

Two months after sharing his diagnosis, the Grey’s Anatomy alum spoke candidly about his journey, including the symptoms that led to his diagnosis.

"I started experiencing some weakness in my right hand," Dane told Diane Sawyer in a sit-down on Good Morning America in June 2025. "I didn’t really think anything of it at the time. I thought maybe I’d been texting too much or my hand was fatigued, but a few weeks later I noticed it got a little worse."

After nine months of appointments, which involved visiting two hand specialists and two neurologists, Dane was told he had ALS.

"I will never forget those three letters," the father of two explained. "It's on me the second I wake up. It's not a dream."

While sharing how the rare degenerative disease has affected him physically so far, he also detailed where he sees the progression headed.

“My left side is functioning, my right side has completely stopped working," Dane shared during his interview with Sawyer. "I feel like maybe a couple more months, and I won’t have my left hand either. It’s sobering."

As Dane goes through this health battle, his wife of over 20 years, Rebecca Gayheart, who filed to dismiss their 2018 divorce in March, has been by his side.

“I call Rebecca. I talk to her every day,” Dane shared during his interview with Sawyer. “We have managed to become better friends and better parents, and she is probably my biggest champion and my most stalwart supporter, and I lean on her."

According to Gayheart, she and Dane are the “best of friends.”

“We are really close. We are great co-parents,” the Beverly Hills, 90210 star explained to E! News April 9, 2025. “We really figured out the formula to staying a family and I think our kids are benefiting greatly from it and we are as well.”

While Dane has been marking milestones with his and Gayheart’s daughters Billie and Georgia, he’s admitted he resents how his ALS is affecting the lives of his family members.

"I'm angry, because my father was taken from me when I was young," the actor, whose father died by suicide when he was a young boy, said during his GMA interview. "And now there's a very good chance I'm going to be taken from my girls while they're very young. At the end of the day, all I want to do is spend time with my family and work a little bit, if I can."

Dane was set to present at the 2025 Emmys alongside his Grey's Anatomy costar Jesse Williams on Sept. 14 but ended up missing the award show. A few weeks after his absence, Dane shared that he had to skip the show because of a hospital visit.

“ALS is a nasty disease," he said during an interview with The Washington Post published on Oct. 2. "So I was in the hospital during the Emmys getting stitches put in my head."

"I was a really upset about it,” Dane added. “But you know, there was nothing I could do about it."

Dane met with doctors and representatives from the I Am ALS organization in September, 2025, and spoke with California congressman Eric Swalwell about federal funding towards research for the currently incurable neurological disease.

“I wanna ring every bell,” Dane told Swalwell in a TikTok video posted by the politician on Sept. 29. “I have two daughters at home. I wanna see them graduate college, get married, maybe have grandkids.”

Dane died on Feb. 19 at age 53 following his yearlong battle with ALS. His family confirmed in a statement to E! that he "spent his final days surrounded by dear friends, his devoted wife, and his two beautiful daughters."

“Throughout his journey with ALS, Eric became a passionate advocate for awareness and research, determined to make a difference for others facing the same fight,” his family's statement continued. “He will be deeply missed, and lovingly remembered always. Eric adored his fans and is forever grateful for the outpouring of love and support he’s received. The family has asked for privacy as they navigate this impossible time.”
