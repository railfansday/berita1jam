Richard Sterban, Oak Ridge Boys’ Bass Singer, Dies at 83

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Sterban died Nov. 14 in Hendersonville, Tennessee, due to complications from bronchitis and pneumonia after fighting a battle with cancer, his family and the music group announced.

Richard Sterban, the Oak Ridge Boys’ bass singer who memorably lent his vocals to their beloved “Elvira” cover, has died. He was 83.

Sterban died Nov. 14 in Hendersonville, Tennessee, due to complications from bronchitis and pneumonia after fighting a battle with cancer, his family and the Oak Ridge Boys announced.

“We appreciate all of your love, support, and prayers. Please continue to lift us up in this difficult time, we find comfort knowing that he is currently face to face with Jesus,” Sterban’s family said in a statement. Born April 24, 1943, in Camden, New Jersey, Sterban began singing at six in Sunday school as a soprano. He sang tenor in his seventh-grade glee club, and upon his eighth grade, his voice dropped to bass.

He studied music at Trenton State College (modernly known as the College of New Jersey), and began singing in a gospel quartet where he met Joe Bonsall, his future Oak Ridge Boys bandmate.

While singing part-time, Sterban joined J.D. Sumner and the Stamps, whom Elvis Presley hired to sing behind him. He then sang and recorded with the King of Rock and Roll, and also appeared in the 1972 concert film Elvis on Tour.

Sterban was then offered the opportunity to join the Oak Ridge Boys after the departure of Noel Fox, which he accepted in 1972. Bonsall joined the group one year later, with Duane Allen, William Lee Golden and Sterban already in the group, which carried on as the faces of the band for five decades.

They performed with Johnny Cash, who helped them sign with Columbia Records in 1973, though it was after opening for Roy Clark in 1975 when they met his manger, Jim Halsey, who would later sign with the group.

They reached success with “Y’all Come Back Saloon,” which reached No. 3 on the country chart in 1977, and led to a string of hits. “I’ll Be True to You” became their first No. 1 song, though 1981’s “Elvira” would become one of their largest successes.

Originally recorded by Dallas Frazier, the Oak Ridge Boys’ version of the track went to No. 1 on the country chart and No. 5 on the pop chart, sold more than two million copies and won a Grammy. Sterban’s bass refrain was a major touchpoint on the song.

The Oak Ridge Boys went on to also become members of the Grand Ole Opry, the Gospel Music Hall of Fame and the Country Music Hall of Fame.

Sterban released his memoir From Elvis to Elvira: My Life on Stage, written with Steven Robinson, in 2012.

Sterban is survived by his wife, Donna; his daughters, Lauren (Tyler) and Tori (Michael); his sons, Rich (Chele), Doug, and Chris; his grandchildren, Mali (Zachary), Nikolas, Tyler (Ryann), Madelyn, Alexandra, Rex, Owen, and Charli Grace; and his great-grandchildren, Salem, Asher, and Theo. He was preceded in death by his mother Victoria Sterban, brother Joseph Sterban and grandson Zachary.

The Best Peanuts Character of ‘A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving’ Isn’t Charlie Brown

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

One would think that, by having a character's name in the title, that character would be the most important in the TV show. There is no point in calling the series Malcolm in the Middle if there isn't a Malcolm that is front and center, right there in the middle, for example. By that logic, the most important character in the beloved TV special A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving is Charlie Brown himself, right? Wrong. The most important character in A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving is arguably its most unassuming: the bookish Marcie (Jimmy Ahrens).

Back inside his home after Lucy (Robin Kohn) pulls the football away at the last moment, when he's running to kick it, yet again, Charlie Brown (Todd Barbee) answers the phone. It's Peppermint Patty (Christopher DeFaria), and after the obligatory "How you been, Chuck?" Patty tells Charlie Brown that because her dad's out of town, she can come to his house and share Thanksgiving with him, ending the decidedly one-sided conversation. He barely has time to collect his thoughts before Patty calls back, saying she's invited Marcie along for dinner. Then she calls back again, saying that she's now invited Franklin (Robin Reed).

The problem is that Charlie Brown won't even be there, with him and his family spending Thanksgiving at his grandmother's house. He can't call Peppermint Patty back, because, as he says, "You never get to say anything." But dinner isn't until 4:30, so he has time to put together something, so it's all hands on deck as he, Sally (Hilary Momberger), Linus (Stephen Shea) and Snoopy (Bill Melendez) set up a table, chairs, and prepare a meal consisting of toast, pretzel sticks, popcorn, and jelly beans.

It's not turkey, but given the time and resources available, it's pretty decent, especially for children (jelly beans for dinner sounds great). So when Patty, Marcie and Franklin arrive, everything's set to go. Linus leads them in a history lesson wrapped up in a grace. The dinner is unveiled and everyone digs in... except for Patty. She is irate, wondering who the blockhead is that came up with the menu when it's supposed to be turkey, mashed potatoes, pumpkin pie, and all the other conventional offerings of a Thanksgiving dinner. As she's ranting, Charlie Brown slinks away, having failed once more.

If Patty wasn't going to let anyone else get a word in edgewise before, she sure as Dickens wasn't going to in her enraged state. Yet it's Marcie — quiet, bookish, intellectual Marcie — that stands alone in the face of Hurricane Peppermint Patty. She doesn't raise her voice, but rather speaks calmly, with a gentle, quiet strength, and challenges Patty, saying: "Now, wait a minute, sir. Did he invite you here to dinner, or did you invite yourself and us too?"

Confronted by the truth, Patty realizes she's 100% in the wrong and has hurt Charlie Brown deeply by being unappreciative of the efforts he made to create something special regardless. It's a complete reversal that only happens because Marcie is brave enough to call Patty out. Marcie then pushes Patty to apologize directly to Charlie Brown, but quickly realizes Patty is too ashamed to do it herself. So she's the one who sets things right by apologizing to Charlie Brown on Patty's behalf, but goes even further by helping Chuck understand that he didn't ruin Thanksgiving, but rather fulfilled its purpose, in this exchange:

"Thanksgiving is more than eating, Chuck. You heard what Linus was saying out there. Those early pilgrims were thankful for what had happened to them. And we should be thankful too. We should just be thankful for being together. I think that's what they mean by "Thanksgiving," Charlie Brown."

A long-time 'Peanuts' character finally gets his time in the spotlight.

In this regard, she serves the same purpose as Linus did for A Charlie Brown Christmas, delivering the lines that underscore the meaning behind the season. With that, they all come together, and as a group goes to Charlie Brown's grandmother's for a true Thanksgiving dinner after she invites them to participate, the proverbial happy ending. None of this happens without Marcie, making her the most important character in the special. Peppermint Patty completely sours the proceedings, Charlie Brown sits by himself to wallow in his own self-loathing over a situation he never even created, and the friend group lies fractured on a day it should be celebrated. So thank you, Marcie, for being the voice of reason for the Thanksgiving season. Enjoy your toast.

Ohio State defensive line produces a season-high in sacks vs Northwestern

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ohio State's defensive line went into the Nov. 14 Northwestern game knowing its pass rush needed to improve.

"We had to get back there. We had to get pressure," defensive tackle Eddrick Houston said. "We had to get sacks and get that pressure off the DBs, who are trying to cover for that long. ... Coverage and rush work together, so we just had to do what we needed to do."

The Buckeyes snapped a two-game losing skid, defeating the Wildcats 28-19 and logging their highest sack total since September 2025 with five.

The defensive line, after failing to record a sack the past four games, accounted for all of the Buckeyes' sacks, which pushed the Wildcats back 25 total yards and led to two fourth downs.

In those games the defensive line went without a sack, Ohio State as a whole had just two, both credited to defensive backs, Jermaine Mathews Jr. and Jaylen McClain. The Buckeyes were ranked 121th in the nation in sacks with 11.

"It's something we've been emphasizing," Ohio State coach Ryan Day said. "Getting more pressure on the quarterback."

Against Northwestern, defensive end Kenyatta Jackson Jr. finished with two sacks despite remaining on the bench for nearly two full drives at the beginning of the game. Has a team-high five.

Alabama transfers Qua Russaw and James Smith Jr., both defensive ends, each recorded a sack, a first for Smith at Ohio State. Will Smith Jr. was the only defensive tackle to sack Wildcats quarterback Nicco Marchiol.

Houston, who has three sacks in his four seasons with the Buckeyes, still took pride in the Buckeyes hitting a season-high in sacks, even though a majority came from the edges rather than the middle of the line.

"It felt great, it all coming together," Houston said. "Rushing four as one, and allowing our ends to eat, but at the same time, while our ends are eating, we can work stuff in the middle to allow us to get sacks as well."

The veteran Buckeye admitted the defensive line had begun to shy away from the pass rush and began to put more emphasis on stopping the run after its performance against Illinois, in which Ohio State allowed 199 rushing yards.

"We've got some great pass rushers," Houston said. "Great guys in there that can pass rush, but we kind of got away from that and focused on stopping the run. Now that we've stopped the run, we've got to get to that quarterback. I just feel like we took that next step."

The run defense was progressing in the three games following Illinois, giving up an average 64.7 rushing yards. But in the losses to USC and Oregon, Ohio State allowed 112 rushing yards per game and conceded five rushing touchdowns, in addition to lacking a pass rush.

Northwestern had been averaging 190 rushing yards per game, the second-most in the Big Ten. On 29 run attempts against Ohio State, the Wildcats posted just 53 yards, marking just the second time they were held to under 100 rushing yards.

The Buckeyes limiting Northwestern's ground game was impactful on the pass rush, especially on long-yardage third down plays, on which Ohio State forced Marchiol into incomplete or short-yardage passes five times.

"When you can put them into a situation where they're one-dimensional, it certainly allows us more [pressure] opportunities," Day said. "There were some times in the game where we struggled on third down, getting off the field, but we got them off schedule a bunch, which allowed us an opportunity to get after the quarterback."

Miami's Scarlett Schremmer DQ'd from LPGA event for unauthorized yardage book

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Scarlett Schremmer's LPGA debut will now end in disqualification, not with a missed cut.

The Miami sophomore was determined by LPGA officials to have used an unauthorized yardage book during competition at The Annika driven by Gainbridge, where Schremmer was competing on a sponsor exemption and shot 69-74.

The LPGA, which first learned about the potential infraction on Thursday before reviewing photo evidence following Friday's second round, issued the following statement: "Schremmer violated Model Local Rule G-11, which, per the LPGA Rules of Play Card, is in effect during all LPGA Tour competitions, except at off-site local qualifying events. This Model Local Rule requires that players are limited throughout their round to using only the yardage book that has been approved for use in the competition."

Schremmer later released her own statement, confirming that her yardage book had illegal green-reading materials in it.

"I had a green reading yardage book in my bag this week that's allowed in college golf but isn't permitted on the LPGA Tour," Schremmer wrote. "That's on me. I should have checked, and it's a lesson learned. I had a great week competing out here and I'm grateful to the Doyle family, Pelican and the LPGA for the opportunity. I am excited to continue to work with the goal of playing out here again. Thank you to everyone who came out and supported me this week."

Prior to Schremmer's DQ, one of her playing competitors, Perrine Delacour, took to social media on Saturday morning, commenting on a Monday Q Info post, "Can we talk about the yardage [book] illegal and LPGA trust the sponsor invite over 2 LPGA players and 2 caddies."

Delacour declined to elaborate when reached by Golfweek's Beth Ann Nichols, instead saying that she handled the matter with the LPGA.

Schremmer recently wrapped up her fall semester with the Hurricanes, during which she won twice, and she enters winter break ranked 17th in the country.

8 Neo-Western Shows That Define The Genre

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The western was one of the first TV genres to gain traction. Like cop shows and variety shows and classic situation comedies, the western is an enduring staple of the television landscape, and like those other genres, it never goes away; it just evolves. Back in the TV western’s heyday, old-school cowboy shows like Lancer and Wagon Train and The Virginian dominated the airwaves. But audiences eventually fell out of love with their tired tropes and rigid formulas and romanticized view of the ugliest period in American history, and the genre had to adapt with the times.

Now, the airwaves (or, rather, streaming-waves) are dominated by neo-westerns, subverting the conventions of those old classics. Neo-westerns can either bring the tropes of westerns into a modern-day setting, like Yellowstone and Justified, or they can do something else entirely, like the spacefaring adventures of Cowboy Bebop or the philosophical meditations of HBO’s Westworld.

Taylor Sheridan has singlehandedly revitalized the TV western with the Yellowstone saga. Yellowstone has proven to be so popular that it’s no longer just a hit show in its own right; it’s been expanded into a Marvel-sized cinematic universe of sequels, prequels, and spinoffs. We’ve seen different eras of the Dutton family, different corners of the Yellowstone universe, and different chapters of the overall timeline.

But the flagship show remains the most compelling. Yellowstone brings the land disputes of Shane and the complicated family dynamics of East of Eden and the horse-wrangling antics of The Cowboys into a modern setting. The show relaunched Kevin Costner’s career and made Sheridan the Shonda Rhimes of dad TV.

Walker, Texas Ranger ran for the better part of a decade, and its success had nothing to do with the subtlety of its storytelling or the profundity of its themes or the poignancy of its character development; it had everything to do with the irresistible star power of Chuck Norris. Norris was one of the greatest action heroes to take to the screen, and Walker, Texas Ranger was the perfect vehicle for his talents.

Viewers spent nine seasons tuning in to watch Norris’ U.S. Marine-turned-Texas Ranger/kung fu teacher Cordell Walker use his martial arts skills to bring bad guys to justice. Walker, Texas Ranger was the last classic TV western before Deadwood and Hell on Wheels would bring a gritty realism to the genre, and it ran for more than 200 episodes.

Kurt Sutter’s creation of Sons of Anarchy was primarily influenced by Hamlet. It reimagines all the archetypes from William Shakespeare’s seminal play — the grieving son, the usurping stepdad, the morally gray mother — and filtered them through the lens of a contemporary California biker gang. Sons of Anarchy is essentially The Sopranos on wheels. It has all the crime family dynamics of The Sopranos, contrasting relatable family situations with life-or-death stakes, but with the rubber-burning thrills of hitting the open road atop a Harley.

While you could accurately describe it as Hamlet meets The Sopranos on wheels, Sons of Anarchy’s setting is very western-coded. Jax Teller and his fellow bikers are modern-day cowboys — on a steel horse, they ride — and all the chases along dusty roads and armed standoffs in the desert feel very analogous to classic TV westerns.

Michael Crichton’s original Westworld film was a pretty straightforward chase movie. The theme park full of robotic cowboys was just a novel setting for a relentless cat-and-mouse pursuit between Yul Brynner’s unstoppable gunslinger and Richard Benjamin’s frightened tourist. But Jonathan Nolan and Lisa Joy took the bones of that old B-movie and turned it into a complex, thought-provoking philosophical examination of artificial intelligence for HBO.

Westworld was poised to be HBO’s sci-fi alternative to Game of Thrones. Where Thrones had branched outside fantasy fans to attract mainstream viewers, Westworld hoped to do the same. Ultimately, it was much too complicated to have that kind of impact. But even just as a western fan, it was fun to see such a grand, cinematic show deconstruct the genre in such an experimental and esoteric way on such a huge, ambitious scale.

Adapted from Tony Hillerman’s Leaphorn & Chee books, Dark Winds essentially brings a neo-western edge to an old-school cop show. It combines elements of westerns, police noirs, and psychological thrillers in a chilling tale of three Navajo Tribal Police officers patrolling the Four Corners area in the American Southwest, circa the ‘70s.

It mixes the frontier justice of a western like Gunslinger with the mystery and intrigue of a neo-noir like Chinatown. Zahn McClarnon shines in the lead role of grizzled cop Joe Leaphorn, and his uneasy alliance with a border patrol agent and an undercover federal officer provides a compelling dramatic anchor for the series.

Cowboy Bebop was the gateway anime that opened a lot of western viewers’ eyes to the joys of Japanese animation in the late ‘90s. This sci-fi western noir takes all the familiar tropes of an old cowboy show — bounty hunters chasing bad guys, drifting from town to town, fighting crime in a lawless wasteland — and launches them to the edge of the universe.

In a future where the Solar System has been colonized, and the human race is more divided than ever, the crew of the Bebop balance their fledgling bounty-hunting career with their heartbreaking personal struggles. Every episode is a dazzling intergalactic adventure, but it’s also a deeply touching human drama about fascinating, layered, sympathetic characters. Cowboy Bebop paved the way for every TV space western from Firefly to The Mandalorian.

After playing a hardware salesman-turned-reluctant sheriff in HBO’s Deadwood, Timothy Olyphant took his gun belt and his cowboy boots and swaggered 130-ish years into the future to play a very different kind of TV gunslinger in Justified. Although it’s set in the modern day, Justified is more of a traditional western than Deadwood was. Deadwood was brutal and bloody and painstakingly historically accurate, but Justified is a lighthearted action series about a U.S. Marshal chasing fugitives across the American frontier.

Justified was based on an Elmore Leonard short story, “Fire in the Hole,” and the dialogue has that Leonard-lite, Tarantinoan clip to it. Olyphant shares an endlessly riveting cat-and-mouse dynamic with his on-screen arch-nemesis Walton Goggins. In its first season, Justified started out as a traditional case-of-the-week show, but it became a more serialized drama starting in season 2, and then it was off to the races.

Vince Gilligan changed the way a story could be told on television with Breaking Bad. David Chase had introduced the notion of the TV antihero in The Sopranos, but Gilligan took it a step further and used the long-form medium of television to chronicle a character’s gradual turn from hero to villain. Tony Soprano is the same character in the pilot episode that he is in the series finale, but Walter White slowly transforms from a timid suburban family man into a ruthless drug lord with plenty of blood on his hands, and Bryan Cranston played every step of that dramatic journey pitch-perfectly.

Gilligan told that story of moral downfall through the lens of a contemporary western. He set Breaking Bad on the dusty streets and sun-scorched deserts of Albuquerque; he put an armed standoff in every other episode; he even had Walt and his cohorts rob a train in the final season.

What are the limitations of eGPUs vs onboard graphics cards for laptops?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

An eGPU can bring desktop-class graphics to a laptop, but cables, bandwidth limits and extra costs can eat into the advantage.

Connecting a desktop graphics card to your laptop seems like a neat solution to one of the oldest problems in portable computing. Use the thin machine for work and travel, and connect to a much more powerful GPU when you get home. An external GPU (eGPU) can do just that, but all that extra graphics power still has to travel through Thunderbolt or USB4 instead of the direct internal connection used by a built-in laptop GPU. That bandwidth bottleneck can eat into the desktop card's advantage, and the cable it's connected to holds sway as well.

In this comparison, "onboard" primarily refers to a dedicated GPU built into the laptop, not the integrated graphics in the laptop's processor. Even when a laptop GPU shares its name with a desktop GPU, the hardware can be vastly different. The RTX 5090 Laptop GPU features 10,496 CUDA cores, 24GB of GDDR7 memory, and a GPU subsystem power range of 95W to 150W. Meanwhile, you'll find 21,760 CUDA cores and 32GB of GDDR7 memory on NVIDIA's desktop RTX 5090. That's a significant gap before even considering bottlenecks, like Thunderbolt.

A modern eGPU typically communicates with the laptop via Thunderbolt or USB4, rather than directly on its PCIe bus, and that's where some of the desktop card's edge is lost. A built-in laptop GPU uses an internal PCIe connection instead.

Thunderbolt 4 has 40 Gbps of overall bandwidth and requires 32 Gbps of PCIe data throughput, while Thunderbolt 5 upgrades the base speed to 80 Gbps and doubles that PCIe requirement to 64 Gbps. Internal laptop GPUs can use wider multi-lane PCIe connections, depending on the laptop's design, giving them more room to move data without the Thunderbolt bottleneck.

That bottleneck is more noticeable when it comes to a powerful desktop card. NVIDIA's desktop RTX 5090 has 1,792GB/s of memory bandwidth and a 575W total graphics power rating, compared with 896GB/s and a 95W to 150W GPU subsystem power range for the RTX 5090 Laptop GPU. Those aren't PCIe bandwidth figures, but they show how much more raw hardware the desktop card brings before the external connection starts holding it back.

Thunderbolt 5 also has a 120 Gbps Bandwidth Boost mode, but that isn't 120 Gbps of PCIe bandwidth for the GPU. Intel says PCIe data throughput remains at 64 Gbps. While USB4 can also support PCIe traffic, a USB-C-shaped port alone doesn't guarantee eGPU compatibility. The host hardware needs the right support, and the enclosure, drivers, GPU and power requirements must all cooperate (because apparently one cable was getting too easy).

Using the laptop's built-in display can add another penalty because rendered frames may have to travel back through the same limited connection. This is why some brands suggest connecting an external monitor directly to the eGPU for better performance. There's no universal performance-loss percentage, since the GPU, laptop, connection, resolution and game all affect the result.

A laptop GPU begins with less raw hardware, but it doesn't have to deal with most of this plumbing. It operates within the laptop's cooling and power constraints, doesn't have to send rendered frames back over an external cable, and remains in the laptop when you pick it up. The portability benefit is more like a suggestion with a traditional desktop-card eGPU. You now have an enclosure, a desktop GPU, and maybe an ATX power supply on the desk, while a big card can add size and noise to the mix.

Let's not forget to talk about the cost associated with eGPUs. As an example, Razer's Thunderbolt 5 Core X V2 supports full-size NVIDIA and AMD desktop cards up to four slots wide. The enclosure also has its own 120mm cooling fan, but it doesn't come with a GPU or even the necessary ATX power supply to keep it running. Those would be added expenses on top of approximately $350 for the enclosure itself.

That math gets considerably friendlier if you have a suitable GPU sitting around after a desktop upgrade. At that point, the enclosure could significantly upgrade your laptop without buying another computer. For its part, Manchester United found its captain Bruno Fernandes, starter after the controversy with Michael Carrick. The Portuguese had openly criticized his coach’s game plan after the defeat against Como (4-1) in the Champions League. In difficulty in recent weeks, Frenchman Leny Yoro occupied the right flank of the Mancunian defense.

Overall dominant, the Mancuniens imposed their rhythm, but the two teams returned to the locker room with a goalless draw.

Between Leny Yoro and Harry Maguire, the hole left in the center was gaping: Rogers only had to slip into this space to present himself against Senne Lammens and steal his ball perfectly (1-0, 49th).
