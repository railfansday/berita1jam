WR Ryan Flournoy continues showing up in clutch situations

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Dallas Cowboys continue to disappoint at just about every turn. They've underperformed in all three phases of the game this season and show no signs of coming anywhere close to the team many believed could be Super Bowl contenders. Their latest loss to the San Francisco 49ers was yet another example of how far this team has fallen.

With everything that is happening in the Cowboys 2026 NFL season, it's really difficult to appreciate some of the good things that have taken place thus far this year. But, that's exactly what we want to do today by shining the spotlight on wide receiver Ryan Flournoy, who quietly having a career year.

Now in Year 3 with the Cowboys, the former 2024 sixth-round pick has gone from someone trying to stick around on the roster to a reliable weapon in one of the most talented offensive units in the entire league. No. 19 is currently having himself a career year and has been absolutely clutching important situations here recently.

To date, Flournoy has caught 38 passes for 466 receiving yards and three touchdowns. That already just about equals his 2025 totals of 40 receptions for 475 yards and four touchdowns. In just over the halfway point of the 2026 season, there's no telling what his statistics will look like at the end of the season if he continues to produce.

Flournoy's success is a testament to his work ethic. He's put in the work both on and off the field and earned the trust of his QB, Dak Prescott. It's obvious Prescott trusts him in any given situation, even if the game is on the line, as evidenced by some clutch plays over the past couple of weeks.

Two weeks ago against the Indianapolis Colts, he made arguably the most impactful play of the game. With 97 seconds left on the clock he made a one-handed catch with All-Pro CB Sauce Gardner draped all over him. That reception was pivotal in the Cowboys regaining the lead and ultimately winning the game.

Although the 49ers ultimately won, Flournoy once again made a couple of clutch plays to keep the Cowboys in the game last week. He ended the game with six catches for 71 yards and a touchdown, but made two key plays. First a big catch in the third quarter down the sideline to move the ball in the 49ers territory and later with his TD catch in the fourth.

With players like CeeDee Lamb and George Pickens ahead of him on the depth chart, Flournoy is never going to be the go-to WR for the Cowboys. That seems to be okay though because he's thriving as Dallas' WR3 this year and most importantly is answering every time his number is called.

It's a little too early to start thinking about what his future may hold with the Cowboys next season, but it's looking bright nonetheless. They were wise to lock him up for another year, especially with George Pickens future with the organization up in the air.

It's going to be really interesting to see how the remainder of the season plays out for Ryan Flournoy. If he continues to produce like he has recently he should finish the year with 60-70 receptions for around 800 receiving yards and a half a dozen touchdowns. Not bad numbers for WR who's third on the Cowboys depth chart.

Istruzioni per l'uso del calciomercato: dallo scouting al moneyball e l'AI, tutto quello che c'è da sapere

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Il calciomercato è il nostro core business, d'accordo. Ma è pure quel concetto astratto che tiene svegli i tifosi durante nelle fasi operative e che alimenta i sogni dei bambini. Dietro le notizie c'è però un mondo che molti non conoscono e che nella giornata di oggi proveremo a raccontarvi nel dettaglio, partendo dai metodi classici per acquistare un giocatore per arrivare ai nuovi modi di fare, sempre più creativi, di agenti e direttori sportivi. Spoiler: l'intelligenza artificiale ha fatto il suo ingresso prepotente anche in questo mondo.

Il mondo delle trattative ha funzionato così per decenni. Impersonificato dagli storici direttori sportivi italiani, basava il suo fondamento sui contatti personali dei vari dirigenti di club. Rapporti più o meno stretti con agenti e intermediari, rete di collaboratori in giro per il mondo e scouting. Tanto scouting. Alcune società ci hanno costruito sopra una fortuna, scovando sui campi di periferia talenti poi diventati icone mondiali. Concetti che esistono ancora ma che stanno piano piano scomparendo.

Nell'ultimo decennio il calcio ha incontrato i dati e le sfaccettature proprie del mondo finanziario. Mettendo tutto nel pentolone ne è uscito fuori il Moneyball, di derivazione dal baseball. In soldoni si tratta di andare ad individuare le inefficienze del mercato, comprando il "prodotto" sottovalutato e non il grande nome. Significa, semplificando ancora, comprare a 5 ciò che in realtà vale 20. In questo ha sempre più peso l'utilizzo dell'AI, con i suoi modelli predittivi che hanno fatto irruzione nelle stanze dei bottoni dei vari club, non solo di Serie A.

C'è poi chi si affida al momento storico per indirizzare le strategie. C'è chi qua e là nel tempo ha cercato di costruire degli "instant team", squadre che puntano su nomi e giocatori di conclamata esperienza per provare a vincere tutto e subito. Raramente questa strada ha funzionato, e infatti si vede sempre meno. O c'è chi scandaglia il panorama mondiale alla ricerca dei migliori parametri zero: un modo semplice per abbattere il costo dei cartellini in favore di ingaggi (e commissioni) più corpose.

Ella Langley’s ‘Choosin’ Texas’ Overtakes Mariah Carey for Most Weeks Atop Streaming Songs Chart

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

"Choosin' Texas" reigns for a record-setting 27th week amid the beginning of the 2026 holiday season.

Nearly two years after Mariah Carey’s “All I Want for Christmas Is You” set a new mark for the most weeks at No. 1 on Billboard’s Streaming Songs chart, a new song has assumed the throne — and it’s the same one that broke Carey’s Billboard Hot 100 longevity record earlier this year.

That’s right: Ella Langley’s “Choosin’ Texas” adds another notch to its belt on the Nov. 21-dated Billboard charts, reigning on Streaming Songs for a record-setting 27th week since the tally’s 2013 inception. Related Noah Kahan Reacts to 2027 Grammy Nominations Snub: 'I Was In My Feelings for a Sec' Katie Atkinson

In the week ending Nov. 12, “Choosin’ Texas” earned 17.8 million chart-eligible streams in the United States, down 5%, according to Luminate.

The song first topped Streaming Songs on the Jan. 31 survey and has led the ranking over multiple periods since, most recently on the Oct. 24 tally prior to its return to No. 1. That week, it tied Carey’s mark of 26 frames, which she set during the 2025 holiday season (Jan. 3, 2026).

Speaking of the holidays, “All I Want for Christmas Is You” returns to Streaming Songs’ top 10 on the latest chart, thanks to 10.1 million streams, a boost of 42%. It first reached No. 1 in 2019 and has topped the ranking for anywhere between three and five weeks every year since, except for 2023, when it was unable to pass Brenda Lee’s “Rockin’ Around the Christmas Tree” for the duration of the season.

It’s worthwhile to bring up 2023, when a holiday song other than Carey’s standard was the most streamed of the season, because the Nov. 21 chart features Wham!’s “Last Christmas” ahead of Carey at No. 4 (11 million streams, a gain of 48%).

However, if “All I Want for Christmas Is You” is able to pass “Last Christmas” once the holiday season kicks into full gear, it’s possible the former could retake its Streaming Songs longevity record from “Choosin’ Texas” — especially as holiday tracks continue to rise while Langley’s current recordholder falls, albeit just 5% on the latest ranking.

For now, “Choosin’ Texas” is the new all-time leader in weeks at No. 1, a record it also added to on the Nov. 21-dated Hot 100, spending its 28th week atop the tally, as previously reported.

John Yoo Joins the Exodus from California; Departs for Texas

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

The University of California at Berkeley's law school loses its last right-leaning professor to teach in public law.

The Daily Californian reports that Professor John Yoo is leaving the University of California at Berkeley's law school. Beginning next year, he will be full-time at the University of Texas at Austin's School of Civic Leadership.

"Let the celebrations at my fan clubs in the City of Berkeley begin," Yoo said in an email. "Great as our university is, it would not allow a school to focus on restoring the central importance of western civilization to the education of all undergraduates." . . .

"I am sorry, however, that my departure will reduce the number of conservative professors in public law at Berkeley by 100 percent," Yoo said in the email. "I hope that the university will take this as an opportunity to consider that the notion of diversity should, most importantly, include ideology, not just the usual race, gender, and economic categories that my colleagues usually mean by diversity."

There had been one other right-leaning law professor at Berkeley, our co-blogger Orin Kerr, but he left Berkeley for Stanford in 2025.

According to Dean of Berkeley Law Erwin Chemerinsky, Yoo's decision to leave the faculty was entirely his own.

"John (Yoo) has been an important part of the faculty for many years," Chemerinsky said in an email. "This was entirely John's decision to retire. … He has indicated to me for a few years his thoughts about retiring. John is a very successful teacher and an eminent scholar." . . .

Yoo's move is also less drastic than it might appear, as he is moving from one blue state to another.  All of the other California transplants that have moved to Austin helped make sure of that (with a major assist from the Republican Party).

Boy George on His Controversial Israel Trip, Ed Sheeran and the Friends He Lost Along the Way

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Culture Club singer names the former collaborator who told him “Shame on you” and backs Robert Kraft’s decision to bar Macklemore from Gillette Stadium: “You can take your freedom of speech over there and do it on the street.”

Boy George wrote his new single, “We Will Dance Again,” after visiting the Nova Exhibition in London, an immersive installation that commemorates the Oct. 7, 2023, attacks on the Nova Music Festival that resulted in the deaths of 378 people and 44 people taken hostage.

He did it, he says, to send a message to his Jewish friends: “Not everybody hates you. You are loved.”

The Culture Club frontman, 65, was not nearly prepared for the massive backlash that ensued, which spilled outside of social media into friendships that went back years. Undeterred, he recently visited Israel and played two sold-out shows at Tel Aviv’s Menora Mivtachim Arena on Nov. 4 and 7. He also met with Israeli president Isaac Herzog and Nova survivors.

With his single now officially released and available on most streaming platforms, Boy George checked in with The Hollywood Reporter on a stop in Chicago.

He opened up about his Israel trip, explained why he used AI to make “We Will Dance Again” and revealed where he stands on the Ed Sheeran/Macklemore controversy that resulted in Sheeran’s entire band walking off of his world tour.

What’s a memory you cherish from the trip to Israel?

One of the sweetest things to me was people from London saying, “Oh, my aunt’s in Israel, can she come and see you? And will you say hello?” And I met a few friends of mine’s families that they haven’t seen for years. And just meeting the Nova survivors, that was probably the highlight of the whole trip, because you suddenly understand why this journey started in the first place.

I saw the Nova exhibition in L.A. It’s a very, very dark exhibit. When you played Israel, didn’t some of the survivors come onstage with you?

Yeah. There was about 13 survivors and also people that lost family. So it was a real mix of people. I think it was really beautiful to let people be seen. And obviously, as a performer, you are uncomfortable because you don’t want to hijack someone else’s experience. You want to show it respect, but at the same time, going to Israel was a massive statement, and I had to be bold about it.

Somebody that reviewed my show said I should have snuck in quietly. I was like, “That wasn’t an option.” And sneaking in quietly right now is not helpful because nobody else is being quiet. So I don’t think it’s the time to be quiet. I think it’s the time to be clear and to be loving. And I’ve made some mistakes in my communication online. There are people I shouldn’t have bothered reacting to.

You’ve said you’ve lost friends over this. Is there anyone you’d name?

Martyn Ware, who was in Human League and Heaven 17. I’ve worked with Martyn. I did a version of “I Wanna Be Your Dog” with him, and we sung onstage together. He came out quite early and said, “Shame on you,” and all of that. I just tweeted back, “You’ve lost that loving feeling. “

Most of them I don’t want to name because I don’t want them to have any more attention than they’ve already had from me.

You’ve said the music industry has been very one-sided on this. Do you find most people in the industry are anti-Israel?

I don’t think they’re anti-Israel. I just think they’re not necessarily informed about history, and it’s become quite trendy to shout certain things out. There’s a lot of pressure to agree with everybody else. Your thought, my thought, are not the deciding factor on what happens in the Middle East. We’ve got nothing to do with it. We’re not going to change it. So what we can do is, in our immediate surroundings, be more loving, be more open, be more kind, respectful, all of those things that you can do.

I was getting messages from friends of mine all over the world. Some people I didn’t even know were Jewish. And I’ve had letters from people. So there is a lot of support out there, but it is scared of itself.

When I saw you speaking up for your Jewish friends on Ireland’s Late Late Show in May, I knew you had something in your heart that needed expressing. I didn’t realize to what extent it would end up expressing itself.

When I decided to make this record, I was sending a message to my friends to say, “Not everybody hates you. You are loved.” And that’s the point I tried to make when I was in Ireland, is that growing up during the punk period, one of my biggest allies was a man called Philip Sallon.

One of the first things he ever said to me was, “You look Irish.” And I said, “I actually am.” And he said, “I’m a Jew and a homosexual.” And that was 1976. And when you’re young and impressionable, you don’t forget those moments. And Philip was a big reason why I got to know so many Jewish people because in London, this one knows that one, that one knows that one.

I can’t sit here and say every single Jewish person I’ve ever met I’ve got on with. Of course I haven’t. But Jewish people are characterful, they’re kind, they’re passionate, they’re funny, they’re bitchy. They’ve got all the qualities that I love.

You said you used AI to make “We Will Dance Again.” What aspect of the song uses AI?

The music I wrote with an AI tool. The melody I [wrote], the words I [wrote] And the reason I used AI was because I didn’t want somebody hanging over my shoulder saying, “Oh, you can’t say that. Oh, that’s too harsh.” And that would’ve happened because I’ve had 40 years of that. Forty years of “That’s too gay, that’s too this, that’s too that, that’s not commercial.”

I make a lot of music. I put out almost 200 tracks in the last two or three years. So I’m always writing. In respect to “We Will Dance Again,” that line, that’s a hook. You hear it straight away. As a writer, you go, “Got it. I got it. OK, there we go.”

I saw a video where you were performing a concert and the music cut out and you kept singing a cappella. I couldn’t tell whether it was intentional sabotage or a technical snafu. What happened?

I think it was a “technical snafu.” I love that word. I don’t think it was deliberate. There was a couple of moments when the mic went and I wasn’t sure whether I’d pulled out the wire. And also the guitar went out in a really pivotal moment, but I kept my cool. We were in the round, so there was nowhere to hide. When you’re in a proscenium situation, you can always dart off into a curtain for a minute, but in this instance, you couldn’t really leave.

What it reminded me of is Ed Sheeran, who went onstage after his band walked out on him and did his show a cappella.

In the moment, you always have to remember that people have paid to see you and that you’re there to perform. You can’t get lost in something, but it can really distract your whole evening. I think it’s really important to go, “No, no, no, I have to get through this.”

Do you have anything else to say about Ed Sheeran in terms of that whole controversy regarding Macklemore being dropped from his tour?

Obviously, the stadium owner, Robert Kraft, I know that he’s Jewish. He spent a long time fighting antisemitism, so he was never going to allow that to carry on where he worked, where he’s an owner.

You have a choice who comes and sits at your dinner table, whether you’re a billionaire or a housewife, you make a choice. And if you’ve cooked the meal and paid for the building, you do have a right as a person, as a Jewish person, to say, “No, you can take your freedom of speech over there and do it on the street.”

I told my editor that “Do You Really Want to Hurt Me” is like a Jewish mother song.

Fast forward to today, and once more the NFC South is hilarious. Brad Pitt Wants to Play a James Bond Villain

The announcement finally arrived this week: Robert Pattinson is James Bond. But before we could even come up for air about Amazon MGM and director Denis Villeneuve’s decision to have arguably the busiest movie star in the business today follow Daniel Craig as 007, the Internet had already moved on. Fans started asking more questions. Who will costar as the next Bond girl? Who’s performing the song? And, most importantly, who would play opposite Pattinson as the villain?

Well, how about Brad Pitt? The F1 star likely didn’t expect a James Bond question when he stepped on the red carpet for the premiere of his latest film, The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth. The spinoff sequel of Quentin Tarantino’s Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood sees Pitt return as stuntman Cliff Booth under the direction of David Fincher, which couldn’t be further from the life of a British MI6 hitman. Still, Pitt was excited to hear for the first time that Pattinson landed the role when a reporter for ExtraTV shared the news. “I love this idea,” Pitt said. “He’s the real deal.”

What Is a Thought Made Of? Scientists Reveal Why Human Consciousness May Be Beyond the Reach of AI

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A familiar song can pull you back decades in an instant, and the chemistry behind that rush of memory may hold clues to what separates human minds from AI.

How does the brain turn a collection of memories into thoughts, emotions, and the experience of being conscious? Scientists have spent decades studying how neurons communicate through electrical and chemical signals, yet the physical processes that allow us to recall experiences, attach feelings to them, and give them meaning remain incompletely understood.

A new scientific review suggests that part of the answer may lie in the complex chemistry surrounding brain cells. Rather than viewing memory primarily as a product of neuronal activity, the researchers propose that interactions among neurons, supporting brain cells, and molecules in their surrounding environment could play a fundamental role in shaping thought and conscious experience.

Dr. Gerard Marx of MX Biotech Ltd. and Prof. Chaim Gilon of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem’s Department of Chemistry present their theory in Consciousness, Memory, and Intelligence: A Mechanistic Perspective. Building on earlier research into how experiences might be physically stored in the brain, they examine whether the chemistry of memory could help explain the origins of thought and consciousness.

Their proposal also raises a question about artificial intelligence: If human memory and emotion depend on the chemistry of living cells, can computers ever develop consciousness simply by becoming more powerful?

Scientists have long studied how neurons communicate to form and retrieve memories. But Marx and Gilon suggest that important parts of this process may also occur in the molecular environment surrounding brain cells.

Their model, called the Tripartite Mechanism of Memory, involves three interacting components: brain cells, particularly neurons and astrocytes; the molecular material surrounding those cells; and chemicals such as metal ions, neurotransmitters, and gliotransmitters.

According to the theory, these components could allow the brain to chemically encode and retrieve information. Rather than serving only as a support structure, the environment surrounding brain cells may actively participate in memory storage.

Astrocytes, star-shaped cells that interact closely with neurons, play a central role in the proposed mechanism. The researchers suggest that coordinated activity among these cells, neurons, and surrounding molecules could contribute to how memories are formed and processed.

A familiar song can bring back memories from decades ago, complete with feelings that seemed long forgotten. Such experiences show that remembering involves more than retrieving information.

Marx and Gilon propose that the emotional content of memories may be linked to their chemical encoding. In their model, signaling molecules, including neurotransmitters and gliotransmitters, help connect stored information with the feelings associated with an experience.

The researchers also suggest that retrieving and interpreting these chemically stored memories may contribute to the emergence of thought. In this view, memory is not simply a record of past events but an active process through which the brain assigns meaning to experience.

Computers and brains both store and process information, but the underlying mechanisms differ. While computers rely on electronic hardware, biological memory involves living cells and complex chemical interactions.

Marx and Gilon argue that this distinction could matter for consciousness. AI systems can perform increasingly sophisticated intellectual tasks, but their ability to process information does not establish that they experience thoughts or emotions.

For example, an AI system can identify a familiar song and describe the memories or feelings it might evoke in a person. Whether such a system could actually experience nostalgia is a different question.

The researchers suggest that if consciousness depends partly on the biochemical processes involved in human memory, greater computational power alone may not be sufficient to reproduce it.

The proposed model offers a possible connection between memory, emotion, and thought, but it does not establish how physical processes in the brain produce conscious experience.

The authors acknowledge that consciousness remains poorly understood and do not claim to have demonstrated that AI cannot become conscious.

Instead, their work suggests that investigating the chemical mechanisms of memory could help explain how stored experiences acquire emotional meaning and contribute to thought. Whether those processes are essential for consciousness remains an open question.

Reference: “Consciousness, Memory and Intelligence: A Mechanistic Perspective” by Gerard Marx and Chaim Gilon, 4 August 2026, International Journal of Psychiatry Research. DOI: 10.33425/2641-4317.1251

How “American Gothic” Took On a Life of Its Own

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

After decades of collecting pop culture riffs on the iconic 1930 painting, Wanda M. Corn traces the work’s canonization and the life of its maker Grant Wood, whose story remains largely overlooked.

You already know the image by heart: Two stern figures stand before an old wooden house. To the left, a blonde, blue-eyed woman wears an apron and a somber expression. Beside her, an unsmiling man in glasses and overalls grips a pitchfork. Since it was first exhibited at the Art Institute of Chicago in 1930, Grant Wood’s painting has joined the ranks of art historical touchstones and shown up in countless ads, books, and memes. But what made this unassuming image so popular, and why does it continue to captivate us today?

In The Couple with the Pitchfork: American Gothic from Easel to Icon (2026), art historian and curator Wanda M. Corn provides some compelling answers. The author began writing about Wood’s painting in 1981, and she has spent decades amassing an archive of “American Gothic”-related imagery that tracks the farm couple across General Mills cereal ads, Gordon Parks’s 1942 portrait of African-American janitor Ella Watson during his fellowship with the Farm Security Administration, magazine covers, COVID-era cartoons, and other surprising riffs. Her book traces the painting’s history from the controversy behind its creation and the bitter fallout it brought to its hapless maker through to its transformation into a pop culture cornerstone and a symbol of Wood’s home state.

Basque Cinema Expands Its (Rapidly) Growing Footprint in Tallinn

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

With Basque filmmakers in the Tallinn Spotlight section and buzzy competition title 'Caballé,' the never-healthier regional production hub is reaching territories not yet conquered: "It opens different doors."

This year, Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF) is letting Basque cinema bask — pun intended — in the spotlight.

Through dedicated investment over the last 10 years, the Basque film and TV output has never been stronger. The region has attracted productions from all over the world thanks to an alluring 60 percent tax rebate — increasing to 70 percent for projects shot in the Basque language, Euskara — allowing the area to lay claim to Catherine Zeta-Jones’ Kill Jackie and Nanni Moretti’s It Will Happen Tonight, among a slew of Spanish projects that catapulted the region to a record 45 Goya nominations earlier this year. Related Stories TV 'Slow Horses' Actress Ruth Bradley to Be Honored at the Oscar Wilde Awards (Exclusive) Movies 'In Starland': How Ray Panthaki, in His Directing Debut, Cast Clarence Maclin in His First Lead Role and Made a Very Personal Film Built on Meritocracy

This week, Tallinn’s Spotlight section represents the booming Basque audiovisual in territory not yet conquered. “It opens different doors,” begins Laura Lizuma, a Latvian producer based in San Sebastian who comes to the festival as part of the Basque delegation this year. “If the Basque country was in the spotlight in Argentina and Spain, that would be understandable — but the Basque industry is very small and when we think about global cinema… It’s a great place to discover new possibilities of co-production because [when else] would a Basque producer come to Estonia or the Baltic states?”

Lizuma arrives in the Estonian capital as a producer on The River Woman, one of three Basque projects selected as part of the Tallinn Spotlight to receive development support. The feature film, directed by Tamara García Iglesias and co-written with Jon Bilbao, is described by Lizuma as “a rather unconventional western — one that takes the familiar codes of the genre and turns them around to tell a story from a woman’s perspective.” García’s film is one of many Basque films hitting this Tallinn edition, joined by the likes of Daniel Romero Bueno’s debut feature The Daughters (Las Hijas) in the Midnight Shivers section and Joana Moya Blanco’s La Koreana from the festival’s industry program.

But the buzziest title of all competes in Tallinn’s official competition selection. Caballé, from Venezuelan filmmaker Patricia Ortega, is a biographical portrait of Spanish opera diva Montserrat Caballé that shot mostly in Bilbao. The movie is a Spain-led co-production between Set Màgic Audiovisual, La Claqueta, Basque company Amania Films, La Charito and Belgium’s Menuetto Film, with Film Factory Entertainment handling international sales.

“I was interested in her contradictions,” Ortega begins to The Hollywood Reporter about what drew her to the famed opera singer, depicted in the film by Begoña Alberdi. “Her strength and vulnerability, her doubts and insecurities, but also her determination to make her way in a world dominated by men.”

Ortega describes the Basque contribution as “an essential part of bringing Caballé to the screen.” This doesn’t just refer to the production side of things, but also to the talent, commitment and “generosity” of those who worked on the film. “Montserrat Caballé was not only a legend, she was also a deeply multicultural woman,” explained the director. “She spoke several languages and worked with people from all over the world. In a way, that is something we experienced while making the film in the Basque Country: people from different places and backgrounds coming together around a shared creative vision. I think she would have appreciated that spirit of collaboration.”

She vouches for the work of the Basque crews and support from the region’s government, saying that Bilbao’s logistical strengths made the filming process “much easier.” Ortega added: “There was also the experience of living in the city for several weeks. We found a place that welcomed us with open arms, with its remarkable variety of landscapes, the generosity and warmth of its people, and its rich and diverse cultural life.”

Those virtues are on display and then some in Tallinn, where international producers are able to get a sense of the strength of the Basque audiovisual industry and production capabilities. That’s according to Esther Cabero Gonzalo, the shorts programmer at Zineuskadi, a company that works strategically to promote the Basque audiovisual sector, partnering with every production output in the region and working with the local government to help facilitate co-productions, distribution and sales deals, as well as securing films a coveted slot at major film festivals.

“Having a delegation in Tallinn creates opportunities to meet people, establish direct contacts and develop potential collaborations,” says Cabero, who adds that a representative for the Etxepare Basque Institute, the organization leading the promotion of Basque culture internationally, will be talking in Tallinn about the tempting tax incentives. “I think all these elements help demonstrate just how much can be achieved in such a small territory,” says Cabero.

The Basque shorts program, Kimuak, had one film — Raúl Barreras’s Meanwhile, Then — premiere in the PÖFF Shorts section over the weekend. Barrera’s feature follows five researchers from the future who arrive in a valley in the Basque Country to record gestures and emotions that have disappeared over time. Through interviews and observations of daily life, they delve into the private lives of the inhabitants — but soon the framework underpinning their research begins to crumble.

Six shorts from previous editions are set to screen through the rest of the festival as part of the Basque Spotlight. Cabero is full of pride showcasing such a broad range of work from male and female filmmakers, as well as documentaries, animations and more, to the crowds in Tallinn. “We can promote our culture, our language, our identity, and make connections with international audiences,” she says. “But it’s also a way to promote those names, the directors, so they can find opportunities abroad and co-productions.”

For Cabero, it isn’t just the draw of Basque talent, production companies, studios and technical facilities that has helped make the Spanish region a thriving film and TV hub, but the historic towns, industrial environments, as well as forests and dramatic natural scenery.

Interestingly enough, it’s also Lizuma’s appreciation for the Basque Country’s sweeping landscapes that will help The River Woman achieve high-scale cinema at a lower cost. “With this project, in particular, the first idea is to shoot in the Basque Country because of the nature,” says Lizuma. “We are planning to shoot between the Basque Country and Navarre, but this project is developed from the beginning to be an international co-production. So we are looking for other territories that we could film [in], […] and we really have the intention to get those woods or those rivers here in [the] Basque Country or the Baltic states,” she says. Basque cinema, she adds, often capitalizes on its “pretty strong” relationship with nature.

Lizuma has lived in Spain for 10 years now and can measure in great depth the success of the concentrated backing of the Basque film and TV sector. “There are a lot of projects coming [to the] Basque Country […] because they can get this tax incentive. So there are a lot of projects that come there with this interest, but also for Basque directors and producers, there are a lot more projects that are not so small anymore. When I came, there were a lot of self-discoveries — very small, very local. Now there are projects that raises three million [euros] in funding. That was impossible before.”

And while Ortega’s award hopeful filmed mostly in one of the region’s busiest and most vibrant cities, what strikes her about Basque cinema is its ability to simultaneously straddle a particular place and culture while speaking to audiences far beyond it. “There isn’t just one Basque cinematic voice,” she says, “there are many filmmakers with distinct sensibilities, and that diversity is one of its great strengths.”

She touts the work of David Pérez Sañudo, who recently presented the brilliant Sacamantecas, Alauda Ruiz de Azúa with the five-time Goya Award-winning Los domingos, and Estibaliz Urresola’s 20,000 Species of Bees, shortlisted for best international feature at the 2024 Oscars. “They are examples of the quality of the work coming out of the Basque Country,” says Ortega.

“I would also like to mention Nerea Torrijos, who was the head of costume design on Caballé. She is a wonderful example of how Basque talent extends across every area of filmmaking,” Ortega adds. This particularly personal testament is indicative of the kind of relationships the Basque audiovisual industry has so far managed to cultivate.
