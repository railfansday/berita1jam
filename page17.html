‘Avalancha’ at 30: The LP That Recharged Cafe Tacvba After the Burnout of ‘Re’

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Thirty years ago, the members of Café Tacvba found themselves physically and emotionally wiped out. The Mexico City quartet had completed an extensive tour promoting its sophomore effort, Re, which since then has been routinely hailed as one of the best albums in the history of Latin rock.

To make matters worse, Tacvba’s label expected a new record, and the demos that the band had been taping between tours were far from ready to become a cohesive whole. Things got a bit tense.

“We played the Viña del Mar festival in Chile, and our longtime producer Gustavo Santaolalla happened to be there,” remembers Tacvba keyboardist Meme del Real. “He saw what was happening to us. ‘You’re always playing covers of songs by other artists,’ he said. ‘Why don’t you make an EP of covers? That would get you back in the studio and working on a different frequency.’ He had the right perspective, and pointed us to a path of healing.”

The result was Avalancha de Éxitos, a mini-album of radically reimagined songs that, experienced as a whole, sounds significantly more epic than the sum of its parts. Released on Nov. 5, 1996, it was not only commercially successful — five of its eight songs were released as singles — but it also made Tacvba stars in countries like Argentina, which had never embraced Mexican rock before.

A brisk, exuberant record, it is also subtly conceptual, depicting Latin American culture as a chaotic, dissonant tapestry where beauty and depth can be found in a musty bolero from decades ago (“No Me Comprendes”), a melancholy pop ballad (Leo Dan’s wide-eyed “Cómo Te Extraño Mi Amor”), or a tropical anthem of hope transformed into son huasteco (Juan Luis Guerra’s socially alert “Ojalá Que Llueva Café.”)

Tacvba’s sense of humor, playful and subversive, injected an adrenaline rush into provocative Mexican rock songs like opening stomper “Chilanga Banda,” with its indolent groove, and the industrial corrosion of “Alármala De Tos,” originally by Eighties troublemakers Botellita de Jerez. David Byrne guests on “No Controles,” an aggro inversion of the 1985 techno-pop hit by Mexican female group Flans (itself, a cover of Spanish band Olé Olé). “I remember that record with so much affection,” says singer Rubén Albarrán from his home in Mexico City. “We were so inspired by the process of stepping out into the streets of the cities that we visited and discovering the spirit of Latin America. We were coming face to face with the realities that have become more obvious since then: the intervention of foreign countries; the genocide of our people. Our resistance was formulated by selecting ingredients from the blend of cultures and histories, and coming up with something new. We were deeply moved by discovering who we really were.”

The same irreverence that Tacvba had exhibited on original songs like “La Ingrata” seeped into the Avalancha sessions.

“We had the impudence not to give a damn about what the composers of the original songs may think,” laughs Albarrán, who fittingly chose the moniker of Anónimo for the project. “Perhaps they would even be offended.”

“It’s so much easier to quote and unquote destroy a song if the author is not present,” adds del Real. “That was a big part of the fun — the element of adventure involved in every single arrangement. And it wasn’t like the songs were required to suffer a profound transformation. It was more like, ‘What would happen if we grab these songs and push them to the limit? “I think the fact they had created a work as imposing and sonically diverse as Re left them in a complicated position in terms of the follow-up album,” says Enrique Blanc, a Mexico-based music journalist and author of the book Café Tacvba — Bailando por nuestra cuenta. “Making Avalancha was like going into recess for them; a fun project echoing their beginnings as a band that played covers.”

Originally, the group planned to record Duran Duran’s debut single “Planet Earth” (according to del Real, a demo still languishes in the vaults.) But Albarrán felt awkward singing the English lyrics, and the song selection remained Latin American throughout — a powerful statement in itself.

“We have a different cultural context than Pink Floyd or Radiohead,” explains Albarrán. “We need to talk about other things that are more important to us and the people we share our lives with. Something happened with Avalancha, where the pristine sound quality achieved by Santaolalla and [co-producer] Aníbal Kerpel merged with our conscious decision not to copy anything from the Anglo mainstream. We have our own emotional world. It’s just as valuable as anyone else’s.”

Interestingly, one of the album’s most haunting moments arrives during an obscure track: “No Me Comprendes,” a cabaret miniature by Cuban eccentric Bola de Nieve. Like most of Avalancha, it conjures up a stately, fetishistic atmosphere by combining psychedelic guitar with hand percussion and a musical saw. Toward the end, the tropical bolero suddenly morphs into a lilting bossa nova segment — almost as if the record itself chose to embrace the power of beauty over chaos and satire.

“That’s one of my favorite Tacvba songs,” says Albarrán. “It’s such a gorgeous moment when that bossa bit blooms — it nourishes my heart and soul. The mocking undertone in my voice mirrors Bola de Nieve’s original interpretation. He was such a strange and uncomfortable character, and the song itself is difficult to pin down. The saw and the bossa segment — it’s like the sound of fairies, or the song of sirens.”

“I remember putting on the headphones and recording my part on a grand piano,” adds del Real. He is in a hotel room in Querétaro, a few hours before stepping onstage for a concert presenting La Montaña Encendida, his debut solo album. “It felt like dining at an expensive restaurant in Paris — the kind of establishment you only get to visit once or twice in your life. I was having such a wonderful time playing, and I think my enjoyment can be heard on the final product.”

It was the album’s closing track, “Cómo Te Extraño Mi Amor,” that became its biggest hit — a quintessential Tacvba moment. Whereas the Leo Dan original is fragile and wounded, Tacvba’s bouncy cover bursts out of the speakers with the shuffle of a ska beat and youthful Beatlesque confidence.

“That’s Gustavo’s [Santaolalla] magic,” says Albarrán. “He captured that fleeting impression, how happy and young we were. We were experiencing success doing what we loved. It was just thrilling, and on this song you can hear the lightness in our step. It wasn’t only the music that we tackled with that kind of energy: so much laughter, and attitude, and desmadre. It was life itself.”

Avalancha’s brilliance was acknowledged by both critics and the band’s fans the moment it was released. It was the record that everyone talked about as 1996 came to a close, and it generated another massive tour. Eventually, Tacvba returned to the demos of original material that had been pushed aside, and in 1999 released the stunning double album Revés/Yosoy.

Still, Avalancha may well be the finest Latin covers album ever made. “I’m so proud of it,” says del Real. “Avalancha helped us realize how vulnerable we could be as a band. It defined our identity while helping us to recover from a few maladies we had picked up along their way. Once again, music and the creative process gave us the chance to rediscover why we remained together. Our group psychotherapy was commanded by a sense of adventure.”

“It wasn’t just us,” Albarrán emphasizes. “We were part of a movement that was taking over Mexico and many other countries. There was Maldita Vecindad and Santa Sabina. We were all reformulating our roots with different results. Music was our medicine and refuge; the one place where we could find an answer for everything.”

Matthew Lillard’s Shaggy Return In New Scooby-Doo Series Is 2027’s Most Anticipated Comeback

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Matthew Lillard is officially returning as Shaggy in a new Scooby-Doo series arriving in 2027, marking what may be the most anticipated actor comeback of the year, if not the decade. For 2000s kids, this is nothing short of a dream come true, while also giving a whole new generation the chance to discover why Lillard's portrayal of the iconic character is so beloved.

As a 2000s kid, I grew up with the live-action Scooby-Doo (2002) and Scooby-Doo 2: Monsters Unleashed (2004) movies. In fact, the VHS tapes for these two movies were easily among the most loved and frequently watched in my collection, seeing hundreds, if not thousands, of playbacks over the years.

As a result, Matthew Lillard became the face and voice of "my Shaggy," just as he did for hundreds of thousands of kids worldwide. To this day, it's truly impossible for me to imagine another actor in the role who could even come close.

Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles' latest series has already delivered one of the coolest redesigns of a classic villain.

Now, 22 years after Monsters Unleashed, Lillard is reprising this legendary role, albeit with a twist. Rather than returning for another live-action project, Lillard will voice Shaggy Rogers in the upcoming series Yokoso, Scooby-Doo!, marking the franchise's first original anime series. And while this isn't quite the R-rated live-action return some fans may have hoped for, I couldn't be more excited.

Yokoso, Scooby-Doo! will be a Japan-set, anime-inspired mystery adventure that pairs Scooby-Doo and Shaggy with several new characters. Notably, in addition to Lillard returning as the voice of Shaggy, longtime Scooby-Doo voice actor Frank Welker will also reprise his role as Scooby-Doo. While the franchise has seen countless animated series reboots over the years, fans can expect Yokoso, Scooby-Doo! to offer something wholly unique.

In addition to marking the franchise's first venture into anime, the series will see classic Scooby-Doo shenanigans collide with Japanese folklore, delivering a completely new setting and adventure unlike anything fans are used to. Produced by Japanese animation studio OLM and distributed by Tubi, Yokoso, Scooby-Doo! has yet to confirm an exact release date but is expected to arrive sometime in 2027, meaning Lillard's return is just around the corner.

While Lillard's Shaggy and Welker's Scooby-Doo will understandably be the stars of the show, they'll also be joined by several new characters, including Yume, a girl with magical powers; Takumi, a gadget whiz; and Scooby-Doo's uncle, Daisuke-Doo (We can only hope that Daisuke-Doo is less nefarious than Scooby's nephew, Scrappy-Doo).

Die-hard Scooby-Doo fans already know what to expect from Lillard's voice acting, as he took over as Shaggy's official animated voice around 2010, after original voice actor Casey Kasem retired from the role. Since then, Lillard has voiced Shaggy in numerous animated films, TV series, and video games. However, Yokoso, Scooby-Doo! will mark his first time venturing into anime as Shaggy Rogers, making this latest return particularly notable.

The kids who grew up watching Matthew Lillard's Scooby-Doo movies are now all grown up, and it feels like the perfect time for the actor and his co-stars, Freddie Prinze Jr., Sarah Michelle Gellar, and Linda Cardellini, to reprise their live-action roles. An R-rated movie bringing back the Mystery Inc. gang for a more mature, all-grown-up adventure could be exactly what longtime fans have been waiting for.

Matthew Lillard is already a horror icon thanks to his roles in some of the most legendary horror franchises of all time, including William Afton in Five Nights at Freddy's and Stu Macher in Scream (1996), one of the original Ghostface killers alongside Billy Loomis. Fans of both franchises would undoubtedly go wild if news broke that Lillard's horror and Scooby-Doo legacies were set to collide, especially if his fellow co-stars returned alongside him.

Major Pokémon Center TCG Orders Delayed At The Last Minute

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Pokémon TCG fans who pre-ordered Delta Reign ETBs still haven't been charged

Pokémon Trading Card Game fans have spent the last couple of days feeling anxious. Delta Reign, the next big TCG set, releases on Friday — and The Pokémon Center hasn't updated anyone about their orders. Typically, the official Pokémon storefront charges for pre-orders a few days before release; shipment updates tend to come the day before launch. It turns out the lack of communication reflects an actual, active issue with orders.

There are three major Delta Reign products set to release on Friday, Nov. 6: the Elite Trainer Box, Booster Bundles, and Booster Boxes. Pokémon Center ETBs, which come with extra packs and promo cards, are particularly prized by fans and resellers.

Pokémon customers from the U.K. recently received a notice from TPC informing them about delays for all three items. The company now says that its Delta Reign products will ship sometime in mid-November, but has not share any specifics beyond that.

Customers in the United States, however, have not been informed of issues with orders yet. Given the lack of charges and shipping updates, it seems likely that it is only a matter of time before TPC breaks the news to everyone. As of this writing, TPC's website is undergoing extensive maintenance.

According to Pokémon fans sharing their communications with TPC customer support, the delays are due to an internal mix-up. The storefront apparently told a number of customers that they had to reauthorize payments despite, in some cases, not actually needing additional information. This may also explain why the TPC website's checkout function hasn't worked for nearly a week.

Though TPC's shipments are delayed, Delta Reign's launch (and the 30th Anniversary's Ultra Premium Collection) is still proceeding full steam ahead at major retailers. One of the exceptions might be local card shops which are still waiting for stock to arrive — but such delays are common.

LIV Golf is Shutting Down: What to Know About Its End

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

LIV Golf announced it is shutting down after months of speculation about the future of the Saudi Arabia-backed PGA Tour competitor. Jon Rahm and Bryson DeChambeau were among the league's top players.

The professional men’s golf tour, founded in 2021 as a competitor to the PGA Tour, announced it is officially closing up shop after months of speculation over its draining finances.

According to a Nov. 5 announcement on the league’s website, in April CEO Scott O’Neil and other LIV Golf leadership began working with independent advisors to “identify the best, value-maximizing path forward for the Company and its stakeholders.”

However, “in light of the current circumstances,” the announcement noted, "we believe shifting toward an orderly wind-down is the most responsible path forward for the League at this time. The dedication of LIV Golf’s players, employees, partners, and fans made everything the League accomplished possible.”

In his own statement, O’Neil reflected on building the league up from the rough.

"LIV Golf was fun, demanding, inspiring, at times challenging—but always rewarding,” he said. "We created memories that I hope will inspire a new generation of fans to follow, play, and fall in love with this great game.”

And though the league is headed towards a wind down, the announcement did note the company will remain open to and consider any “value-maximum alternatives” that might arise.

After its launch, LIV instantly caught the attention of the golf world by poaching players like Jon Rahm, Bryson DeChambeau, Brooks Koepka and Phil Mickelson from the PGA Tour.

And while the future of the tour—which is named after the Roman numerals for 54 as a nod to its 54-hole tournaments—had remained up in the air for quite some time, O’Neil had assured staff in April that the company was on, or rather in, the green.

“I want to be crystal clear: Our season continues exactly as planned, uninterrupted and at full throttle," O’Neil wrote in the email, obtained by multiple outlets. "While the media landscape is often filled with speculation, our reality is defined by the work we do on the grass.”

Indeed, the CEO—who took over as head of the company in 2025 from Greg Norman—emphasized his confidence that the company would remain in a good spot through the end of 2026.

“We are heading into the heart of our 2026 schedule,” he continued, “with the full energy of an organization that is bigger, louder, and more influential than ever before.”

Considering the company was only five years old, O’Neil encouraged his employees to think of the growing unease toward the company as regular “startup pressure.”

“We signed up for this because we believe in disrupting the status quo,” he continued. “We have faced headwinds since the jump, and we've answered every time with resilience and grace. Now, we answer by doing what we do best: putting on the most compelling show in sports.”

Nevertheless, LIV’s recent scorecard read differently.

Last year, Eugenio Chacarra, who started his professional golf career with LIV in 2022 before exiting in 2025, criticized the tour for the way it treated him as a player.

“I see what it’s like to win on the PGA Tour and how your life changes, how you get major access and ranking points,” Chacarra said in an interview with Flushing It Golf. “On LIV, nothing changes; there is only money.”

The fate of the golfers currently under the LIV tour remains unclear, but Koepka previously made the jump from LIV to the PGA Tour earlier this year.

Meanwhile, PGA Tour golfer Max Homa told Golfweek in April it would be “ridiculous” to not allow LIV players into his own tour. As he put it, “Everyone deserves a place to play golf.”

Christian Horner Doesn’t Sound Like He’s Done With Formula 1 Yet

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

“I feel like I’m too young to retire,” he says.

“How would you introduce yourself to somebody that doesn’t know you?” I ask the man across the table.

“Christian Horner,” he says, then without pause, “somebody that used to be involved in Formula 1.”

Considering this is a man who could have truthfully responded “Commander of the Order of the British Empire” or “husband of Ginger Spice,” the choice to define himself by his past rather than present or future is hard to miss. His is a familiar name and a familiar face to even casual F1 fans. The Brit’s sharp tongue and vaguely Daniel Craig-ish visage made him a lead character of Netflix’s Drive to Survive in its first years when his Red Bull Formula 1 team was banking win after win, thanks also to the talents of people like Max Verstappen and Adrian Newey.

But no hot streak lasts forever, no matter how good you (or the people you work with) are. Horner was ejected from the team after the 2025 British Grand Prix in the wake of both a sexual harassment accusation scandal the year before and a general downturn in the team’s performance, reportedly receiving a €60 million platinum parachute in exchange for his departure from the sport.

For most, that might seem a sign from the universe to take up a hobby—say, gentleman farmer. But Horner isn’t most men. He’s still out there, orbiting F1 like a geosynchronous satellite: a long ways off but still locked above it, looking down at the whole of the world he left. As such, he’s been speaking at industry events like Sportico Invest London and the Autosport Business Exchange in New York, where I sat down with him before he went on stage.

Unsurprisingly to anyone who saw him on Drive to Survive, or at any of the 10,000 media events he did during his F1 career, Horner still has plenty to say. Even if he is trying to be polite about it.

“The racing has been entertaining, don’t get me wrong. I’m watching these races and seeing cars overtaking in Melbourne and other circuits that you don’t normally overtake at,” he says when discussing the current F1 season. “But they’re not really overtakes as opposed to ... drive-pasts.”

He is not, it seems, a particular fan of the 2026 power unit regulations that have caused consternation amongst teams and fans alike by forcing the gas-electric hybrid propulsion systems to split their output 50/50 between internal combustion-sourced and electron-derived power. Horner describes it as the biggest issue for F1 to tackle at the moment. “You would assume it would be a relatively easy fix, just to change that ratio, but it seems a little more complicated,” he says. “You hear the drivers not liking how these cars are to drive, because you’re not rewarded for driving quickly through a fast corner, for example.”

“I think they’re doing the right thing for 2030,” he adds, referring to FIA president Mohammed Ben ‌Sulayem’s plans to ditch the current hybrid V-6 powertrains for V-8 units. “I think it’s unfortunate for the current presidency because he inherited this set of regulations. So of course, he’s looking to address that for the next round of significant change to engine spec.”

The 2026 season’s power unit frustrations haven’t been all bad, at least in terms of entertainment value. They’ve played a role in keeping the constructors’ championship battle interesting, even if the driver’s championship was swept away by wunderkind Kimi Antonelli.

“It’s interesting, I think different teams have had different moments this year,” he says. “Mercedes started very strong; Ferrari then had a moment where they looked like they were gonna put a challenge together around Barcelona time—seeing Lewis [Hamilton] win his first race, Charles [Leclerc] winning at Silverstone. And McLaren started to get their act together, looked pretty racy around Zanvoort and Budapest time, and then obviously, the Red Bull cars have come on strong.”

But those cars at front of the field are practically in a different league from the other seven teams in this season, which Horner—despite his own successes captaining Red Bull teams that ran the table with Brady-Belichick efficiency—says isn’t ideal for the sport. “I think you want as many cars in the fight as you can get, and the best way to achieve that is stability. Because then they all [close on] the same solutions, and just iterate those same solutions. So if you want close racing, just leave those regulations, those chassis regulations, alone. And you’ll see them all converge onto one solution—the better solution.”

In other words, simplify, then add budget. Formula 1, as he points out, is both one of the most expensive sports in the world and the biggest team sport on the planet. “You’ve got over 2000 team members between engine and chassis, all producing two cars for twenty-four races a year.” Running all as team principal, unsurprisingly, is like spinning plates and juggling bowling pins at the same time, even before factoring in the spotlight.

“Challenges ... there are many. There’s the scrutiny you come under. The politics, the competition of people trying to nick your staff, your drivers, your sponsors. It’s such a competitive industry. There’s so much at stake with large amounts of money involved” he says. “It’s dog-eat-dog in that respect. You gotta get up pretty early in the morning to stay ahead of the rest.”

Having done so for so many years—and most of those seeing an above-average amount of success for the position—certainly suggests the 52-year-old Brit has what Liam Neeson might describe as a particular set of skills. Would you, I ask him, eventually like to circle back to Formula 1?

“I think ...” Here, he pauses. “You know, I had an incredible run. I’m very proud of what I did. It didn’t end the way in the way I would have liked. I’m still relatively young ... I would only go back—knowing the sacrifices it takes—for the right project. Only if I was in a position where I could influence change, drive change within the organization, with like-minded people that wanted to win ... I’ve won close to one-third of the races I entered. I don’t want to go back just to take part.”

But he’s not pigeonholing himself. “I have time on my side to be able to make decisions. I feel like I’m too young to retire,” he says. “Honestly, sitting right here, right now, I have no idea where the next twelve months are going to take me. And I find that kind of exciting.”

Is MAHA dying—or will it be reborn? What's next after crushing midterm losses?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

RFK Jr.’s Make America Healthy Again movement was in trouble before voting began.

As Democrats blew away their Republican rivals in an unprecedented midterm sweep Tuesday, the Make America Health Again movement also got bowled over, raising questions about the fate of the motley movement.

MAHA—which has centered around anti-vaccine activist and current Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.—saw many of its favored candidates and allies lose, while vocal adversaries of Kennedy won. And the coalition was splintering under stress even before the voting started; in recent months, supporters have accused Kennedy and the Trump administration of inaction and betrayals in their two years in power.

Now hobbled in Washington, shedding supporters, and showing little to no influence in voting booths, is MAHA on its deathbed? Or will it be reborn—possibly into a new group? Could it be assimilated into a blue platform—after sloughing off the more repulsive, conspiratorial aspects of Kennedy’s ideology? These are the questions circling in the aftermath of Tuesday’s bloodbath.

In a social media poll Wednesday afternoon, Mary Talley Bowden—a dejected MAHA activist, anti-vaccine advocate, and doctor reprimanded for prescribing ivermectin to treat COVID-19—polled her followers about what should come next. She offered three options: Start a third party, join the Democrats, or overthrow the GOP. As of publication time, joining Democrats trailed at just 3 percent of the votes. The remaining votes were roughly split between overthrowing the GOP—whatever that means—and forming a third party, which had a small lead at 49 percent.

That MAHA could fade out entirely or fracture into negligible shards seems like a very real possibility. Its collection of disperate groups and varied agenda items has always made its cohesiveness seem tenuous. A Politico poll in June revealed that people who identified as MAHA had different ideas of what the movement’s priorities are (or should be), as well as different political leanings, and sometimes opposing ideologies.

Some within the movement embrace (or at least accept) Kennedy’s fringe beliefs, such as his zealous anti-vaccine views, his rejection of germ theory, and his false claim that 5G is damaging our brains and causing cancer. Some also believe, like Kennedy, in chemtrails and want all mRNA vaccines banned. There are members who support his plan to ban water fluoridation and also seem fine with his false claim that Lyme disease was created by the US military as a military weapon.

Others, meanwhile, have been attracted to MAHA by the more reasonable, non-absurd issues Kennedy touts. Those include improving the quality of Americans’ food and diets, reducing their exposure to environmental contaminants, and limiting the influence of industry and corporate interests on Americans’ health, particularly limiting the sway of pharmaceutical and chemical companies.

With various priorities, Kennedy and MAHA leaders have spent the past two years finding ways to disappoint their members. Some like Bowdon are angered that Kennedy has yet to ban mRNA vaccines. In February, Kennedy sparked particularly intense backlash among supporters for embracing Trump’s decision to increase domestic production of the pesticide glyphosate—a pesticide many in the MAHA movement wanted banned. Kennedy further fueled feelings of betrayal in September by appearing at a MAHA-branded summit that had hefty corporate sponsorships, including from behemoths such as Walmart, Anthropic, OpenAI, Grail, and UnitedHealth.

“MAHA was supposed to expose the revolving door between money, industry and government, not build its own,” Robert Malone, a fringe scientist and previous Kennedy ally, wrote in a scathing response to the summit.

Going into the midterms on Tuesday, the number of disenchanted MAHA members had mounted. In October, Tiffany Cianci, a former Kennedy supporter, published a MAHA scorecard, which lists 42 promises that Kennedy and leaders made to the MAHA members and then largely broke. The scorecard reports 37 promises broken, five partially fulfilled, and zero kept. One of the “partially kept” promises was removing synthetic dyes from foods, particularly children’s foods. Kennedy announced an effort to do that—with much ballyhoo—but later admitted that the FDA did not ban or even have an agreement with food companies to remove the dyes. Instead, Kennedy said he has an “understanding” with food companies. The dyes remain in foods.

In a press release, Cianci wrote that the scorecard revealed “an abysmal string of broken commitments and betrayals.” It also contained a direct quote from her, saying: “I believed deeply in the promise that MAHA represented, particularly the possibility of confronting chronic disease and building a healthier future for America’s children. But belief was never supposed to mean blind loyalty.”

The press release included quotes from several other prominent MAHA leaders, all expressing disappointment with Kennedy and the Trump administration. Later in October, Calley Means, a top ally and senior advisor to Kennedy, resigned from his position, reportedly “disillusioned” and frustrated by delays in food policy changes sought by MAHA.

Another prominent figure to fall out with Kennedy was Vani Hari, an online influencer who goes by “food babe” and has previously identified as a “MAHA Mom.” As voting results were streaming in Tuesday night, Hari posted a comment on social media that she had given Politico months earlier, saying: “Women don’t come out to vote for glyphosate and war,” referring to the pesticide Kennedy had embraced and the Iran war.

In the wake of the blue sweep, it’s clear that more than half of the 24 MAHA-endorsed candidates lost their races Tuesday. That includes Senate candidate Ken Paxton in Texas and Zach Lahn, who ran for governor of Iowa. Lahn in particular was considered a “dream MAHA candidate,” and his campaign was seen as a test of MAHA’s political influence. Kennedy himself headlined a political rally for Lahn last week, calling it “the most important election going on in this country.” Lahn lost to his Democratic opponent, Rob Sand, who flipped the seat with an 11-point spread, turning the office blue for the first time in 20 years.

Alex Clark Voetberg, who hosts a wellness podcast called Culture Apothecary for Turning Point USA, echoed Hari, pointing to the glyphosate betrayal as a reason for the defeats. “MAHA moms stayed home and it could’ve swayed some of these races that were unbearably close. Should’ve listened to our warnings on protecting glyphosate—but you needed Bayer for the war,” Clark Voetberg wrote on social media.

Otherwise, MAHA also lost allies, such as Senator Roger Marshall (R-Kan.), who founded a MAHA caucus in the Senate. Rep. Zach Nunn (R-Iowa) lost his seat in the House despite reports that Kennedy allegedly intervened in the race to help him win. Meanwhile, critics of Kennedy notched wins, including Representative-elect Richard Pan, a physician from California; Senator-elect Annie Andrews, a pediatrician from South Carolina; and Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed, an epidemiologist with a medical degree from Michigan.

Many congressional Democrats are already raring to hold Kennedy to account and remove him—and several of the newcomers are eager to help make it happen. In August, Andrews laid out her plans for health priorities to South Carolina Democrats, saying, “I believe we need to fully fund the CDC and the NIH, and I believe we need to impeach and remove RFK Jr.”

El-Sayed has also sharply rebuked Kennedy and said he looks forward to holding him “accountable for all of the grift.” After Kennedy made egregiously bigoted remarks against Arabs and falsely claimed they don’t contribute to science and literature, El-Sayed called out Kennedy’s lack of qualifications to be US health secretary. “He is an expert in people who have contributed nothing to science,” El-Sayed said. (Kennedy has no background in science, medicine, or public health. He also has a loathsome record of making bigoted and racist comments.)

On Wednesday afternoon, Sen. Angela Alsobrooks (D-Md.) wrote on social media: “The time is now. RFK Jr. must resign or be fired. End of discussion.”

If Kennedy is removed from office, the outlook for MAHA in its current form seems even murkier. On the one hand, Kennedy’s removal may make it easier for those in the movement to shed the divisive, abhorrent aspects of his ideology and focus on broadly popular issues—healthy foods, environmental contaminants, and industry influence. Such a move may help MAHA cement itself as a sustainable political fixture. But some of the most vocal figures in the MAHA realm are doggedly loyal to Kennedy, throwing such a transition into doubt.

Zen Honeycutt, an anti-vaccine activist and founder of Moms Across America, is one of those Kennedy loyalists. Honeycutt drew attention before the election for reaching out to Democrats on common causes. Her group endorsed several blue candidates, including Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez of New York, Sen. Cory Booker of New Jersey, and Rep. Chris Pappas in New Hampshire (all of them won their races).

“We expect Democrats and Republicans to work together,” Honeycutt told Politico earlier this month. “Health is not a political issue. You know, poison is not partisan. Cancer is not choosy.”

The sensible comment might suggest Honeycutt doesn’t care which party or politician follows through with MAHA’s goals, just as long as they’re achieved. But this is not actually Honeycutt’s position. Her intentions were made clear in exchanges on social media with Bowden, who harshly criticized Honeycutt for crossing party lines, particularly for talking with Ocasio-Cortez. “You understand she’s trying to use you?” Bowden wrote.

Honeycutt explained that she was working to smooth Kennedy’s path in Washington, not hand power to Democrats, who she said had been “fascist in mandating vaccines.” She instead turned the criticism to Bowden, saying the MAHA infighting and mudslinging was what was hurting the cause and handing Democrats wins.

On Wednesday, Honeycutt reaffirmed her allegiance to Kennedy, warning Democrats that impeaching him would be “extremely unwise.” She reposted a comment from one of her followers, writing: “I’m willing to give Dems a chance with their new power before I judge. I’m an independent. Kennedy is my red line. If they cross it I want them to get absolutely fucked in 2028.”

Still, there are some inklings that part of MAHA could be absorbed into blue platforms—whether the leaders of the movement in its current form like it or not.

Earlier this month, Brinda Adhikari, a journalist and co-host of the podcast Why Should I Trust You?, argued that MAHA wasn’t dying but remaking the Democratic Party to be more like its old self. In a Substack post titled How MAHA Is Making Democrats Democrats Again, Adhikari pointed to a health policy document from House Democrats with a section, led by Ocasio-Cortez, titled “Reclaiming MAHA and Protecting American Families.”

Adhikari wrote that some of the issues touted by MAHA are a natural fit for the Democratic Party. She stretched back to Rachel Carson’s seminal Silent Spring in 1962 and the progressive environmental activism that elevated issues of health, pesticides, toxic chemicals, and corporate influence over public health. “These were once bread-and-butter Democratic issues,” Adhikari wrote. “But somewhere along the way, Democrats lost some of their identity as the party leading these fights.”

Adhikari and Democrats credit Kennedy with redirecting attention to these issues, which have broad support among voters.

“Americans’ concerns about chronic disease and food safety, especially that which is linked to corporate influence over environmental regulation and public health, are legitimate and long overdue for a serious legislative response,” the House Democrat’s policy document reads. “The Make America Healthy Again (MAHA) movement has galvanized a significant segment of the Republican Party and attracted many previously apolitical and non-traditional voters, yet many of the concerns raised by the movement are bipartisan in nature.”

A KFF poll in May found that 75 percent of Americans want stricter regulations on food additives—a prominent MAHA priority—and 64 percent want stricter regulation of pesticides in agriculture, another key MAHA agenda item.

But Kennedy and the Trump administration have not only failed to deliver on these issues, but they have also spoiled the movement, using “the legitimate concerns of American consumers about food safety to spread misinformation about vaccines, public health, and science,” the policy document reads. “Their rhetoric has systematically led to the erosion of trust in public health, the science community, and decreased vaccination rates, leading to fatal outbreaks in some cases.”

Democrats intend to reclaim the popular health and environmental issues, free of Kennedy’s corrosive anti-science rhetoric. The document outlines plans to restore regulations that protect clean water and air damaged by the Trump administration, advance policies to keep toxic substances out of food, and further Food is Medicine policies, which provide produce prescriptions to American families to improve nutrition and health.

Adhikari predicts a political shift. “I increasingly think MAHA’s most unexpected legacy may be pushing Democrats back toward their own roots: challenging corporate power, fighting dangerous chemicals in our food supply, and holding toxic polluters accountable,” she wrote.

MAHA leaders tied to Kennedy and the anti-vaccine movement, like Bowden and Honeycutt, are unlikely to get behind such a shift. This could cause MAHA’s political power—however strong it actually is—to fracture. MAHA leaders have claimed repeatedly that their followers have the ability to sway elections. If MAHA hadn’t been betrayed or at least disappointed by Kennedy and Trump in the last two years, MAHA could have prevented Tuesday’s Republican wipeout, they have said.

But the polling data doesn’t support the idea that keeping MAHA in its current form maximizes its political power. While MAHA does have significant support among voters—41 percent of US adults say they support MAHA—that support is primarily tied to the health, environmental, and food safety issues, which are popular among non-MAHA supporters, too. Further, just 4 percent of MAHA supporters cited vaccines and medical choice—Kennedy’s key issues—as their reason for supporting the movement, the KFF poll revealed.

Beyond MAHA’s primary issues, both voters overall and MAHA supporters specifically said MAHA issues weren’t top of mind this election; instead, both groups named the cost of healthcare as a bigger issue than those prioritized by MAHA (the MAHA movement is not associated with the issue of health care reform). A separate KFF poll on election day found that 50 percent of voters thought that the Democratic Party was better able to handle healthcare, while only 30 percent of voters said the same of the Republican Party.

For now, it’s unclear exactly how—or if—MAHA will influence the political landscape going forward. It could break apart, live on through revamped Democratic policies, or try to form a new political party entirely. And, apparently, there’s also the possibility it will try to overthrow the GOP.
