Barbara Broccoli Appointed President Of UK’s National Film & Television School

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

EXCLUSIVE: Barbara Broccoli has been named the first female president of the UK’s National Film and Television School (NFTS).

The storied James Bond producer becomes President just a few weeks before the opening of a building dedicated to her father, the legendary Cubby Broccoli.

Barbara Broccoli will build on her longstanding relationship with the NFTS and commitment to championing emerging filmmaking talent.

She succeeds David Puttnam, another iconic British producer who takes on the honorary role of President Emeritus in recognition of his outstanding contribution to the NFTS and its continued success. Broccoli is one of the industry’s most influential producers. She and her brother Michael G. Wilson were given an Honorary Academy Award in 2024. A year later she handed creative control of the iconic James Bond spy franchise to Amazon MGM Studios. The world is now eagerly anticipating Amazon’s announcement over who will play the next 007. Watch on Deadline

The NFTS’ Cubby Broccoli Building at its Beaconsfield campus will open in a few weeks. Officially named by Barbara Broccoli earlier this year in honour of her late father, the building will provide teaching facilities, creative spaces and the school’s first on-site student accommodation.

Broccoli said: “It’s a tremendous honour to become President of the National Film and Television School, following in Lord David Puttnam’s footsteps. Having been involved with the NFTS for many years, I have seen first-hand the extraordinary talent it develops and the vital role it plays in shaping the future of our industry. With the opening of The Cubby Broccoli Building taking place in January, this is especially meaningful. My father believed in the power of filmmaking and in giving talented people the opportunity to succeed.”

NFTS chair Sophie Turner Laing welcomed the “landmark moment” and thanked Puttnam, “whose support, wisdom and unwavering belief in the NFTS have been invaluable.”

Last month, British producer Pippa Harris and YouTube UK boss Alison Lomax joined the NFTS board.

Golshifteh Farahani Is a Widow Fighting the Exploitation of Kids as the Taliban Nears Kabul in ‘When She Hears the Bells’ (Exclusive Clip and Chat)

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Chabname Zariab’s Tallinn-debuting debut feature includes images shot in Afghanistan and shines a light on women and a "taboo" topic in a story that is "a personal responsibility and an act of quiet resistance."

Golshifteh Farahani‘s (Alpha, Pirates of the Caribbean: Dead Men Tell No Tales, The Pear Tree) new film, writer-director Chabname Zariab’s debut feature When She Hears the Bells (Les Clochettes de Kaboul), is both a thriller and a call to stand up for human rights.

France-based Iranian actress Farahani stars as Leila, a young widow seeking a fresh start in Kabul, the capital of Afghanistan. But she soon finds out that the kitchen, in which she finds work, is a so-called bacha bazi house. These are establishments in which boys and young men are forced to dress in women’s clothes and dance for entertainment – and for sexual and other exploitation. At the same time – it is 2021 – the Taliban is moving closer and closer to Kabul. Leila tries to flee and also help a group of boys escape before the city falls. Sayed Hashimi (13 Days, 13 Nights) co-stars in the film, which includes images actually shot in Afghanistan. Related Stories Movies Compassion and Bureaucracy Clash in 'A Job Well Done,' Which Explores Immigration Through Social Workers' Eyes Movies "I Look Good With a Weapon in My Hand": Priyanka Chopra Jonas on Trading Weapons for Words in 'Bluefly'

When She Hears the Bells premieres in the First Feature Competition of the 30th edition of the Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF) in Estonia.

Pierre Cottereau handled cinematography, while the editors are Xavier Sirven, Héloïse Pelloquet and Julie Duclaux. Zariab directed When She Hears the Bells, which she co-wrote with her sister Parande Zariab. Produced by France’s Alta Rocca Films with Germany’s Starhaus Filmproduktion, Belgium’s Versus Production and Arte France Cinema, the film will be distributed in France by Le Pacte, in Germany by Grandfilm and O’Brother in Belgium. Le Pacte is handling sales.

Writer-director Zariab, an Afghan creative based in Paris, wanted to take on repression across society. About bacha bazi, she says: “It is a symbolic reflection of the state of this society, where laws enforce the separation of men and women and leave room for certain abuses, of which this is one of the most appalling.”

And she adds: “The story also highlights the reality for women both before the Taliban’s return and since August 2021, when millions saw their rights erased and the world largely looked away. For me, telling this story is both a personal responsibility and an act of quiet resistance.”

THR can now exclusively premiere a clip from When She Hears the Bells before sharing an email interview with Zariab about the film and how it mixes genre elements with a cinematic tribute to solidarity and the bonds forged when standing shoulder-to-shoulder to protect human rights and the vulnerable. Watch the exclusive clip right here.

And here is what the writer-director says about When She Hears the Bells.

Where did you shoot most of the film? And I heard you also shot parts in Afghanistan. What can you share about that?We had first considered shooting in Iraqi Kurdistan, whose landscapes and faces strongly resemble Afghanistan’s. We went on location [with] scouts there, then changed course at the last minute because of the bombings between Iran and Israel in 2024. The film was ultimately shot almost entirely in Casablanca, Morocco.Some of the images were indeed shot in Kabul, because I wanted authentic footage of that city. It was essential. But it was out of the question for us to go there. So I called a friend who is a cinematographer in Afghanistan — I’m not giving his name, for his safety — and we worked remotely. I would send him screenshots of my film so that the wide shots would blend well with the images I had shot in Morocco, or I would ask him to go to neighborhoods and roads that I know. These images are mainly wide shots, meant to capture landscapes and neighborhoods, so that the film rings true without exposing the people who live there.

Why did you want to tell a feature film story about bacha bazi in Afghanistan and what does the practice of bacha bazi say about life and society there?I am very sensitive to the cause of children and animals, whom I consider to be the most vulnerable beings in the world. It must be said that bacha bazi is not the subject of the film but the arena in which we follow the journey of an Afghan woman, Leila, played by Golshifteh Farahani, who tries to save children before everything collapses in the Kabul of 2021.Coming back to bacha bazi, it is a practice that has existed in the region for centuries. It has disappeared completely in some countries. It was also on its way to disappearing in Afghanistan. When I was a child, in the 1980s, the practice existed in remote areas but no longer in the big cities. Then came the Taliban’s arrival in 1996 — the moment when my family and I left the country where I was born to settle in France — and it was completely banned, according to many reports, such as one by the NGO The New Humanitarian.

But it surged again with the arrival of American troops, who had been ordered to turn a blind eye to bacha bazi in order to win over Afghan warlords. This scandal was confirmed years later by journalistic investigations, as well as by official Pentagon reports in 2017. Little by little, having “a bacha” — literally, having “a boy” — became a social marker, a sign of wealth and power. Today, according to the Afghan press, such as Amu TV, “the new Taliban” have “adopted” the practice. According to its journalists, the perpetrators are rarely prosecuted.Above all, it reveals the impunity that persists thanks to the influence of those who practice it. Bacha bazi is only one symptom among others of a violent society that separates women from the social landscape, and therefore from desires. Imagine a society in which men and women live separately and are not allowed to cross paths. To me, that is quite frightening.

You made a short film about bacha bazi that was focused on the boys. I love that your feature film also shows us women’s lives and difficulties. How important was it for you to show us this women’s perspective, both before and after the Taliban’s return, and what do you hope audiences will take away from the film?My short film focused on the boys because my concern at the time was what children endure, in my country of origin as elsewhere. A child who grows up in fear or violence carries the marks for life, and it is through childhood that a nation’s destiny is shaped.When the Taliban took Kabul in 2021, I was developing another project, set in Paris but still tied to Afghanistan. I decided to abandon it in order to make a film set in the country. I could not ignore the cause of women, who already held a fragile place in that society and whom the Taliban are trying to erase more and more. Today, women are no longer allowed to work and girls are no longer admitted to school beyond the age of 12. What I hope audiences take away is the distress of an entire society. I believe my country is going through a very deep collective suffering. It was a deliberate choice to show the period before the fall of Kabul from a woman’s point of view, because the period after, the whole world has seen on the news, and still sees today.I also wanted the film to end on that collapse, and on the question of those who leave, because fleeing horror when everything is falling apart is both a positive escape for Leila, the character, and no guarantee of a peaceful life afterward. I’m thinking of Dheepan by Jacques Audiard, which begins on the other side of the fence.

How did you get Golshifteh Farahani to star in the film? She brings so many layers and dimensions to her role.The hardest part was making contact with Golshifteh. It took several months. The first time I saw her was in About Elly by Asghar Farhadi. I held on to her image for a long time. Then I followed her work in Chicken With Plums by Marjane Satrapi and later in The Patience Stone (Syngué Sabour) by Atiq Rahimi, where she played an Afghan woman and spoke Afghan Persian almost perfectly. That is also one of the films that made me feel this character could only be for her.

Her commitment to women’s causes mattered just as much, because it echoed what I wanted to tell. So I waited until she agreed to meet me. Once she learned about the subject, she accepted very quickly. She is an extraordinary actress and an extraordinary woman. She was incredibly supportive on set. Many of my actors were non-professionals or came from the theater. Golshifteh not only mastered her own performance but also guided the other actors, and almost choreographed her movements with the children to help them act and to keep takes from being interrupted.

She is a woman who breaks down barriers, and we were on the same page about that. In the evenings, we sometimes gathered in one another’s apartments to play music and sing. Golshifteh can play many instruments. One of the actresses was also a singer, and we had other musicians in the group. Those evenings were incredible, full of emotion. We were Afghans and Iranians together, united by what we had in common: our culture and our exile. And Golshifteh was also key in fostering that cohesion. It was beautiful!

How did you decide on the title for the film ‘When She Hears the Bells,’ or ‘The Bells of Kabul’ in France?My short film was called Au bruit des clochettes (At the Sound of the Little Bells), and its English version was When You Hear the Bells. The feature film also tells the story of the country. It is a story within history. For the French version, I wanted to put Kabul in the title and keep “the little bells,” which are somewhat enigmatic — for a Westerner, you don’t know what the little bells are. People from the East — Iranians, Afghans, and so on — know exactly what it refers to.

It was interesting, in fact, because during casting, some of the people we contacted refused to audition simply upon learning the title. They knew exactly what it was about: the little bells are bacha bazi. And they refused because the subject is too taboo, too sensitive. For the English version, since Leila ends up in a bacha bazi house, When She Hears the Bells seemed right to me because it puts the female character back at the center. It is her own story, the story of an ordinary woman who becomes a heroine and whose journey becomes extraordinary.

Is there anything you would like to mention?Thank you. This film was born out of a concern. If audiences come away with a better understanding of what Afghan women, and Afghans more broadly, are living through today, and with the feeling that this suffering deserves to be faced head-on, then it will have done its part. Because indeed, today, the world has once again abandoned this country to its fate. There are so many misfortunes and new conflicts that have emerged that people no longer talk about this population. It was important to me to make this film in order to shine a light on them.

For the financing, we received the trust of many partners, who were moved by the subject and convinced of the necessity of this story. Now I hope the film will find an echo and a distribution commensurate with the importance I attach to it.

Right now, winter is beginning, and the country’s economic situation is catastrophic. People struggle to feed themselves and to heat their homes. Children are dying of malnutrition in some regions. There is no work, no real future. There is also a cultural genocide: attempts are being made to erase the Persian language and other languages in favor of Pashto, the language spoken by most of the Taliban.

Very recently I read that in a Hazara neighborhood — the ethnic group to which the character Marie in my film belongs, as one of the two friends at the hair salon — the Taliban gave families 72 hours to leave their homes. Homes that they had bought, and that are now being confiscated from them. This shows that the persecution of the Hazara also continues with complete impunity. Not a day goes by that I don’t follow what is happening there.

I have a deep love for this wounded country and its people. Afghanistan is a country of many ethnic groups, some of which try to crush the others while defending themselves against outside aggression, all in the most brutal poverty. Yolande Zauberman ended her documentary M with a quotation from Franz Kafka: “I am among my own with a knife to protect them, I am among my own with a knife to attack them.” I think I could have borrowed it too.

InfoQ Homepage News LinkedIn Replaces Argo CD with a Custom GitOps Engine for Kubernetes Deployments

Nov 12, 2026 4 min read

In a recent post, LinkedIn revealed it has replaced Argo CD with its own GitOps engine. This new system deploys applications across over 250 Kubernetes clusters. It supports more than 4,000 services and manages over 100,000 deployments each week. According to a post, deployment infrastructure failures fell from 925 to 63 despite a 3.7x increase in deployment volume. The median time to detect and conclude a failed deployment dropped from 16.1 to 2.45 minutes.

LinkedIn started with one Argo CD instance per cluster, a topology it chose because Argo CD already managed its cluster add-ons that way. Clusters run up to 5,000 nodes each. A central deployment orchestrator spawned an ephemeral pod per deployment, with Argo CD hidden behind it. The team lists seven problems that emerged at scale. Argo CD doesn't have an observedGeneration, so you can't link sync and health status to a specific deployment. LinkedIn created a Lua health check that uses a tracking annotation to address this. A Synced and Healthy application could also resolve to an unintended revision when stale repo-server cache entries were used.

Two of the issues were architectural. Argo CD maintains one watch per group-version-resource across Applications. LinkedIn’s clusters have low resource-type diversity. This means etcd churn from one app can slow syncs for all apps. In one test, sync took under 3 minutes on a cluster with 60,000 pods and over 10 minutes with more than 80,000. The second was server-side-apply PATCH. Kubernetes truncates managedFields once an object has had more than 10 distinct field managers, and the API server then forgets who owns a field. In LinkedIn's AI workloads, which run multiple containers per Pod, a container removed from a manifest could therefore survive on the live Pod. The team had pruning disabled because destructive operations in production require approval. The remaining problems were CRD schema changes that required controller restarts across the fleet. Some OutOfSync states did not reflect real drift. Error messages were buried in boilerplate.

LinkedIn evaluated Flux but found it shared the same controller paradigm, so it built the engine as a library. Each deployment runs in its pod, with no shared controller state. Updates use PUT for full replacements, not PATCH. They retry on resourceVersion conflicts with exponential backoff. A 3-way diff across the live object, the previously deployed manifest, and the newly rendered manifest detects drift, and genuine drift fails the deployment. The same pod then watches the object's status until it converges or times out, and the API server unwraps errors for the user. Objects missing from the rendered manifest become deletion candidates, subject to delete protection, and convergence logic for LinkedIn's CRDs is built in.

The comparison uses the same calendar month a year apart. The migration ran from July 2025 to April 2026, which makes May 2026 the first clean post-migration month. The infra failure count covers only failures caused by the deployment system and excludes drift errors, since the two systems define drift differently. LinkedIn states that the improvement isn't solely due to the new engine. The orchestrator's error classification and reliability also got better during this time. The median duration for successful deployments is similar for both systems. However, the mean and tail have improved. The team attributes this to a namespace-scoped informer cache.

The engine is internal, and no open-source release is mentioned. Argo CD remains in use at LinkedIn for cluster add-ons and system services. The team had also found that maintaining 200+ Argo CD instances made each upgrade a multi-week project.

Teams running Argo CD at scale can check two things without a rewrite. Count the field managers on long-lived objects, since managedFields truncation can leave orphaned containers that produce no error. Then check whether sync latency tracks total pod count on shared clusters, which points to watch contention. LinkedIn concludes that the build-or-extend decision relies on the operational model. They say that per-deployment isolation, not controller tuning, allows the system to scale with compute.

LinkedIn is not the only large operator to build deployment logic around Kubernetes rather than adopt a general-purpose GitOps controller. Uber’s Up platform assigns each deployment to a dedicated controller. This is the Uber Deployment Controller. It translates the intent into Kubernetes primitives and sends status updates. This helps the workflow move forward. Uber keeps a long-running controller, while LinkedIn runs one ephemeral pod per deployment with no shared controller state.

Airbnb went further back and built its own tooling years earlier. In a talk at QCon London 2019 on InfoQ, infrastructure engineer Melanie Cebula introduced kube-gen. This internal framework creates Kubernetes configuration from one versioned project file. It also includes a deploy tool that applies or replaces objects and restarts pods, so changes take effect right away. Airbnb's deploy tool also returns an atomic success or failure, and a container that restarts for 30 seconds at the start of a rollout counts as a failed deploy. Airbnb was then at roughly 125,000 production deploys a year.

How to Build a Resilient Personal Investment Plan

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Home » Op-ed » How to Build a Resilient Personal Investment Plan

Build a resilient personal investment plan with clear goals, diversified assets, and practical risk management. Learn how to assess costs, liquidity, and alternative investments before committing your money.

A sound investment plan ties your financial goals, time horizon, risk tolerance, and available resources together into one coherent strategy. Without that connection, even well-intentioned investment decisions can end up working against you. Diversification—spreading investments across and within asset classes rather than concentrating in a single holding—remains one of the most consistently cited principles in personal finance education. Some investors, while researching ways to diversify, come across physical assets such as gold bars as one example of exposure outside traditional stocks and bonds. This isn’t a recommendation; it’s simply an illustration of the kind of asset category investors may compare when evaluating a broader plan. This article focuses on the decision-making principles, risk management practices, and due diligence steps that apply regardless of which specific assets you ultimately choose.

Before selecting any investment, figure out what the money is actually for. Emergency savings, a short-term purchase, retirement, education funding, and long-term wealth building each come with different requirements for accessibility and acceptable volatility. Money you’ll need within the next year or two generally calls for safer, more liquid holdings. Money earmarked for goals decades away can typically absorb more fluctuation in exchange for growth potential.

It helps to separate two related but distinct concepts: risk tolerance, which is how much market fluctuation you can emotionally stomach, and risk capacity, which is your financial ability to absorb losses without jeopardizing your goals. Income stability, outstanding debt, the size of your emergency reserve, number of dependents, and investment time horizon all factor into risk capacity. Writing down your goals and constraints before researching specific investments helps prevent decisions driven by excitement or fear.

Several types of risk deserve separate attention: volatility (short-term price swings), permanent loss of capital, liquidity risk (difficulty converting an asset to cash without taking a discount), inflation risk (the erosion of purchasing power), and credit risk (a counterparty’s inability to meet its obligations).

Asset allocation refers to dividing a portfolio among categories such as stocks, bonds, cash, real estate, and alternative assets. Each category tends to respond differently to economic conditions, which is why diversification can reduce concentration risk—though it can’t eliminate the possibility of loss altogether. A realistic target allocation, one you can stick with through market stress rather than abandon at the first sign of volatility, matters more than a theoretically optimal one you can’t maintain. Rebalancing—periodically returning a portfolio to its intended mix—is a discipline rooted in preserving your chosen risk profile, not in predicting short-term market movements.

One educational approach organizes a portfolio into broad functions: liquidity for near-term needs, stability for capital preservation, growth for long-term objectives, and diversification for assets that may behave differently from your core holdings. This framework is illustrative only and shouldn’t be treated as a universal formula.

Key takeaway: A resilient portfolio begins with goals and constraints—not with chasing the latest fashionable investment.

Alternative assets are sometimes considered for diversification, potential inflation resilience, or reduced dependence on traditional financial assets. Gold, for instance, is occasionally discussed as a diversifier with no direct credit exposure, though its price can still fluctuate meaningfully. Historical behavior should never be mistaken for a guarantee of future performance.

Exposure to an asset like gold can take several forms: physical ownership, exchange-traded products, mining-company shares, or futures and other derivatives. Each form carries its own market, operational, counterparty, liquidity, and regulatory characteristics. Owning a physical asset is fundamentally different from owning a financial product whose value is merely linked to that asset’s price.

Understanding the economics of physical ownership requires a few plain-language definitions: the spot price is the current market price for immediate delivery; the purchase premium is the amount charged above spot price; the resale spread is the difference between what you pay and what you’d receive upon selling; storage and insurance costs are ongoing expenses tied to safekeeping; and liquidity describes how quickly and efficiently you can convert the asset back to cash.

Looking only at the quoted purchase price overlooks the full picture. The total cost of ownership includes premiums, storage, insurance, and eventual resale spreads. Before purchasing, it’s worth checking:

Be wary of unsolicited offers, urgency-based sales tactics, guaranteed-return claims, or requests for unusual payment methods. Compare multiple independent sources and hold on to receipts and ownership records. Product specifications, legal treatment, and tax rules vary by jurisdiction and change over time, so verify details through authoritative sources and qualified professionals.

Physical assets come with a storage decision to make: home storage, bank or institutional safe-deposit arrangements, or professional third-party vaulting. Each option involves trade-offs in privacy, access, security, insurance costs, and estate planning complexity. Physical possession introduces practical risks—theft, loss, damage—that don’t apply in the same way to securities held in a regulated brokerage account. Maintaining a detailed inventory and folding relevant assets into your estate documents is worthwhile. The safest approach depends on individual circumstances, local laws, and available insurance coverage.

Consider two hypothetical investors. The first spends time comparing dealers, calculates her total ownership costs, and limits her purchase to a small percentage of her portfolio in line with a written plan. The second, swayed by a high-pressure phone pitch promising rapid appreciation, commits a large sum without comparing alternatives or understanding the resale terms. The contrast says something about process quality, not about any specific asset.

Revisit your plan after major life changes—a new job, marriage, inheritance, retirement, or a significant shift in debt. Market movements alone don’t necessarily call for a strategic overhaul. Scheduled reviews should cover goal progress, emergency reserves, asset allocation, fees and ownership costs, and beneficiary or estate information. For decisions involving significant assets or complex obligations, consulting a qualified financial, tax, or legal professional is a wise move.

Before adding an alternative asset, consider:

A durable investment plan rests on clear objectives, genuine diversification, reasonable costs, adequate liquidity, and informed risk management—not on any single product. Physical precious metals may suit some financial plans, but they’re not automatically appropriate for every investor. Compare alternatives objectively, verify claims independently, and steer clear of decisions made under pressure. Take time to review your overall portfolio, write down the intended role of each holding, and seek qualified advice whenever a decision exceeds your own expertise.

The US, who had been drawn in Group J alongside Montenegro, Chile and Algeria, will not longer participate in the competition in the Middle East.

The well-being of our players and staff will always be our top priority.”

The letter sent by US Soccer to the players on Wednesday was obtained by the Athletic.

FIFA reportedly have the ability to replace the North American country in the tournament, although a direct replacement is yet to be formally announced. Given that sourcing a replacement would require a team convening at incredibly short notice, it's possible FIFA keep Group J at just three teams.

With Pochettino heading to Man Utd, the US are not messing around.

The US were supposed to be one of the more exciting teams heading to the tournament, with the likes of Malik Jakupovic (Philadelphia Union), Robert Turdean (Chicago Fire) and Vinny Garcia (LA Galaxy) among the assortment of rising teen talents that coach Alex Aldaz was set to have at his disposal.
