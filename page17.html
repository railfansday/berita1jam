The FAA is finally phasing out floppy disks from air traffic control

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The US Federal Aviation Administration is getting some probably overdue upgrades. The flight agency announced today that it is modernizing some air traffic control systems that have to date been running on floppy disks. You read that right: floppy disks.

For those of you too young to remember, floppy disks were precursors to CDs as a means of storing and running digital data, particularly computer files and programs. IBM developed the first one back in 1971. The FAA systems in question date back to the 1980s. Unsurprisingly, the bulletin from the agency says the coming upgrades will give air traffic controllers faster access to information, reduce repair expenses and improve safety for public air travel.

The FAA's diskette systems contain "vital weather and other critical information" for air traffic controllers, and they're still in use at more than 400 facilities around the country. They'll be replaced by a more modern system called Enterprise – Information Display System (E-IDS), which is comprised of a digital display for relaying status information, updates and other alerts to air traffic teams. The FAA plans to have E-IDS installed in 435 sites by the end of 2028; the first five locations to receive the upgrades are the Atlantic City International Airport, Nashville International Airport and Greater Rochester International Airport, as well as data processing hubs in Atlanta and Salt Lake City.

Despite the age of floppy disk tech, they've actually remained more common in government systems than you might expect. The US Air Force only just ended its use of 8-inch diskettes tied to nuclear missile launch silos in 2019. The Japanese government announced in 2024 that it had phased out all but one of its systems that still relied on floppy disks.

How doctors diagnose and treat rhabdomyolysis in 10 steps

Rhabdomyolysis diagnosis and treatment explained in 10 steps, from the creatine kinase blood test and urine checks to IV fluids, potassium control and dialysis

In the early 1940s, British physician Eric Bywaters described crush syndrome in victims of the London Blitz. His account helped define a problem that emergency doctors still screen for today. When enough skeletal muscle is damaged, the harm does not stay in the muscle. It spreads through the bloodstream to the kidneys and the heart.

That condition is rhabdomyolysis, often shortened to rhabdo. It is the breakdown of skeletal muscle, and it releases the cells' contents into the blood. Those contents include myoglobin, a protein that stores oxygen in muscle, along with the enzyme creatine kinase, potassium and phosphate. In large amounts, these substances can overwhelm the body's ability to clear them.

Crush injuries are only one route to muscle breakdown. The condition can also follow intense exercise, a long period lying immobile or a reaction to certain medicines. That range means a young athlete and an older adult found on the floor after a fall can both end up with the same diagnosis.

Speed matters because the two main dangers can develop quickly. Acute kidney injury is a major complication. A sharp rise in blood potassium can disturb the heart's rhythm. Early treatment aims to head off both.

This guide to rhabdomyolysis diagnosis and treatment walks through 10 steps clinicians commonly use. It starts with the symptoms and medical history that raise suspicion. It then moves through the creatine kinase blood test, urine checks, kidney and electrolyte panels and heart monitoring. The final steps cover intravenous fluids, potassium control, dialysis, surgery for dangerous pressure inside muscles and the search for an underlying cause.

The steps are a selection, not a complete or ranked clinical protocol. Hospitals adjust care to each patient's cause, severity and other health conditions. Nothing here replaces individual medical advice.

Dark urine can be a sign that myoglobin from damaged muscle is passing through the kidneys. Anyone who notices dark urine along with severe muscle pain should seek urgent medical care.

The classic signs of rhabdomyolysis are muscle pain, muscle weakness and dark urine that looks like tea or cola. Many people with the condition do not have all three. Doctors treat these symptoms as a reason to order tests, not as proof of the diagnosis.

Rhabdomyolysis is the breakdown of skeletal muscle, the tissue that moves the arms, legs and trunk. When those muscle cells are damaged, their contents leak into the bloodstream. The main substances released are myoglobin, creatine kinase, potassium and phosphate. Creatine kinase is an enzyme that muscle cells use to manage energy. Potassium is a mineral that muscle cells hold in high concentration.

Myoglobin is the protein that stores oxygen inside muscle fibers. Once it reaches the blood, the kidneys filter it out. In large amounts, it turns urine reddish-brown or dark brown. That color change is a sign a patient can see without any test.

Muscle pain in rhabdomyolysis tends to be deeper and more persistent than ordinary soreness after a workout. It often involves large muscle groups such as the thighs, calves, shoulders or lower back. The affected muscles may feel tender to the touch. Weakness can make it hard to climb stairs, rise from a chair or lift the arms overhead.

The full triad is missing in many cases. Some patients report pain and weakness but pass normal-looking urine. Others notice dark urine with only mild aches. Patients who are very ill or confused may not be able to describe pain at all.

Other complaints can appear alongside the core signs. These include fatigue, nausea, vomiting and a general sense of being unwell. In severe cases, a person may produce very little urine. That drop in output can signal that the kidneys are under strain.

Dark urine has other possible causes, including dehydration and bleeding in the urinary tract. That overlap is another reason symptoms alone cannot settle the question.

Anyone who has dark urine together with severe muscle pain should seek urgent medical care. Rhabdomyolysis can injure the kidneys and disturb the heart's rhythm. Both risks are easier to manage when the condition is identified early.

A careful medical history and a hands-on physical exam help doctors identify what triggered the muscle breakdown and how much local damage it has caused. The questions cover recent injury, exertion, immobilization, heat, infection, substances and medications. The exam looks for swollen, tense or tender muscles.

The history often begins with physical trauma. Crush injuries from a building collapse, a car crash or heavy machinery compress muscle and cut off its blood supply. Clinicians also ask whether a patient lay in one position for a long time. Someone who fell at home and stayed on the floor for hours may have this kind of injury. So may a person who lost consciousness after drinking or taking drugs. In both cases, the weight of the body pressing on the same muscles for hours can damage them.

Extreme exertion is another common thread. Doctors ask about recent workouts, especially intense or unfamiliar ones such as a first spin class, a long-distance race or demanding military or athletic training. Hot, humid conditions add to the strain on working muscle. Questions about heat exposure and heat stroke usually follow. Seizures matter for a related reason. Prolonged, forceful muscle contractions during a seizure can injure muscle tissue in much the same way that overexertion does.

The medication and substance review is detailed. Statins, the widely prescribed cholesterol drugs, are a recognized cause. Doctors also check whether a patient takes other drugs that can raise statin levels in the blood. They ask about alcohol and recreational drugs such as cocaine and amphetamines. Infection is on the list too. Some viral and bacterial illnesses can set off muscle breakdown on their own.

The physical exam centers on the muscles themselves. Clinicians press on major muscle groups to find areas that feel swollen, firm or painful. They watch closely for signs of compartment syndrome, a dangerous rise in pressure inside a closed muscle compartment, often in the leg or forearm. Warning signs include pain that seems out of proportion to the injury and pain when the muscle is passively stretched. Numbness or tingling in the limb is another red flag.

Family history can change the direction of the workup. If relatives have had muscle pain, cramps or dark urine after exercise, doctors consider an inherited metabolic myopathy. That is a genetic condition that limits how muscles produce energy.

A blood test for creatine kinase, or CK, is the central test doctors use to confirm rhabdomyolysis. Many clinicians treat a level above five times the upper limit of normal, often cited as about 1,000 U/L, as support for the diagnosis.

CK is an enzyme that muscle cells use to manage their energy supply. It normally stays inside those cells. When skeletal muscle is injured and the cell membranes break down, the enzyme leaks into the bloodstream. A standard blood draw can then measure it.

The result is reported in units per liter. Each laboratory sets its own reference range. That is why the threshold is often framed as a multiple of the upper limit of normal rather than a single fixed number. The 1,000 U/L figure is a common shorthand, not a universal cutoff.

The enzyme comes in several forms. The version found mainly in skeletal muscle is called CK-MM. A different form, CK-MB, is concentrated in heart muscle, and CK-BB is found in the brain. The routine total CK test does not separate these forms. Doctors read the result alongside the patient's history and physical exam.

Timing shapes how the number is read. CK usually climbs after the muscle injury and reaches its peak 24 to 72 hours later. It then begins to fall. A sample drawn soon after the injury can therefore understate how much muscle has been damaged.

For that reason, doctors rarely rely on a single measurement. They repeat the test over the following days to see whether the level is still rising or has started to come down. A steady decline is a sign that muscle breakdown has slowed and the patient is recovering. A level that keeps rising, or climbs again after falling, can point to ongoing injury.

CK also has an advantage over myoglobin, the other protein released from damaged muscle. Myoglobin is cleared from the blood quickly. It can be gone by the time a patient arrives for care. CK lingers longer. That makes it the more dependable marker of muscle breakdown.

A raised CK does not settle the diagnosis by itself. Hard exercise can push the level up in healthy people. A modest elevation also has other possible causes. Clinicians weigh the figure against symptoms, the likely trigger and other test results before concluding that a patient has rhabdomyolysis.

A urine dipstick that reads positive for blood, paired with microscopy showing few or no red blood cells, points doctors toward myoglobin in the urine. That mismatch is a fast bedside clue to rhabdomyolysis. A negative result, however, does not rule the condition out.

The logic rests on how the dipstick works. The blood pad on the strip does not detect red blood cells directly. It reacts to heme, the iron-containing part of hemoglobin. Myoglobin also contains heme. The strip therefore changes color whether the heme comes from intact red cells, free hemoglobin or myoglobin released by damaged muscle.

Microscopy separates those possibilities. A clinician or lab technician examines a centrifuged urine sample under a microscope and looks for red blood cells. Sometimes the dipstick shows a strong blood signal while the sample holds few or no red cells. In that case, the heme is likely dissolved in the urine rather than coming from bleeding in the urinary tract.

That still leaves two candidates: myoglobin and free hemoglobin. Free hemoglobin appears when red blood cells break apart inside blood vessels, a process called hemolysis. Doctors often look at the patient's blood plasma to tell the two apart. Hemolysis tends to tint plasma pink or red. Myoglobin is a smaller molecule that the kidneys filter out quickly. As a result, the plasma usually keeps its normal color.

A dedicated urine myoglobin test exists. At many hospitals, though, it is not run on site. The result may not come back fast enough to guide early decisions.

The quick clearance of myoglobin is also the main weakness of urine testing. Myoglobin can leave the blood and urine before a patient reaches the hospital, particularly when symptoms began days earlier. It can also be present at levels too low to darken the urine or trigger the dipstick. For that reason, doctors confirm the diagnosis with a creatine kinase blood test rather than with urine findings.

Myoglobin in the urine matters beyond diagnosis. In large amounts it can injure the kidney's tubules, the small channels that process filtered fluid. Its presence prompts closer attention to kidney function throughout the illness.

Blood tests for kidney function and electrolytes show doctors whether muscle breakdown is harming the kidneys or disturbing the body's chemistry. The key markers are creatinine and blood urea nitrogen for the kidneys, plus potassium, calcium, phosphate, uric acid and acid-base status.

Kidney damage is the main reason these tests matter. Myoglobin released from damaged muscle passes through the kidneys' filters. In large amounts, it can injure and clog the small tubules that process urine.

Creatinine is a waste product that healthy kidneys clear from the blood. When filtration slows, creatinine builds up. Blood urea nitrogen, or BUN, is a second waste marker that also rises when the kidneys falter. Doctors read the two together. A single result offers a snapshot. The trend across repeated blood draws shows whether kidney function is holding steady, getting worse or starting to recover.

Electrolytes tell a parallel story. Muscle cells hold large stores of potassium and phosphate. When those cells break open, both minerals spill into the bloodstream. Early results therefore often show high potassium and high phosphate. Kidneys that are already struggling clear these minerals more slowly. That can push the levels higher still.

Calcium often moves in the opposite direction early in the illness. The blood level tends to drop as calcium binds with the surplus phosphate and collects in injured muscle tissue. The pattern can reverse later. During recovery, calcium held in damaged muscle can return to the circulation and leave the blood level high.

Uric acid is measured because it also comes from broken-down muscle cells. Those cells release purines, which the body converts into uric acid. A raised level adds to the strain on kidneys that are already under pressure.

Acid-base status completes the picture. Injured muscle releases acids into the blood, and damaged kidneys excrete them less efficiently. The result can be metabolic acidosis, a chemical imbalance that doctors detect through blood bicarbonate levels and, in sicker patients, a blood gas test.

These results guide care well beyond the initial diagnosis. A combination of rising creatinine, climbing potassium and worsening acidosis points to a more severe course. The chemistry can shift over the first several days. Care teams therefore repeat these tests rather than relying on one set of numbers.

An electrocardiogram, or ECG, shows doctors whether high potassium released from damaged muscle is disturbing the heart's electrical rhythm. That disturbance is the most immediate threat to life in rhabdomyolysis. For that reason, an ECG is one of the earliest tests a patient with suspected muscle breakdown receives.

Potassium is concentrated inside cells, and skeletal muscle holds most of the body's supply. When large amounts of muscle break down, that potassium spills into the bloodstream. If the kidneys are also injured, they cannot clear the excess at their usual pace. Blood levels can then climb further.

The heart depends on a tight balance of potassium across cell membranes to generate each beat. When blood potassium rises too high, the electrical signals that coordinate the heartbeat slow down and become unstable.

The test itself is quick, painless and done at the bedside. Small electrodes placed on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity as a tracing on paper or a screen.

Clinicians read that tracing for a recognized sequence of changes. Tall, narrow, peaked T waves are often the earliest sign. As potassium rises, the PR interval can lengthen and P waves can flatten or disappear. The QRS complex reflects the electrical signal behind the main pumping contraction, and it can widen. In the most severe cases, the tracing can blur into a sine wave pattern. That pattern may come before ventricular fibrillation or cardiac arrest.

These changes do not always appear in neat steps. Some patients with dangerously high potassium show few ECG changes. A normal tracing does not make a high blood result safe. Doctors read the ECG alongside the lab value rather than relying on either one alone.

Low calcium, another common early finding in muscle breakdown, can also leave a mark on the tracing. It tends to lengthen the QT interval.

In severe cases, patients are placed on continuous cardiac monitoring, often in an intensive care unit. Constant monitoring lets staff see rhythm changes the moment they appear, without waiting for the next blood draw. An abnormal tracing moves potassium-lowering treatment to the top of the care team's priorities.

Early intravenous fluid with isotonic saline is the cornerstone of rhabdomyolysis treatment. It keeps the kidneys flushed and urine flowing. That lowers the chance that proteins released from damaged muscle will injure the kidneys.

The kidneys face several pressures at once when muscle breaks down. They filter myoglobin out of the blood. In concentrated urine, myoglobin can collect inside the tiny kidney tubules and block them. It can also injure the cells lining those tubules directly.

Blood volume is often low at the same time. Fluid moves out of the bloodstream and into swollen, injured muscle. Many patients are also dehydrated from heat, hard exertion or hours spent immobile on the floor. Less blood reaching the kidneys means less urine. Slower urine gives myoglobin more time to settle in the tubules.

Isotonic saline addresses both problems. Its salt concentration is close to that of blood. That lets it stay in the circulation long enough to restore volume and raise blood flow to the kidneys. Timing matters. The chance to prevent acute kidney injury is greatest before the kidneys have begun to fail.

Doctors adjust the infusion rate according to how much urine the patient produces. A commonly cited target for adults is roughly 200 to 300 mL of urine per hour. Reaching that number requires close measurement of output. Hospitals often place a urinary catheter so nurses can record it hour by hour.

The same fluid that protects the kidneys can cause harm if it builds up in the body. Fluid overload can lead to swelling. It can also put fluid in the lungs, which makes breathing harder. The risk is higher in older patients and people with heart failure. It is also higher in anyone whose kidneys have already stopped making much urine. Care teams track weight, breathing, oxygen levels and the balance between fluid given and urine passed. If urine output stays low despite fluids, more saline can add to overload without restoring kidney function.

Two add-ons have a long history in rhabdomyolysis care. Sodium bicarbonate has been given to make the urine less acidic. The theory is that myoglobin is less likely to form blockages in alkaline urine. Mannitol, a sugar alcohol that acts as an osmotic diuretic, has been used to push more urine through the kidneys.

Evidence that either one adds benefit beyond adequate saline is limited. Neither is part of routine treatment. Bicarbonate carries its own risk. Making the blood more alkaline can lower the amount of active calcium in circulation. Mannitol depends on working kidneys to draw out urine. In a patient whose kidneys are not responding, it can worsen fluid problems instead of relieving them.

Doctors treat high potassium in rhabdomyolysis urgently. They give intravenous calcium to protect the heart and insulin with glucose to push potassium back into cells. Low calcium is usually left alone unless it causes symptoms or changes on an electrocardiogram.

Most of the body's potassium is stored inside cells, including muscle cells. When muscle fibers break apart, that potassium spills into the bloodstream. High levels make heart muscle electrically unstable. That threat is why this imbalance gets attention first.

Intravenous calcium is often the opening move when potassium threatens the heart. It does not lower the potassium level. Instead, it stabilizes the membranes of heart muscle cells and makes them less prone to dangerous rhythms. Its protective effect is temporary. It buys time for other treatments to take hold.

Insulin comes next. The hormone drives potassium out of the blood and into cells, lowering the measured level fairly quickly. Glucose is given alongside it to keep blood sugar from falling too low. Blood sugar is then checked repeatedly. Albuterol, a drug better known as an asthma treatment, can shift potassium into cells in a similar way.

Shifting potassium into cells does not remove it from the body. Doctors also use measures that clear it. These include medications that bind potassium in the gut and diuretics that increase its loss in urine while the kidneys still work. When the kidneys cannot keep up, dialysis becomes an option.

Low calcium follows a different logic. Early in muscle breakdown, calcium moves out of the blood and settles into injured muscle tissue. It often combines there with phosphate released from the same damaged cells. Adding calcium at this stage can enlarge those deposits.

The deposits do not stay put. As the muscle heals, stored calcium can return to the bloodstream. Levels can then swing from low to high. Calcium given earlier in the illness can make that rebound worse.

For that reason, doctors generally hold off on calcium replacement. The exceptions are patients with symptoms of low calcium, such as tingling, cramps or muscle spasms, and patients whose electrocardiogram shows changes. High potassium with heart rhythm changes is its own case. Calcium is given then to protect the heart, even though the risk of deposits remains.

Dialysis and fasciotomy are reserved for the most serious complications of rhabdomyolysis. Doctors turn to dialysis when acute kidney injury progresses to kidney failure that other treatment cannot control. They turn to fasciotomy when swelling muscle triggers compartment syndrome.

Dialysis does the filtering work the kidneys can no longer manage. A machine draws blood through a catheter and passes it across a membrane that pulls out waste and excess salts. The cleaned blood then returns to the body. In rhabdomyolysis, specific problems drive the decision to start, rather than a single lab value.

The first is high potassium that keeps climbing despite medical treatment. Doctors call this refractory hyperkalemia. Dialysis removes potassium directly from the blood. That makes it the fallback when drugs that shift or flush out potassium are not enough.

The second is severe acidosis, a buildup of acid in the blood that failing kidneys cannot clear. The third is fluid overload. A patient who stops making urine while receiving large volumes of intravenous fluid can accumulate fluid in the lungs and tissues. Dialysis can pull that excess fluid off. The fourth is uremia, the toxic buildup of waste products the kidneys normally excrete. It can cause confusion, nausea and inflammation around the heart.

Dialysis is not a way to wash myoglobin out of the body. Conventional dialysis removes myoglobin poorly. The protein is too large to pass easily through standard membranes. The treatment's role is to manage the consequences of kidney failure while the kidneys have a chance to recover.

Fasciotomy addresses a different emergency. Muscles in the limbs sit inside tight compartments wrapped in fascia, a tough connective tissue that does not stretch. When injured muscle swells within that space, pressure can rise high enough to squeeze off blood flow. Muscle and nerves starved of blood can die. That adds to the muscle breakdown already under way.

A fasciotomy is a surgical incision through the fascia that releases the trapped pressure. Surgeons often leave the wound open at first. They close it or cover it with a skin graft once the swelling goes down. A delayed fasciotomy can leave permanent nerve and muscle damage in the affected limb.

Treatment for rhabdomyolysis is not complete until doctors find and remove whatever set off the muscle breakdown. Patients who have repeated episodes, or episodes after only modest exertion, may also be tested for inherited muscle disorders such as McArdle disease and carnitine palmitoyltransferase II deficiency.

How doctors remove the trigger depends on the cause. If a medication is the likely culprit, clinicians stop it. A cholesterol-lowering statin is one example. An infection is treated directly, with antibiotics when bacteria are responsible. Heat stroke calls for rapid cooling of the body to bring core temperature down.

This step matters because supportive care limits the damage but does not end it. Fluids and electrolyte management protect the kidneys and heart. They do nothing about an ongoing cause. If the offending drug or illness stays in place, muscle cells can keep breaking down, and the threat to the kidneys and heart continues.

A single episode after an obvious trigger, such as a crush injury or an extreme workout, does not usually prompt a search for an underlying disorder. The picture changes when episodes recur, or when ordinary activity brings them on. That pattern suggests the muscle itself may have trouble producing energy.

McArdle disease, also called glycogen storage disease type V, results from a deficiency of the enzyme muscle glycogen phosphorylase. Without it, muscles cannot break down their stored glycogen for fuel. People with the condition often develop cramps and fatigue early in exercise. Many describe a "second wind" once their muscles shift to other fuel sources.

Carnitine palmitoyltransferase II deficiency, or CPT II deficiency, affects a different energy pathway. The enzyme helps move long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria, where they are burned for energy. Attacks tend to follow prolonged exercise, fasting, illness or cold exposure rather than short bursts of effort.

Workup for these conditions can include genetic testing, specialized blood tests and, in some cases, a muscle biopsy. A confirmed diagnosis helps explain past episodes. It can also shape how a patient handles meals, illness and physical activity later on.

Returning to exercise after an episode is guided by CK levels and symptoms. The return is usually gradual, with intensity raised in steps rather than all at once. New muscle pain, weakness or dark urine during that period is a reason to stop and seek medical care.

10 workouts and conditions tied to exertional rhabdo, from spin to military

Exertional rhabdomyolysis workouts range from spin classes to military training; here are 10 workouts and conditions tied to the muscle injury and its warning signs

Exercise is supposed to break muscle down a little so it can rebuild stronger. Push the dose far enough past what the body is ready for, and the damage stops being a training signal. It becomes a medical problem that can reach the kidneys.

That condition is exertional rhabdomyolysis, often shortened to rhabdo. It is the breakdown of skeletal muscle fibers brought on by physical effort. When those fibers rupture, their contents spill into the bloodstream. Two of those contents matter most. Myoglobin is a protein that stores oxygen inside muscle cells. A heavy load of it in the blood can injure the kidneys. Creatine kinase, or CK, is an enzyme that doctors use to gauge how much muscle has been damaged.

The classic warning signs are severe muscle pain, weakness and dark urine the color of tea. Many patients do not show all three at once. Swelling in the affected muscles can also appear, along with soreness far beyond the usual post-workout ache.

Diagnosis rests on a blood test for CK. A level of roughly five times the upper limit of normal is a commonly used threshold for the condition. Treatment is medical care, centered mainly on intravenous fluids.

The risk does not come from any single sport. It builds from a familiar set of contributors: unaccustomed or extreme exertion, heavy eccentric work in which muscles lengthen under load, heat and dehydration. Sickle cell trait, certain drugs and supplements, and a recent illness can also raise the odds. Those factors show up across very different settings, from a boutique fitness studio to a military training base.

The slides that follow cover exertional rhabdomyolysis workouts and conditions — 10 settings where those factors can converge. Some are named formats, such as indoor cycling classes, CrossFit-style sessions and college football conditioning. Others are circumstances that can turn an ordinary routine into a risky one. Examples include a hard return after a long layoff and training in hot, humid weather. The list is a selection, not a ranking. It does not claim to cover every way the injury occurs, and its order says nothing about which activity causes the most cases.

Case reports in the medical literature describe exertional rhabdomyolysis after spin classes, mostly in beginners who rode hard in one of their first sessions. The common thread is an unconditioned rider working against heavy resistance at high effort.

Indoor cycling classes use stationary bikes with a weighted flywheel. Each rider controls the resistance with a knob or lever. An instructor sets the pace, often to music, and calls out climbs, sprints and standing intervals. That format lets a newcomer try to match regulars who have trained for months, even when the newcomer's legs are not ready for the load.

The thighs do most of the work on a spin bike, especially the quadriceps at the front of the leg. In published spin-related cases, thigh pain and swelling are the dominant complaints. That fits the mechanics of the exercise. Every pedal stroke loads the same muscle group, and a hard class strings together long efforts with little recovery between them.

The case reports do not establish how often this happens. No reliable incidence figure exists for spin classes. What the reports share is the profile of the rider. They mostly involve people new to the format rather than experienced cyclists who have built up tolerance over time.

The warning signs are easy to misread after a first class. Some soreness after any new workout is expected, so a rider may dismiss thigh pain that is far worse than usual. Pain severe enough to make walking or climbing stairs difficult is a reason to seek medical care. So are legs that swell or feel weak, or urine that turns dark after the ride.

For first-time riders, the risk described in these reports comes from the gap between conditioning and effort. Resistance is the variable a rider controls most directly. Keeping it lower in early classes and raising it over several sessions narrows that gap.

CrossFit-style workouts that pack very high repetition counts into a short window are a recognized setting for exertional rhabdomyolysis. CrossFit has acknowledged the risk through its own cartoon mascot, Uncle Rhabdo.

Uncle Rhabdo is drawn as a clown hooked up to a dialysis machine, with his kidneys and other organs spilling onto the floor. He came from CrossFit itself, not from outside critics. The image links the training style's intensity to the kidney damage that severe muscle breakdown can cause. A second clown, Pukie, represents the vomiting that can follow a hard session.

The danger in this format comes from density. A single session can call for dozens of repetitions of one movement, such as pull-ups, push-ups, burpees or wall-ball shots. Many workouts are scored on time. Others ask for as many rounds as possible, a format known as AMRAP, within a fixed window. Rest between rounds tends to be short or absent. Muscles pushed toward failure again and again in a compressed period accumulate damage faster than they can recover.

Newcomers are a particular concern. A person new to this style of training may try to match the pace or volume of experienced members in a group class. Their muscles have not adapted to that workload. Going all out from the first session skips the gradual build-up that conditioning normally provides.

CrossFit programming includes scaling, which means cutting the load, the repetitions or the difficulty of a movement to fit an athlete's current ability. Scaling lets a beginner follow the same workout structure at a lower dose. It is the format's own built-in tool for limiting how much new work a muscle takes on at once.

Published medical case reports have described rhabdo after CrossFit-style training. Those reports do not establish how often it happens. They do show the condition turning up in ordinary gyms, not only on military bases or at endurance events.

Early symptoms after a high-rep session can pass for normal soreness. The difference is degree. Pain, swelling or weakness far out of proportion to the workout calls for a doctor, not another training day. After a session heavy in pull-ups or push-ups, those symptoms may concentrate in the upper arms.

Other factors can compound the strain of a dense, high-volume workout. Dehydration, a recent illness and certain drugs or supplements are all recognized contributors.

Military training is a setting where exertional rhabdomyolysis is formally tracked. The U.S. Armed Forces Health Surveillance Division reports on cases among active-component service members in its Medical Surveillance Monthly Report, known as the MSMR.

The division draws those counts from the Defense Medical Surveillance System, a database of medical encounters across the services.

That surveillance shows the injury is not spread evenly across the force. The reports break cases down by service branch, sex, age and race. Rates differ across each of those groups.

Three conditions recur as drivers in military settings: heat, an abrupt ramp-up in training and repeated exertion without enough rest. Each one adds strain to muscle in a different way. Recruit training can combine all three in the same week.

Much military conditioning happens outdoors, often in uniform and gear. Heat raises the physical load of any given effort. When it is paired with dehydration, working muscle is under more stress than the same workout would put on it in cool conditions.

Recruits arrive from civilian life with a wide range of fitness levels. Basic training then puts them on a fixed schedule of daily physical training, marching and drills. For someone who was mostly inactive beforehand, that change in workload is abrupt. Muscle that has not adapted to a workload is more vulnerable to breakdown when it suddenly meets one.

The third driver is repetition across days. Damaged muscle fibers need time to recover. When a unit trains hard again before that recovery happens, damage from one session can add to damage from the next. A volume of work that a body tolerates once can become a problem when it is repeated without rest.

Recruits also have limited control over the pace. Training follows a unit schedule, so a trainee cannot simply skip a session to let sore muscles recover.

In basic training, soreness is part of the routine, which can make warning signs easier to dismiss. Pain far beyond what the day's training should cause is one warning sign. Weakness that makes ordinary tasks hard, such as climbing stairs or lifting a rifle, is another. Swelling or dark urine is also a reason to see medical staff.

In January 2011, 13 University of Iowa football players were hospitalized with rhabdomyolysis after a single offseason workout built around back squats. The episode is a well-documented example of exertional rhabdo striking several athletes on the same team at once.

The players involved were not beginners. They were Division I athletes on a Big Ten roster. Fitness built over months of training does not fully protect against a session that demands far more volume than the body has recently handled.

The back squat helps explain the scale of the injury. The lift loads the largest muscles in the body — the quadriceps, hamstrings and glutes — along with the lower back. When a workout piles many sets of a lift like that into one session, a large mass of muscle tissue absorbs damage at the same time. More damaged tissue means more myoglobin and creatine kinase spilling into the bloodstream.

Timing was the other factor. The workout came during the offseason, when players are not in the weekly rhythm of games and in-season practice. A sudden jump in volume after time away from heavy training is one of the clearest patterns behind exertional rhabdo, whatever an athlete's baseline fitness.

A team setting widens the reach of that risk. Everyone in a group session does the same work, at the same time, under the same coaching plan. A session that overwhelms one player can overwhelm many.

Warning signs can be easy to dismiss in football. Sore, heavy legs are routine after a squat day. The difference is degree: pain and swelling well beyond normal soreness, weakness that makes walking or climbing stairs difficult, or urine that turns dark. Those signs call for prompt medical evaluation rather than another day of training.

The Iowa hospitalizations show how a standard lift can become dangerous through dosage. The lesson concerns the size of the first heavy session after a break, not the choice of exercise.

Heavy eccentric training, in which a muscle lengthens while resisting a load, damages muscle fibers more than raising the same weight does. Slow negatives and high-volume lowering work are the clearest examples. They are a recognized route to exertional rhabdomyolysis when the dose outruns a lifter's conditioning.

Every repetition has two halves. The concentric phase shortens the muscle, as when the arms curl a dumbbell up. The eccentric phase lengthens it under tension, as when the dumbbell is lowered back down with control. Lowering into a squat, descending from a pull-up bar and running downhill all load muscles eccentrically.

Muscles can produce more force while lengthening than while shortening. That lets a lifter control a weight on the way down that they could not lift on the way up. Some programs use this through negatives, in which a partner helps raise a heavy load and the lifter lowers it alone. Other programs stretch the lowering phase to several seconds per repetition.

Eccentric contractions also cost less energy than concentric ones at the same load. A set can feel manageable, with less breathlessness and burn, while the muscle absorbs substantial mechanical strain. That gap between the effort a lifter feels and the damage the muscle takes makes eccentric-heavy sessions easy to misjudge.

The strain shows up as microscopic disruption inside the muscle fibers. In most cases the result is delayed onset muscle soreness, which typically builds over the following one to three days. Exercise physiologists use eccentric protocols in laboratory research because they reliably raise markers of muscle damage, including creatine kinase.

The line between ordinary soreness and rhabdo is a matter of degree. Pain far beyond normal stiffness points to a medical problem rather than a hard workout. So does a limb that swells or loses strength, or urine that turns dark. Blood markers of muscle damage after eccentric work can keep climbing for days. Symptoms that worsen instead of easing are a reason for a medical evaluation.

Muscles adapt to this kind of loading quickly. One eccentric session substantially reduces the damage caused by a similar session later, a phenomenon known as the repeated bout effect. The risk is concentrated in the first exposures, especially when volume or load is high from the start.

Long-distance races reliably push creatine kinase above normal, yet most finishers never develop clinical exertional rhabdomyolysis. Marathons and ultramarathons serve as a contrast case. They show that a high lab number and a dangerous muscle injury are not the same thing.

A marathon covers 26.2 miles, or 42.195 kilometers. An ultramarathon is any race longer than that. Common ultra distances include 50 kilometers, 100 kilometers and 100 miles. Hours of repeated foot strikes cause small-scale damage to muscle fibers over the whole course. That damage lets CK leak out of injured muscle cells. As a result, its level climbs in the blood after a race.

A raised CK reading on its own does not settle a diagnosis. Rhabdomyolysis as a clinical condition involves muscle breakdown heavy enough to cause symptoms. In serious cases, the myoglobin released from that muscle puts the kidneys at risk. Many runners with elevated post-race levels feel nothing worse than the stiff legs that follow any long effort. Serious cases among race finishers are uncommon.

Preparation explains much of the gap. Runners who build mileage over months condition their muscles to the specific stress of distance running. The protection that develops after an initial damaging session is known as the repeated-bout effect. A well-prepared marathoner reaches race day with muscles already adapted to hours of impact. That places a planned race in a different category from a workout the body has never done before.

Risk rises at the extremes. The longest ultramarathon distances carry more exposure than a standard marathon. Hot race-day weather and dehydration also raise the odds of tipping from ordinary soreness into a medical problem. Starting a race while recovering from a recent illness adds to the strain. So does taking certain drugs or supplements that affect muscle or kidney function.

The practical warning for distance runners is pain out of proportion to the effort. Legs that stay weak instead of loosening up over the following days are one signal. Urine that turns dark brown, like cola, is another. Either is a reason to get medical care promptly rather than rest at home and wait it out.

Returning to full-intensity training after weeks or months away is one of the best-established triggers for exertional rhabdomyolysis. The core problem is unaccustomed exertion. Muscles that have lost conditioning are asked to absorb a workload they once handled but no longer can.

A layoff can start in many ways. An injury, a surgery, a demanding stretch at work, a holiday season or an offseason can all pull a trained person away from the gym or the road. The reason for the break matters less than the loss of muscle tolerance that builds while it lasts.

Exercise physiology has a name for that tolerance. The repeated bout effect describes how one session of unfamiliar exercise protects muscle against damage from a similar session later on. Regular training keeps that protection in place. Time away lets it fade. Returning muscle ends up closer to the state of muscle that has never done the exercise at all.

Experienced athletes face a specific risk here. They tend to remember the weights, distances, paces and repetition counts they handled before the break. Picking up at those numbers on the first day back can cause far more muscle damage than the identical session would have caused at peak fitness.

Illness adds another layer. A recent illness is a recognized contributor to exertional rhabdomyolysis. When sickness was the reason for the layoff, the returning athlete may carry two risk factors into the same workout.

Telling ordinary soreness apart from muscle breakdown can be hard in the first day or two after a comeback session. Stiffness that is uncomfortable but manageable is expected. Pain far out of proportion to the workout, swollen or weak muscles and dark urine point to something more serious. Those signs call for medical evaluation.

The practical response is a gradual return. Starting well below previous loads and volumes, then building across several sessions, gives muscle time to readapt. That build-up restores the protective effect the break erased before the hardest efforts resume.

Hard exercise in heat and humidity raises the risk of exertional rhabdomyolysis because the body has to cool itself and fuel working muscle at the same time. Heat and dehydration are recognized contributors to the muscle breakdown, and they add to the strain of an overly demanding workout.

Sweat is the body's main cooling tool during exercise. It works when the moisture evaporates from the skin. Humid air slows that evaporation, so sweat drips off without carrying away much heat. As a result, core temperature climbs faster, even at a pace that would feel manageable on a cool, dry day.

The heart then faces competing demands. Blood is sent to the skin to shed heat, while working muscles need blood to deliver oxygen and clear waste. As fluid is lost through sweat, total blood volume shrinks. Less blood reaches the muscles and the kidneys, and the strain on both grows.

Dehydration matters twice in rhabdo. It makes muscle fibers more vulnerable during the workout. It also leaves the kidneys less able to clear the myoglobin that damaged muscle releases afterward. Concentrated urine and reduced kidney blood flow give that protein more opportunity to cause injury.

Heat-related muscle breakdown can also overlap with exertional heatstroke. Heatstroke is a medical emergency in which core body temperature rises to dangerous levels. Rhabdomyolysis is a known complication of heatstroke. Confusion or collapse during hard exercise in heat calls for emergency care rather than rest and fluids at home.

Heat acclimatization is the body's gradual adjustment to training in warm weather. Over repeated sessions, sweating begins earlier and plasma volume expands. Coaches and military trainers use a measure called wet-bulb globe temperature to decide when to scale back outdoor work. It accounts for humidity, sun and wind as well as air temperature.

Dark urine after a session in a heat wave or a humid gym is a reason to seek medical care. So is muscle pain or swelling out of proportion to the workout. A CK blood test can confirm whether muscle breakdown is under way.

High-intensity bootcamp and interval classes can lead to exertional rhabdomyolysis when a participant stacks repeated all-out efforts with little recovery. The format packs a large volume of hard muscular work into a short window.

Bootcamp sessions typically string together bodyweight drills such as burpees, jump squats, walking lunges, push-ups and mountain climbers. Many of those movements load the same muscle groups again and again. The thighs and glutes, for example, may absorb squats, lunges and jump landings within a few minutes of each other.

Jumping drills add a specific kind of strain. Each landing forces the quadriceps to lengthen while they brake the body's fall. That lengthening action, known as an eccentric contraction, is the type most closely tied to muscle damage. A class built around jump squats, box jumps or skater hops repeats it many times in a single session.

Interval formats compress effort further. The protocol associated with Japanese researcher Izumi Tabata calls for 20 seconds of maximal work followed by 10 seconds of rest, repeated for eight rounds. Tabata's original research used stationary bikes, not the mixed bodyweight circuits that now often carry the name. Many classes borrow that structure or similar work-to-rest ratios. Short rests limit how much the muscles recover between bouts.

The group setting adds a separate pressure. Participants follow the instructor's clock rather than their own condition. Some classes also display heart rates or scores on screens around the room. Keeping pace with a room of regulars can push a participant to work harder than their muscles are prepared for.

Ordinary soreness and an injury tend to follow different patterns. Delayed onset muscle soreness usually peaks one to three days after exercise and eases with gentle movement. Pain after a bootcamp class that keeps building needs medical evaluation. So does soreness that comes with swelling, marked weakness or dark urine.

Sickle cell trait is a recognized risk factor for exertional rhabdomyolysis during hard, sustained exercise. Athletes who carry it face greater danger when a session pushes them to maximal effort without enough rest between bouts.

The trait is not the same as sickle cell disease. A person with the trait inherited one copy of the gene for sickle hemoglobin and one copy for normal hemoglobin. Most carriers have no symptoms in daily life, and many do not know they have it. Someone with sickle cell disease inherited two affected copies and lives with a chronic illness.

The risk emerges under physical stress. During intense exertion, red blood cells in a carrier can change into a rigid, curved sickle shape. Those cells can clog small blood vessels in working muscle. Muscle deprived of oxygen-rich blood can then break down quickly. Heat, dehydration and the thinner air at high altitude can add to that strain.

In a carrier, the breakdown can surface during the workout itself rather than the next morning. An athlete may slump with sudden weakness or report muscle pain well beyond normal fatigue. Dark urine afterward is another signal. Any of these calls for prompt medical care.

College sports have taken up the issue. The NCAA has made sickle cell trait part of its athlete-screening and precaution policy. That brings the condition to the attention of team medical staff. Knowing an athlete's status lets coaches and trainers plan sessions with it in mind.

The common precautions follow the same logic that applies to other rhabdo risks. Intensity builds gradually across weeks rather than days. Longer rest between hard repetitions lets muscle recover before the next bout. Stopping a drill at the first sign of unusual weakness takes priority over finishing it.

The trait does not rule anyone out of sport. Carriers compete at every level, from high school to the professional ranks. Screening gives staff a chance to account for a known vulnerability, both in how a workout is designed and in how they respond to early symptoms.

A look at 10 medications and supplements that can cause rhabdomyolysis, from statins to red yeast rice, and the warning signs of this muscle injury

Some of the most commonly prescribed drugs in the U.S. and U.K. carry a label warning about rhabdomyolysis, a serious form of muscle injury. The same warning appears on the labels of an intravenous antibiotic, an anesthetic, a gout treatment and an HIV drug. A supplement sold without a prescription, red yeast rice, contains a compound chemically identical to a prescription statin. Knowing which products carry that risk, and why, helps patients recognize trouble early.

Rhabdomyolysis is the rapid breakdown of skeletal muscle. When muscle cells break apart, they release their contents into the bloodstream. Two of those substances matter most. Myoglobin is a protein that stores oxygen in muscle. In large amounts, it can injure the kidneys as they try to filter it out. Creatine kinase is an enzyme that doctors measure in blood tests to gauge how much muscle damage has occurred.

The usual warning signs are muscle pain, weakness and dark, tea-colored urine. The pain can be more widespread or severe than ordinary soreness after exercise. It can also appear without an obvious cause. Anyone who notices these symptoms while taking a drug linked to muscle injury should contact a clinician promptly.

Medications that can cause rhabdomyolysis do not all work the same way. Some damage muscle directly. Others raise risk mainly when combined with a second drug that slows how the body clears the first. Kidney impairment, older age and higher doses can also tip the balance. For several items on this list, the interaction matters more than either drug on its own.

These products raise the risk of muscle breakdown in some people, but most people who take them never develop the condition. A warning on a label describes a possibility, not an expectation. No one should stop a prescribed medication without first talking to a clinician. A prescriber can weigh the risk, adjust a dose or order a creatine kinase test if symptoms appear.

The 10 medications and supplements below are a selection, not a complete list. They range from cholesterol drugs and an HIV treatment to a sports supplement whose link to muscle injury remains unsettled. Each entry explains what the risk looks like and who faces it most. Where a product has an official label, the entry also notes what that label or the FDA states about it.

Every statin sold in the U.S. carries a label warning about myopathy and rhabdomyolysis. The risk is higher at larger doses, in older patients, in people with kidney impairment or an underactive thyroid, and when a statin is combined with certain other drugs.

Statins lower LDL cholesterol by blocking HMG-CoA reductase, an enzyme the liver uses to make cholesterol. The class includes atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, simvastatin, pravastatin, lovastatin, fluvastatin and pitavastatin. They are among the most widely prescribed medicines in the world.

The labels describe a range of muscle effects. Mild aches sit at one end. Myopathy, meaning muscle pain or weakness with raised creatine kinase, sits further along. Rhabdomyolysis sits at the severe end. Statin labels ask patients to report unexplained muscle pain, tenderness or weakness promptly. That instruction carries extra weight when the symptoms come with fever or a general sense of feeling unwell.

Dose matters, and simvastatin offers the clearest example. In 2011, the FDA restricted the 80 mg dose of simvastatin to people who had already taken it for 12 months or more without signs of muscle injury. The agency also advised against starting new patients at that dose.

Drug interactions are the other major driver. Simvastatin and lovastatin are broken down mainly by the liver enzyme CYP3A4. Drugs that strongly block that enzyme can push statin levels in the blood much higher. The labels for these two statins list the antibiotic clarithromycin, the antifungals itraconazole and ketoconazole, and HIV protease inhibitors among the drugs that should not be combined with them. The same labels warn against grapefruit juice, which inhibits the same enzyme.

Not every statin works through that pathway. Pravastatin and rosuvastatin rely far less on CYP3A4. Prescribers sometimes choose one of them when a patient needs a drug that would clash with simvastatin or lovastatin.

The labels spell out several of these patient factors in detail. The atorvastatin and simvastatin labels name age 65 or older as a risk factor. For severe kidney impairment, the rosuvastatin and simvastatin labels call for a lower starting dose. The rosuvastatin label also notes that patients of Asian descent can reach higher blood levels of the drug. It advises considering a 5 mg starting dose for those patients.

Pairing gemfibrozil with a statin raises the chance of muscle toxicity more than most other statin combinations. Gemfibrozil interferes with the way the body clears statins, so statin levels in the blood can climb higher than intended.

Fibrates are a class of drugs prescribed mainly to lower triglycerides, a type of fat in the blood. Gemfibrozil, sold under the brand name Lopid, is one of the older members of the class. Fenofibrate is the other main fibrate in use in the U.S. A fibrate is sometimes added to a statin for patients whose triglycerides stay high after their LDL cholesterol is under control.

The problem with gemfibrozil lies in drug metabolism. It blocks glucuronidation, a process that helps break down many statins. It also inhibits CYP2C8, an enzyme involved in clearing some of them. When those pathways slow, more statin circulates for longer. Higher statin exposure is a known driver of muscle injury.

This interaction has shaped regulatory decisions. Cerivastatin, sold as Baycol, was withdrawn from the U.S. market in 2001 after reports of fatal rhabdomyolysis. A number of those cases involved patients who were also taking gemfibrozil. The current simvastatin label lists gemfibrozil as contraindicated, meaning the two drugs should not be taken together.

Other statin labels take a similar line. The rosuvastatin label advises avoiding gemfibrozil and caps the rosuvastatin dose at 10 mg if the two are used together. The Lopid label itself advises that combining gemfibrozil with a statin should generally be avoided.

Fenofibrate is generally regarded as having a smaller interaction with statins. It does not interfere with statin clearance in the same way gemfibrozil does. That is why it is often the fibrate chosen when a combination is considered necessary. A smaller interaction is not the same as none. The pairing still calls for attention to muscle symptoms.

Fibrates carry their own risk as well. Their labels warn of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis even when the drug is taken without a statin. Kidney impairment adds to the concern. Fenofibrate dosing is adjusted for people whose kidney function is reduced.

Unexplained muscle aches or weakness on a fibrate and statin combination are worth raising with the prescriber before either drug is changed.

Prescription niacin carries a rhabdomyolysis warning when it is taken at 1 gram a day or more alongside a statin. The concern is tied to that high, cholesterol-lowering dose. It does not apply to the small amounts of niacin in food or a standard multivitamin.

Niacin is vitamin B3. At nutritional levels, it helps the body convert food into usable energy. The recommended dietary allowance for adults is 16 milligrams a day for men and 14 milligrams for women. That is far below the amounts used to treat cholesterol.

Doctors have long prescribed niacin in much larger amounts for cholesterol problems. At gram-level doses, it lowers LDL cholesterol and triglycerides. It also raises HDL cholesterol. Extended-release prescription versions, such as Niaspan, were developed for this lipid-lowering role.

The muscle risk appears when those doses overlap with statin therapy. Prescription niacin labels state that rhabdomyolysis has been reported when lipid-altering doses of 1 gram a day or more are combined with a statin. Patients on the pairing are told to report unexplained muscle pain, tenderness or weakness promptly.

The pairing has also lost regulatory support. In 2016, the FDA withdrew approval of Advicor and Simcor. Those products combined extended-release niacin with lovastatin and simvastatin in single tablets. The agency concluded that adding niacin to a statin had not been shown to reduce heart attacks or strokes. On that basis, it found the benefits no longer outweighed the risks.

Over-the-counter niacin sits in a separate category, but the line can blur. Some supplement bottles sell 500 milligram tablets. Two of those tablets reach the 1 gram mark that appears on prescription labels. A person taking a statin and a high-dose niacin supplement may land in the same territory the label warning describes, even without a niacin prescription.

The tolerable upper intake level for adults is 35 milligrams a day from supplements and fortified foods. That limit is set to avoid flushing, the warmth, redness and itching that high doses commonly cause. Flushing is the best-known niacin side effect, and it is distinct from muscle injury.

Anyone on a statin who also takes high-dose niacin, prescribed or bought off the shelf, can raise the combination with the prescribing clinician before changing either one.

Daptomycin is an intravenous antibiotic for serious skin and bloodstream infections, and its label warns of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis. The label tells clinicians to check creatine kinase levels weekly during treatment. It also advises them to consider pausing statin therapy while a patient receives the drug.

Daptomycin belongs to a class of antibiotics called cyclic lipopeptides. It is derived from the soil bacterium Streptomyces roseosporus. In the U.S. it is sold under the brand name Cubicin and in generic versions.

The drug works against gram-positive bacteria, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, known as MRSA. It is used for complicated skin and skin structure infections and for Staphylococcus aureus bloodstream infections. Patients receive it through a vein, typically once a day, in a hospital or through home infusion.

Daptomycin is not used for pneumonia. Lung surfactant, the substance that coats the inside of the air sacs, inactivates it.

Muscle injury is one of several warnings on the label. A blood test for creatine kinase, an enzyme released by damaged muscle cells, can show a rising level before muscle injury becomes severe. Weekly testing gives the treating team a running record across a course of therapy that can last several weeks.

The label also sets out when to stop. It advises discontinuing daptomycin in a patient who has unexplained muscle symptoms and a creatine kinase level above 1,000 units per liter. That is roughly five times the upper limit of normal. It also advises stopping in a patient with no symptoms whose level reaches 2,000 units per liter or more, about 10 times the upper limit of normal.

Some patients need closer watching than the weekly baseline. The label calls for more frequent creatine kinase checks in people with kidney impairment. The same applies to people who are taking a statin or took one shortly before starting daptomycin.

Statins carry their own muscle warnings. Taking one alongside daptomycin puts two drugs with the same type of risk in use at once.

Kidney function matters for a separate reason. The kidneys clear daptomycin from the body. For people with significantly reduced kidney function, the label calls for less frequent dosing.

The label also directs clinicians to tell patients to report muscle pain or weakness during treatment. A new symptom of that kind does not need to wait for the next scheduled blood test.

Colchicine, a drug for gout and familial Mediterranean fever, can damage muscles and nerves. That neuromuscular toxicity can progress to rhabdomyolysis. The danger climbs when the kidneys are impaired or when another drug, such as the antibiotic clarithromycin, slows the body's removal of colchicine.

Colchicine is a plant alkaloid originally drawn from the autumn crocus, Colchicum autumnale. It works by interfering with microtubules, the protein structures cells use to move and divide. That action calms the inflammatory response that drives a gout flare. The same mechanism explains why the drug can harm other tissues when levels in the body build too high.

Familial Mediterranean fever is an inherited condition marked by repeated bouts of fever and inflammation of the lining of the abdomen, chest or joints. People with it often take colchicine daily for years to prevent attacks. With use that long, correct dosing and careful attention to interactions become central to safety.

The muscle problem is tied closely to how much colchicine circulates in the blood. The kidneys and liver clear the drug. When kidney function is reduced, colchicine lingers longer and can accumulate. U.S. labeling calls for dose adjustments in patients with kidney or liver impairment for that reason.

Drug interactions follow the same logic. Clarithromycin blocks an enzyme called CYP3A4 and a transport protein called P-glycoprotein. Both help the body remove colchicine. Other drugs with similar blocking effects include the antifungal ketoconazole, the HIV drug ritonavir and the transplant drug cyclosporine. The colchicine label warns against combining it with strong inhibitors of this kind in people who already have kidney or liver impairment. Grapefruit juice also appears on the label's list of interacting substances.

A second category of risk comes from pairing colchicine with other medicines that can injure muscle on their own. The label names statins and fibrates among them, along with the heart drug digoxin. Each of those combinations calls for closer monitoring by the prescriber.

New muscle weakness or pain during colchicine treatment warrants a call to the prescriber. The label advises considering a temporary pause or a full stop in treatment when signs of neuromuscular toxicity appear.

Propofol, a widely used intravenous anesthetic and sedative, can cause rhabdomyolysis as part of a rare complication called propofol infusion syndrome. The risk is tied mainly to high doses given over a long period in intensive care, not to the brief doses used to put patients under for surgery.

Propofol is given through a vein and acts quickly. It comes as a white, milky lipid emulsion, which earned it the nickname "milk of amnesia" among clinicians. Anesthesiologists use it to start general anesthesia. It is also a common choice for procedural sedation during tests such as colonoscopy.

In intensive care units, the drug does a different job. Patients on mechanical ventilation often need continuous sedation for days, and propofol can run as a steady infusion. Its effects fade quickly once the drip stops. That lets clinicians lighten sedation to check a patient's neurological status.

Propofol infusion syndrome sits at the far end of that prolonged use. Muscle breakdown is only one part of it. The syndrome can also bring metabolic acidosis, a dangerous buildup of acid in the blood, along with heart problems such as abnormal rhythms and heart failure. The drug's U.S. label adds high potassium levels, raised blood fats, an enlarged liver and kidney failure to the list of features.

Those problems can compound one another. Kidney damage from the muscle breakdown leaves the body less able to clear potassium and acid from the blood. That can deepen the chemical imbalances already present. The exact mechanism that sets off the syndrome is not fully understood.

Detection in the ICU depends on lab work rather than symptoms. A sedated patient cannot report muscle aches or weakness. Clinicians instead track creatine kinase and acid-base balance through blood tests and keep the heart under continuous monitoring. Cases have been reported in both children and adults. The U.S. label states that propofol is not indicated for sedating children in intensive care.

When the syndrome is suspected, the usual response is to stop the infusion and switch to another sedative. Supportive care follows, and it can include dialysis.

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is a rare but serious reaction to antipsychotic medications, and it can lead to rhabdomyolysis. Its defining features are high fever, severe muscle rigidity and a change in mental status, such as confusion or reduced alertness.

The muscle damage comes from the syndrome itself. Sustained, intense rigidity and a dangerously high body temperature can break down skeletal muscle tissue. Blood tests during an episode therefore often show a sharp rise in creatine kinase, the enzyme that leaks out of injured muscle. When enough muscle breaks down, the kidneys face the same threat they face in any other case of rhabdomyolysis.

The reaction is tied to the way antipsychotics work. These drugs block dopamine receptors in the brain. Dopamine helps regulate both body temperature and muscle tone. The syndrome has been described with older, first-generation antipsychotics and with newer, second-generation ones. U.S. prescribing labels for antipsychotics carry a warning about it.

The syndrome also disrupts the autonomic nervous system, which runs functions people do not consciously control. Heavy sweating, a fast heart rate and swings in blood pressure can appear alongside the fever and stiffness. That cluster of signs helps clinicians separate it from other causes of fever and confusion.

Timing offers another clue. Cases often develop in the first days or weeks after someone starts an antipsychotic or has the dose raised. The reaction can also appear later. Dehydration and rapid dose increases are recognized risk factors.

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is a medical emergency, and it is treated in a hospital. Care usually centers on stopping the antipsychotic under medical supervision, cooling the body and giving intravenous fluids. The fluids also help protect the kidneys from the myoglobin that damaged muscle releases. In some cases, doctors add medications aimed at relaxing muscles or restoring dopamine activity.

Clinicians also have to rule out look-alike conditions, including serotonin syndrome and infections such as meningitis. For someone taking an antipsychotic, the combination of rigid muscles, high fever and altered alertness calls for urgent medical attention rather than a wait-and-see approach.

Raltegravir, an HIV medication sold under the brand name Isentress, lists rhabdomyolysis and myopathy among side effects reported after the drug reached the market. Its label also notes raised creatine kinase, the muscle enzyme clinicians measure to detect muscle damage.

Raltegravir belongs to a class of drugs called integrase inhibitors. These drugs block integrase, an enzyme HIV uses to insert its genetic material into the DNA of a person's cells. The FDA approved raltegravir in 2007. It was the first drug in that class cleared in the U.S. It is taken by mouth as part of a combination of antiretroviral drugs rather than on its own.

The muscle-related entries on the label come from two places. Creatine kinase elevations appeared in clinical trial participants who received raltegravir, according to the label. Rhabdomyolysis and myopathy appear in the postmarketing section. That section covers problems reported after approval, once a drug is in wider use. Reports of this kind are submitted voluntarily from a population of unknown size. For that reason, the label gives no frequency for these events. It also does not establish that raltegravir caused each one.

The label's practical guidance centers on drug combinations. It advises caution when raltegravir is given to people at higher risk of myopathy or rhabdomyolysis. That group includes people already taking other medications known to cause these conditions. Statins and fibrates, both used to lower cholesterol or other blood fats, fall into that category. Their own labels carry myopathy warnings. HIV treatment involves more than one drug, and many patients take medicines for other conditions as well. A complete medication list gives the prescriber what is needed to weigh that risk.

Anyone taking raltegravir who notices unexplained muscle pain, tenderness or weakness can raise it with the clinician managing their HIV care. Interrupting antiretroviral therapy without medical guidance carries its own risks.

Red yeast rice can raise the risk of muscle injury in much the same way a statin does. Its main active compound, monacolin K, is chemically identical to lovastatin, a prescription cholesterol drug.

The supplement starts as ordinary rice fermented with a mold called Monascus purpureus. Fermentation turns the grains a deep red. The same fermented rice has long served as a food coloring and flavoring in East Asian cooking. It also appears in traditional Chinese medicine.

In pharmacies and online stores, red yeast rice is usually sold in capsules marketed for cholesterol support. The body does not distinguish between monacolin K from a supplement bottle and lovastatin from a prescription. A label describing the product as natural does not change its chemistry.

The amount of monacolin K varies from one product to the next. One brand may contain very little of the compound. Another may contain far more. In the U.S., dietary supplements do not need FDA approval before they go on sale. A buyer often has no reliable way to know how much monacolin K a given bottle actually delivers. The FDA has warned consumers about red yeast rice products found to contain lovastatin.

That uncertainty matters most for people who already take a prescribed statin. Adding red yeast rice stacks a second statin-type compound on top of the first. The muscle-related risks of the two add together. Lovastatin's own label carries warnings about myopathy and rhabdomyolysis, and those warnings describe the same compound found in the supplement.

A separate concern has nothing to do with monacolin K. Some red yeast rice products have been found to contain citrinin. Citrinin is a toxin produced by molds, and it can harm the kidneys. That is a second hazard for the same organs that rhabdomyolysis puts under strain.

Anyone taking red yeast rice should list it alongside prescriptions when a doctor or pharmacist reviews their medications. Leaving it off can hide a doubled statin-type dose from the person checking for interactions.

Creatine has not been shown to cause rhabdomyolysis in healthy people. Case reports describe the muscle injury in people taking the supplement, but those reports do not establish that creatine itself was responsible.

Creatine is a compound the body makes on its own. It is produced mainly in the liver and kidneys from the amino acids glycine, arginine and methionine. Most of it is stored in skeletal muscle. It also comes from food, chiefly meat and fish.

As a supplement, creatine is most often sold as creatine monohydrate. Athletes and people who lift weights use it to support short bursts of high-intensity effort.

Unlike the prescription drugs on this list, creatine carries no FDA-approved label warning about muscle injury. In the U.S., it is sold as a dietary supplement. The FDA does not approve supplements for safety or effectiveness before they go on sale.

The name invites a mix-up. Creatine kinase, the enzyme used as a blood marker of muscle damage, shares part of its name with the supplement. The two are different substances. A high creatine kinase reading reflects injured muscle, not the amount of creatine someone has taken.

Creatine affects a second blood test in its own way. The body breaks it down into creatinine, a waste product that doctors measure to estimate kidney function. Regular supplement use can raise creatinine readings without any kidney damage. That rise can complicate how a kidney test is read in someone being checked for muscle injury.

The published case reports usually involve other factors. Strenuous exertion and dehydration often appear alongside the supplement in these cases. Intense exercise is itself a recognized cause of muscle breakdown, known as exertional rhabdomyolysis.

That overlap makes it hard to separate any effect of the supplement from the effects of the workout and the fluid loss around it. Someone who develops muscle injury after an extreme training session while taking creatine may have been injured by the training alone.

OpenAI Found 948 Linux Security Flaws—And Figured Out How to Exploit Them

OpenAI says its autonomous security agents discovered 948 distinct vulnerabilities in the Linux kernel, developed working exploits and drafted fixes for review—all for roughly $1.2 million in API credits.

“We chose Linux to study how effectively frontier models could find vulnerabilities and demonstrate their security impact in a codebase of this scale and importance,” OpenAI said in results published Wednesday.

The Linux kernel powers cloud servers, Android devices and much of the world's critical computing infrastructure. The flaws identified in OpenAI's experiment were local privilege-escalation vulnerabilities: An attacker would first need limited access to a vulnerable machine, but could then potentially obtain root privileges and take full control.

OpenAI's agents scanned about 2.8 million lines of kernel code, or roughly 7% of the project. “Our prototype identified and analyzed 2,308 candidate Linux kernel findings, dynamically reproduced 1,724 vulnerabilities, and produced 948 local privilege escalation (LPE) exploits that could allow an unprivileged local user to gain root access” OpenAI said.

After grouping exploits by their underlying cause and proposed fix, researchers counted 948 distinct flaws.

The October run cost $1,213,430 in API credits, equivalent to around $1,279 per vulnerability. OpenAI compared that with historical $20,000-to-$70,000 payouts for Linux privilege-escalation exploits at hacking competitions, suggesting a hypothetical value of $18.9 million to $66.3 million if the same results were found manually.

An earlier GPT-5.6 Sol campaign found 166 unique privilege-escalation vulnerabilities in July for $513,690. A newer, unreleased cyber model found more than five times as many in October, even after the kernel had undergone months of fixes.

OpenAI also said its agents used at least 10 exploitation techniques unfamiliar to the researchers and outside experts they consulted—not merely existing tricks retrieved from training data.

AI companies finding critical bugs are just another proof of how systems are capable of performing advanced cybersecurity research achieving results that previously required significant human expertise.

Last week, Anthropic disclosed that an unreleased version of Claude had discovered and exploited serious vulnerabilities in widely used software, including Chrome and Linux. Some of those exploits could execute malicious code remotely with little or no user interaction.

The economics of this research were already changing before OpenAI's announcement.

Researchers at Vidoc Security reproduced several vulnerability discoveries associated with Anthropic's powerful Mythos AI using publicly accessible models for less than $30 per file.

Those tests showed that finding vulnerabilities was becoming increasingly accessible, although reproducing a flaw is different from developing a reliable exploit capable of compromising a protected system.

OpenAI's latest experiment goes further by automating much of that exploitation process, creating a potential advantage for defenders trying to patch software before criminals can take advantage of the same weaknesses.

The company warned that attackers with limited access to an outdated operating system may increasingly be able to escalate their privileges with AI assistance.

OpenAI is attempting to restrict access to its more capable cybersecurity models through verified security programs. Its Daybreak Red initiative provides approved defenders with access to specialized AI tools for vulnerability research, exploit testing, and mitigation development.

New Accounting Seed Research Exposes the Hidden Workload Behind AI in Finance

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A survey of nearly 200 business and finance professionals at small and midsize companies finds fragmented data, constant output checks, and new governance work are severely limiting what AI delivers for the business

COLUMBIA, Md., Nov. 5, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Accounting Seed, the AI-native accounting platform built on Salesforce, released its latest research report, The Hidden Workload Behind AI Productivity in Finance. The survey found that 82% of finance team members at U.S. small and midsize companies would get more value from AI if their financial data were in one system.

Many businesses are expecting a leap in productivity from AI layered on top of financial systems that don't share data and lack the business context behind it. When the data underneath is scattered and stripped of meaning, AI inherits every gap, and finance teams are left to sort it out.

Scattered data and missing context leave finance teams cleaning up after AIAI is helping finance teams work faster, but it has also shifted new work onto them. When financial data is spread across disconnected tools, someone still has to pull the information together, decide which numbers to trust, and check AI's output before it can be used. Without access to a company's financial data, policies, and accounting practices, AI also lacks the context needed to provide insights that support strategic business decisions.

"When your financial data lives in six places, your AI has six places to be wrong," said Nasser Chanda, CEO of Accounting Seed. "You can give AI the skills of the smartest analyst in the world, but if it's working from scattered spreadsheets and three versions of the truth, someone still has to figure out which numbers to trust. That's the hidden workload. The next wave of productivity will come from AI that works from a single source of truth, understands how your business operates, and can deliver trustworthy strategic insights as a result."

Reducing the friction around AI in financeNearly all executives surveyed (98%) say they use AI in finance to increase their team's output, not reduce its size. But when employees spend their saved time preparing data, correcting results, and managing new governance requirements, that capacity never reaches the business.

Reducing that friction starts with the system underneath AI. When financial and operational data live in one system under one set of access controls, AI works from a single version of the truth, and teams spend less time cleaning up behind it.

"When accounting and operational data share one platform, AI can work with information across the business and understand the context behind it without teams having to piece it together first," said Ryan Sieve, CTO of Accounting Seed. "And when that access is governed by existing permissions and trust boundaries, companies can expand what AI does without creating a separate set of controls for every new tool."

The full report, The Hidden Workload Behind AI Productivity in Finance, includes findings across executives, finance leaders, finance team members, and Operations & IT professionals, showing where AI is delivering value, where it's creating new work, and why adding more AI tools won't fix a company's underlying data issues.
