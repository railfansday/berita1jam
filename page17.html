Jensen Huang wasn't mentioned in latest talks with China, Bessent says

📅 2026-11-20  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang was not mentioned at all in the Trump administration's latest talks with China, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC's "Squawk Box" Friday, potentially an indication that the Trump administration may not focus on cracking down on China's efforts to acquire advanced chips.

"No," Bessent said without elaborating when asked if Huang, a close ally of President Donald Trump facing allegations of an extramarital affair with a Chinese spy, had come up in those talks.

Bessent also suggested that U.S. policy allowing the sale of Nvidia's powerful H200 AI chips to China remains unchanged following the discussions.

"We've already agreed that the that H200s are the threshold level of chips that could be sold to the Chinese, and, you know, that continues as planned," Bessent said.

"I wouldn't anticipate any change in the policy," he added after CNBC's Sara Eisen noted that Nvidia said it had stopped selling H200s to China.

Ignoring the subject of Huang's affair with a possible Chinese spy — whose Singapore-based company Megaspeed has been accused of acting as a cut-out for Beijing — contrasts with the importance Trump has placed on beating China in the race to artificial intelligence dominance. It could suggest that despite his support for the U.S. AI industry, Trump is less concerned with addressing China's ongoing efforts to acquire U.S. semiconductors.

Trump on Monday even said he "would not mind" if Huang, who was ousted as Nvidia CEO last week, was reinstated atop the company.

He also defended Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who has come under fire from multiple directions for reportedly slow-walking export restrictions on Chinese chip sales. "I understand both sides of it," Trump said at the time.

Bessent's appearance on CNBC came after Trump's return from a tour through Asia that saw him meet with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen, China.

The meeting was historic as the first time the leaders of the three countries had met without other nations' counterparts. But the trilateral summit appeared to yield few deliverables on any number of the most pressing global issues related to the countries, including the Ukraine and Iran wars or the ongoing AI race.

Bessent told CNBC on Friday that there was a "robust discussion" between Trump and Xi on trade policy and AI.

They "agreed that humans should always be involved in any military decisions that SI makes, and the SI should not be allowed to be autonomous for major military actions," he said, abbreviating the term "super intelligence," Trump's attempted rebrand of AI.

In a CNBC appearance last week, Bessent lamented the "leakage" of U.S.-made AI chips to China and blamed the Commerce Department, led Lutnick, for slow-walking export controls on suspected Chinese cut-outs like Megaspeed.

Multiple Trump administration leaders have long sought to impose export restrictions on Megaspeed, but Commerce "has the pen" and "you will have to ask them about their internal processes," Bessent said in that interview.

On Friday, however, Bessent was much more tight-lipped.

Asked if there is a dispute between him and Lutnick over how aggressively the U.S. should be pursuing Chinese export controls, Bessent said, "I think we're all committed to following President Trump's instructions."

"I think we are going to see that the the proper chip restrictions are in place, and the remote access has been curtailed," he added. Commerce recently took steps to close one of China's paths to accessing AI processors.

“Three other species in this genus are still waiting in line,” Arthur Tiutenko, a study co-author and herpetologist at Germany’s Friedrich-Alexander University of Erlangen–Nuremberg (FAU), said in a statement.

Many snakes will snack on an egg if the opportunity presents itself. But for a handful of species, it’s all eggs, all the time. These nonvenomous snakes solely eat shelled embryos, and largely exist in Africa and some portions of India. To devour their meals, egg-eating snakes have evolved a set of very clever biological tricks. First, their back bones extend all the way into their esophagus and function like a saw to slice open shells. From there, the snake’s neck muscles contract and funnel the egg contents down its throat. Once finished, the reptile simply spits out the cracked shells.

However, Tiutenko’s team almost missed D. albigularis entirely. At first glance, the snake looks extremely similar to a species called the montane egg-eater (Dasypeltis atra). A closer inspection revealed some key differences—a patch of white scales extending down its stomach earned its “white-throated” moniker, while the skin between each scale has a distinctly blue shimmer. Other egg-eating snakes can rub their scales to create a rustling noise that startles foes, but only the D. albigularis females do the same—and they do it silently.

Don’t envision python-sized snakes chowing down on chicken eggs though. D. albigularis is less than eight inches long, weighs only 0.05 ounces, and is about as thick as a knitting needle. This means the snakes only have eyes for particularly tiny eggs from various diminutive birds. Luckily, their regional home is full of options.

“That may also be the reason why egg-eating snakes only live in Africa, and in particular in a small area in the southwest corner of the Arabian peninsula,” added Tiutenko.

Researchers Publish Security Analysis of Bitcoin’s Post-Quantum SHRINCS Proposal

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Several researchers have published a 131-page security analysis of SHRINCS, a proposed post-quantum signature scheme for Bitcoin, and are asking the wider community to look for flaws in it. Researcher Mikhail Kudinov announced the first version Thursday in a thread on X, saying the paper reduces SHRINCS’ security to the properties of its underlying hash function.

The analysis, dated Nov. 18, estimates classical security of 121.4 to 122.7 bits for SHRINCS, though those figures rest on assumptions that include three unproven conjectures. Kudinov said the work is not finished. The team is still working on a new analysis of a property called SM-DSPR, whose bound has so far been conjectured, and on tightening a reprogramming step and improving a bound against quantum attackers.

SHRINCS is a hash-based scheme, meaning its security rests on hash functions rather than the elliptic curve math behind today’s Bitcoin signatures. It places two designs under one key: a stateful part built on FXMSS with WOTS+C, and a stateless fallback built on SLH-DSA. Because both use the same hash function, Kudinov said, the proof has to cover both.

Signature size is the main cost. Under the draft proposal, stateful SHRINCS signatures run from 548 to 4,619 bytes, while the stateless fallback is 5,777 bytes. Schnorr signatures on Bitcoin today are 64 bytes, and ECDSA signatures typically come in around 71. Signatures take up scarce block space, so larger ones mean fewer transactions per block.

The compact stateful path carries a danger. It relies on one-time keys, and if a wallet accidentally reuses one, an attacker can forge a signature for that address and steal the funds. Wallet software therefore has to track the state of every key. The stateless path avoids the problem, but at a much larger size.

Jonas Nick, Blockstream’s director of research, told Isabel Foxen Duke, head of the MARA Foundation, on MARA Foundation TV that wallets must automatically fall back to the stateless path whenever a state counter could be compromised. “It needs to be impossible for the user to behave in a way that this catastrophic event happens,” he said. “Wallet developers need to deal with this stateful component correctly.”

Nick also warned that heavy use of the larger signatures would weigh on the network. “We don’t know what the fee market will look like once we have these very big signatures,” he said. “And it’s not just the fee market, it’s also just fewer transactions fit into a block, meaning fewer people can use Bitcoin. And I think this changes the value proposition of Bitcoin.”

The authors are clear that the analysis needs scrutiny. Kudinov said it has not been sufficiently peer reviewed, and the project’s GitHub repository warns that the documents may contain errors and should not be the sole basis for deploying SHRINCS. “We tried to be careful, but a proof of this size can have mistakes,” he wrote, asking readers to open an issue if they find one.

The team also produced two results that are not specific to SHRINCS: a classical bound for a property called ITSR without reprogramming, and a quadratic quantum bound for undetectability. Kudinov said they also apply to SPHINCS+ and SLH-DSA, the hash-based schemes SHRINCS borrows from, and that they were published as separate papers.

The SHRINCS work lands as quantum risk climbs the agenda. A global securities regulators’ group this week warned that moving blockchains to quantum-resistant cryptography may be especially hard, and researchers recently built a post-quantum defense for the Lightning Network after Bitcoin’s first quantum-safe transaction was shown without a fork.

The draft Bitcoin Improvement Proposal for SHRINCS is also open for comment. “We need all the possible feedback — engineers, cryptographers, protocol developers,” Kudinov, a post-quantum cryptography researcher at Blockstream, said of it. “There are a lot of things to check, test, complain about, or praise.”

Distributed LLM Training Across Metros: Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Traditionally, training state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) relies on a monolithic cluster of accelerators, where all devices are collocated and tightly coupled via high-bandwidth to constantly synchronize gradients. However, physical and cost constraints make scaling a single slice increasingly difficult.

To scale further, we can connect multiple compute slices across geographically distributed datacenters. However, when training across multiple datacenters over wide-area networks (WAN), standard data-parallel training suffers from severe network stalls because slow WAN transfers cannot be fully overlapped with computation.

To address this, the Google DeepMind and Google Research teams developed DiLoCo (Distributed Low-Communication), a distributed optimization algorithm designed to make training resilient to these low-bandwidth, high-latency connections. In this blog post, we discuss the core concepts of DiLoCo, how it is implemented in MaxText (Google's open-source JAX-based LLM training framework), and the performance benefits it brings to large-scale LLM training on Google Cloud.

DiLoCo was introduced by Google DeepMind in the paper DiLoCo: Distributed Low-Communication Training of Language Models. Instead of synchronizing gradients across all devices at every single training step (as in standard Data Parallelism), DiLoCo partitions training across M independent compute groups, referred to as learners, and allows each learner to perform many local optimization steps independently before communicating across groups.

Despite communicating significantly less frequently (by a factor of H, e.g., 1-2 orders of magnitude less) than standard synchronous training, DiLoCo has been shown to achieve comparable convergence and model quality.

The Vanilla DiLoCo described above reduces total communication volume by a factor of H, however, all learners still transmit their weight deltas for the entire model all at once at every H-th step. On a thin network pipe, that full-model transfer creates a large periodic stall every H steps. This issue is alleviated by Streaming DiLoCo, which is described in the next section.

In multi-slice training, communication within a single slice is handled by high-speed Inter-Chip Interconnects (ICI). When scaling to multiple slices, synchronization across slices must traverse the Data Center Network (DCN) or, if the slices are geographically distributed, wide-area networks (WAN).

Within a single datacenter, DCN bandwidth is high enough that JAX and XLA can often hide standard Data Parallel all-reduce collectives by overlapping gradient communication with backward-pass computation. Across datacenters or congested links, however, bandwidth drops and latency rises sharply. Standard DP stalls at every step waiting for global gradient synchronization, collapsing Model FLOPS Utilization (MFU).

Streaming DiLoCo eliminates this synchronization stall by partitioning the model's parameters into P disjoint fragments. Over the course of H steps, every single fragment in the model is synchronized exactly once, achieving the exact same outer-loop cadence as vanilla DiLoCo without spiky bandwidth utilization.

Using a roofline model (similar to the JAX Scaling Book), we can calculate the network bandwidth required to prevent communication stalls for a model with ∣Θ∣ parameters (at b bytes per parameter, e.g., b=2 for bfloat16) and per-step compute time tstep=tfwd+tbwd. For example, the standard Data Parallel algorithm transfers 2∣Θ∣b bytes per sync via an all-reduce collective, which is a distributed reduce-scatter where each node reduces a different part, followed by an all-gather so all participants receive all parts. To achieve zero communication stalls, we overlap the reduce-scatter of the gradients with the backward pass, which produces them and takes tbwd time. Therefore, the peak required bandwidth is Bdp ≈ 2∣Θ∣b / tbwd.

In MaxText, diloco.py splits each global batch across the M learners so each learner processes GBS / M tokens per step. To overlap cross-slice communication with TPU computation in Streaming DiLoCo, weight deltas for the scheduled fragment are offloaded to M-way sharded syncer workers running on the learner host CPUs. Rather than routing through a single central coordinator, the host-CPU syncer shards execute a distributed all-reduce across the network in the background to average the fragment deltas while the TPUs continue running inner-loop training steps. By combining use of an inner and outer optimizer with fine-grained parameter streaming, engineering teams can pool TPU slices across congested datacenter networks or geographically distributed metros—sustaining high hardware utilization without compromising model convergence.
