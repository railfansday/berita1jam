Boogie Fland listed as game-time decision for battle with Houston

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Florida basketball could be without one of its key backcourt contributors heading into its highly anticipated matchup against No. 8 Houston after guard Boogie Fland aggravated a right shoulder injury during the top-ranked Gators' 86-78 victory over Notre Dame on Tuesday.

Fland initially injured his shoulder against FSU in Tallahassee on Nov. 10. The Florida point guard would miss their next outing against Merrimack on Nov. 13.

According to Zach Abolverdi, Fland is now considered a game-time decision for Wednesday's showdown against the Cougars, leaving his availability uncertain as Florida prepares for a rematch of the 2025 national championship game.

"I think he just kind of reaggravated the same thing," head coach Todd Golden said after the game. "I don't think it's anything too serious, but obviously with it being the shoulder, it was just bothering him and (it's) something that's kind of hard to play through. So we'll check him out tonight, see how he looks and we'll make a decision in the morning to decide if he can play tomorrow or not."

Florida, now 5-0, meets Houston, who is also undefeated at 4-0, in the semifinals Wednesday at 4:30 p.m. ET on ESPN, in a rematch of the 2025 national championship game.

"I don't think (Fland's absence) affected us too negatively to be honest," Golden said. "I thought our guards did a great job. Generally, Boog's an organizer offensively, kind of keeps us in our stuff, and I thought Denzel Aberdeen and Urban Klavzar did a good job that way. ... I would say the one area that probably affected us was in the defensive end in the second half.

"He's obviously a fantastic defender, missed that a little bit, but obviously we want him out there and it's hard when we don't have him, but I thought our players stepped up and made it so it didn't affect us too negatively today."

Fland entered Tuesday's contest averaging 10.0 points and 3.7 assists over three games this season. If the Gators are without him against Houston, head coach Todd Golden said sixth man Urban Klavzar would move into the starting lineup alongside Denzel Aberdeen.

Golden previously turned to sophomore guard Alex Lloyd when Fland missed Florida's matchup against Merrimack. However, Klavzar has since strengthened his case for a starting role after delivering a career-high 24 points off the bench against Notre Dame, including three 3-pointers and a perfect 11-for-11 performance from the free-throw line.

"He started the second half and played great," Golden notes. "I think it's really good for our team from an organizational standpoint knowing that, around the 17- or 16-minute mark, that he's coming in and there's a lot of comfort for the four other players on the floor having him out there."

He also said, "I love having an incredible player in the sixth-man role. It's a huge value and advantage for us. But I think he could do just as good a job if he needs to start, which could be the case tomorrow."

Backpack’s US approval could threaten offshore exchanges’ grip on crypto futures trading

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The exchange plans to bring American and international traders into one regulated marketplace, potentially shifting liquidity and price discovery toward the US.

Crypto exchange Backpack has secured US regulatory approval to launch a futures exchange aimed at uniting American and international trading liquidity.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) designated Backpack's TLD Markets as a regulated futures exchange on Nov. 16, three days after approving its affiliated clearinghouse, TLD Clearing.

The approvals establish the regulatory foundation for Backpack to offer leveraged crypto perpetual futures to American traders while connecting them to the company's international trading operations.

However, Backpack's ambitions extend beyond entering the American derivatives market. The company wants to consolidate its global trading business under a single US-regulated exchange, eliminating the separation between domestic and overseas customers.

“There will be no Backpack international vs Backpack USA. Our entire platform will be operating on our CFTC regulated venue.”

The move represents a significant shift for a company that began in Chicago nearly four years ago before expanding its operations overseas.

Ferrante recalled that Backpack was “forced offshore” early in its development, prompting the company to establish operations across international markets while pursuing regulatory approval.

He described the CFTC approval as the culmination of nearly four years of work to return to the United States.

The proposed structure could reshape how American traders access cryptocurrency derivatives, particularly perpetual futures, which allow investors to maintain leveraged positions without a fixed expiration date.

Historically, regulatory restrictions have separated American crypto derivatives markets from much of the trading activity concentrated on offshore exchanges.

This fragmentation can limit available liquidity, widen the difference between buying and selling prices, and increase the cost of executing large transactions.

Backpack wants to address this by bringing eligible American and international traders into a shared order book, allowing their buy and sell orders to interact within the same regulated marketplace.

Guardrail para seguradora

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 18 • Originally published at g.cloud

Guardrail para seguradora é um mecanismo técnico de governança de IA que impõe restrições operacionais e éticas em sistemas de inteligência artificial usados no setor, garantindo conformidade com as diretrizes da Superintendência de Seguros Privados (SUSEP) — especialmente nas áreas de subscrição, sinistros e atendimento ao cliente. Não há norma específica sobre guardrails na regulamentação atual, mas sua adoção é alinhada às boas práticas de gestão de riscos exigidas pela SUSEP.

Um guardrail é uma camada técnica de contenção que atua antes, durante e após a execução de um modelo de IA. No setor segurador, ele impede que sistemas automatizados tomem decisões contrárias à política de subscrição, apliquem critérios discriminatórios em avaliações de sinistro ou gerem respostas enganosas em canais de atendimento. Diferente de um filtro pós-processamento, um guardrail bem projetado opera em tempo real e é integrado ao fluxo de dados — por exemplo, bloqueando entradas com dados sensíveis não autorizados ou forçando revisão humana quando a confiança do modelo cai abaixo de 85%.

A SUSEP não usa o termo “guardrail”, mas exige mecanismos equivalentes por meio da Circular nº 695/2024, que obriga as seguradoras a instituir políticas de governança de tecnologias emergentes. O art. 5º exige “controles técnicos e procedimentais capazes de assegurar a integridade, confiabilidade e auditabilidade dos sistemas automatizados”. Isso inclui validação contínua de modelos, monitoramento de desvio de desempenho e mitigação de viés — funções centrais de qualquer guardrail robusto.

Comece com mapeamento de riscos por processo: subscrição (viés em scoring), sinistros (decisões inconsistentes com o contrato) e atendimento (geração de informações incorretas). Em seguida, adote camadas complementares: input validation (ex.: rejeição de CPF inválido ou dados biométricos não consentidos), model constraints (ex.: limitação de variáveis sensíveis no treinamento), e output moderation (ex.: verificação de conformidade com cláusulas padrão da SUSEP antes de emitir resposta). Ferramentas como IBM Granite podem ser configuradas com custom guardrails via RAG e prompt engineering controlado — desde que documentados e auditáveis.

Q: Existe penalidade específica para falha em guardrails?

Muse privacy invasions are freaking out Meta staffers, internal chats show

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Erratic Muse outputs sharing random bank, Bitcoin balances aren’t real, Meta says.

After some early adopters of Meta’s Muse AI agent complained that it was spying on private messages and behaving unpredictably earlier this fall, Reuters exposed internal chats showing that Meta staff had raised the same alarms.

When testing the agent, staff warned that Muse appeared to be accessing encrypted private messages that users did not expect the bot to review, could be mixing up conversations between users, and even potentially sharing sensitive information users would never expect to be exposed.

Some unexpected Muse outputs responded to questions that staffers never asked.

“Hmmm,” one Meta staffer wrote in an instance where Muse sent the definition of “money laundering” unbidden. “Got a random ping from Muse about something I definitely did not ask for… .”

In at least one case, Muse told a staffer surprised by a similarly out-of-context offer to message a seller about a coffee table that it had “pulled in some context about a coffee table pickup that doesn’t seem to be yours.”

According to Reuters, that employee flagged the troubling output, writing, “this was ‘not my conversation’” and “expressing concern about a ‘possible cross-account context leak.’”

Raising the most concern were random outputs that appeared to share private user data. For example, a perplexed Meta staffer chatting with Muse about fishing got a random extra response, seemingly sharing balances from two unknown bank accounts. That output said, “BTW, two bank updates, your Capital One balance is $2722.58, your Charles Schwab balance is $5899.57.”

At least one staffer said they lost money testing Muse, Reuters reported. That staffer reported that Muse “accidentally rolled me into a premium insurance plan” for their dog that charged them $888 per month instead of their prior rate of $200. “Couldn’t recover,” the staffer said.

Meta’s spokesperson, Daniel Roberts, told Reuters that these were “isolated incidents,” and internally employees were told that “the agent appeared to be hallucinating messages in response to built-in prompts pushing it to engage with users in between sessions.” In those cases, an AI team member said the hallucinations were “unfortunately” biased “towards the most uncomfortable topics, like finances/security/safety, which sucks.” In some cases, instead of bank details, Muse hallucinated Bitcoin balances, the team member said.

A Meta spokesperson told Ars that “employee testing is core to the product development process and the entire point is to get feedback, so we can implement safety and privacy protections and improve products. Importantly, employees have access to different versions of Muse than the public, so they’re often reviewing features, functionality, and behavior not available elsewhere.”

Speaking to Reuters, Roberts reiterated that “many of the messages flagged by employees as potential account mix-ups were hallucinations” that Meta had already addressed.

But Roberts declined to respond to Reuters’ questions on the prevalence or persistence of the issues the AI team member discussed. And Reuters’ report emphasized that the outlet independently was “unable to establish whether Muse was giving users other people’s bank account balances and other sensitive data, or making up the information.”

Meta started receiving privacy-related complaints from Muse users in September. An Inc. Magazine reporter, Jason Aten, was shocked when it became clear that Muse was digging through private messages on his Mac without permission, reporting that he instead “explicitly” chose to deny Muse that access. As Meta delayed responding, the agent claimed it was simply parsing text in push notifications, but Aten found that “wasn’t true.” Rather, the agent was syncing messages from a local database, which Aten said was activated without his knowledge.

Eventually, a Meta spokesperson, David Singleton, responded to Aten on social media. Singleton insisted that Aten must have opted into granting “macOS system-level Full Disk Access” with the “Messages connector” enabled, if Muse was reading his private messages.

But Aten said the response wasn’t good enough.

“Email me, David,” Aten responded. “Full Disk Access off. Muse synced 187k lines from my messages chat [database]. Something’s not great. Also, that Full Disk Access doesn’t say anything about your message database, and I specifically gave Muse a goal that had nothing to do with messages, so it had no business doing that even if I had given it permission.”

Around the same time, Singleton responded to another user warning that his Muse appeared to be hacked. In that case, the user said that Muse tried to access an email service after claiming the user asked it to.

“I didn’t even open the app today at all,” the user wrote, vowing to delete the app and report it to Apple. “This is crazy.”

In a lengthy response providing more technical details, Singleton confirmed that Meta had received “a few reports” of such cases “where people thought their Muse was hallucinating or leaking other people’s data.” As he explained, the issues arose after Meta deployed an update giving Muse instructions on “how to engage with the user and ask them for confirmation before taking specific actions.”

It seems those instructions “landed on every message, not just messages pertaining to those specific actions, so its impact was wider than intended,” Singleton said. Also: “the way this instruction was provided to the model was done in a way that the model was not expecting (i.e. was not trained to expect).”

“We investigated with urgency and determined that this was caused by a bug introduced in the product that caused the model to hallucinate and was NOT a security or privacy issue,” Singleton wrote. “The bug has now been fixed.”

Aten warned that even though you can’t rely on Muse to explain its own behavior, the agent’s response coupled with Meta’s did little to rebuild trust after the shocking intrusion into his private messages.

“If we grant Singleton’s premise that I managed to unknowingly click something that enabled this capability, I still think that’s really bad,” Aten wrote. “You should not design your system in a way that people end up surprised by this kind of thing.” And if you do, you should not make “user control the central pitch” for marketing Muse, Aten suggested.

“If an AI agent is going to read my private messages, there should be a moment when it clearly asks me whether that’s okay,” Aten wrote.

Since Muse launched in September, it has been downloaded more than 10 million times, tapping Meta’s vast user network to quickly become one of the most-used AI agent tools.

But staffers warned Meta that users may be blindsided by some actions the agents take, depending on the operating system the app is connected to. For example, one staffer was surprised to find that “Muse was set up by default with permission to access push notifications on phones with Google’s Android operating system.” That, too, gave access to private message preview text the staffer had not intended for the agent’s review.

Meta says that Muse users can trust the company to keep their data private. It also warns users that Muse can make mistakes, while building into its terms that users are responsible for actions taken by Muse (like maybe buying things you don’t want or making doctor’s appointments you don’t need). The tech giant said it’s continuously updating user protections, conducting internal testing, and prioritizing user safety.

However, Reuters worked with staff at Signal and the AI Now Institute to test if they could replicate that Meta staffer’s and others’ Muse experiences. On Android devices, they found that Muse could read Signal messages by bypassing Meta’s “Connector” tool and instead relying on permissions granted during initial setup.

On iPhone, requests were blocked, Reuters reported. But on Apple’s macOS, Muse repurposed “settings Apple offers to help people with disabilities operate their computers” to retrieve messages, including screenshot tools.

That testing “indicated the issue described by the Meta employees was ongoing and could be affecting others,” Reuters reported. Overall, Meta seemed to be interpreting permissions from Muse users “broadly,” Reuters concluded, in ways that privacy advocates and regulators may disagree with.

Signal President Meredith Whittaker told Reuters that Meta launched Muse “without showing any respect for basic privacy or cybersecurity.” She demanded that Meta make changes to give app developers like Signal “consistent technical ways” to “mark their applications as off-limits to AI agents,” Reuters reported.

Part of that change would require operating systems to enforce rules that app developers set to rope off AI agents from sensitive data, Whittaker suggested. And following Reuters’ reporting, both Apple and Google are already weighing potential changes to “give people more control over what they authorize with AI agents.”

Apple, in particular, seemed disturbed by how Muse may be manipulating accessibility tools for people with disabilities. In October, Apple had already announced changes coming in response to users complaining about Muse accessing private messages without their knowledge or explicit consent. At that time, Apple concluded that “some developers were using the Full Disk Access feature in ways that could put users at risk.” And they promised to “introduce additional controls to ensure that users who genuinely wish to grant an app this extraordinary level of access can only do so with very explicit user action.”

“We are committed to ensuring users understand exactly what they are allowing, so they can make informed choices about their data and privacy,” Apple said.

Our Go-To Beginner Running Shoe Got Mixed Reviews—Here’s Why the Brooks Ghost 18 Is Still Worth Considering

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Fit concerns divided our testers, but here’s why the shoe still shines when you find the right width.

We earn a commission for products purchased through some links in this article. Why Trust Us?

Our usual go-to shoe recommendation for new runners has always been the Brooks Ghost. It’s the ideal trainer to choose when you’re overwhelmed with all the offerings out there, unsure where to start or which shoe is right for you. The Ghost was a part of the quintessential runner starter pack:

But this year was different. When we covered the Ghost 18’s release in May, our staff had already logged a few weeks of mileage in the shoe. And the verdict was... mixed.

It’s been six months since that initial testing, and during that time, the brand’s cushier model, the Brooks Glycerin 23, surpassed the Ghost in terms of popularity and positive feedback. What it all came down to was fit.

When we evaluate running shoes, we rate key attributes like weight, flexibility, and durability. Predictably, the Glycerin 23 scored a lower overall average score in weight compared to the Ghost 18 (the former is tenths of an ounce heavier than the Ghost). Other features between the shoes were relatively similar, except when it came to comfort. The Glycerin 23 received high scores, whereas the Ghost 18’s comfort was rated high by men and very low by women. The keyword here was “crammed.”

“I had no cramming of my toes,” said Glycerin 23 tester Maura Dalek. “My feet had plenty of room to move around in and kept my feet dry and cool.”

Juxtapose this with Ghost 18 tester and Deputy Editor Mallory Creveling’s comment: “The length was great; the width not so much...[My] forefoot area [felt] crammed the more I ran.”

It wasn’t just women testers who felt this version of the Ghost ran narrower than previous iterations. Art Director John Hamilton said he would have felt comfier in a wider fit. Despite his discomfort, he still gave the Ghost 18 a high rating.

Like eating mushrooms or drinking a martini neat, sometimes running in a shoe is an acquired taste. While it’s not the advice I’d give to runners shopping for running shoes (“You’ll eventually love it. Really!”), it’s a pickle we sometimes allow ourselves to fall into: a model you’ve always liked and the newest one doesn’t feel the same, but you’re going to stick it out anyway.

So I asked testers what they thought of the Ghost 18 months later. Creveling said she gave the shoe several chances, and had maybe two pain-free runs in them.

But here’s where our testing is limited: The Ghost 18 is available in several widths, including narrow, wide, and extra wide. Our samples come in the standard width (B for women, D for men), and our testing pool includes runners of various foot shapes. Fit varies across brands, and yes, even from model to model.

Here’s one piece of gear advice I can’t emphasize enough (people in the back, do you hear me?): It’s important to try on a shoe and go for a test run in it first before making a commitment. Another underrated piece of advice is getting your foot measured to see if you need a narrower or wider width. Your feet change over time due to aging, life events, and gravity.

One tester who had no issue with the shoe’s fit had this to say: “High [score] overall for the latest edition of the Ghost. As an owner of previous Ghost models, this one is a worthy successor. Everything that makes the Ghost great as a daily trainer is present here—stability, durability, great on wet roads or loose gravel.” And on the fit? “Plenty of room in the toebox...Super comfortable.”

Runner-in-Chief Jeff Dengate was also satisfied with the Ghost 18’s comfort. “Brooks continues to nail the fit,” he said. “It’s one of the reasons I love their shoes—no matter how a model evolves, I know I’m going to get a good wrap. Even in the Ghost, which is a little more spartan for padding and luxury, it still wraps the foot well and feels good no matter how far I run.”

He is referring to the familiar touch points we know and love about Brooks shoes: a plush heel collar that fits securely around your ankle, a flat ribbed tongue that looks like the cuffs on a cozy knit sweater.

Though the shoe doesn’t pack as powerful a punch with DNA Loft v3 foam (nitrogen-infused EVA) compared to shoes with TPEE or PEBA, it’s a reliable mile muncher, making it worthy for logging those everyday workouts when you’re building toward a race or just getting started with running.

At $150, the Ghost 18 is comparable to the $145 New Balance Ellipse. If you want a slightly heavier, cushier daily trainer that costs $175, go with the Glycerin 23. If you want a true Goldilocks shoe that’s slightly lighter with a generous amount of cushioning, go with the Ghost 18. Just consider your width options before buying!
