‘Glee’ Cocreator: ‘Sadness’ Over Cory Monteith & Naya Rivera’s Deaths Will ‘Be a Part of’ Show Reboot

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

"We're still figuring a lot of it out," Ian Brennan said in an interview of the reboot rumored to include Lea Michele.

Gleeks may have been delighted when Glee‘s official social media channels announced on Oct. 21 that the musical dramedy would be returning for a reboot. Despite the good news for fans, show cocreator Ian Brennan warned that it’s not going to be all good times when the show returns.

“You’re catching these characters at a totally different part of their life,” Brennan told Variety in an interview published Thursday (Nov. 19). “The timing of these characters thinking, ‘Maybe that was the best time in my life, being in that choir in high school’ and then once you think that you’re like, ‘Oh, that show’s going to work.’”

What’s more, two core characters are obviously missing, something that would unsurprisingly play a part in the characters’ lives in the reboot. Star Cory Monteith, who portrayed Finn, died at age 31 in July 2013, while the show was between seasons. Glee would go on to pay tribute to the actor in the third episode of season five, which included the character’s death, though not a cause of death. (Monteith died after mixing alcohol and drugs, according to the B.C. Coroner’s report at the time.)

Naya Rivera, who played Santana, died in an accidental drowning in July 2020 at age 33, five years after Glee wrapped with its sixth and final season in 2015. The actress died while trying to prevent her son, Josey, from drowning while the two were boating on Lake Piru in California. The little boy, who was 4 at the time, was found safe.

“We’re still figuring a lot of it out,” Brennan told Variety of the reboot and two of the stars’ deaths. “That’s going to be part of it, that sadness and that loss. But still in all, I think [Glee] is too big of a thing to sort of let lay. There’s something about that feels right right now.”

While the TV producer believes it’s a good time to bring the show back, he admitted to the publication that he didn’t always feel that way about a potential reboot. “I wasn’t that excited about it,” he said, noting that whenever the topic came up, he’d wonder why it should even happen. “For whatever reason, in the last six months or so, I completely did a 180 about it. It just seems the idea is right. I think the time is right. Now I’m really excited about it. It’s going to be a lot of fun.”

Show creator Ryan Murphy had been teasing a potential reboot this year, even telling Entertainment Tonight in September that he had just finished the script. He shared at the time: “Brad [Falchuk], Ian [Brennan] and I, who wrote all the first two seasons, have written something.”

After the reboot received a series order by Disney+ and Hulu, reports that certain cast members were already in talks to return began to surface. Among them was Lea Michele, who portrayed Rachel Berry, though her return is not yet officially confirmed. Chris Colfer, who played Kurt Hummel, shared during an interview that he was in talks to return, while Harry Shum Jr. (Mike Chang) — who currently stars as Dr. Kwan on Grey’s Anatomy — left it a bit vague when he spoke to Entertainment Weekly in late October during his stint on Dancing With the Stars, saying he was “really, really busy.” However, the actor added, “But I would love to keep continuing because I love [Glee].”

Also among the popular Fox show’s stars — who also landed on Billboard‘s charts collectively as the Glee cast, including an impressive 207 songs on the Billboard Hot 100 — were Jane Lynch as Sue Sylvester, Matthew Morrison as Will Schuester, Kevin McHale as Artie Abrams and Darren Criss as Blaine Anderson.

Situated between flashbacks and flash-forwards (not entirely unlike Yellowjackets), mystery/thriller The Wilds sees an airplane full of teenage girls crash on the way to an empowerment program in Hawaii. It quickly becomes clear that the accident was engineered and that the whole thing is some sort of social experiment, and the survivors are forced to compete against each other if they want to live. The show understands the ways in which young women are exploited and expected to compete against each other in the real world, and the ways in which we'll do just about anything when survival is on the line. Stream The Wilds on Prime Video.

There's even more going on, as the show shifts back and forth between 1993, 1994, and 1995 to slowly reveal the missing pieces. Season two takes on a different story set in 1999 and 2000, and the forthcoming (surprise) third season will shake things up again. The emphasis here is on mystery, but it's a show about the aftereffects of trauma and of the dark secrets that can tear families and communities apart. Stream Cruel Summer on Hulu.

Juicy import Elite follows a group of working-class friends who earn scholarships to Las Encinas, a fictional private school that is, in the show’s universe, the most exclusive in Spain. What they find there is snobbery, mystery, murder, and lots and lots of sex (between and among characters of various sexual orientations and numerical groupings). The smart, wonderfully trashy show ran for a shocking (in our modern streaming era) eight seasons, with a couple of regional remakes. There's no supernatural stuff here, but plenty of dark deeds done with youthful vigor. Stream Elite on Netflix.

A teenager, Mae (Madeleine Arthur), is discovered by the side of the road with weird religious symbols carved into her skin. In Sammy Baloji’s Hands, ‘Objective’ Materials Reveal New Truths About African History

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In the mid-2000s, after he completed his master’s degree in information sciences and communication at the University of Lubumbashi, Sammy Baloji started working with the French Institute. The director of the institute, also based in Lubumbashi, was interested in colonial architecture and cultural heritage history, and he collaborated with academics researching those topics.

Baloji’s work for the French Institute mainly entailed photographing events and later working with researchers from France. But he was already on the way to becoming the artist he is today. Related Articles Actor Says He Removed His Mother's Art from Venice Biennale Installation as a Pro-Israel Protest French Artist Claude Lévêque Ordered to Stand Trial for Rape and Sexual Assault of Minors

Working at the French Institute, he recalled in an interview with ARTnews, “really shifted my perspective, in terms of outlining photography or research, because I was interested in the way that [the researchers] were addressing the city or buildings or cultural heritage from a historical and objective perspective. I was mainly interested in people’s experiences, the testimonies of people who are living [in Lubumbashi like] myself.”

The two opposite approaches opened up the question of how he could “use the so-called scientific objective materials and bring them close to the human experience, to the architecture of local sites.”

The inquiry led the artist to make one of his first significant projects, Mémoire (2006), a photography collage series of colonial-era black and white archival images and colored prints showing contemporary Lubumbashi in the Katanga province, which is rich in mineral resources including copper, uranium, lithium, diamonds and cobalt.

Baloji’s work for the French Institute had allowed him to observe the social, environmental and political impact of mining in Katanga. Now, using a combination of photography and research, he was able to underscore how Belgian colonization had shaped the Democratic Republic of Congo, whose history of extraction he has continued to explore.

Works continuing to build on the themes of Mémoire can currently be seen in two high-profile showings. Baloji is making his debut US solo with “When Extraction Becomes Form It Speaks In Borrowed Tongues,” at the Wexner Center for the Arts in Columbus, Ohio, on view through February 14, 2027. The exhibition explores how Europe’s decorative arts and architecture still include elements of Congo’s colonialist history and the extraction of natural resources. It takes a critical look at the impact on land, people and cultural forms, speaking to complicated relationships between Central Africa, Europe, and the Americas.

Concurrently with the Wexner Center show, Baloji’s work can be seen at the Venice Biennale, both in the main show, curated by the late Koyo Kouoh, and in the national pavilion of the Democratic Republic of Congo. In the main exhibition at Venice, Baloji is showing three sculptures and 16 collage prints. Two of the sculptures reference works from a 19th-century Belgian colonial museum and the national collection built in the 1970s by the former President of the Democratic Republic of Congo, Mobutu Sese Seko.

“I started to realize that the colonial system was not just about controlling territory but also occupying societies and imposing new rules,” said Baloji, who splits his time between Brussels and Lubumbashi.

In the Wexner show, Baloji is exhibiting Suspension (Debris of History, Matters of Memory), a commission conceived with architect Gloria Cabral and in collaboration with art historian Cécile Fromont. Drawing from the design of historical Kongo cloth, the suspended large-scale sculpture is made of mining and construction waste, the latter from recent work at the Wexner Center. Meanwhile, a new tapestry called Rethreaded Indies, made by Baloji and Fromont, addresses the French Old Indies tapestry series (1637–1730), produced at the royal Gobelins manufactory in Paris, connecting that with Kongolese weaving. The works use textile and architecture to correct misinformation about African histories, showing how European colonization has shaped the continent’s narratives, often for the worse.

“I would say my interest in his work is how he weaves these histories together, and in a way that I find extremely compelling,” said Julieta González, the head of visual arts at the Wexner Center and the curator of the artist’s exhibition. Baloji’s work, she added, “is important because it weaves together so many historical events and situations but with materiality that speaks to the particular situation he’s critiquing, which is the legacies of colonialism” in the Democratic Republic of Congo. The show she curated, she said, offers the public “an insight into [the artist’s] very research-oriented practice and how it unfolds in so many different mediums [which includes] video, sculpture and photography.”

Baloji was born in 1978 in Lubumbashi and grew up with, in some sense, limited knowledge about the history of Belgium’s colonization of the Democratic Republic of Congo. (Connecting that and resource extraction in Katanga with global or political history came later following reports of Congo supplying Iran with uranium, which the Central African country denied.) In secondary school, Baloji cofounded a collective that read and shared books from libraries and schools that they had access to. He was also drawing at the time, producing some of his first artworks.

His parents did not encourage his plan to study at the Fine Art Academy, so for his master’s degree, he studied information sciences and communication, which happened to align with the artistic practices he was interested in. Ironically, or maybe coincidentally, the course Baloji pursued to appease his parents so he could make his own career choices ended up being formative to his practice.

Baloji is also known for more than just his art. In 2008, he cofounded the Lubumbashi-based Picha Art and Research Center and the Lubumbashi Biennale, whose ninth edition, cocurated by Congolese-Belgian curator Sorana Munsya and Ethiopian-Eritrean-Italian artist Jermay Michael Gabriel, opened in October. The art space and biennial are platforms that help expand access to the arts and support local artists with needed infrastructure, especially because of limited state support.

Camille Ostermann, an independent art consultant who worked on Congo’s national pavilion at the Venice Biennale, said, “I’ve admired [Baloji’s] collaborative practice [since the early 2010s]: works co-authored with historians, exhibitions built around artists he invites. But it was at the Lubumbashi Biennale, which Picha organizes, that I saw this spirit at work. In a city shaped by mining, by what is extracted and carried elsewhere, Picha does the opposite: it builds, gathers, gives back. Many artists told me their paths had been changed by Picha and Sammy’s mentorship. Like Ibrahim Mahama in Tamale or Yinka Shonibare in Lagos, Sammy works internationally while staying rooted in Lubumbashi, deliberately keeping the two connected: what he builds abroad can open doors for others at home.”

Picha Center and the Lubumbashi Biennale are also, in some ways, an extension of Baloji’s research and practice. The biennial, he said, can start conversations about extractivism and ecological crisis, as well as talk about “the impact of international and economic interests on the environment and societies.” It was a statement that could easily have been applied to many of his artworks.

When It's Always All About "Me"

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

We’re living in the age of “me.” We see most things through the lens of “self”: How’s this experience going for me, and how am I doing? What does this mean for and about me? How’s this person perceiving me? Does my “me” like this or not? Our culture teaches us to be the protagonist of our life, make something of ourselves, be “somebody”—to actualize and optimize ourselves. We should be exceptional, the “star” of our own story, hero of our own journey.

From the moment we can gaze into a mirror, those around us start saying, “That’s you!” “Look at you!” “There you are!” We start being recognized (and celebrated) as a “me,” a “someone” who’s her own unique identity, the one doing this life. A few years later, we learn to advocate for and protect that “me,” snatching toys from other little people, saying “That’s mine,” “Give me my…” We build a sense of possession, of being a “me” who has things or doesn’t.

Soon after (and for the rest of our life), we take on the project of that “me” and start working on behalf of our “me.” We become our own protector, PR rep, agent, and biographer, the narrator of our self-story—the story of “me.”

So too, the message we learn is that this “me” must be moving forward in life, continually progressing towards some better future version of ourselves, a new-and-improved, happier-and-better “me.” We can never rest because we must continue stacking up evidence of our advancement. As we are and where we are, we’re not enough—our “me” still hasn’t done enough. There’s some place, or so we’re taught, at the end of all our progress, where we will have progressed far enough and become enough to make the project of “me” complete, and set it down. Then we’ll be able to experience life directly without our “me” in the way.

We don’t just nurse at the bottle of progress; we also feed on the narrative of materialism, which offers us a system of values and ideas about what “counts” as progress. As the narrative goes, money, success, power, and lots of shiny things will earn us the elusive enough-ness we seek, and the right to stop focusing on our progress and the project of “me.” The problem is that this promised place of enough-ness, where we can finally stop protecting ourselves and progressing on our hero’s journey, never comes. No matter how much we achieve and prove about our worth, enough-ness eludes us, so we must continue pursuing the advancement of our individual “me.”

The self-help market is built around this same premise: that the individual is a project to be continually improved. The message is that our “me” needs to be fixed, or at the very least, needs to be better than she is. We’re supposed to be living more authentically, manifesting the life we deserve, putting ourselves first, distinguishing ourselves, and always becoming our best self (which is definitely not this self).

Self-help teaches us to relentlessly focus on our own experience and how we’re doing in every moment—how our life is going, and, most importantly, how the project of “me” is going. We learn to gauge and evaluate the present moment by whether it gives us what we want and need, whether it’s “good” for us. While paying attention to our own experience and how the present moment is affecting us is profoundly important and necessary for a healthy person, as is the ability to honor one’s own wants and needs, the problem is that this self we’ve learned to place at the center of our attention and indeed existence becomes the operating system through which we experience all of life. We don’t experience life directly, what’s actually happening; we experience what our life says about us, which we also invent and create in our mind.

Does this situation, interaction, or opinion make me feel good about me? Do I feel safe, secure, liked, important as a result of it? Does it support my self-image, the story I tell myself about myself? Am I getting what I need to shore up the version of myself I need to function? We encounter, experience, and react to life through a self-referencing lens, as a reflection of and commentary on who we are.

At the same time, our self-focus is motivated by our most basic instinct: survival. On an evolutionary level, we’re always trying to protect our existence as a species—to stay alive, continue being. Long ago, when we lived in the forest, we had to focus on threats: other animals that might eat us or our offspring, branches that could land on our heads, a dark sky that meant our nest would be flooded. Seeing reality through the lens of how it would impact our survival—through the lens of “me” and mine has deep biological roots.

And indeed, statistically, it made more sense to pay attention to what could kill us than what could delight us, like finding a great berry to eat. This now translates into “negativity bias,” our tendency to focus on what’s negative over what’s positive. But threats in modern times are generally not branches or other animals, but rather threats to our ego, self-esteem, and the grand story of who we are. In everyday life, it’s the psychological front on which we now feel continually endangered. It’s our “me” that has to constantly be on the lookout for danger.

All this to say, we have many good reasons to put ourselves at the center of the universe, and to see life through a personal lens of what it means for and about “me.”

The irony, however, is that our constant self-focus and the effort we put into shoring ourselves up, protecting and proving our self, promoting the “story” of our self, is actually an attempt to make ourselves feel happy and secure. And yet, it ends up making us feel unhappy and insecure. In the system we’ve designed, the relentless defense and hyper-vigilance to the self’s survival can never end. But in fact, it can… we just need to step out of the system.

In part 2, I’ll illuminate the subtle and not-so-subtle suffering that our “me” lens creates. I will also propose a radical solution. Spoiler alert: it will go against everything you’ve ever learned.

Silicon Valley’s reaction to the Nvidia drama is rife with blind spots

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Hello again, and welcome back to Fast Company’s Plugged In.

Jensen Huang was scheduled to speak at this week’s Semafor Silicon Valley & The World conference, which he cochaired. For reasons too obvious to require explanation, he didn’t make it to the event. And yet he dominated the on- and off-stage conversation so resoundingly that it might as well have been titled “Silicon Valley, The World & Jensen Huang.”

As tech notables sat for interviews—among them Sam Altman, Clay Bavor, Jeff Bezos, Andy Jassy, Yann LeCun, Fei-Fei Li, and David Sacks—moderators kept requesting their take on Huang’s shocking ouster from Nvidia. Most lavished praise on his entrepreneurship, but begged off saying much more on the grounds that the circumstances remained murky. Bezos’s comments were typical: “I don’t know enough of the details and I think probably most of us don’t know. . . . What I can tell you for sure is that Jensen is a remarkable guy who founded and built a remarkable company.”

Speakers at Semafor’s event approached the subject more gingerly than many in the scrum on X where Huang declared on Tuesday that “if the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO.” Skimming the 58,000 replies to his post, I didn’t see much discussion of the specifics of the case: Huang’s confessing to an extramarital affair with an Nvidia business partner who was allegedly also a spy for China, the questions that raised relating to Nvidia chips possibly being smuggled into China, and reports that he had two additional affairs with Nvidia employees.

Instead, Huang’s X followers mostly had his back. Tech industry celebs such as Aaron Levie, CEO of Box; Palmer Luckey, cofounder of Anduril; and Linda Yaccarino, former CEO of X, shared a few words (or emojis) of support. Others were civilians who expressed their enthusiasm for Huang’s theoretical rehiring in the form of truly weird AI slop: Jensen as Jesus at the Last Supper, Jensen popping out of a casket, Jensen lip-syncing Michael Jordan’s comments upon his return to the Chicago Bulls.

Despite reports of disagreement among Nvidia’s board members, the company continues to rule out Huang’s return. Given the unfolding legal and geopolitical elements of the story, a speedy Sam Altman-style reinstatement would appear to be fantasy in even the rosiest of scenarios.

The revelations thus far have already prompted the Trump administration to tighten restrictions on chip exports to China. Huang has agreed to testify before the Senate Banking Committee on December 1. Democrats, who will regain control of the Senate and House of Representatives in January, are salivating at the opportunity to dig into questions such as whether Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick facilitated the diversion of Nvidia chips to China. And California’s attorney general has subpoenaed Nvidia in search of any evidence that Huang’s affair led to chips or confidential information making their way to China.

Bottom line: Everyone who says we don’t know enough to come to a firm conclusion about the precise nature of what happened and its appropriate repercussions is on far firmer ground than those who think Huang was unfairly forced out and should be welcomed back.

But for all the miasma of uncertainty clouding this drama, it’s already told us plenty about Silicon Valley. Its most admired, envied, seemingly essential CEO being shown the door, even as the company he cofounded is still booming, is a deeply unsettling black swan event, and I haven’t seen many in the industry unblinkingly confront its implications. It’s surely not a coincidence that Gina Raimondo, the former U.S. commerce secretary—who is a technocrat, but not a creature of the Valley—had the bluntest assessment of the situation I heard at the Semafor conference: “Any kind of corruption, politics, dealing with spies, [there should be] no tolerance for it.”

Paul Graham, cofounder of Y Combinator, coined the term “founder mode” in 2024. It celebrates startup founders who refuse to cede control to professional managers, regardless of how big their brainchildren become. Decades before the concept had a name, Huang exemplified it. Instead of growing more remote from the minutiae of the company’s operations as it became a behemoth, he had his hands in everything—which is how he wound up with a reported 60 executives answering directly to him.

Over Huang’s 33 years as Nvidia’s CEO, he took risks that no MBA-brandishing suit would have dared. None was gutsier than his 2006 decision to pivot from focusing on 3D graphics to building a high-performance general-purpose computing platform. That prescience paid off when artificial intelligence became an epoch-shifting opportunity, leading to the company’s 90% market share in the chips that power generative AI.

In recent years, as Nvidia has jockeyed for the title of the world’s most valuable company, Huang has been no less daring. Whatever you think of the AI industry’s “circular financing,” in which many players take financial stakes in their own customers, Nvidia’s willingness to write huge checks to companies in its own ecosystem has been critical to AI’s growth. And as expectations for the technology prop up the stock market, it’s not just the tech business that might look very different without Huang’s high-stakes gambling. So might the entire U.S. economy.

Huang the public figure has become as unique as Huang the fearless entrepreneur. Once an unassuming engineer in an Nvidia shirt who went by his birth name of Jen-Hsun Huang, he reinvented himself into the genially bombastic, leather jacket-wearing rock star I interviewed in 2024. Only Altman rivaled his omnipresence as a speaker at tech industry events. Huang, who relished engaging in stunts such as frolicking with robots onstage, was a lot more fun. I can understand why people who never met him take his downfall personally.

If you can’t envision a tech industry without Huang at its core, you have every incentive to wish for any possible outcome that would reinstate him to his previous eminence. But Huang himself took risks that made a happy ending far less likely.

All evidence indicates that his initial strategy for dealing with the mess he created involved misleading Nvidia’s management and board, not to mention members of the press. Huang’s undisclosed relationships seem to have violated Nvidia’s own code of conduct. I have seen many people argue that they should still be forgiven. But to my knowledge, nobody has explicitly maintained that a CEO lying to his board is tolerable, at least if that CEO is Jensen Huang. Maybe that’s because the case is impossible to make.

Another bad bet Huang made was his tight relationship with President Trump, which he was flaunting as recently as September. It apparently influenced the president to dismiss an October 2025 intelligence briefing on Huang’s relationship with Megaspeed’s Alice (no relation) Huang. Trump may not have been doing Huang any favors, given that the story blew up in both of their faces at the worst conceivable time, after the Democrats’ wins in the midterm elections.

As Daring Fireball’s John Gruber noted this week, tech executives have historically steered clear of hugging any political party so close that it would complicate their relationships in Washington, D.C., should the balance of power shift. Huang abandoned this approach during Trump’s second term. So did other executives, but only Elon Musk may suffer graver consequences.

Huang’s unplanned departure also left Nvidia scrambling to deal with a fundamental bug in the logic of founder’s mode. Any company that’s lucky enough to matter for decades will, at some point, be run by a nonfounder—maybe even a dreaded professional manager. Responsible founders, no matter how hands on, should prepare for that day. Many do not.

Along with everyone else in the Valley, Huang apparently couldn’t picture a post-Jensen Nvidia. I hope he was kidding when he said he’d like to run the company for another 30 or 40 years and then be replaced by a robotic version of himself. But his failure to establish any clarity around a succession plan is a deadly serious problem for the company he founded. (His daughter, son, and son-in-law all work at the company, but not in roles that leave any of them as an heir apparent.)

Nobody else in tech ever had a career like Jensen Huang. His present quandary is part of that story, but I don’t expect it to be his only legacy. He might even rebound in some manner that doesn’t involve him getting his old gig back. Assuming the mystery surrounding this chapter eventually subsides, I hope the facts of the matter leave the industry wiser—if sadder—about aspects of its culture that it usually does its best to ignore.

Shrek 2 Remastered Fails To Capture The Magic Of Its Source Material

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

When I first heard the announcement for Shrek 2 Remastered, I couldn’t wait to get my hands on it. As I’m sure many of you can relate to, movie tie-in games were a major part of my childhood. While the rest of my family rented their favorite films from Blockbuster, I always made a beeline for the games section, where I eventually discovered one of the best licensed games from the early 2000s, Shrek 2.

To this day, I’m still a major sucker for licensed games, so revisiting a modern version of a classic wasn’t even a question. Now that I’ve played it, however, I’m left wondering if a PS5 port of the original would’ve been a better option. While this is a remaster, not a full remake, playing it back-to-back with The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (2026) has been a jarring and eye-opening experience that highlights the right and wrong ways to bring back a beloved game from the past.

While there are plenty of games that benefit from the traditional remaster treatment of updating visuals while leaving gameplay mostly unchanged, Shrek 2 likely would’ve been better off with the reverse.

Despite their age, Shrek 2’s original graphics had their own charm, whereas improvements to character responsiveness could’ve transformed the game into a great experience for both new and returning players. Instead, Shrek 2 Remastered retains the same frustrating controls while the visuals have become something unsettling at best and downright nightmarish at worst.

Little Red, also known as Lil’ Red, was almost entirely left out of the marketing for Shrek 2 Remastered, to the point that many believed she’d been removed entirely. Now it’s a bit clearer why that decision might’ve been made. Most of the characters have a disturbing look to them now, but Lil’ Red’s makeover is certainly the most terrifying.

Characters' softer features and movie-accurate appearances have been replaced by lifeless eyes, intense contrast, and enhanced details, leaving them almost unrecognizable compared to their appearances in the 2004 release and the movie it’s based on. I'm also left wondering why so much effort was put into redesigning characters when the cutscenes haven't been formatted to fill the screen and are often cut off before they're finished.

Though the visuals were certainly disappointing, I might’ve been able to overlook them if they'd been accompanied by new gameplay, but that remains unchanged. Most stages feature four characters that a solo player or a group of players can switch between to complete the required tasks.

Nearly every character is needed over the course of a single level due to their unique abilities, preventing one player from steamrolling others through the game. However, it also means solo players will be forced to use characters with less favorable controls.

Aside from Lil’ Red, who is actually somewhat enjoyable to play, thanks to her ranged apple throws and more fluid movements, most characters have limited attack ranges and feel stiff and unresponsive. Additionally, you’ll likely find yourself swapping characters more often than necessary because trailing characters (usually Shrek) frequently get stuck on obstacles and can’t free themselves.

Perhaps the one thing still saving this remaster from being a complete disappointment for me is that I’m playing local co-op with my partner, which has been fun simply because the game is so unforgiving. On more than one occasion, we’ve had to repeat a level multiple times to the point of delirious laughter. It’s not difficult, per se, but the lack of lives and poor controls make it brutal nonetheless.

Unfortunately, all the aforementioned issues make it difficult to imagine new players getting much enjoyment out of this remaster. Some older fans may be able to overlook the subpar gameplay in favor of nostalgia and the chance to play the game again, but I am finding it difficult. I’m grateful to have the opportunity to experience it on modern consoles with my partner, but that’s where my enjoyment ends.

If you’re a new player curious about the hype surrounding the original, this remaster isn’t the way to discover it, and I’d recommend skipping it. On the other hand, if you’re a returning player eager to revisit the game and have no way to play the 2004 version, this will scratch the itch. If you do opt to play, however, I’d insist on playing multiplayer. Shrek 2 has always been better as a shared experience, but that’s especially true now in Shrek 2 Remastered.

Players do need to sign in to their Epic account to access multiplayer, which has been a major point of frustration for those playing on Steam in particular, so keep that in mind.

Overall, I’m walking away from Shrek 2 Remastered heartbroken. Had it received the full remake treatment with refined mechanics and a visual overhaul that enhanced rather than stripped away its original charm, this could have been an incredible experience for everyone to enjoy together. As it stands currently, however, I’m just left wishing I had a copy of the original game.

The One Thing About GTA 6's Ending to Know Before You Get Too Far In

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

All of your actions have consequences in Leonida

While playing through Grand Theft Auto 6, you might notice that there are a lot of systems that can alter your experience. There’s the relationship system, your criminal profile, fitness routines that change your physique, and plenty more that gives you a lot of control over Jason and Lucia. In the moment-to-moment gameplay, it can be a little hard to figure out how much of that actually has an impact on the game.

Be warned, though: every choice you make really, really matters. GTA 6 features multiple endings that will be shaped by how much you engage with its systems. And it never makes it clear that those choices matter.

[Ed. note: This article contains spoilers for GTA 6’s ending.]

While I won’t reveal how my playthrough ended, I will note that I was faced with a binary choice that could have pushed me toward two different endings. GTA 6 has a few branching narrative moments like this where players are presented with two choices, so that lined up with the rest of the game. In my mind, I assumed that the game only had two endings. That was very incorrect.

That revelation came when Polygon spoke with Rockstar North co-head Rob Nelson ahead of GTA 6’s release. I had just rolled credits on the game, and Nelson was eager to learn which ending I got. I told him, and made a comment about the binary choice. He revealed that there were actually several possible endings with varying degrees of difference. Nelson said that some of them are small, maybe changing a bit of dialogue in a scene.

Others are more drastic. The climactic moment of my ending took place on a specific bridge on the map. Nelson said that some endings don’t go there at all. And while I’ve yet to see every ending play out (nor do I even know how many endings there are), I’ve already gotten the sense that the fates of certain characters can even change from ending to ending. If someone tries to spoil exactly what happens in GTA 6’s ending for you, know that you might end up getting surprised when something completely different occurs in your playthrough.

So, what determines which ending you get? Nelson told me that a few different factors go into it. The most obvious one is the choices that you make during branching story moments. You can also probably guess that the relationship system plays into it too. Your criminal profile is a factor as well, so even your choice of using lethal force in various situations can make a difference. On top of that, your ending can also change based on how much money you’ve stashed into your family savings account by the time you hit the credits. Needless to say, no decision goes without consequence in GTA 6.

If you’re stressing out about getting the “bad” ending by accident, don’t worry too much. Nelson noted that there aren’t exactly good or bad endings in GTA 6. Without spoiling where my story went, I can see where he’s coming from. The ending I got made perfect sense with the way I had been playing. And even within that moment, I still had a choice as to how it could play out. So long as you’re making deliberate choices in GTA 6 and not just ignoring systems for the heck of it, you should hopefully land on an ending that matches your playstyle. But keep all this in mind if you decide to ignore Jason and Lucia’s relationship. You’ll feel that eventually.
