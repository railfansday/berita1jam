Kenyan doomsday cult leader given life sentence for murdering 191 people

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Paul Mackenzie's followers believed he was "Jesus's mouthpiece", the judge said last month

A Kenyan court has sentenced the leader of a doomsday cult, Paul Mackenzie, to 200 years in prison for murdering 191 people, all but one of them children.

Alongside 28 others, Mackenzie was found guilty of the murders for instructing members of his cult to starve their children to death so they could "meet Jesus".

Justice Diana Mochache said Mackenzie bore the "primary criminal culpability" for ordering his followers to fast to death, in one of the world's deadliest episodes of cult activity.

"The sentence is appropriate and just, to send an unmistakable message that such atrocities warrant the maximum penalties allowed by law," the judge added.

The self-styled pastor was arrested in 2023 after the discovery of more than 400 bodies in shallow graves in Shakahola forest near the Kenyan coast. The sprawling investigation into the cases shocked Kenya and drew international attention.

The court sentenced Mackenzie to 100 years in prison for homicide and another 100 years for offences related to starvation.

The sentences within each category will run concurrently, but the two sets will run consecutively, bringing his total prison term to 200 years.

Mackenzie also faces separate manslaughter and terrorism charges, which he denies.

Prosecutors had sought the death penalty, arguing that the scale and circumstances of the killings warranted the harshest punishment.

However, Justice Mochache declined to impose the death penalty, despite ruling that the case met the threshold for its consideration.

"Nevertheless, the court declines the invitation to impose the death penalty on any of the victims," she said, adding that capital punishment would eliminate any prospect of rehabilitation and disengagement. The death sentence has not been carried out in Kenya since 1987.

Mackenzie was tried alongside 28 accomplices, including guards who enforced his fasting orders and gravediggers who helped conceal the victims' bodies in mass graves.

They received varying prison sentences of up to 160 years.

Justice Mochache described the killings as "cold and calculated", saying the perpetrators had shown no remorse and had even declared their willingness to fast to death themselves.

Before the hearing began on Friday morning, Francis Wanje, who lost eight relatives including two grandchildren in Shakahola forest, told the BBC's Newsday programme that he was "hoping for... a sentence which will match his [Mackenzie's] deeds in the forest".

Mackenzie and the co-accused had asked for leniency from the court.

Before their conviction on 19 October, they had all pleaded not guilty and repeatedly insisted that nobody was forced to die.

More than 400 bodies were dug up in Shakahola forest

Mackenzie's followers believed he was "Jesus's mouthpiece", Justice Mochache said as she delivered her eight-hour judgement last month.

Those who opposed Mackenzie's teachings were beaten to death.

One of his co-accused said he had told his followers that anyone who "resisted the directive to fast must be forced to go to heaven through kicks and blows".

Burials inside the forest where the cult was based were called "weddings" and those who died "were celebrated as having wedded with Jesus" the judge said.

The judge found that the deaths arose from an organised system, including religious teachings and directives that compelled followers to follow the deadly fasting.

Post-mortem results showed the cause of deaths to be starvation for most victims, while others died from suffocation, strangulation or head injuries.

The cause of death could not be determined for some, with their bodies having decomposed, while others died soon after their rescue as they were so emaciated.

The Shakahola deaths have shone a spotlight on the lack of oversight of rogue religious activities and how they could have carried on for so long.

The gravity of the case prompted President William Ruto to set up a commission of inquiry, although this was later declared unconstitutional.

Investigations by the state-funded Kenya National Commission on Human Rights (KNCHR) revealed that local administration and law enforcement officials had repeatedly turned a blind eye to the cult's activities.

"We expected at that time the police would take action immediately to go and rescue the other members there, but it didn't happen," said Wanje, who reported the case to the police in March 2023.

Wanje said a relative of his in the police force and a former cult participant had managed to save his grandson at the time. The child told the police what had happened and showed them the grave of his siblings.

"It was [supposed to be] his last day.. they [would have killed] my grandson."

The cult leader had previously been arrested and later released for lack of evidence.

Mackenzie established Good News International Ministries in 2003.

Over the next 16 years, the church grew in popularity, drawing thousands of followers in its branches across Kenya.

The church ran a television station called Times TV which would broadcast his sermons from his church in Malindi.

His End Days sermons included conspiracy theories that described education as "evil", healthcare as "against the doctrine" and digital identity cards as "the mark of the devil".

The main church shut down in 2019 and Mackenzie moved to Shakahola forest, encouraging his devotees to follow him.

While the conclusion of the murder cases may bring a sense of justice for the victims and their families, the convictions only account for 191 victims, while more than 400 bodies were exhumed from the forest.

The Pacers are getting a sobering reminder of how fragile NBA success can be

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

It is tempting to hand-waive everything that has happened to the Indiana Pacers in the last year and a half as simply the work of some vengeful spirit, unfairly punishing an innocent fanbase who never did anything to deserve this kind of sustained injury catastrophe and lottery luck that would sink any franchise, in Indiana or on the Moon. And hand-waive we shall. But waive enough hands, and the cumulative effect of this bad luck will start slipping through our fingers unnoticed, like grains of sand piling up into a castle the size of the Gainbridge Fieldhouse, threatening to crush us all if we don't make infrastructure improvements.

I was out on the Pacers coming into the year, though I never found the time to publish that take anywhere preseason with the NFL kicking off and the MLB headed for the World Series and then World War III. — you'll just have to trust my editors that I've been talking about it. My concern lay on the fact that a 50-32 Pacers team in 2024-25 had ridden one of the most absurd stretches of clutch shot-making ever to Game 7 of the NBA Finals, lost Tyrese Haliburton to a torn Achilles in one of the most heart-wrenching injuries in NBA history, lost said Game 7, lost Myles Turner to some Bucks stupidity and then went 19-63 (yes, that is nineteen and sixty-three) the following year with a roster I thought … was not that bad.

Every Pacers take I heard last year from people in NBA media more connected than I started and ended with the same two words: "gap year." This 19-63 record was, of course, intentional, as the team tanked for a top draft pick to play when Haliburton was back the following season. But then they made a splashy move, wagering that draft pick on a bold 50-50 with the Clippers for Ivica Zubac, protecting that pick in a specific way that meant if it fell out of the top four, they would lose it. Well … it did, and their 19-63 record netted them nothing in one of the most stacked top fours we've ever seen. Once again: heartbreaking.

Then the actual 2026 season rolled around, and their roster looked solid; at the very least, it would be competitive, if not actually a Finals contender in my mind. And it was going reasonably well for about seven minutes until Haliburton suffered a hamstring injury (like are you serious right now) and the rest of the team got injured along with him. Now 3-6, last week's lineups would make you think they were tanking again.

Their actual players will slowly make it back in time, but the question must officially be asked (no, not "is this team injury prone?" that's a stupid question): do the Pacers have the roster to cope with even one injury, and can this group survive a wide-open but packed Eastern Conference, one with aspiring playoff teams popping out of drawers? Betting markets aren't really sure what to think, with Indiana holding the ninth-best odds in the East at 18-1 on FanDuel. They're squarely out of the top group of Boston, New York and Philadelphia, and not even really in the second group of Toronto, Detroit and Cleveland either.

These are trying realities, and beg one concerning question: even fully healthy, are the Pacers a contender in the first place?

The argument for Indiana coming into the year was basically that Tyrese Haliburton was one of the five most important players in the league, if not actually one of the five best. Sure, there was tanking last year, but many of Indiana's NBA Finals roster played a decent number of games in 2025-26 and they still couldn't get to 20 wins. Unless this was the single most coordinated, organizationally coherent tanking job in history, with unprecedented buy-in from … Pascal Siakam, the only explanation for the 19-63 record was that Haliburton was basically Michael Jordan-level important for Indiana.

And he definitely was the linchpin of everything they did. But none of the advanced stats suggested that the team would literally evaporate without Haliburton. He's not some kind of Net Rating god, and I repeatedly found myself confused as to how the Pacers were losing every game despite a noticeable talent advantage on other teams in their tanking bracket.

That's a long way to say that I don't think that roster was actually that good, and the Finals run was more of an out-of-control party of unrepeatable clutch ridiculousness than it was the actual quality of Indiana basketball. If you accept that (you may not, but just … accept it for now so the grammar of the next clause works), your opinion of this Pacers team in 2026 comes down to how you feel about their upgrades — Kelly Oubre Jr. is a fine depth wing, but Ivica Zubac is the money question. Is he actually better than Myles Turner was in 2024?

With Tyrese Haliburton, I think he can be. Haliburton is a one-of-one elite table-setter for big men, and Zubac needs him on the floor to elevate his offensive game beyond simple, prodding interior hoops. Sidebar: I saw a Pacers fan complaining on Reddit that he doesn't feel like Zubac even looks to shoot the three-pointer even when he's wide open, which I find hysterical since he has made one singular 3-pointer in his 11-year career. But hey, he's open.

So far, though, Zubac has been uninspiring on the Pacers, and he hasn't been a particularly special defender in a few years like Turner was in his prime (yes, that is me declaring Myles Turner's prime over, have you watched a Bucks game recently?). Zubac must be a walking double-double for the Pacers to raise their ceiling.

Without Haliburton, this is all completely moot. They're in survival mode right now, needing to stack wins wherever they can find them until they can bring back a competent roster nightly. But even with Haliburton, I think this roster might just be bringing a squeaky plastic hammer to a knife fight. Like, you might be able to block some slashes and win hand-to-hand combat, and nobody in the eastern conference has a gun … but we have a squeaky plastic hammer. And right now, it has a hole in it.

Meta buys Conductor, whose app runs AI coding agents in parallel

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The startup’s team joins Meta Superintelligence Labs, chief executive Charlie Holtz said. Conductor Cloud shuts down this month, and its desktop app gets updates only until early 2027.

Meta has bought Conductor, a startup whose app lets software engineers run several AI coding agents at once. Its team will join Meta Superintelligence Labs, chief executive Charlie Holtz said. Axios first reported the deal on Wednesday, and Meta did not disclose the price.

Holtz confirmed the deal on X and in a post on Conductor’s site.

“We have some big, crazy plans for the next interface. If you liked Conductor, you’re gonna love what’s coming,” Holtz wrote on X.

He said the team would work with Nat Friedman and Alexandr Wang at the lab, and that it would have “something new” for users early next year. Meta confirmed the deal to TechCrunch but declined to comment further.

Conductor launched its app in July 2025. It runs coding agents from labs such as Anthropic and OpenAI in parallel, each working in its own copy of a project. Holtz and chief technology officer Jackson de Campos founded the company in 2024, Axios reported.

The desktop app will keep getting new models and critical bug fixes until early 2027, Conductor said.

The deal came the same day OpenAI said it would buy Pierre Computer Company, which makes tools for storing and managing code. Both deals target developers, whom Axios called the most important power users of AI. They compete with products such as Anthropic’s Claude Code and OpenAI’s Codex.

Meta launched its own coding agent, Muse Code, in August. Its Muse assistant has passed 10 million users. Its $2bn purchase of agent startup Manus fell through after China blocked the deal.

How Mid-Sized Businesses Can Thrive In Economic Uncertainty

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nik Froehlich is the CEO and Founder of Saritasa, an Information Technology and Services company, since 2005.

When the economy tightens, most mid-sized companies respond the same way. They cut travel, headcount, projects and tools. Cutting feels decisive, and in the short term, it often works.​

I have watched this pattern for a long time; first while scaling a concrete business across multiple states, and then over 20 years building a custom software development firm. Cost cutting is sometimes necessary. When it becomes the primary strategy, though, it usually delays the real problem. You end up with a thinner company still running the same broken processes.​

The companies that come out of a downturn stronger use the pressure differently by fixing how the work actually gets done.​

Inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022, the highest level in four decades, and the federal funds rate climbed from near zero to above 4% within a year. Capital became expensive and operating costs rose. Four years later, the pressure has not eased: Consumer prices were still up 3.4% year over year in August 2026, well above the Federal Reserve’s 2% target. Research across three recessions from Harvard Business School found that the companies most likely to outperform after a downturn cut costs mainly by improving operational efficiency rather than through blanket reductions.​

When I mention “efficiency software,” I mean systems that remove friction from the actual work: the rework, the duplicate data entry, the workarounds people build when a process breaks. The gains are rarely glamorous, and they show up across very different industries.​

Wingstop, a fast-casual restaurant chain, invested in digital ordering and workflow software and now processes more than 60% of sales through digital channels. That shift contributed to a 25% increase in same-store sales over two years while easing labor pressure in its restaurants.

Texmark Chemicals, a mid-sized Texas processor, deployed industrial IoT and predictive maintenance software across its plant. The company projects a 50% reduction in planned maintenance costs and expects to recover roughly 1,000 labor hours a year previously spent on manual equipment inspections.

In retail, Sam’s Club piloted an AI-based demand forecasting tool that improved accuracy enough to roll out across every club. Mid-sized retailers have since adopted the same approach at a smaller scale to reduce excess inventory and stockouts.​

The common thread in these cases has little to do with the technology itself. In each one, someone took ownership of a specific operational problem and built a system that made the work better.​

Before approving any efficiency initiative, I apply a two-part test. Someone must own the outcome once the system is live, and the system must still be usable and affordable three years out.

Most projects fail one or both parts. They get built, they look good in the demo and then they slowly become someone else’s problem. Custom software creates lasting value when it is designed around clear ownership and long-term maintainability.​

One way to find a good candidate is to watch where the hours go. I ask teams to list the tasks that take up the most time each week, and then sort that time into two groups: hours spent doing the work itself and hours spent clearing the way for it. Manual, repetitive tasks usually take the bulk of the time. I look for areas where a small fix can result in an outsized impact. For example, better tracking of hours and materials in the field can speed up billing and cut down on invoice disputes, even though the change itself is small. These kinds of changes rarely make the list of transformation projects. ​

The strongest candidates automate manual processes, improve workflow visibility, strengthen customer service and retention or tighten inventory and supply chain operations. Research from McKinsey & Company suggests digital collaboration tools alone can yield efficiency gains of 20% to 30%. ​

I also use a “subtraction test” to differentiate between a real, measurable improvement and one that moves cost somewhere else. A worthwhile initiative retires something; when a new tool adds a system without taking anything away, someone still has to learn and maintain it, so the friction usually ends up in a different department. Before I approve anything new, I want the team to name what goes away.​

Measuring impact requires choosing one number before the work begins, such as hours spent on a task or days to send an invoice, and recording where it stands. Then, I check that number shortly after launch and again several months later, since early gains often fade once people drift back to old workarounds or the person who championed the project moves on. An improvement that holds up at the second check has usually become part of how the team works, and that is the clearest sign it will pass the three-year test.​

In uncertain times, pure cost cutting feels safe. It is also incomplete. A company can only cut so far before it damages its ability to deliver. The better path pairs disciplined cost control with targeted investments that make the remaining work more productive. That is harder than issuing a mandate. It requires an honest look at how the work gets done and where the friction lives.​

After twenty years of watching mid-sized businesses try both approaches, I keep arriving at the same conclusion. The companies that treat efficiency as an ownership problem come out of the cycle stronger than the ones that simply got smaller.​​

Earth's Gravity May Have Kept the Early Moon's Volcanic Heart Beating

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

We typically think of the Moon as a static, floating object that has remained the same for basically its entire lifetime, with an occasional asteroid impact serving as an exception. That understanding is grounded in physics - the Moon should have cooled rapidly after forming when a huge protoplanet hit the early Earth around 4.5 billion years ago. But, some lunar material returned from the Changâe-5 mission was notably formed only around two billion years ago, yet appeared to be volcanic in origin. So how did a world that was supposed to have settled down without volcanism remain hot enough to create these materials more than two billion years after its violent birth? A new paper from Miao Chen of the Chengdu University of Technology and his colleagues, and recently published in the journal Planet, offers an explanation - tidal heating.

Letâs first set the stage of the Moonâs creation. Our best guess is that it was created when another early protoplanet, named Theia, slammed into Earth in a massive collision, creating a chunk of debris that coalesced into the Moon. However, that amount of debris was relatively small - only about a quarter the size of the Earth itself, which meant the internal heat generated during its formation would rapidly radiate away, turning the Moon itself geologically dormant.

But when Changâe-5 returned some lunar basalts from Oceanus Procellarum back to Earth, laboratory dating showed them to be only around two billion years old. Basalts are only formed in active volcanoes, or other environments with similarly high pressures and temperatures. Environments that, at least according to traditional geological theory, shouldnât have existed on the Moon two billion years ago.

Fraser explains why the Moon is moving farther away.

Despite that, scientists have tried offering other solutions. Radioactive elements, such as potassium and uranium, emit some heat as they decay. But there wasnât enough un-decayed material left on the Moon to offer the amount of heat needed to form basalt two billion years ago. Asteroid strikes could cause localized high impact thermal spikes, but the heavy bombardment period that gave the Moon most of its craters had ended long before the basalt was formed.

So instead the authors turned to gravity - specifically to a phenomenon called tidal heating. This happens when a celestial body is squeezed, stretched, and kneaded as it travels in an elliptical orbit around a larger parent body. Perhaps the most famous example of this in the solar system is Io. This moon of Jupiter is constantly erupting due to the tidal squishing it undergoes during its orbit around the largest planet.

Obviously, the Moon doesnât seem to be undergoing any such squishing currently. However, two billion years ago, the Moon was much closer to the Earth than it is today, orbiting our planet in sixteen days rather than the twenty seven it takes now. Its orbit was also much more eccentric, looking much more like an oval than a circle. All of which means that Earth itself exerted huge rhythmic tidal forces on the Moon' s interior during the course of its orbit.

Fraser details Tidal Locking, which also affects the Moons geology.

Weâve known that for awhile, but astronomers have always modeled the Moon as a mostly uniform sphere when trying to understand how these gravitational squeezes affected it. However, scientists knew this was a simplification - there is an ultra-low viscosity zone of rock wrapping itself between the Moonâs core and mantle. When the research team plugged a more realistic 3D representation of the mantle setup into a convection simulation, they noticed that a ton of energy was directed specifically at two symmetric regions along the lunar core-mantle boundary.

These regions, which they named Tidal Heating Anomaly Zones (THAZs) , absorbed a huge amount of energy. They calculated that, around 3.2 billion years ago, despite taking up only 2.5% of the mantleâs total volume, the tidal strain on these regions delivered 150 gigawatts of power - more than four times the amount of energy generated by radioactive decay.

This seemed to be the driving force behind much of the Moonâs geological history. It helped keep the lunar core warm, which helped sustain a magnetic field for much longer the models originally predicted (and was a puzzle when scientists realized that Apollo lunar samples seemed to have formed in a strong magnetic field). It also drove huge mantle plumes that swelled toward the crust. Billions of years later, long after the tidal engine has calmed down, those stranded pockets of mantle melted to produce the basalts seen in the Changâe-5 samples.

If thereâs one thing thatâs clear from the paperâs theory, it's that we still donât know a lot about the Moonâs geological history. This new model offers some elegant answers to some of the most persistent questions in that area, but as of now itâs still only a theory. Maybe some of the numerous lunar missions planned over the next decade will be able to provide more definitive data to finally solve the evolutionary questions of our nearest neighbor.

For years, Jobe Bellingham has been in the shadows of his older brother Jude.

Now, for the first time since they were children, the duo are poised to perform on the same stage. Only this time it won't be under the lampposts of Stourbridge, but the floodlights at Wembley.

Jobe, 21, has been called up to England's senior squad for the first time and is often described in similar terms to his elder sibling: relentlessly ambitious, focused, and a quick learner who is wise beyond his years.

'You have to have an X-Factor and Jobe does,' said Regis Le Bris, his old manager at Sunderland.

That X-Factor appears to run in the Bellingham blood. Earlier this week Jude was unveiled as the new underwear heart-throb for Calvin Klein, channelling his inner David Beckham in a smouldering photoshoot. No pressure, Jobe.

Their main difference on the pitch is that Jobe, while versatile, tends to occupy a deeper-lying role in midfield – and that actually increases the chances of Thomas Tuchel fielding them together. There is every chance that, against Croatia or Spain next week, we will see just the fourth pair of brothers to feature for England since the Second World War.

Jude and Jobe Bellingham's dream of playing for England together may finally come true

The duo grew up playing together and both joined the Birmingham City academy (Instagram: judebellingham)

The duo visited the Spanish Grand Prix in September with matching shirts and haircuts

If you were a few pints deep in the Formula One hospitality area, you'd be forgiven for thinking they were the same bloke – especially when their footballing trajectories are so similar.

Jobe began his career at Birmingham City, like Jude, before moving to Sunderland and now Borussia Dortmund, where his brother also played. Jobe, who is two years younger than his superstar brother, is now in his second full season with the Westphalia giants.

He has previously admitted his trepidation at following in Jude's footsteps, once telling the German club's in-house media channel: 'I was biased against joining Borussia Dortmund for a very long time.

'I had anxieties, fears, and you want to be your own man. But I don't think I should let those stop me from making the right decision.'

The comparisons are inevitable, though, when you share the same name and your potential also happens to be through the ceiling. It is a theme Jobe will simply have to contend with wherever he roams.

In Dortmund's 2-2 draw with Bayern Munich on Saturday, the resemblance was striking as he delivered a standout performance – even having the ball in the back of the net, though his goal was ruled out for a Carney Chukwuemeka foul.

At one stage, Bellingham backheeled the ball over Aleksandar Pavlovic, won the ball back off Dayot Upamecano, drifted past Pavlovic with a skill that left him on one knee, and drove about 30 yards towards goal before firing a shot just over the bar.

Jude and Jobe are pictured together at a Louis Vuitton Paris Fashion Week show in 2023

In that ten-second flash, he displayed the sort of tenacity, ingenuity, and swagger which we have come to associate with Jude.

Healthcare mergers and acquisitions are in no short supply as providers, health tech companies, payers and other industry players look to expand their businesses and gain a competitive edge. Here’s a roundup of new deals that were revealed, closed, rumored or called off during the month of October.

Rush University System for Health and Endeavor Health announced plans to merge, becoming a 12-hospital nonprofit with nearly $11 billion in annual revenue. The resulting entity would command over 400 care sites, employ more than 46,000 people including over 3,400 clinicians, and reach over 2 million Northern Illinois and Northwest Indiana patients per year. The pair are targeting an early 2027 close and outlined plans to commit $1 billion of Endeavor funds toward care in the region.

Private equity firm CD&R and healthcare conglomerate McKesson plan to acquire Option Care Health, a provider of infusion therapy services, in a deal valued at $5.8 billion. The purchase gives equity holders a per-share offer of $32.05 and is expected to close in the first half of 2027. CD&R would be the majority stakeholder in the company, which is the country’s largest independent at-home and alternate site infusion services provider.

Bon Secours Mercy Health announced plans to acquire a majority stake in Surgery Partners, among the country’s largest outpatient surgical care companies, in a take-private deal valuing the acquiree at $5.7 billion. Bain Capital Private Equity will roll over its 39% share of the company as it is purchased by a Bon Secours affiliate at $23.50 per share. Surgery Partners includes more than 200 locations and about 5,000 affiliated physicians across 30 states. The organizations are targeting an early 2027 close.

Risant Health, Kaiser Permanente’s subsidiary value-based care platform, announced Integris Health as its next regional health system acquisition. Financial terms were not disclosed, though the deal is aiming for an early 2027 close. Integris is Oklahoma’s largest nonprofit provider with 10 acute and 10 specialty hospitals with $3.3 billion in total operating revenues during the 2025 fiscal year.

MultiCare Health System during October picked up the final needed regulatory approval to absorb Samaritan Health Services. The nonprofits then consummated their 18-hospital combination on Nov. 1.

Hackensack Meridian Health has signed a definitive agreement to pick up Central New Jersey’s Hunterdon Health, which would grow the state’s largest health system to 19 total hospitals. The agreement outlines a $200 million capital investment from Hackensack Meridian, plus another $30 million to roll out Epic’s electronic health record.

Cencora (formerly AmerisourceBergen) signed a definitive agreement to acquire American Oncology Network, a collection of more than 350 oncology providers, in a deal valuing the network at $2.4 billion. Cencora said it expects to pay $1.7 billion to increase its 4% interest to 75%.

Parkview Health is acquiring Ovation Healthcare, a nationwide shared services vendor for providers. The 16-hospital Indiana nonprofit did not disclose financial terms, but said it hopes to close the deal within weeks. Parkview views the pickup as an opportunity to boost its administrative capabilities, which could be offered to other rural systems.

Parkview Health also wrapped its acquisition of Goshen Health, a 105-bed hospital with a network of physicians.

Independence Health System, a five-hospital nonprofit in western Pennsylvania, joined the West Virginia University Health System on Oct. 1. The deal was announced late last year and brings a five-year, $800 million capital commitment from WVU Health System toward the Independence hospitals and communities, which will "immediately" begin operating under the WVU Medicine brand.

Kettering Health plans to add Knox Community Hospital as its 15th medical center. The Ohio systems signed a definitive agreement for their deal, which will include undisclosed commitments toward Knox’s facilities, equipment and technology.

Nationwide Children’s Hospital plans to acquire the pediatric practice of Knox Community Hospital in spring 2027. Nationwide plans to combine the practice with one of its affiliates and continue providing services to Knox.

Allegiance Health Management said it plans to divest four Louisiana hospitals: Minden Medical Center, Northern Louisiana Medical Center, Acadian Medical Center and Mercy Regional Medical Center. No potential buyers or deal timelines were given.

Orlando Health closed its acquisition of RMC Health System, an Alabama organization with a 375-bed medical center. The deal was first announced in April, and brings five years of “significant” investment into RMC’s facilities, equipment and technology.

HCA Healthcare sold 31 home health and hospital agencies to Deaconess Associations. The facilities were located across eight states. Financial terms were not disclosed.

MaineHealth added York Hospital to its Southern region, marking the 79-bed facility as the system’s 10th hospital.

Ascension Seton said it is acquiring three locations in the greater Austin area from Family Health Systems, one of which is Auton-based Family Hospital. The deal is set to close later this year.

Cone Health entered an agreement to acquire three pharmacies in Reidsville, North Carolina: Carolina Apothecary, Belmont Pharmacy and Rx Care.

Humana struck a deal shifting out Welsh, Carson, Anderson & Stowe for BlackRock for equity in a joint venture running about 150 of its CenterWell locations. The deal sees BlackRock buying out WCAS’ equity for about $2.8 billion.

Horizon Blue Cross Blue Shield of New Jersey is acquiring the New Jersey operations of Summit Health, which includes CityMD urgent care sites. Financial details or a potential timeline were not disclosed.

Redesign Health plans to merge with Sheba Medical Center’s innovation arm, ARC Global, creating a roughly $2 billion company. The pair hope to build an end-to-end platform that helps health systems to turn ideas originating with their own clinicians and operators into technology products and standalone companies that can then be scaled across a global network. To help do so, the pair also agreed to acquire strategic advisory and intelligence firm AVIA.

Teladoc Health acquired Teledigm Health, which provides hybrid clinical services and specialized care support for rural and regional health systems, the public virtual care company announced alongside its Q3 earnings. Financial terms were not disclosed. Teladoc leadership said picking up its longtime partner will help it grow with providers serving rural communities.

RadNet, an imaging center operator and seller of radiology technology, is acquiring Onrad, a teleradiology services company with 130 contracted radiologists. The deal is expected to close early next year and will bring Onrad shareholders a collective $350 million plus 5 million RadNet shares, with additional performance-based earnouts.

iRhythm wrapped its acquisition of cardiac remote patient monitoring company VitalConnect for $237.5 million. The deal will help iRhythm evolve from ambulatory cardiac monitoring toward “a broader cardiac monitoring and intelligence platform,” its CEO and president told Fierce Healthcare.

Zocdoc acquired insurance technology company Sohar Health, the first acquisition in its nearly 20-year history and part of its push to evolve beyond an appointment-booking marketplace into broader healthcare access infrastructure. Financial terms were not disclosed.

Carenet Health, which provides patient engagement and navigation tools to more than 400 healthcare partners, acquired Orbita, an AI engagement and communication company that does business with providers. Financial terms were not disclosed.

Tegria, a healthcare consultancy and services company, acquired cybersecurity, cloud hosting and managed IT services company CloudWave for an undisclosed sum.

Chapter, a Medicare navigation and retirement advisory company, has acquired Hively, a personalized retirement navigation and membership startup, for an undisclosed sum. The pair intend to launch in-person and online community programming for retirees surrounding retirement and aging topics, a move beyond Medicare navigation alone.

Sagility, a provider and payer technology services company, acquired Chirok Health, which offers clinical documentation improvement, medical coding and revenue integrity services for an undisclosed sum.

Investment firm Sopris Capital said it acquired Blackford Analysis, an AI medical imaging platform, from Bayer for an undisclosed sum. Sopris said it intends to deploy the company’s tools across its healthcare portfolio.

Emergence, a private equity firm, acquired digital intake and online reputation management software company Clinect for an undisclosed sum.
