Zelda: Ocarina of Time Remake - How To Get A Free Hylian Shield

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time has so many items and pieces of equipment that it's hard to keep track of what is in each game. However, select iconic gear sets are shown as motifs throughout the entire series, like the Master Sword. Another piece of reliable equipment is the Hylian Shield, which, depending on the game, can be easy to get.

In Ocarina of Time, you can buy it outright at one of the first major shops in Castle Town, but 80 Rupees is a big ask for new players who may not yet know the best ways to earn Rupees. Here's how to get one for free without paying the Rupee tax.

Early in the game, before you enter Dodongo's Cavern, Ocarina of Time will constantly remind you that you need a better shield than your initial Deku Shield that you picked up in the opening moments; which burns up on contact with flames.

As a result, you'll want to pick up the Hylian Shield, the classic reliable shield found in multiple Zelda games and one of the most iconic pieces of equipment in the series.

To find it, head to the graveyard behind Kakariko Village. When you enter the village, walk straight ahead and to the right, following the path up to the graveyard. Walk all the way to the back, near the big grave area near the hill. Go behind the grave in the gallery above, and pull the stone until it reveals a hole.

Drop in, and be prepared to either run or battle a formidable foe right away.

Inside the hole is a ReDead, an iconic and terrifying enemy throughout the series, including Ocarina of Time. They can scream to stun you, then grab you to suck your health, so you'll need to approach them carefully. Thankfully, it's easy to run past them, grab the shield in the chest, and then dash out before the ReDead can react.

As Young Link, you can't use the Hylian Shield to its full potential because it's a little too big for him. Link will crouch and hold the shield above him, so it's less useful than the Deku Shield in some situations. However, I would keep it equipped in Dodongo's Cavern regardless, because it won't burn and can help protect you from vertical threats.

You keep the Hylian Shield when you turn into Adult Link. As Adult Link, you can wield the Hylian Shield like a normal shield, blocking attacks from the front.

There is another way out if you can't locate the Hylian Shield in the graveyard for whatever reason, or you lose it somehow. The Bazaar in Castle Town sells a Hylian Shield for 80 Rupees, which you can reduce to 60 Rupees by talking to the guard near the trail to Death Mountain at the edge of Kakariko Village.

While you're there, give the guard the Keaton Mask, the first mask from the Happy Mask Shop.

There are other chests that contain Hylian Shields, but these are the easiest ways to grab them early in the Ocarina of Time remake.

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time’s Changes to the Infamous Water Temple Are Actually Brilliant

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Zelda's most infamous dungeon is no longer a frustrating rite of passage

With The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time’s Switch 2 remake out now, long-time Nintendo fans are in for a lot of warm nostalgia. A trek through the lost woods, duets with Sheik, a return to the Bombchu Bowling Alley — all of those wonderful moments return in a faithful remake that feels every bit as magical as the Nintendo 64 classic. But with all those wonderful moments comes a painful reunion with one of the most hated moments in Zelda history. Yes, you will have to return to the dreaded Water Temple.

Don’t worry! Even though the Ocarina of Time remake is careful not to change too much about the original game, it does make some small tweaks to a dungeon that still haunts a generation. It’s mercifully easier to navigate now thanks to a few minor but smart updates that go a long way.

The Water Temple is the most infamous dungeon in the original Ocarina of Time, so much so that it has been a bit of a running joke for decades now. The gist is that it’s a four-story structure built around a central room, and it’s flooded with water. To access certain areas, you need to find rooms in the dungeon that allow you to change the water level throughout the temple. Easy enough on paper, but it’s a bit of a pain in practice. Due to the limited visual information you get in-game, it requires you to keep a mental map of where each water adjustment point is, as well as how each of the three levels will change the layout of the dungeon. It’s a headache to keep everything straight, thus earning it a reputation as a frustrating rite of passage.

While that structure hasn’t changed much in the remake, Nintendo does make it easier to get through with a few smart decisions. That came at the request of Eiji Aonuma, who directed the original Ocarina of Time. “I had some overdue homework: I wanted to do something about the Water Temple I created 28 years ago,” Aonuma said in a recent Nintendo developer interview. “Since it became well known for its difficulty in the original game, I had the team make some adjustments in this version.”

One of those adjustments just comes down to clearer signposting. Building off the dungeon’s Ocarina of Time 3D redesign, the doors that lead to water controls are now clearly marked with symbols showing you exactly how the water level will change. You no longer have to remember which door leads to the room that drains the room, or which one will get you to floor two.

On top of that, each of the routes are now color-coded. You’ll find variously colored pipes around each of the three doors, and those visually lead you to the control room if you follow them through the dungeon. For instance, when you enter the route to the room that brings the water to its lowest level, you’ll notice that some blue pipes signal that you should float to the water’s surface. Follow that hint and you’ll find that the pipes lead to clearly marked pedestals for you to play your ocarina on. All of that makes it significantly easier to remember exactly where you need to go at all times.

Arguably, the more impactful Water Temple change comes from a top-level control decision that just so happens to make life easier in the dungeon. Part of why the Water Temple is such a pain is because you need to use a few key items throughout it. Your ocarina, Iron Boots, Hookshot, and more all play major roles in solving puzzles throughout the temple. Since the original game only gave you three item slots to work with, you were often left juggling around your item shortcuts in the main menu. It’s a minor pain point, but one that stands out when you’re constantly swapping items.

While the Switch 2 remake still has its own item management quirks, it wisely keeps your ocarina mapped to the D-pad at all times. Your boots get a quick swap button on the down button there too, meaning that you don’t need to waste an item slot on the Iron Boots. With those two necessities free up, you can dedicate your ZR item slot to the Hookshot and only swap to other items as needed with a quick menu on the R button. That decision cuts out any need for pause-menu management, allowing you to move through the dungeon more fluidly.

For the ‘90s purists, these changes might sound a little sacrilegious. As much as players claim to hate the Water Temple, that complaint invariably comes with a fondness for a genuinely memorable bit of frustrating design. “Why shouldn’t today’s youths struggle through the Water Temple like their elders once did?” you may ask. I, for one, welcome the help, just as I support the idea of today’s students having their loans canceled even though I had to pay mine off. The new visual design tweak makes for some sensible signposting that still feels part of the dungeon’s architecture, and the control solution just makes it easier to stay immersed in a well-built dungeon. New generations of players needn’t suffer like us old Zelda fans once did. Swim free, ye tadpoles.

meet the wienermobile, the 1930s quirky pedal car that put hot dogs on wheels

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

from its first appearance on american roads in 1936 to its miniature interpretations by bird corporation and konek, the oversized hot dog has become an enduring piece of advertising and automotive culture.

Two vintage Oscar Mayer Wienermobile pedal cars, produced by Bird Corporation and Konek Inc. during the 1990s, are appearing together at auction, bringing a miniature version of one of America’s most recognizable promotional vehicles back into focus. Offered as a single lot without a reserve, the two new-old-stock examples reportedly have never been ridden by children. Their bright orange bodies, yellow bun-shaped bases, and playful automotive details recall a design that has spent nearly nine decades turning an oversized hot dog into a familiar sight on American roads.

The story of the Wienermobile begins in 1936, when Carl G. Mayer, nephew of Oscar Mayer founder Oscar F. Mayer, proposed a hot dog-shaped vehicle as a traveling advertisement for the company’s products. Built by Chicago’s General Body Company, the original measured approximately four meters long and featured a metal body shaped like an oversized wiener, mounted on wheels. Its two open cockpits accommodated a driver and Little Oscar, the promotional character of the brand.

As the vehicle became an established part of Oscar Mayer’s advertising, successive generations introduced new interpretations of the original concept. The 1958 model, designed by industrial designer Brooks Stevens, introduced a rounded, bubble-like front and more integrated bun-shaped elements. Later versions experimented with larger bodies, updated chassis, and elaborate interiors, always retaining the unmistakable hot dog silhouette.

Oscar Mayer discontinued the program in the late 1970s, shifting its attention toward television advertising. Public enthusiasm for the vehicle, however, prompted its return in 1986 for the company’s 50th anniversary. New generations followed, and the Wienermobile resumed its travels across the United States, appearing at parades, public gatherings, supermarkets, and promotional events.

Over the years, the hot dog on wheels inspired toys, merchandise, and other branded interpretations, translating its instantly recognizable shape into objects that could be collected and played with. The two pedal cars now offered at auction on Bring a Trailer belong to this broader history.

Although they share the same orange-and-yellow color scheme, the Bird Corporation and Konek examples feature different construction methods. The Bird version has a single-piece, hot-dog-shaped plastic body mounted over a yellow base, while the Konek model uses a two-piece orange body with Oscar Mayer and Wienermobile decals across its front, rear, and sides.

Both combine molded plastic exteriors with metal frames, orange wheels, yellow seats, and black steering wheels with yellow horn buttons. Twin foot pedals power the miniature vehicles, while faux headlights, grilles, taillights, and windows complete their automotive appearance. The Konek example measures approximately 114 centimeters long, 53 centimeters wide, and 48 centimeters tall.

Today, the Wienermobile remains part of Oscar Mayer’s identity, with six vehicles operated by parent company Kraft Heinz. Its continued presence on American roads speaks to the unusual longevity of a marketing idea conceived in the 1930s. The hot dog-shaped vehicle has become a recognizable piece of American visual culture and continues to inspire new interpretations nearly 90 years after its first appearance. The two pedal cars’ appearance at auction offers a chance to revisit the history of the Wienermobile, a promotional vehicle that brought advertising, automotive design, and popular culture together. Sony is enjoying a sharp profit spike in its latest quarterly financials, largely driven by its PlayStation business and the blockbuster success of Spider-Man: Brand New Day. According to the company’s September earnings, Sony’s net profits climbed to 449.60 billion yen (about $2.84 billion) for the three months ended September — a 44 percent increase compared to the 311.4 billion yen ($1.96 billion) it reported for the same period last year.

Spider-Man: Brand New Day was a notable contributor, which released in July and became the third-highest grossing movie in history by earning $2.52 billion at the global box office. That’s a huge boost for Sony Pictures Entertainment, which posted earnings of $581 million for the three months ended September — a 527 percent jump from the $93 million it reported for the same period in 2025.

Sales of Playstation 5 consoles have fallen, with 2.6 million units reported this second quarter compared to the 4 million that were sold in Q2 2025. While the Sony-exclusive Marvel’s Wolverine sold 2.4 million units against a reported budget of $300 million, PlayStation game sales generally have increased for both digital downloads and physical discs. Zillennials Don’t Feel the Need to Grow Up, <i><em>Club Kid </em></i>Knows Why

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Jordan Firstman’s deferred coming-of-age tale speaks to the realities of living in an unwieldy time

I’m 29, an age that has become a catalyst for conversations about what it means to be an adult, about settling down (or not), and, perhaps above all, how to show up for my own well-being (do I need to party less?) It’s a volley of topics that are often the subject of yearly think pieces, even when we know the conclusions they’ll reach. Millennials and Zillenials feel pressured by older generations to live a conventional and ambitious life, even as they’ve squeezed young people’s hope for a future.

The New York Times detailed this dilemma just last month, speaking to thirtysomethings across New York City who felt discouraged by their situation and, to put it plainly, left behind. The article pointed out that the long-running Harvard Youth Poll found, for the first time ever, that most young Americans don’t believe they will surpass their parents financially. In the last 30 years, the cost of living has skyrocketed, and, today, mounting loans and debt among young people have made a house feel out of reach. Looking even younger, Gen Z is feeling the brunt of the economy, with Fortune finding that two-thirds of parents say their Gen Z adult kids rely on them (and Playboy going so far as to connect the housing crisis to the sex decline). Why stop partying if the rent’s too high, if there’s no reasonable expectation that you’ll find security, buy a house, or live easily past 2050?

This growing frustration is likely why audiences—including myself—are connecting so deeply with influencer-turned-director Jordan Firstman’s sensational feature debut, Club Kid. The film offers a tender look at arrested development and unexpected fatherhood, but above all, it delivers an incisive look at the struggle to let go of youth when the world feels increasingly grim. The idea of partying instead of worrying is alluring: to exist in a space free from responsibility. Peter (Firstman), a thirty-something party promoter deeply enmeshed in New York’s underground queer club scene, lives in the apartment his late mother left him, sells drugs on the side, and his only real daily concern is recovering for the next party. The moment he crosses the threshold of any club, he plunges into a fugue state fueled by drugs and sex. Peter clings to his youth (his name aptly echoing Peter Pan syndrome), shirks all responsibilities, and actively defers a much-needed inward look.

Peter’s wake-up call arrives as a 10-year-old boy named Arlo (Reggie Absolom)—a son he unknowingly fathered years after hooking up with a man who insisted they turn their dalliance into a threesome with a woman. A decade after that fateful night, the woman’s best friend (played with drug-fueled frenzy by Kirby Howell-Baptiste) shows up at his door, a young child in tow, with news that the mother has died by suicide and Peter needs to step up. It’s a parable tailor-made for a worn-down generation that sees no reason to stop the party. It takes a ketamine stork of sorts to change course.

Club Kid revels in the growing pains. Firstman avoids reducing the conflict to a simple clash over parenting Arlo or his personal struggle to break free from the party lifestyle; instead, Club Kid unfolds as a tender parallel coming-of-age, where Peter grows up as he learns to parent Arlo, a quiet, curious kid grappling with the loss of his mother. Firstman embraces the missteps, letting us see the beauty of his life as it intermingles with his newfound parenting. The whirlwind of responsibility—of unwitting fatherhood—plays like a fairy tale, a wondrous reality that feels increasingly distant from what a thirtysomething can reasonably expect today.

Watching Club Kid, I considered my own parents, who conceived me (by happy accident, I’m told) at 27 and 29. They worked at a pizza restaurant together before opening their own bar. My childhood felt wrapped up in Atlanta’s nightlife scene; somehow, completely inexperienced, they still learned to balance their lifestyle with their newfound roles as parents. I lived as the only kid (with my two-years-younger brother) in my parents’ world for several years, often sitting with the bar regulars for hours while my parents worked, or being actively watched over by the cooks. It felt like tapping into another world, but also brought us closer. They didn’t give up their life for me; they made me a part of it. These days, this story sounds like a romantic ideal, a whirlwind adventure only possible without looming loans and the high cost of living. I simply couldn’t imagine doing the same today.

What makes Peter’s story compelling is how he navigates the responsibilities of fatherhood without fully forgetting himself. Some critics have challenged the film, claiming that it’s stigmatizing the queer party scene, but in his telling, he doesn’t rebuke his former lifestyle, just the parts of it that were bad for him. His friend even welcomes the news of his fatherhood with excitement, spending time with Arlo without passing judgment. Even if he eventually needs to distance himself from these friends’ party habits, it doesn’t need a blow-out fight. Life happens, and people move on.

That’s why it’s important that Firstman returns to the real problem at the end of the movie. A vindictive call to social services by Peter’s ex-best friend (Cara Delevigne) leads to Arlo’s deportation and the new father-son duo’s separation. It brings to light the real-life anxieties looming over this heartwarming story. Sure, Peter did not file the proper adoption papers, and his lack of money becomes damningly apparent when he stares down health-care premiums and legal bills in the face of an unrelenting bureaucracy.

Firstman refuses to provide a pretty picture; he instead studies the anxieties of millennials, a generation perennially unsure of the world around them, and distills them into a heartwarming, deferred coming-of-age story. Firstman dreams of who we can become, if only given the chance to grow up in a world where steady ground is hard to find. Peter is growing up alongside his son, hiccups and all.

Consumers are spending on physical gifts even as travel tops wish lists, Visa survey finds

📅 2026-11-05  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Consumers are hoping for more travel and experiences this holiday season, but physical gifts continue to win the lion's share of their wallets, according to a new report from Visa on Thursday.

The report found that more than half of survey respondents are putting travel and live events at the top of their wish lists. Still, only 15% of those respondents are prioritizing spending on experiences for others, while 27% of them plan to favor physical gifts.

That gap between what shoppers want and what they plan to buy is largely due to higher travel costs, lower consumer sentiment and an emphasis on value, according to Michael Brown, Visa's principal U.S. economist.

"There is some interesting trade-offs there that we're seeing on the part of consumers," Brown told CNBC. "If I'm traveling more, I'm probably going to be gifting those physical goods less, so this is really the broader context."

The survey was conducted among roughly 4,660 U.S. adults ages 18 and older, designed to be representative of the broader population of consumers.

Brown said budget pressure also factors into traveling for the holidays, as Visa expects more people to celebrate the holidays "a little bit closer to home" than in the past given rising airfares and general inflation. In September, consumer prices rose 3.7% annually, driven higher by surging energy costs.

Airlines have hiked the price of plane tickets this year as their fuel costs have risen by billions of dollars due to the war in the Middle East. And even if fuel prices drop, executives have said fares are likely to stay high, with fares up more than 22% in September compared with a year earlier, according to the consumer price index.

Those trends mean consumers will likely be more intentional about where they're spending, especially as inflation drives up prices across the board.

"Consumers are aspirational. They want those experiences, they want to travel, they want to be out in the world," Brown said. "However, the economic reality is with prices rising, with economic uncertainty, they need to balance the reality of today's economic environment with those aspirations."

The report also found that only households with an income above $200,000 spent more on trips departing between Nov. 1 and early next year compared with 2025 into 2026. On the other hand, many other income groups pulled back on their travel spending.

"If you think about that segment, a lot of them own equities, and the equity market runup over the last several months has created a lot of positivity among that specific affluent demographic, and I think that's one of the factors that continues to motivate even aggregate macro spend today," Brown said.

Meanwhile, less affluent shoppers are placing an emphasis on value. Visa's consumer survey from October showed that roughly one-third of consumers with an income below $100,000 are going to switch to lower-cost brands or stores when shopping for holiday gifts this season.

Though Visa is projecting a 4.5% year-over-year increase in holiday sales growth, much of that figure is due to inflation. Without those price increases, sales are expected to rise just 1.1% this year, down from the 1.3% increase during the 2025 holiday season.

Ultimately, Brown said the survey findings may be encouraging for the broader retail scene this holiday season. With less emphasis on travel and more projected spending on physical gifts, it could boost the sector at a time when spending is under pressure.

At the same time, he said that consumers may just be expecting to spend more this holiday season because they've become accustomed to the higher prices.

"But, of course, consumers are internalizing higher prices of things, and I think that's why they're reporting higher anticipated spend this year," Brown said. "I think these numbers just need to be looked at through the lens of the inflation and particularly consumers' perception of where inflation is going to go. I think that weighed heavily on consumer psychology this season."

A half-built neutrino telescope on the floor of the Mediterranean Sea searched for cracks in one of physics' most basic invariances. It came up empty, but its limits rival those of detectors many times its size.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

The view from inside a detector building block, with the yellow buoy of a nearby detection unit clearly visible.

A particle-detecting telescope sunk more than a mile beneath the sea off the coast of France has tested a core principle of Einstein's relativity and found no cracks.

The detector, called Oscillation Research with Cosmics in the Abyss (ORCA), detects elusive particles called neutrinos. As a doctoral student, Alba Domi rode onto the Mediterranean aboard one of two boats that helped build the telescope, lowering some of its first detection units to the seafloor about 1.5 miles (2.4 kilometers) below the waves off Toulon, France.

Now, data from those first detection units have been used to test one of the founding rules of Einstein’s relativity: the idea that the laws of nature look the same no matter which way you face or how fast you move.

The team found no sign that this rule, called Lorentz invariance, is broken. But even with a detector only a fraction of its planned size, they set limits that compete with those of far larger and longer-running experiments.

The results were published Wednesday (Nov. 4) in the Journal of Cosmology and Astroparticle Physics.

Physics rests on two successful theories: general relativity, conceived by Albert Einstein more than 100 years ago, and the Standard Model of subatomic physics.

"General relativity describes the universe well on a cosmic scale, and the Standard Model of particle physics describes the microscopic scale; really, the single particles, how they behave," said study co-author Lukas Hennig, a doctoral student in advanced physics at Erlangen-Nürnberg.

Get the world’s most fascinating discoveries delivered straight to your inbox.

Both theories assume Lorentz invariance to be true. But in extreme settings, such as the first moments after the Big Bang, the two theories don't mesh. That has fueled a decades-long hunt for a theory of quantum gravity that unites them. Some candidates, including versions of string theory and loop quantum gravity, suggest that space-time might have a hidden "grain" at the tiniest scales — and that Lorentz invariance, "one of the most basic assumptions we make about our universe, might be violated," Hennig said.

Any such violation would be extremely small. To spot it, physicists would need a probe that could travel long distances and barely interact with anything along the way. Enter the neutrino.

Neutrinos are nearly massless particles that pass through Earth every second and carry zero charge. They barely interact with anything and come in three types: electron, muon and tau neutrinos. As neutrinos travel, they morph from one type into another in a predictable pattern that physicists call neutrino oscillation.

An optical module attached to the Dyneema ropes of the detection unit.

If Lorentz invariance were broken, that oscillation would change, with the effect growing more pronounced the farther the neutrinos traveled. So the team focused on neutrinos created when cosmic rays slam into the atmosphere elsewhere on the globe before traveling up through the planet to the detector. Some of the tested neutrinos crossed the entire 7,900-mile (12,700 km) width of Earth.

ORCA is part of the KM3NeT neutrino telescope. Each of its detection units is anchored to the seabed and holds 18 glass spheres packed with 31 light sensors apiece. When a neutrino collides with a particle in the water, it produces a faint flash of blue light that the sensors catch. The many sensors in each sphere help the team pin down which direction the light came from, Domi said.

Earth itself serves as a filter. The researchers selected only particles traveling upward through the planet, and they were able to weed out the flood of other particles from the atmosphere that would otherwise swamp the signal. "Only the neutrinos can get through," Hennig said.

That setup differs from that of IceCube, the Nobel Prize-winning detector buried in Antarctic ice. "Ice scatters the light more than water, but on the other hand, water absorbs the light more than ice," Domi said, which changes how each team reconstructs a neutrino's energy and path.

The researchers used 5,828 neutrinos collected over about 17 months between 2020 and 2021. They found that the oscillations matched standard physics. That means if Lorentz violation exists, it must be weaker than the limits the team set.

Those limits matter for theorists. If a model "predicts a Lorentz invariance violation [stronger than the new limits], the data rule it out. In that case, you can be pretty sure that this model is not correct," Hennig said.

The team also set the first direct limits on a kind of violation that would treat muon neutrinos and tau neutrinos differently.

The detector's limits on several key measures are competitive with those from Japan's Super-Kamiokande, which used 12 years of data, and from IceCube, which used two years of data from its full array.

"I'm surprised that we have competitive results with only six detection units," Hennig said. Today, 44 units are in the water, and the collaboration plans to activate 108 by 2030.

The best tests may still lie ahead. Because Lorentz-violating effects grow with distance and energy, neutrinos from distant cosmic sources are "actually the best candidates to do this kind of research," Domi said. The team plans to repeat the search using ORCA's sister detector, ARCA (Astroparticle Research with Cosmics in the Abyss), which sits off the coast of Sicily and is designed to catch those high-energy cosmic travelers.

If space-time does have a grain, those particles may be among the first to feel it.

The sitcom, which he has been developing for nearly four years, follows three best friends—Ethan, Sam (indie actor and musician Olive Gray), and Abi (Emily Fairn, last seen in House of Guinness)—who have been an unbreakable, albeit dysfunctional, trio since college. But they are now in their late 20s, kicked out by a crappy landlord, with boring boyfriends, jarring exes, and futile hook-ups, all while figuring out the life they want.

JW Anderson shirt, sweater, and shorts. Berluti leather shoes. Pantherella socks.

A dazzling suite of cameos—Lena Dunham, Andrew Scott, Amelia Dimoldenberg, and Zadie Smith as a narrator (“obviously unreal,” he says of them all saying “yes”)—cement this as the new quarter-life crisis comedy to enter the canon. “It was really nice having actually funny people just being funny,” he says of his “incredible” cast. “And then I get all the credit. Perfect!” The ironic part? That Reich has never himself had flatmates. “I lived with my parents until I was 25 and then immediately moved somewhere by myself.” He realizes it’s a question that’s going to come up in interviews. “Maybe I start lying.”

Saint Laurent by Anthony Vaccarello leather coat, poplin shirt, silk tie, flannel trousers, and leather shoes.

Born and raised in Islington, Reich describes himself as a “mummy’s boy” who was “really into bugs and birds” as a child. (He also had a pet rabbit that roamed freely around his bedroom.) Studying English at the University of Cambridge, where he performed with Footlights, nudged him towards stand-up: his 2022 Edinburgh Fringe show Literally Who Cares?!—a self-obsessed salute to the narcissism of Gen Z—became an HBO comedy special and drew the attention of Dunham (“she is the funniest person I’ve ever met”), who was so impressed that she wrote a part for Reich in her 2025 Netflix show Too Much.

Reich says he’s “not really up to much at the moment,” although the suitcase next to him suggests otherwise. When we speak he’ll soon be off to Paris for the Dior show, New York for Pride, then back to Paris for the Robyn concert (“it’s like Pride squared”) before coming home to “binge Off Campus all by myself, sobbing.” It’s a hefty itinerary, alongside which he’s cooking up Season 2 of It Gets Worse and, naturally, “finding love and getting a brand deal.”

If you’ve never pictured yourself walking down the aisle in a white gown, consider this your sign to go with your gut. Black wedding dresses are a striking alternative for brides who want something unexpected and aren’t afraid to break with tradition. Opting for a darker palette is hardly a new idea, either: Sarah Jessica Parker famously wore a black Morgane Le Fay design for her 1997 nuptials, and Chloë Sevigny chose an all-black look for her 2020 city hall ceremony. More recently, Emma Roberts tapped Monique Lhuillier to design a black look for her after-party ensemble—proof that you can buck convention at any stage of your big day.

Moody as it may seem, a black wedding dress is also seasonless; the trick is finding one in a fabric that feels right for the setting and time of year. Thicker materials like velvet and heavy crepe are perfect for fall and winter fêtes, while silk and airy organza add a lighter touch for warm-weather celebrations (a breezy black mini on the beach is not taboo!). A two-tone design—say, a gown with an ivory bodice and inky skirt—offers the best of both worlds if you’re not quite ready to fully part with white.

But styling is where a midnight-hued gown really develops its personality. Classic pearls can soften a voluminous silhouette, while a cathedral-length veil dials up the drama. Black gemstones and metallic heels are also a considered pairing for an after-dark aesthetic. Just remember: If your dress is already heavily embellished, there’s no need to pile on the jewelry—your look is the party. Perhaps best of all, this is a versatile investment: A black wedding dress can easily become a closet staple post-vow exchange.

Ahead, discover 24 black wedding dresses from designer favorites like Vivienne Westwood, Wiederhoeft, Emilia Wickstead, and Simkhai.

Hosting a black-tie wedding? The sophistication of a black gown lends itself perfectly to this dress code, from structured A-lines to corseted columns and draped floor-length numbers. Go full fairy tale with McQueen, fashion-forward with Vivienne Westwood, or keep things simple with Taller Marmo.

Your guests aren’t the only ones who get to have fun with an experimental cocktail dress code; follow their lead with a shorter hemline or a splashy, fully beaded design. Complete your look with a bow-adorned hair clip (a playful veil alternative) and a pretty posy, and there’ll be no mistaking who the bride is.

Courthouse weddings are low-key by nature, leaving plenty of room to go big with your style. Style with a birdcage veil for a nod to tradition.

From a Tuscan villa to an English estate, a destination event can take you just about anywhere, and a black dress is the perfect canvas no matter the backdrop. A slip style in full noir is a timeless pick; though if you’re headed somewhere warm, an airy lace design by Tory Burch or an open-back midi from St. Agni is equally fitting.

Spinach Artichoke Baked Potatoes

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

This baked potato stuffed with spinach-artichoke dip is the ultimate solo comfort meal. Classic twice-baked potatoes require two trips to the oven to deliver a pillowy, gussied-up filling and crispy skins all in one bite. Here, I’ve streamlined the workflow with a microwave. By zapping your spuds to start, you can reduce the overall baking time without sacrificing texture. Pick a large russet (or Idaho) for the best results; their relatively high starch and low moisture content helps them bake up airy and light, not gummy.

The rich, velvety filling that melts over top comes together in moments while your spud cooks. Cream cheese and mozzarella pack in enough dairy richness for a satisfying cheese pull and mellow out the subtle bitterness of baby spinach and nutty tang of artichoke hearts. (It’s important that your cream cheese is room temperature before you mash it in with the veggies. If it’s still got a chill from the fridge, it won’t fully incorporate.) The Worcestershire sauce is optional, but just a few shakes from the bottle bring instant savory depth to the filling.

Most recipes serve multiple people. In Me, Myself, and Dinner, each dish serves one and only one. That's you! Because whether you live solo (almost 30% of Americans do) or are enjoying a night to yourself, sitting down to a meal should be something to look forward to.

Preheat oven to 425°. Poke 1 large russet potato (about 12 oz.) all over with a fork; rub with avocado or vegetable oil to coat and sprinkle with kosher salt. Microwave potato on a microwave-proof plate on high 5 minutes. Turn potato over and microwave on high until fork-tender, about 5 minutes more. Let potato sit until just cool enough to handle.

While potato is cooling, microwave 5 oz. baby spinach with 2 Tbsp. water in a medium microwave-proof glass bowl until wilted, about 2 minutes. Carefully press leaves to release as much liquid as possible, then drain off liquid. Coarsely chop spinach.

Mix 2 Tbsp. cream cheese, room temperature, in a medium bowl until smooth. Add spinach, 4 quartered artichoke hearts (from a 14-oz. can), coarsely chopped, 2 Tbsp. finely grated Parmesan, 2 Tbsp. coarsely grated mozzarella, ½ tsp. garlic powder, and ¼ tsp. Worcestershire sauce (if using) and fold to combine; season with salt and freshly ground pepper.

Slice potato in half lengthwise and lightly fluff flesh in each half. Set on a small foil-lined rimmed baking sheet. Divide spinach mixture between potato halves, piling high (don’t pack too tightly). Sprinkle more Parmesan over. Bake potato halves until very tender when squeezed and skin is wrinkled and crisp, 20–25 minutes. Let cool slightly before eating.
