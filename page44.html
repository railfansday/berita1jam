Scripts can’t run AI factories: Dell moves cluster management from the server to the rack

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As artificial intelligence clusters grow to supercomputing scale, the hard part of the AI factory is no longer buying hardware. It is keeping racks, switches, GPUs and cooling running once they are switched on.

That operational burden is reshaping what infrastructure providers sell. Dell Technologies Inc. has spent the past year pushing its AI Factory beyond servers and storage, and customers are now asking for best practices and better tools to stand up and run what they buy, according to Andrew Vaz (pictured, left), vice president of product management for the Telecom Systems Business at Dell.

“What we are seeing is a fundamental shift from procuring supply to actually standing up and running these very complicated AI clusters and data centers,” Vaz said. “One of the interesting things that we’ve seen is it’s actually now impossible to manage these things with just a few scripts, manually, in an old world manner. The toolsets are becoming more important.”

Vaz and Saurabh Kapoor (right), director of product management and strategy for AI compute and networking solutions at Dell, spoke with theCUBE Research’s John Furrier and Gemma Allen at SC26, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed supercomputing, rack-scale management, AI observability and why networking sits on the critical path for AI clusters. (* Disclosure below.)

Dell used the conference to introduce Dell OpenManage Rack Scale and Dell Fabric Manager, two tools for running AI infrastructure from deployment through daily operations. OpenManage Rack Scale manages 100,000 devices in its first release, up from 8,000 servers for its predecessor, OpenManage Enterprise, Kapoor explained. Systems such as Nvidia Corp.’s NVL72 racks bundle compute, power shelves, switches and coolant distribution units into one unit.

“OpenManage Enterprise, the previous product we had, was perfect for servers. Server was the unit of compute,” Kapoor said. “But you have seen in the last 18 months, the world has moved very quickly to larger ecosystems and infrastructures. Rack is the new unit of play.”

Visibility is the other half of that equation. Dell’s iDRAC controller pulls more than 320 metrics from every server, and an AI-assist chatbot can query that telemetry through local large language models, according to Kapoor. A single cooling leak can damage millions of dollars of gear, so every layer has to report in, Vaz noted.

“It could be something as simple as, ‘Hey, this transceiver’s about to die that you can’t diagnose with metrics you have today,'” Vaz said. “You need to use AI to go look at it. You need telemetry off the system to go collect all of that, correlate it, run your algorithms on them, train it and understand what’s happening.”

The network is where those pressures concentrate. Dell Fabric Manager turns validated designs built with Nvidia and Advanced Micro Devices Inc. into deployable blueprints across different network operating systems and silicon. Agentic AI is also reshaping traffic, and enterprise bandwidth is outrunning a projected 3.5-fold increase, Vaz noted.

“We’re getting blown away by it. It’s more like nine to 10x,” Vaz said. “It’s going everywhere: east, west, north, south.”

The company also relies heavily on Amazon, Google and Microsoft for computing infrastructure and sales. These companies are both investors in Anthropic and competitors developing their own AI models, creating potential conflicts of interest.

These commitments and partnerships will be important considerations for investors assessing Anthropic’s long-term costs and financial risks.

Some people inside the Trump administration are quietly criticising Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, Semafor reported on Wednesday. The issue is his comments on a run of US tech outages, and a trip he took after last week’s flight ground stop.

Prediction: $200 a Month Invested in SpaceX Could Be Worth This Much by 2035

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Regular contributions can build a meaningful position, while several return scenarios show what a monthly SpaceX investment could become.

A $200 monthly investment in Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX +3.16%) might not seem like much. After all, this is a company valued at almost $1.9 trillion. However, if there's one thing that investing has taught me, it's this: Small, regular contributions can add up over time.

Indeed, a $200 monthly contribution alone, before any gains or losses, will total $21,600 in just nine years. Yet the real power of investing isn't just in the contributions; it's also in the returns. Let's see what's possible for someone who puts those contributions to work in SpaceX stock.

First, let's drill down on the details of this hypothetical plan, as they'll become important later. Let's assume that each month, starting in January 2027, a person contributes $200. They use that money to purchase SpaceX stock (via fractional shares). This nine-year plan will run from January 2027 to December 2035, encompassing 108 monthly contributions totaling $21,600 over its full term.

To see what's possible, let's examine a few potential outcomes. For starters, SpaceX stock could deliver no return at all; in other words, a 0% annual return. In this highly improbable case, the account value would be equal to the contribution amount of $21,600. A more reasonable annual return figure might be the stock market's long-term average, 10%. In that case, the market value of the investment rises to $35,023. A 15% annual return results in $45,702. A bullish scenario of 25% annual returns results in $81,412. Finally, a bearish case: A negative 10% annual return results in a final figure of $14,244.

So which of these scenarios is most likely? The answer depends on growth. SpaceX is far more than simply a rocket company. Elon Musk views it as a package encompassing space launch, satellite connectivity, and advanced computing. These areas overlap: Space launch helps build satellite connectivity, while advanced computing makes launches safer, more efficient, and more frequent.

As for growth, SpaceX is growing by leaps and bounds. The company went public only in June 2026, but revenue growth has been exceptional so far. In its third-quarter earnings report, the company delivered 161% year-over-year revenue growth, fueled by its artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure.

According to Yahoo Finance, analysts expect SpaceX's 2027 revenue to grow to $181 billion, up nearly 270%. If the company can deliver growth at this level, the stock could more than justify its massive valuation. Moreover, profitability could soon be on the horizon. SpaceX's AI compute and its Starlink network both benefit from massive economies of scale. Such assets are very costly to build, but enormously profitable once operational. Consequently, Yahoo Finance estimates that SpaceX will generate $4.06 in earnings per share in 2027, equal to about $52 billion in net income. That would be a massive jump from the roughly $3 billion gain the company will likely post in 2026.

There are bearish scenarios for SpaceX, too. First off, the company's AI investments could backfire. Think of SpaceX as an AI landlord, renting out expensive data center capacity to companies like Alphabet and Anthropic. Therefore, if customers pull back on spending, SpaceX could be left holding very expensive, yet underutilized, data center real estate. In addition, political pushback on the growth of data centers and their energy consumption could further sap demand, leading to disappointing results for SpaceX.

Second, the company's launch business faces several risks. For one, launch delays would have a cascading effect on Starlink, as projections assume a transition from Falcon 9 to the reusable Starship system. If Starship can't deliver the anticipated Starlink V3 satellites, Starship's unit economics would suffer. What's more, even if Starship is otherwise ready to go, regulatory friction could plague its full rollout.

In other words, SpaceX's growth isn't a given; however, I remain optimistic about the company's ability to navigate these and other potential challenges.

Given its massive growth potential, it seems likely that SpaceX stock will deliver solid returns over the next nine years. Indeed, I believe it will outperform the market. That would put my estimate of its annual returns above 10%. To be conservative, let's assume a 15% annual return. Using the earlier calculation, a person could invest $200 a month in SpaceX stock and see it grow to around $46,000.

Granted, this is a hypothetical scenario, and SpaceX stock carries risk.

Nonetheless, a regular savings and investment routine is a clear way to build and grow a nest egg.

Latin America's mega-prisons: Can El Salvador's model work?

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

El Salvador's mega-prison has become a model for governments across Latin America seeking to combat organized crime. But experts question whether the approach is effective, sustainable or compatible with human rights.

Around 15,000 inmates and a capacity of up to 40,000, prison sentences of up to 1,000 years, strict security measures and an extreme isolation regime. Staged images show inmates with shaved heads and tattoos being subdued by heavily armed guards.

These are just some of the facts circulating about El Salvador's Terrorism Confinement Center (CECOT), a symbol of President Nayib Bukele's security policy and a possible model for other Latin American countries seeking to combat organized crime.

More than a dozen governments in the region have proposed building similar "mega-prisons," although most would have a far smaller capacity. A range of experts consulted by DW question the effectiveness and sustainability of these initiatives.

De facto mega-prisons — that is, overcrowded prisons with excessive inmate populations — existed in Latin America long before Bukele, says criminologist Jose Luis Perez Guadalupe, former president of the National Penitentiary Institute and former interior minister in Peru. Lurigancho Prison in Lima, for example, designed to hold 3,000 inmates, has housed nearly 11,000 for decades.

But ever since CECOT was launched in 2023, the concept of a "mega-prison" has been primarily linked to maximum-security facilities. For Olga Espinoza, an academic specializing in prison systems at the School of Government of the University of Chile, this association is problematic because it implies that the entire prison population requires "extremely severe" punishment.

According to Espinoza, placing the entire prison population in maximum-security facilities would amount to inefficient and extremely costly "over-intervention." These facilities require substantial investments in infrastructure, technology, specialized staff, anti-corruption measures and social reintegration programs.

It would also be ineffective because "international evidence indicates that when people with different profiles are placed in the same space, there is a high risk of the spread of criminal behavior," the Chilean expert adds.

Several criminal groups across the region even have their roots in overcrowded prisons: Tren de Aragua in Venezuela; Los Lobos, Los Choneros and Los Tiguerones in Ecuador; and Primeiro Comando da Capital (PCC) and Comando Vermelho (CV) in Brazil.

Even when the proposal involves simply building large-scale facilities, investing in a single structure may seem attractive given the difficulties of developing public infrastructure in Latin America.

However, "there is insufficient evidence to support the claim that building mega-prisons is more cost-effective," says Renato Rivera, a senior analyst at the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime (GI-TOC).

The United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners, adopted in 1955, "the recommendation has been to build medium-sized prisons housing between 1,000 and 2,000 inmates rather than mega-prisons," explains Perez Guadalupe, a professor at the Universidad del Pacifico in Lima.

"Mega-prisons, apart from being impersonal, can become extremely dangerous," says Guadalupe.  As an example, the GI-TOC's Rivera highlights the widely reported prison massacres in Ecuador in 2021 that took place in the country's largest and most populated facilities.

Experts acknowledge that mass incarceration has coincided with a dramatic decline in violence in El Salvador and has strengthened the public's perception of safety. Despite reports of arbitrary arrests, abuses and more than 500 deaths in state custody in the country, announcements of new mega-prisons project an image of a strong state confronting organized crime across the region.

However, they warn that this political and symbolic impact does not guarantee sustainable results. Achieving them would require a "clear commitment" to external oversight, human rights protections and social reintegration programs, which governments have yet to demonstrate, Espinoza says.

Perez Guadalupe is particularly critical of the Salvadoran model, describing it as "pure propaganda." He believes the images released from CECOT violate human dignity. He also argues that the state must demonstrate "moral superiority over those who break the law."

Although transnational organized crime represents a growing challenge across the region, the Peruvian criminologist considers El Salvador's case exceptional.

Following the mass arrests carried out under the state of emergency introduced by Bukele, the country has one of the highest incarceration rates in the world. CECOT was designed to permanently detain members of gangs such as MS-13 and Barrio 18, which are considered extremely dangerous.

However, the reality of other Latin American prison systems is different. The question is not where to lock people deprived of their liberty away forever, but rather "how we want them to leave: worse or better," Perez Guadalupe argues, pointing out that most will eventually return to society.

In theory, the official alternative is provided by the UN Nelson Mandela Rules for the treatment of prisoners, updated in 2015. They establish principles for modern prison management, including respect for human dignity, an absolute prohibition on torture and degrading treatment and social reintegration as a central goal of imprisonment.

In practice, Latin America has developed innovative approaches in this direction. These include the APAC model in Brazil, which operates facilities without guards, weapons or violence; Conflict Prevention and Resolution Committees in Argentina; and the Alternative Criminal Justice Center in the Mexican state of Sonora.

Experts also point to the need for maximum-security prisons for specific inmate profiles, but on a smaller scale and with more precise classification of the prison population.

Finally, they emphasize that prisons, including mega-prisons, primarily address the consequences of organized crime rather than its root causes. Poverty, social exclusion and a lack of opportunities require public policies focused on employment, education and social integration to reduce the conditions that enable crime to expand in one of the world's most unequal regions.

The KMI panel has five members, made up of three former players and/or coaches, plus one representative each from the Premier League and PGMOL (Now ‘Pro Ref’).

It was set up at the start of the 2022/23 season to give an independent assessment of decision-making rather than relying on the views of PGMOL or the clubs themselves.

The judgement is intended to provide an arm’s-length assessment of all major match incidents but gives weight to the decision of the referee in subjective situations.

Taking into account the laws of the game and the Premier League’s interpretation.

The Premier League Key Match Incidents Panel have looked at the Fulham v Newcastle United match that was played on Saturday 17 November 2026 and voted on these two incidents.

On 33 minutes: Dan Burn goal disallowed by the referee because of a challenge by Yoane Wissa on Timothy Castagne. The Var not asking the referee on the pitch to review his decision.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Build a Rocket Boy has officially entered administration, following reports in September that the MindsEye developer was permanently closing. Unfortunately for co-CEOs Leslie Benzies and Mark Gerhard, Build a Rocket Boy owes a lengthy list of creditors roughly £62 million ($81 million). So, how does BARB plan to recoup these losses? You guessed it; they’re suing the saboteurs.

As spotted by RedbullCola on ResetEra, an administrators’ proposal—written by Opus Restructuring LLP—went live on Build a Rocket Boy’s Companies House page this morning. It is quite the read, detailing the MindsEye developer’s full list of creditors and exactly how it plans to repay them.

Its two largest debts are owed to the Diamater Dislocation Master Fund, a Cayman Islands hedge fund, and Marvel Rivals developer NetEase. Build a Rocket Boy owes the former roughly £4.2 million ($5.5 million), and the latter over £36 million ($47 million). It also owes ChatGPT owner OpenAI $25, which is a curiously small sum to include.

Of course, the real question is how BARB plans to repay their creditors. The answer: via vengeful litigation. According to the administrator’s proposal, Build a Rocket Boy is still pursuing legal action against the “saboteurs” that co-CEO Mark Gerhard alluded to back in July ’25, a mysterious number of people who he claimed were deliberately working to destroy MindsEye‘s success. In a leaked video, Gerhard stated that he thinks “a very big American company” called Ritual Network, a YouTube and TikTok management agency based in the United Kingdom, are to blame. Content creator Cyber Boi, who was threatened with a “formal cease and desist demand” in MindsEye’s Discord server for releasing a video titled “Leslie Benzies is in the Epstein Files,” is represented by Ritual Network.

Curiously, the administrator’s proposal states that these “detailed investigations” against “a number of third parties” are considered “to be the largest asset of the administration.” That is, of course, if said litigation is successful.

Speaking of litigation, in April, 2026, the Independent Workers’ Union of Great Britain (IWGB) announced that several former MindsEye developers had initiated legal action against Build a Rocket Boy for allegedly installing the “invasive surveillance software” Teramind on their work devices.

Why is this relevant all of a sudden? Well, the administrator’s proposal states that Build a Rocket Boy’s “computer equipment that was utilized by staff” has been “wiped” by the company’s IT department. Curious.
