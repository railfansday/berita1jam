Muse can now place calls to U.S. businesses — if you pay

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Meta’s Muse, which now has over 10 million users, the company said last month, has been working to make consumer AI a mainstream product for everyday users. Placing calls is one of the ways it aims to get there, as it could handle another task on people’s to-do lists.

Notes Alexandr Wang, Meta’s Chief AI officer and head of its Superintelligence Labs division, the feature could be used to take care of calls people don’t want to make, like those to their cable company, the DMV, or to a restaurant that doesn’t offer online reservations.

Previously, only a select number of Muse early adopters were able to try this feature after asking the AI for access. Now, anyone who pays for Muse can make this type of request. Currently, Muse subscription plans start at $20 per month for 500 million Muse tokens per week. Heavier AI users can opt to pay $100 per month for 3 billion tokens.

Muse is not the only AI agent able to place calls to businesses; the startup Instinct has been offering a similar option, as has Google’s Gemini. However, while the AI tools are now certainly capable of handling the task, the reception on the other end of the line can be mixed. Many businesses and receptionists hang up on calls from AI agents as soon as they identify that it’s not a human on the phone. But with AI now better able to mimic a real human voice, being able to tell the difference between a customer and an AI working on the customer’s behalf will get tougher.

Despite the challenges, this feature is one that could appeal to consumers who have grown frustrated with the state of customer service over the years. For too long, customers have been forced to navigate complex phone trees, and then sit on hold waiting for a human representative to pick up. If they’re lucky, that person can actually help them, and not transfer them to another department. Now, those consumers could potentially use their AI, with its infinite patience, for this tedious task instead.

While calls remain a paid-only feature for the time being, the recently added option to make a Muse email address will soon roll out to free users, Wang said this week.

AI Is Dividing Product Designers: Exploring vs. Building

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Design Engineer at Matter Labs (creators of ZKsync and Prividium). Creator of DecentWealth, ScrollChess, and a few other apps.

Design Engineer at Matter Labs (creators of ZKsync and Prividium). Creator of DecentWealth, ScrollChess, and a few other apps.

Design Engineer at Matter Labs (creators of ZKsync and Prividium). Creator of DecentWealth, ScrollChess, and a few other apps.

A week ago, I attended PUSH UX in Munich, Europe's largest conference on product design and user experience, featuring speakers from Anthropic, Wise, Google, IKEA, n8n, and other prominent organizations. On my way home, I kept thinking about one recurring theme: product design teams are approaching AI from fundamentally different perspectives.

Some are cautiously experimenting with AI while trying to fit it into established processes. Others have already integrated it into their daily workflows, connecting research, prototyping, design, and code with fewer handoffs. The difference goes beyond tool adoption. It reflects how teams understand their work, organize collaboration, and measure product value.

Paulo Oliveira from IKEA highlighted a fundamental problem with the way organizations approach AI:

"Most efforts start with tools, copilots, and automation, and mistake faster cycles for value. The result is a pile of pilots nobody adopted, and a lot of people wondering what happened to the work they were good at. The alternative: to build something that fits, lasts, and makes an organization better at what it already does well. That starts by refusing the AI first, AI everything instinct."

I think this captures one of the central challenges in product design today.

Adding AI to an existing workflow can make individual tasks faster without improving the product or the way a team operates. Organizations can accumulate tools and experiments while leaving the underlying processes unchanged.

Meaningful adoption starts with understanding where teams lose time, which handoffs create friction, and where AI can improve how products are researched, designed, built, and validated.

For years, product design and software development operated as distinct stages. Designers worked in Figma, documented interactions, prepared specifications, and handed their work to engineers for implementation. This division made sense when building and testing functional interfaces required substantial engineering resources. AI-assisted development is changing that relationship.

After years of designing fintech and blockchain-based products, I kept encountering the same limitation: I could design an experience without having full control over how it was implemented.

About a year ago, I started building websites and applications from scratch with AI tools. As I described in my previous HackerNoon article, Claude Code Finally Made Me an Engineer After 10 Years in Figma, my workflow shifted from almost entirely Figma to roughly 90% Claude Code.

Working closer to production code lets me build, test, and iterate on interfaces directly, with fewer handoffs between design and engineering.

For product teams, this opens up several possibilities:

This doesn't mean every designer needs to become a full-time software engineer. Deep specialization remains essential, particularly in complex products. However, designers can work closer to implementation and contribute beyond the traditional boundaries of their roles.

Another exciting development is how AI is changing prototyping. Think about how difficult it used to be to create an interactive prototype for a specific device, such as an Apple Watch or an in-car infotainment system. Even without writing production code, designers can now use AI to build interactive prototypes tailored to specific hardware and explore how their ideas work in real-world contexts.

As AI makes prototyping, coding, and exploring alternatives easier, the focus of design work is shifting toward decision-making and product quality. Understanding whether it solves the right problem, meets user needs, and works reliably requires experience and critical judgment.

This is where faster workflows can create lasting value. Teams have more opportunities to test assumptions, explore alternatives, and identify problems before committing significant resources to a solution. However, individual productivity gains aren't enough. Organizations need to connect design, engineering, and product teams around shared workflows, clear quality standards, and meaningful evaluation of AI-generated output.

I'm particularly interested in how this can shape an AI-first product culture. The goal is to encourage experimentation while maintaining the technical rigor, security, and reliability that deep tech products demand.

Many designers argue that AI-generated product experiences lack quality. Are they right? Sometimes. Can the results improve? Absolutely.

AI-assisted research and discovery can produce shallow or generic results, especially when designers rely on AI without applying their own expertise and critical judgment. The quality of the outcome depends on how thoughtfully people guide, evaluate, and refine the process. Julia Bastian from Alasco explored this challenge in a session on AI discovery slop, examining how to recognize and address low-quality AI-generated research and insights.

The PUSH UX conference reinforced how differently the design community is responding to AI. Some teams are still finding their footing, while others are already changing how they work. My own experience has made me more comfortable working across design, product strategy, and engineering, but every team will need to find its own approach.

The important shift is to reconsider established workflows, explore where new capabilities can make a difference, and take greater ownership of business outcomes.

Michael John Roberts Dies: ‘TripTank’, ‘BoJack Horseman’ Animator, Director & Producer Was 48

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Michael John Roberts, an animator and director of such series as Comedy Central’s TripTank and Netflix’s BoJack Horseman, died of glioblastoma Wednesday, November 11, in Los Angeles. He was 48.

His death was announced by his family, including his wife, the One Life to Live and Gossip Girl actor Bree Williamson. The announcement states that Roberts died peacefully at home surrounded by his family.

“With heavy hearts, we share the passing of Michael John Roberts,” the announcement reads. “Michael saw the world a little differently. A gifted artist with an extraordinary talent, he could find something worth exploring in what others might overlook. His creativity was not limited to what he put on paper; it shaped the way he thought, questioned, and understood the world around him.”

Born December 20, 1977, in Toronto, where he was raised, Roberts got his start as an animator in the late 1990s and early 2000s on such children’s series as the animated Angela Anaconda and the animation-live action hybrid The Hoobs, both produced by Canada’s Decode Entertainment (The Hoobs was co-created and co-produced by The Jim Henson Company). Watch on Deadline

Although starting in children’s television, Roberts would become best known for his work on animated comedy geared to adults. He co-created and directed Comedy Central’s 2014-2016 sketch comedy series TripTank, which featured voice work from Bob Odenkirk, Larry David, Niecy Nash, Zach Galifianakis and others, and he animated the 2015 opening sequence of BoJack Horseman (he would direct two episodes of the Netflix series).

He subsequently directed episodes of, among others, the Bill Burr series F Is For Family, the TBS series Final Space, MrBeast Lab, the 2023 Apple TV series Strange Planet and, most recently, a 2026 episode of Masters of the Universe: Tales From Eternia. As a producer, his credits included the 2018 John Cena-Kat Dennings YouTube Red series Dallas & Robo, the 2024 series Hot Wheels Let’s Race and a 2026 TV special from the Hot Wheels franchise.

In addition to his wife, who played the popular character Jessica Buchanan on One Life to Live from 2002 to 2012, Roberts is survived by son McGreggor; sister Jane Roberts; niece Gwen; nephew Vaughan; and cousin Hrothgar Cashmore, whom he saw as a brother.

“We were supposed to have more time,” his family wrote. “More conversations over a good cup of coffee. More spirited debates about matters both profound and wonderfully insignificant. More laughter, more stories, more music and more ordinary moments that would someday become extraordinary memories.

“Although his illness came suddenly and the time that followed was heartbreakingly short, it cannot define the man he was. His life is better remembered through what he created, the conversations he inspired, the friendships he nurtured, and the love he gave so freely to those closest to him.”

‘La Bola Negra’ Creatives Faced a Ticking Clock to Finish the Film for Cannes, But Waiting for Snow Almost Kept the Ending From Being Shot

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

“La Bola Negra” editor Alberto Gutiérrez knew he was up against time — the plan was that they wanted to take the film to the Cannes Film Festival, and the deadline was March.

Filming began in November, and Gutiérrez was editing the movie. But there was one problem. The film didn’t have its ending, and it was a pivotal scene.

Directed by Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrossi (known as Los Javis), the film, now playing in theaters, is based on an unfinished play of the same name written by Spanish playwright Federico García Lorca in 1936. Tragically, Lorca never got the chance to finish the play. He was shot dead by nationalist forces after being imprisoned at the beginning of the Spanish Civil War. Related Stories 'Nobody Wants This' Season 3 Opens With 6.2 Million Views in Four Days Gwyneth Paltrow's 'Goop Guide' Show Coming to Netflix and Spotify, in Which She Explains 'How to Live Goop' in 'All the Ways That Can Be Defined'

The film navigates three timelines. In one, Carlos (Milo Quifes) is a young man trying to join a casino group in Granada, Spain. His membership is decided by a vote using white balls for acceptance and black balls for rejection. He is rejected because he is gay. At the heart of his story is conflict, as he has to make choices in life and deal with his family.

When he goes to the casino to plead his case, he denies he’s gay. He wakes up covered in snow. As is thematic with Lorca’s poems, snow means death. He hears his grieving mother and asks her for a second chance after realizing that he’s dead. The snow is a metaphor about the cycle of life.

That key part with snow had yet to be shot. The filmmakers were hoping it would snow in Madrid in December. It didn’t. Gutiérrez says, “We went into January thinking it would snow.” Again, it didn’t.

But they were determined to include the snow sequence and not fake it because snow was integral to Lorca’s plays.

The clock was ticking on whether they would meet the deadline to finish the film and submit it to Cannes.

It still hadn’t snowed in Madrid. But it did snow in Granada, the southern region of the country. They decided they would go there to shoot it. “But it was February and we had one month before the Cannes deadline,” Gutiérrez says.

Gutiérrez explains, “We couldn’t do screenings of the movie because we couldn’t get people to see two hours of a movie and then tell them to watch the ending in theaters.”

The team had one month to work together to pull it off. “We said, ‘We have to trust each other and our instincts because if we start listening to everybody’s opinion on the film, we won’t make it to Cannes.’”

They worked around the clock in that last month to finish the film.

One person they did trust was Penélope Cruz. In the film, she plays Nené Romero, a cabaret singer, and they brought her into the equation.

But Gutiérrez felt immense pressure. “I had this huge responsibility because the script was so good, and I had to be as good as the rest of the film,” he recalls, crying in the next room as Cruz watched. “When the film stopped, someone knocked at my door. Javier Ambrosi told me, ‘I want you to meet somebody,’ and suddenly Penelope entered the room. She asked me, ‘Oh, but why are you crying?’ I said, ‘I’m not sure about the movie. I’m not sure if this is the best editing.’ She told me to stop crying because it was perfect.’”

With Cruz’s stamp of approval, they decided to send “La Bola Negra” to Cannes and it was accepted.

“We had two weeks to make changes, and I remember even then we were not sure about it,” he says.

Gutiérrez remembers the film premiering at the Lumiere Theater, with Tilda Swinton seated in front of him. He laughs, “I was watching the movie through the eyes of Tilda Swinton for two and a half hours. And I thought, ‘OK, this is the movie. This is what we’ve made.’”

After the film ended, it received a 16-minute standing ovation and the Best Director Award.

“We decided there that this was the final edit of the movie,” Gutiérrez says. “I think it’s good because maybe if we had continued editing, we wouldn’t have this special movie that we have now.”

“La Bola Negra” will stream on Netflix on Dec. 2.

Emmylou Harris, Sheryl Crow, Elvis Costello, More Celebrate Gram Parsons at the Troubadour

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Psych-country pioneer Gram Parsons’ induction into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame this week has been a long time coming. Few cult heroes have had as much of a lasting impact as Parsons, who in the decades since his death in 1973, has inspired literally generations of musicians, despite never having achieved commercial success. That was evident in the lineup for “A Song for Gram,” a tribute concert held Wednesday at the Troubadour in Los Angeles, which featured disciples and devotees of the “cosmic American” sound Parsons pioneered.

The concert (produced by Parsons’ daughter Polly along with Vector Management, the Rock Hall, and Rolling Stone co-editor in chief Shirley Halperin) brought together collaborators (Emmylou Harris), longtime champions (Elvis Costello, Sheryl Crow), and acolytes (Jesse Welles, Margo Price, Madison Cunningham), alongside musical scholars like Jim Lauderdale and Black Crowes, who regularly perform songs from the Parsons canon. With “cosmic accompaniment” by stellar house band Beachwood Sparks, the totality of talent channeled the singer-songwriter’s magic while exploring the entirety of his deep-but-short catalog. Selections came from his solo work and his time in the Flying Burrito Brothers, the Byrds, and early days with the International Submarine Band.

Polly Parsons kicked off the night with a speech that set out the vision of Gram’s music. “He looked at the landscape of American song,” she said from the stage, “and saw a map where all the roads connected … It was a seamless tapestry that wove together the deep, raw heartbreak of traditional country, the spiritual urgency of soul, the gritty energy of rock, and the storytelling of folk.”

Though each of those touch points was felt at some point in the night, as is often the case with multi-artist tribute concerts, the lower-billed, younger artists felt most imbued with Parsons’ spirit, starting with Grahame Lesh, who opened the musical portion with the International Submarine Band warbler “Luxury Liner.” Lesh — the son of Grateful Dead bassist Phil Lesh — wasn’t the only next-gen kin paying tribute: Harper Simon, Paul’s son, was on the bill as well, leading his way through a charismatic (and poignant for the times) “One Hundred Years From Now” and, later on, “Still Feeling Blue.” Editor’s picks The 250 Greatest Albums of the 21st Century So Far The 100 Best TV Episodes of All Time The 500 Greatest Albums of All Time 100 Best Movies of the 21st Century

Neo-folk songwriter Jesse Welles, for his part, said he realized that country music could also include rock energy by discovering Parsons’ catalog. His solo version of “Hickory Wind” was Dylanesque in the best way — complete with harmonica — while his duet with Margo Price on “Return of the Grievous Angel,” faithful to the original with Emmylou Harris, sounded like the two walked off the record onto the stage. Local favorite Madison Cunningham also blew the audience away: hitting her upper register during “Hearts on Fire” seemed nearly impossible, while her nylon fingerpicking on “You Don’t Miss Your Water” was the most impressive guitar playing on a night that included a bevy of Hummingbirds, B-benders, and dreadnoughts.

Considering the L.A. setting, where Parsons spent his later years living and recording, the sweetest moment of the night was surprise guest Sheryl Crow, who describes singing “Juanita” with Emmylou Harris as being “like heaven.” The duo had recorded a version of the song for 1999’s  Return of the Grievous Angel: A Tribute to Gram Parsons, and nearly three decades later, their voices blended so beautifully that a rapt crowd turned silent to give their full attention. (During soundcheck earlier in the day, Crow marveled at sharing the stage with Harris, calling the experience “epic,” to which Harris replied, “You’re no slouch yourself.”)

Harris was rightly the performer emeritus of the night, crooning alongside everyone from Elvis Costello (“Sleepless Nights”) to Jim Lauderdale (“The King of Broken Hearts,” recorded in 1991 as a duet with Harris) to Mike Campbell, who conjured the spirit of his late bandmate Tom Petty for “Love Hurts,” another highlight of the night. Even when she wasn’t onstage, Harris’ presence was known: both Price and Grace Potter played songs without her help but clearly within her influence, their big vocals rousing the room on “Christine’s Tune” for the former and “Ooh Las Vegas” for the latter. Naturally, the night ended with two big group sing-alongs: “Wild Horses,” famously recorded by Parsons before the Stones made it a hit, with verses traded between Chris Robinson, Lauderdale, and a finally-on-key Costello; and “In My Hour of Darkness,” which felt like a generational handoff as the younger performers took the lead, with dozens of people onstage leaning into the cosmos, sending one of rock’s great, underappreciated voices finally into the stratosphere.
