Mitsubishi Electric Research Lab CEO on How He Reached the C-Suite

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthony Vetro’s journey from multimedia pioneer to executive

Kathy Pretz is the editor in chief of The Institute, IEEE's member publication

At the 2025 IEEE Vision, Innovation, and Challenges Summit, IEEE Fellow Anthony Vetro discusses the need for open innovation models to keep pace with rapid technological change taking place at Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories.

The trajectory from researcher to top executive at a leading R&D organization doesn’t happen overnight. It took Anthony Vetro nearly 30 years to climb the corporate ladder at Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories, in Cambridge, Mass. He joined MERL in 1996, right out of college, and he has worked there ever since.

Vetro probably is best known for his research in multimedia signal processing, focusing on video compression.

He became an IEEE Fellow in 2011 for his contributions to video coding, three-dimensional television, and multimedia adaptation.

Mitsubishi Electric has moved away from making televisions. It now focuses on developing systems including ones used for defense, electronics, the energy industry, and space exploration.

His ascension up the executive ranks began in 2018, when he was named a vice president and director of MERL. In 2023 he was appointed its president and CEO.

He also works closely with the company’s new U.S.-based innovation facility in Cambridge. And he recently took on a dual role as deputy head of Mitsubishi Electric’s global R&D activities, including in Asia and Europe.

Vetro credits IEEE with helping him develop his management skills after he served in several IEEE Signal Processing Society leadership positions. He also learned from his key roles in establishing international standards for digital image formats and video compression, he says.

“Between my IEEE activities, standards work, and some of the business shifts in Mitsubishi Electric, I eventually graduated to more management leadership roles,” he says.

Vetro is the son of immigrants. His father came to the United States from Italy and his mother from Sweden when they were both in their early 20s. They settled in New York City, on Staten Island.

Although neither parent went to college, his father stressed the importance of education to his son at an early age.

“My father started in the mailroom and worked his way up to eventually managing communication accounts and infrastructure for large financial companies,” he says. “My mother worked in banks, first as a teller and later doing bank reconciliation after pursuing some professional certifications.

“Through their jobs, they saw people with college degrees getting better positions and promotions, so education was something really ingrained in me.”

Vetro says he developed a passion for mathematics and engineering in middle school. He was accepted into the Staten Island Technical High School, a specialized pre-engineering institution. There he solidified his interest in pursuing a career in the field.

“A lot of students didn’t know what they wanted to major in at college, but I knew fairly early,” he says.

He was accepted into the accelerated bachelor’s and master’s engineering degree programs at the Polytechnic Institute of Brooklyn (now New York University’s Tandon engineering school). He began his bachelor’s degree program in 1992 and the master’s program in 1994.

“As part of the master’s degree program, I needed to do a thesis,” he says. “I wasn’t sure exactly what it would be on. Soon after taking my first course on image processing, I was certain that was an area I wanted to work in.”

It was an interesting time, he says, because broadcast television was going through a major transformation, shifting from analog to digital during the early 1990s. It was long before streaming services, he notes.

“I feel that industry-level engagement in IEEE is very vital to the organization in maintaining high relevance in the community and fulfilling its mission of fostering technological innovation and excellence for the benefit of humanity.”

“The idea of being able to come up with compact ways to represent this very rich information was really appealing to me,” he says. “I’m also a visual learner. I liked seeing images, and there was some nice math behind them, too.”

After he earned both degrees in 1996, Mitsubishi Electric hired him for its technical staff. He conducted research on multimedia signal processing, focusing on video compression.

“The opportunity to work on projects around digital TV broadcasting and image processing attracted me,” he says.

He conducted research in video conferencing, Internet and mobile streaming, and video coding technology, widely used for broadcasting, DVDs, and Blu-ray discs. He developed several technologies including digital TV receivers and displays, surveillance and camera-monitoring systems, automotive equipment, and satellite imaging systems.

Another advantage of joining the company, he says, was that it allowed him to pursue a doctorate in electrical engineering, also from Polytechnic, while working.

“That really sealed the deal,” he says. He received the degree in 2001.

After six years as a technical staff member, he was promoted in 2007 to manage the group working on digital video coding, processing, and streaming. Seven years later, Vetro became its deputy director, a top-level management position that he held until 2018.

“After working for about 10 years exclusively on video compression processing technology, there were some shifts in our company’s business. Its overall strategy began to change,” he says, “so I became interested in broadening my experience beyond video.”

He was asked to lead the lab’s speech and audio team. It was more development-oriented, he says, and focused on vehicle infotainment and speech interfaces before Alexa and Siri existed. He pivoted the team’s focus more toward research and established strategic paths tailored to emerging opportunities within the organization. Recognizing emerging trends in sensing and information security, he formed groups that worked on them.

To help spur innovation between Mitsubishi Electric and its U.S. customers, the company in June opened Serendie Street Boston, its first digital transformation hub in the United States. It’s colocated with MERL.

The new office is modeled after the first Serendie Street collaboration space, which Mitsubishi Electric launched in 2024 in Yokohama, Japan.

The word serendie is a combination of serendipity and digital engineering, according to the company’s website. Serendie is the digital platform designed to foster collaborations between data, people, and technology, it says. The space where people interact to extract ideas from the data is called “the street.”

The platform brings together researchers, startups, customers, and partners to identify new products, test them, run pilot programs, and scale them up.

Vetro says he’s excited about the endeavor.

“Serendie Street [Boston] aims to strengthen the bridge between our global R&D efforts and real-world industry challenges. The hub is intended to bring our partners and customers together to engage in more cocreation activities, where we listen to their problems and work together to solve them,” he says. “I think the company has come to recognize that we can’t just do it all internally by ourselves.”

The technologies being worked on include robotics, energy solutions such as integrating renewable energy into the grid, and more energy-efficient data center systems.

The decision to colocate the hub with MERL offices was deliberate, Vetro says.

“The area has a very rich ecosystem,” he says. “We have Harvard, MIT, and many other universities a stone’s throw away.”

Vetro joined IEEE in 1992 as a student member during an event the Polytechnic’s student branch was holding. He enjoyed reading the IEEE Potentials student member publication and other periodicals, he says.

“In the earliest stages of my career, IEEE was a place where I could attend conferences, read journals, and stay on top of the latest research and trends,” he says. “Over the years, I was able to build a professional network, which has been helpful. That facilitated a lot of research collaboration with university professors attending the conferences.

“In more recent years, IEEE has provided a great platform for industry to exhibit thought leadership and discuss real-world problems we’re facing. I think these discussions may have even set some research directions among the academic community.”

Vetro is an active volunteer for the IEEE Signal Processing Society. He has chaired several of its technical committees and conferences, and he served as an associate editor for some of its journals.

He says he’s most proud of establishing the society’s industry board and serving as its inaugural chair. The board formed a corporate engagement program, launched the Industry Signals newsletter, developed short courses tailored to industry, and holds career fairs at the society’s flagship conferences.

Vetro also had a hand in MERL sponsoring two IEEE technical field awards: the Flanagan Speech and Audio Processing Award and the Fourier Award for Signal Processing.

“Industry-level engagement in IEEE is really very vital to the organization in maintaining its relevance to the community and fulfilling its mission of fostering technological innovation and excellence for the benefit of humanity,” he says.

As ubiquitous as the sun is to us today, our star is not eternal; one day, albeit in billions of years, it will die. While massive stars die in supernova explosions, the sun and stars of similar mass die quieter deaths. But that doesn't mean these stars completely go out with a whimper.

New observations collected using the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA), the world's largest radio telescope comprised of 66 antennas in northern Chile, reveal that the sun's death throes may be different from what scientists had initially believed.

NASA Jet Propulsion Lab (JPL) researcher Raghvendra Sahai, who led the investigation, explained the shape the ALMA observations took: "It's essentially a physical snapshot of an event that, until now, we've mostly inferred from theoretical models. We've caught, for the first time, a star's final nuclear heartbeat, frozen in gas."

Sun-like stars "die" when they run out of hydrogen in their cores. This means that the fusion of hydrogen to helium ceases in the stellar core, and because this nuclear process is a star's main energy source, the star no longer has the outward push to support itself against the inward push of its own gravity.

As the star's core collapses to create a stellar remnant called a white dwarf, the outer layers where hydrogen is still fusing to form helium swell out. This expansion leads to a relatively short-lived "red giant" phase. Eventually, these outer layers will cool and disperse, leaving the central white dwarf surrounded by a so-called "planetary nebula" shell of debris.

During the red giant phase, as the stellar core is surrounded by shells of hydrogen and helium, these layers are occasionally rocked by flares in nuclear activity called thermal pulses. During these episodes, stellar material is flung into space.

To better understand this phase in stellar evolution, the team focused ALMA on a star called IRAS 06530-0213, located around 12,000 light-years away in the constellation Monoceros. This revealed a giant ring of gas that appears to be a frozen fossil of the star's last thermonuclear flare-up. This thermonuclear fossil rings an hourglass-shaped planetary nebula in the act of forming.

"Most of us think of stars as unchanging points of light, but this is a real-time snapshot of a star's death throes," Sahai said. "It's the brief, violent transition between a bloated red giant and a glowing planetary nebula, frozen mid-action."

The team was able to determine that this gas shell of IRAS 06530-0213 is around half a light-year wide and was ejected into space around 6,500 years ago. It represents the first direct evidence of the last thermal pulse before this kind of star enters the final phase of its life.

The team thinks that for around 2,000 years after the pulse, the star lost mass at a lower rate. This mass loss then increased sharply over the following 1,000 years. This led to the creation of a lumpy shell of gas now seen as a tangle of material filaments around the dying star. The fact that these filaments have a spiral shape could indicate that IRAS 06530-0213 has an unseen partner orbiting it, stirring the gas.

Meanwhile, the hourglass-shaped nebula immediately around the star began to take shape around 700 years ago, meaning that the red giant phase for IRAS 06530-0213 only ended very recently, in cosmic terms at least.

There are also unexpected elements to the ALMA observations of IRAS 06530-0213.

The team discovered an unexpected abundance of heavy versions, or isotopes, of elements like carbon, oxygen and nitrogen around IRAS 06530-0213.

This doesn't conform to models of how stars like IRAS 06530-0213 forge elements and mix them into their surroundings, though similar signatures have been seen around odd stellar objects, with violent bursts of nuclear fusion thought to be the cause.As well as seeing something unexpected, the researchers didn't detect something they expected to observe.

Dying stars like IRAS 06530-0213 have previously been known to have companions, typically sitting with them inside compact disks of dust. These disks are indirect evidence of material being dragged from the close companion star to the dying star. This is a process scientists have used to explain jet-sculpted hourglass shapes.

However, ALMA failed to detect such disk around IRAS 06530. The team suggests that this could be because a disk existed, albeit briefly, during which time it powered a short burst of high-speed jets that helped sculpt the hourglass, following this the disk simply faded away.

"Dying stars so often have hidden companions that reshape their ejected material into intricate, bipolar or multipolar forms instead of simple spheres," Sahai concluded. "IRAS 06530 shows that the story is more complicated than the tidy explanation we've been using."The team's research was published on Friday (Nov. 20) in The Astrophysical Journal.

Inside WNBA Star Cameron Brink and Ben Felter’s Stanford Wedding—and Her Four Bridal Looks

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

WNBA basketball player Cameron Brink and rower-turned-robotics founder Ben Felter were the rare college students who were able to find love at the height of Covid. Since they were athletes, the Stanford freshmen were in the tiny group allowed on campus during the fall of 2020. “The only people I socialized with were my basketball teammates—until the upperclassmen on my team had us fill out an ‘athlete mingle survey,’” Cameron says.

She begrudgingly filled out the form, was matched with Ben, and later received an email with the subject, “attempting to mingle.” In the message, Ben wrote: “I wanted to chat you up but thought DMs were a bit creepy, so here’s my number.” Cameron—who goes by Cam—says she jumped up and down in her room, elated that he was gentlemanly enough to reach out with his number.

“From there, we had three dates where Ben showed me his hometown of Palo Alto, and he asked me to be his girlfriend five days before we won a national championship,” says Cam. “He is my forever good luck charm.” She adds her friends and teammates Lexie and Lacie Hull were the ones who paired them together and is “forever grateful for their vision.”

Their relationship grew as they graduated college and Cam had begun playing as the Los Angeles Sparks’ forward. On September 30, 2024, Cam was on a trip to Paris for fashion week, and Ben knew it was the perfect opportunity to propose. “I snuck over from California to surprise her with the ring,” he says. “She thought she was going to an important after-party, but really, it was just me waiting for her on the terrace of the Shangri-La Hotel overlooking the Eiffel Tower that was shining with pink lights that night.” At first, Cam was so stunned by the view that she didn’t realize there was no party. “I’ll never forget the expression of pure surprise on her face when it finally sank in that it was me standing in front of her,” remembers Ben. “I told her that it is my purpose to make her happy before getting on one knee and asking her to marry me. She fell to the ground crying and luckily said ‘yes.’”

The couple always knew they wanted to tie the knot at Stanford’s Memorial Church. “Walking past the ornate mosaics on my way to class was always the best part of my day,” says Cam. At first, the bride was nervous that they would have to be on a waiting list for up to four years, but luckily, the university gives alumni a shorter waiting period. The couple set the date for November 14, 2026, with a reception at the Ritz-Carlton, Half Moon Bay, just 30 minutes away.

Cam says she took on the nickname “bridechilla” in her engagement period, attributing her details-focused planner Kristi Lee for making everything so seamless. “Other than working with the church’s clergy, daydreaming with my mom, and scrolling with Ben most nights on Pinterest for inspiration, we were lucky enough to have our family and wedding planner to carry most of the load,” she says. “I guess I always knew it would be beautiful because I have the best circle of humans imaginable that helped the vision come to life.”

Another MVP on the wedding team? “My stylist Shiona Turini was our North Star when it came to what both Ben and I wore,” says Cam. “When she agreed to help me find my dream wedding dress, I never knew it would end with two custom looks from Danielle Frankel, the designer I have been bookmarking since my sophomore year of college.” Cam first tried on styles at Frankel’s Los Angeles salon and found two silhouettes she loved. Frankel herself helped collaborate on customizations. “We ended up perfectly mixing what I envisioned with Danielle’s additions and creating a gown that felt timeless yet extravagant,” the bride explains. For the ceremony, Cam would wear a silk wool deep-V column dress with crinkle organza godets that helped form a full, bubble skirt. Later at the reception, she would change into a column gown crafted with an exaggerated neckline and textural crinkle organza strips. Each gown was accessorized with Manolo Blahnik satin heels and desert diamond studs by Irene Jewels.

Frankel also helped dress the groom for his walk down the aisle with a custom single-breasted tuxedo jacket and bow tie. “The silk lapels were the same fabric as my dress, and it paired together so perfectly,” says Cam. The bow tie is now part of a new menswear offering by the designer. “Ben also wore the watch that I gifted him from our friend Eric Wind—a vintage Rolex Root Beer GMT-Master from the ’70s.”

A trip to New York for fittings coincided with the city’s bridal fashion week, and Cam was invited to see Di Petsa’s first bridal collection. “I knew right away I had to wear the silk long dress with pearl details for my rehearsal dinner,” says the bride. “I adore pearls and wanted jewelry that played off of the pearl details of the back of the Di Petsa gown.” She eventually settled on jewelry from Mateo New York, and also had pearl details from Jennifer Behr added to her hair for the night. The bride also found another Di Petsa look with draped tulle and lace to wear at the after-party. “The discreet pearl at the belly button was so special,” Cam says. “We paired it with one of my favorite shoe designers—Amina Muaddi’s lace heels. But to be honest, I kicked off the shoes at the end of the night and ended up in a pair of New Balance sneakers!”

To finish off every look with the right glam, Cam had her makeup artist Jocelyn Biga make a trip from New York for the celebration. “The day wouldn’t have felt complete without her as she’s done my makeup for all of the biggest events in my life: the WNBA draft, our engagement, and now the wedding,” says the bride.

The wedding party was also outfitted for the big day. Amsale made custom dresses in deep blue for the bridesmaids, and groomsmen wore classic tuxedos. “For our entire group of children, Maison Ava created a bespoke wardrobe,” explains Cam. “The girls' looks had hand embroidery, hand-cut and hand-appliquéd floral motifs, crystals, beading, and 3D flowers, [while] the boys had custom velvet tuxedo jackets and wool trousers. It was so incredible to see all of the kids in their looks, and they also had tiny, custom New Balance sneakers at the end of the night!”

After a morning getting ready with their closest circles and taking portraits along the California coastline, the couple reunited at their alma mater to exchange vows at the Memorial Church. “The light was shining through the stained-glass windows so brightly all I could see was Cameron’s beautiful silhouette as she walked down the aisle with her parents, Greg and Michelle, and I tried to hold back tears,” remembers Ben. Cam admits she tripped right before walking down the aisle (and thankfully, her mom came to the rescue), but all her nerves washed away once Ben lifted her veil. “I felt overwhelmed by the support from our loved ones, and most of all I felt the deepest sense of gratitude and belonging looking into Ben’s eyes,” she says. The bride’s godmother read scripture, the couple exchanged personal vows, and their pastor gave wishes for their future, including “a peaceful home filled with joy, laughter, and one day, the sound of little feet running around wearing number 22.”

Once the newlyweds paraded down the aisle and did the dip they practiced all week, they sped off to the Ritz-Carlton, Half Moon Bay in a 1957 Rolls-Royce. “I asked our driver for his best marriage advice since he’s been with his wife for 58 years,” recalls Ben. “He said, ‘It’s not that complicated. I just do what she says.’ Cameron elbowed me and got a kick out of that.” After portraits at sunset, the bride and groom joined their guests for cocktail hour on the grounds.

“The rest of the night was filled with so much joy, belly laughs, and dorky dance moves,” says Ben. The reception moved indoors to a space filled with florals and greenery curated with the help of Flowers by Edgar. Dinner kicked off with the couple’s first dance to “Until I Found You,” sung by their flower girl Ryan. The night was also peppered with speeches from the bride’s father and the groom’s brothers. As the dance floor lit up, guests could also head outdoors to indulge in a cigar lounge. “The energy in the ballroom was unmatched, erupting multiple times with cheers and toasts,” adds Ben. “We couldn’t be happier with how the best day of our lives concluded.”

Now off on their honeymoon in Kyoto, the pair share they are in a bubble of newlywed bliss. Says Cam: “We couldn’t have dreamt of a better day and are feeling so incredibly blessed to have shared it with all of the people whom we love so dearly.”

Heating the water takes most of the energy your washing machine uses, so set everyday loads to cold and run the washer only when it’s full. Save hot water for the few loads that need it to kill germs or cut grease.

The washer’s motor is the cheap part of a load. Energy Star puts water heating at about 90% of a clothes washer’s energy use.

Take a standard washer, which uses about 20 gallons a load by Energy Star’s count, and assume a hot wash heats half of that, 10 gallons, from 50 to 120 degrees. With an electric water heater, that takes about 1.7 kilowatt-hours, roughly 30 cents at 17.23 cents per kilowatt-hour, the 2025 average residential price from the Energy Information Administration.

A gas water heater that gets about 80% of its fuel’s heat into the water does the same job for about 11 cents, at the agency’s 2025 residential average of $15.34 per thousand cubic feet. Switching from hot to warm can cut either cost in half, Energy Star says, and the cold cycle cuts the cost further. An Energy Star front-loader uses about half the water of a top-loader with an agitator, so switching it to cold saves less.

Hot water is still worth paying for on a few loads. During a stomach bug, the CDC says to wash clothes, towels, or bedding with vomit or stool on them in hot water on the longest cycle, then machine dry them on the highest heat. Greasy work clothes are the other case, since Energy Star’s advice to wash cold makes an exception for oily stains. Wash everything else on cold, with a detergent made for cold water, as the South Carolina Energy Office advises.

Run fewer, fuller loads, too, and start them at night or on weekends if your utility charges less at those times. A washer uses about the same energy whatever the size of the load, Energy Star says, so two half loads cost about twice what one full load does. If a small load can’t wait, set the water level to match it. For the rest of the electric bill, start with heating, cooling, and hot water.

A family that switches eight warm loads a week to cold saves up to $61 a year with an electric water heater, or $22 with gas.

The biggest limitations of ChatGPT

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From fabricated citations to forgotten instructions, ChatGPT still has some frustrating blind spots.

It's no secret that ChatGPT can write up emails, become your software troubleshooter and even dumb down complicated questions in an instant. But it's also pretty capable of making up responses so polished that you might not think to verify the shaky facts behind them. If only most of us had the confidence that ChatGPT has when it is hallucinating. Even after OpenAI brought its advanced GPT-6 model to all ChatGPT users in October 2026, the chatbot still has the tendency to fabricate supporting evidence, misread an ambiguous question and even forget instructions amid a lengthy conversation.

Some shortcomings are tied to the model or version of it that you're using, along with any plugins you've added. And just because you're paying for ChatGPT Plus or the $100 ChatGPT Pro plan doesn't mean you're guarded against the chatbot's biggest limitations. To be fair, this isn't a problem unique to OpenAI, as most of these shortcomings are common in AI assistants we compared, like Claude.

Topping the list of ChatGPT's biggest quirks is its bad habit of hallucinating, or crafting details that seem legitimate but are entirely made up. Packing all the confidence of a subject-matter expert, ChatGPT will cite studies that don't exist and make them almost believable with imaginary quotations too.

OpenAI is quite upfront about this and warns that ChatGPT could invent quotes and cite fake sources. Using ChatGPT Search does make things better, but just because the response has some live links doesn't make the final statement correct. While an expert on the topic could probably catch the counterfeit sources upon closer inspection, this could be troublesome for someone looking for references in uncharted territory.

ChatGPT can also follow detailed instructions and acknowledge a mistake you've pointed out, only to ignore it a few prompts later. One user on OpenAI's community forum reported that ChatGPT reused earlier details in a report and presented them as verified, then repeated the mistake after admitting it.

But then, that's one person's experience and not exactly strong evidence of this being a problem across the board. That said, researchers found that the reliability of language models tends to go down as input length increases, even if the tasks aren't inherently complex. The researchers tested 18 different models, using older versions of OpenAI's GPT, Google's Gemini and Anthropic's Claude, so the findings may not be an accurate depiction of how GPT-6 performs. And just because you've fed your AI model an enormous amount of context doesn't guarantee it will make the most of it.

If you're working on a lengthy project across several days, being mindful of the changes requested and repeating the given instructions is part of the play. Sure, it can get frustrating that a tool that should be saving you time needs so much babysitting, but the alternative is far more time- and labor-intensive. OpenAI also acknowledges that ChatGPT's Memory feature doesn't retain every detail from every conversation anyway.

And to better understand said science, I spoke with Zakia Rahman, MD, FAAD, a clinical professor of dermatology at Stanford University School of Medicine.

Red-light therapy didn’t start as a treatment for antiaging and recovery. It actually came about as an accidental solution for hair loss in the 1960s, when Hungarian scientist Endre Mester was researching whether exposure to red light could treat cancer in hairless mice. Imagine his surprise when the mice started growing hair.

Red-light therapy has come a long way since then. According to Rahman, red-light wavelengths are thought to activate the mitochondria in skin cells, which may encourage an uptick in the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to promote new growth. Red-light therapy may also improve blood flow to hair follicles.

Importantly, iRestore's devices are FDA-cleared—a safety designation from the US Food and Drug Administration indicating that a product is “substantially equivalent” to another legally marketed device. There are three hair-growth devices in iRestore's lineup: the entry-level Essential ($425), the mid-tier Professional ($799), and the Elite ($1,699). Each contains iRestore’s proprietary Lumitech technology, which combines LED lights and medical-grade lasers. These laser diodes, called Vixo lasers, operate in the range of 655 to 680 nanometers (nm). (Preliminary studies with mice have shown that using red light in the 600- to 660-nm range, and infrared wavelengths in the 800- to 850-nm range, can promote cell regeneration, pain relief, and more.)

The idea is that this dynamic duo of lights and lasers can penetrate deeper into the skin than ordinary red light with lasers, while the LEDs ensure light is distributed across the scalp for maximum efficacy. Together, they reach a maximum of 2,500 milliwatts (mW) in the Elite, compared to the Professional's 1,410 mW and Essential's 600 mW.

The Elite is iRestore’s top-of-the-line offering and has the most LEDs of the three laser hair-growth systems, with 500 diodes in total—that's 300 lasers and 200 LEDs. The Essential has 51 lasers and 69 LEDs for a total of 120 diodes, while the iRestore Professional has 82 lasers and 200 LEDs for a total of 282 diodes. The Elite was also designed to cover more of the scalp than the other two models and requires 12 minutes of daily use, while the Professional and Essential require 25 minutes every other day.

If price is not a factor, the Elite is the no-brainer choice due to its higher-concentrated light therapy, shorter use time, and potentially faster results.

The helmet arrived in hefty red packaging containing the helmet, a portable battery pack (annoyingly, this is a $99 add-on purchase), a charging cord, and a red-and-black zippered travel case. It feels very fragile, and I wouldn't throw it around—then again, you probably wouldn't plan to shove such an expensive device in the back of your bathroom vanity.

In 2022, Iraq’s National Museum in Baghdad reopened after being closed for three years following political unrest. Then in 2024, the museum closed again, this time for renovations. Now, it’s scheduled to open on December 1.

The Art Newspaper reported that the museum will open with several new galleries, such as the Abbasid and Islamic galleries. There will also be specially curated wings.

The museum will display artifacts including winged bulls (lamassu) and stone carvings depicting legendary Assyrian monarchs, ancient clay tablets and historical prisms recording legal codes, calligraphic friezes, stone carvings, glassworks, and mosaics that have been recovered and restored.
