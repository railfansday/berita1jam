Bruno Fernandes has revealed Mauricio Pochettino told Manchester United to be more aggressive at half-time of their dramatic draw with Sporting.

United went in at half-time trailing 2-0, but came back to draw 2-2 despite having a pair of goals controversially ruled out.

It was a dramatic affair in Lisbon, but it transpires Pochettino had some refreshingly simple advice at the break.

"[At half-time the manager] wanted more aggression. More bodies,” Fernandes explained. “The first goal comes from that.”

That first goal was product of a penalty won when Lisandro Martinez charged into the Sporting box.

Fernandes went on, saying United were frustrated not to have won: “We need points to get through. We need to focus on the next one. Now the moment is to get back to the Premier League. We need to stick together and fight for our lives.”

Senne Lammens endured a long evening, as he conceded the penalty which handed Sporting their second penalty of the day, from which the opener was scored, and dropped the ball to permit the hosts’ late equaliser.

But Fernandes was quick to put an arm around his keeper as he added to TNT Sports: “We gave a penalty away where Senne [Lammens] saved it. We gave one or two counters, but we were very good in recovery positions.

“Mistakes are part of football and from that you get goals. Everyone has to take the blame for everything we do good and we do bad as well.

“We need to be together in the good and in the bad moments.”

Sens. Warren, Van Hollen lead push to scrap SEC’s Regulation Crypto and innovation exemption

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In a letter sent to SEC Chair Atkins, Sens. Warren and Van Hollen said the agency's "Regulation Crypto Assets" would allow crypto firms to circumvent securities laws.

Top Democrats Sen. Elizabeth Warren and Chris Van Hollen are demanding that Securities and Exchange Commission Chair Paul Atkins take down its crypto rulemaking governing how crypto projects can raise capital and how tokenized securities can be traded.

In two separate letters sent to Atkins on Tuesday, Sens. Warren, Van Hollen and Tina Smith and other Senate Banking Committee members said the agency's "Regulation Crypto Assets" would allow crypto firms to circumvent securities laws, weaken state jurisdiction and could harm investors.

"The proposal would create a new 'tailored securities offering regime' for certain investment contracts involving crypto assets," the senators wrote. "However, the proposal only serves to help crypto assets escape from the SEC's regulatory regime and gives them special treatment—with less oversight—compared to other traditional securities."

In August, the SEC proposed crypto offering rules to offer startup and fundraising exemptions for digital asset offerings. It allows for a "startup exemption" that would exempt offerings of up to $5 million from registration rules under the Securities Act of 1933 for four years — a federal law focused on anti-fraud and requiring certain disclosures. The proposal also includes a "fundraising exemption" for offerings up to $75 million for a year.

Warren is poised to take on a more prominent role following Democrats’ victories in November's midterm elections, which handed the party control of both chambers of Congress. Warren has been critical of crypto in the past and has particularly brought up concerns around illicit finance and money laundering. Meanwhile, Van Hollen could be the next Senate Majority Leader. He has since challenged current Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer for that role.

Sens. Jack Reed and Tina Smith join the two in the second letter asking the SEC to withdraw its innovation exemption — a measure released in September to bring capital markets onchain through the trading of tokenized stocks. It is a five-year exemption that would exempt certain trading venues, called tokenized securities venues, from being defined as an exchange and also exempts specific liquidity providers from being defined as a dealer under securities law when they trade stocks or provide liquidity through automated market makers.

The senators said it would allow platforms to operate outside of certain safeguards, including involving fair pricing and market transparency.

"The order would allow TSV platforms to operate a shadow stock market outside longstanding regulatory protections designed to ensure fair and transparent markets, impose new burdens on publicly traded companies seeking to protect their shareholders, and create unnecessary risks of illicit financial activity," the senators wrote.

The three senators also said the innovation exemption could cause capital markets to splinter.

"As concerned stakeholders have pointed out, fragmenting our securities markets by creating a lightly regulated alternative marketplace for tokenized securities unnecessarily risks dividing liquidity into isolated pools and expanding opportunities for corrosive regulatory arbitrage," they said.

A separate group of Senate Democrats have also brought up concerns about the innovation exemption. Last week, Sens. Angela Alsobrooks, Ruben Gallego, Mark Warner and Catherine Cortez Masto said it could open the door for illicit finance actors and also had concerns about protections for retail investors around pricing.

The SEC did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

I was running out to grab a cup of coffee and asked my co-worker if I could grab one for her. No biggie, just a common courtesy. I literally didn’t even go out of my way. But when I returned, she couldn't have been more grateful. Sometimes when we do small, simple things, we realize later how much they meant to others.

It could be something like a thoughtful message or a quick check-in where we take a few minutes to listen.

The thing is, we often underestimate the impact of these simple gestures. We are all so busy these days, caught up in daily responsibilities and checking all the boxes to complete everything on our list, that we tend to forget how much we can effortlessly offer to others.

Of course, in this fast-paced world, we are always striving for more from life. And, at the same time, it's important to pause and recognize what we have and how it can be shared with those around us.

Just take a moment to think about some of the people who make a difference in your life. Perhaps it was that friend who checked in on you during a difficult time or a supervisor who encouraged you. They may not have given their generosity much thought, but I bet you remember how it made you feel, right?

Let me share with you a research study that supports this idea. A 2023 study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology found that people frequently underestimate how positive acts of kindness impact others. In one study, participants who gave strangers hot chocolate thought the recipients would be happy, but in reality, the recipients were incredibly grateful for the gesture.

Generativity, a concept developed by psychologist Erik Erikson, describes how a person wants to contribute to the lives of others and make a difference for future generations. This is evidenced in the things that we do, like mentoring others, supporting a friend or family member, or sharing lessons.

It’s important to mention that the research demonstrates a strong connection between helping people and well-being. In 2020, Psychological Bulletin published a meta-analysis of more than 200 studies and found a consistent relationship between helping people and positive well-being.

It’s important to mention that giving to others doesn't mean we are neglecting our needs. It just means that we are also helping one another build relationships. And the research also shows that a sense of connection really matters.

Sadly, we are often so busy managing all that we have to do that we don't think about how we can support others.

You will both benefit if you reach out, offer a little encouragement, listen, or just let someone know they are on your mind. The point is that it doesn't have to be this big grand gesture. Small acts of kindness go a long way.

Life's most meaningful moments happen with people who took the time to listen, remember, or encourage, or helped us feel less alone. Don’t wait for more time or money, or until you've figured everything out, before you try to make a difference. Just start with where you are.

Take a moment and think about one small act of kindness that was bestowed upon you. How can you do the same for others?

Study: Meta settlement needs YouTube, TikTok to succeed

When Meta settled a massive social media addiction lawsuit brought by multiple state attorneys general in August, it invited YouTube and TikTok to join the terms.

Meta, which agreed to implement new restrictions on teen use, argued that the safeguards amounted to a new industry standard. They included a two-hour daily time limit, removing nighttime access to Meta apps by default, and no notifications during school hours.

"These protections will only be truly effective if we work with our peers — TikTok and YouTube — to put the same measures in place," Meta said in a statement at the time. Neither social media platform has joined the settlement since.

Now, a new study published in JAMA suggests Meta was right about the potential effect of YouTube and TikTok agreeing to the restrictions.

The authors analyzed existing data from ongoing research known as the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, which tracks teen screen time and social media habits. They looked at data from 1,803 participants over a five-year period between Nov. 2020 and Aug. 2025.

They found the Meta apps Instagram and Facebook accounted for only 20 percent of total daily social media use amongst those participants. Snapchat, TikTok, and YouTube comprised the remaining apps named in the litigation.

Additionally, the authors found that significantly more teens would benefit from the time limit, nighttime access, and notification restrictions if the other companies implemented them.

"I think many people hailed that settlement with a huge sigh of relief, but unless the other companies join suit, these data suggest it's not going to make a huge difference," Dr. Dimitri Christakis, principal investigator at the Center for Child Health, Behavior and Development at Seattle Children's, said in a statement. Dr. Jason Nagata, an associate professor of pediatrics at the University of California, San Francisco who was not involved in the study, said the real-world phone-use data allowed the authors to examine how the settlement's proposed protections might come into play.

The estimated reach of the restrictions is also hypothetical.

Major developers, legal experts, and 32 states back Epic in Apple’s SCOTUS appeal

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In a significant development in Apple’s Supreme Court appeal of a civil contempt ruling, Epic Games has received backing from 32 states and the District of Columbia, along with major developers, legal experts, former federal judges, and more. Here are the details.

Apple’s ever-sprawling fight against Epic Games over its App Store policies is currently being fought on two fronts: in the Supreme Court, where Apple is seeking to overturn a civil contempt ruling, and in the district court, where the companies are disputing what commission, if any, Apple can charge on purchases made through external payment systems.

The civil contempt ruling came after Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers found that Apple had violated an earlier injunction issued in its App Store dispute with Epic.

In a nutshell, after she ordered Apple to allow developers to direct users to alternative payment methods outside the App Store, the company responded by allowing external payment links, but imposed a commission of up to 27% on purchases made through them.

Since then, Apple has been trying to overturn the civil contempt ruling, basically arguing that the original injunction did not explicitly prohibit the commissions and other restrictions it imposed on external purchases.

After the Ninth Circuit upheld the contempt finding and denied its request for rehearing, Apple took the case to the Supreme Court, which agreed to hear its appeal.

Importantly, the Supreme Court will only consider the legal standard used to uphold Apple’s civil contempt ruling, not the separate question of what commission, if any, the company can charge on purchases made through external payment methods.

That will still be determined in the district court, where proceedings have continued despite Apple’s attempts to put them on hold pending the Supreme Court’s decision.

With oral arguments scheduled for January 11, 2027, the case has recently seen a flurry of activity, including Apple’s opening brief in September and Epic’s response earlier this month.

Apple also received support from several organizations in September, including the Washington Legal Foundation, the International Center for Law and Economics, the Computer & Communications Industry Association, and the U.S. Chamber of Commerce. The US government also submitted a brief supporting neither party, arguing that the contempt finding was unjustified over Apple’s 27% commission, but warranted over its restrictions on button designs.

Which brings us to the latest developments: a series of amicus briefs filed in support of Epic Games.

Last week, a total of 9 amicus briefs were filed in support of Epic Games, including one led by Utah and joined by 31 other states and the District of Columbia.

This particular brief notes that “the district court correctly held Apple in contempt for continuing to engage in anticompetitive conduct barred by a prior injunction,” adding:

“The court found that Apple engaged in unlawful anti-steering restrictions and required Apple to lift those restrictions. Apple then lifted them in name only, imposed new (and substantially similar) strictures on developers that mirrored the old ones and produced the same result. Worse still, Apple did all this in bad faith: Apple knew its behavior was wrong and actively hid that fact from the court, including by providing false testimony. Under any reasonable reading of the district court’s injunction, Apple’s conduct is worthy of civil contempt.”

The states also argue that “Injunctions in competition cases cannot afford complete relief without addressing the ongoing effects of anticompetitive behavior,” and that “[c]onstruing competition injunctions narrowly defeats their purpose and prevents meaningful redress.”

Similar arguments appear throughout the other briefs, which include submissions from Spotify, Match Group, and Microsoft, as well as nonprofit organizations such as the American Antitrust Institute (AAI), Public Knowledge, and the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF).

In their joint brief, Spotify, Match Group, and Microsoft argue that they “have first-hand experience with the effects of Apple’s conduct and evasion efforts,” adding that “Apple’s new restrictions were no different from the unlawful rules they replaced.”

As for the brief filed by former federal judges, they warned that “[i]n cases like these, where enjoined parties seek to circumvent an injunction through reliance on its literal text, courts must have the power of contempt to deter and remedy evasion. Otherwise, courts would be forced into a game of whack-a-mole, trying to anticipate every possible means of evading an injunction […]”

Arguments by the Little Tech Association, AAI, Public Knowledge, and the EFF all follow a similar line, with some also addressing how Apple’s restrictions on external payment links could affect consumer choice, pricing, and competition.
