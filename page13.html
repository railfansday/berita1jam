Forget Takeout: Customers Love These Frozen Mexican Foods

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you're lucky, you live just a short ride away from a half dozen quality Mexican restaurants serving bubbling bowls of pozole, punchy tacos, and sultry stews of slow-cooked meats. But not everyone lives in communities blessed with quality Mexican cooking. And that's a major bummer, because this regional cuisine is simply electric, balancing deep flavors with fresh herbs, warm spices, and piquant chiles. Mexican food plays with different textures and shapes, allowing you to compose bites that are simultaneously creamy and crisp, cool and spicy, savory and sweet.

Thankfully, you can enjoy decent Mexican and Tex-Mex-inspired food from the comfort of your couch. And all it takes is a trip to your local supermarket. First, let's preface this entire article by saying that a good restaurant will almost always outdo a frozen dupe. Still, there is something to be said for the convenience and cost savings of air-frying a chimichanga or microwaving a burrito for lunch. I turned to the internet to find frozen Mexican meals comparable to what you might get at a restaurant or fast-casual takeout spot. After sorting through hundreds of reviews and comments, I put together a list of the top customer-favorite Mexican appetizers, sides, and main meals available in the freezer aisle.

The foundation of a good tamale is corn, specifically corn that's been soaked in an alkaline solution (AKA nixtamalized), which transforms the dense starchy kernels into a pliable, dough-like carbohydrate called masa. Making masa (and tamales) requires time, skill, precision, and technique. But if you don't possess any of the above, then heating up a frozen tamale is your best option.

There are a surprising number of choices in this department, but customers are especially fond of the Trader Joe's versions. Like many Trader Joe's items, people gripe that these come in and out of stock with about as much predictability as a June weather forecast. However, the cheese and green chile and the chicken and cheese varieties are consistently the most reliable (and available). Tamales make a good side dish, especially if you want an alternative to potatoes or pasta, or, like one Redditor, you can steam one and bring it for lunch. A negative is that since they have to be reheated, the masa interior can get a tad dry. To remedy this, Trader Joe's shoppers recommend a side of salsa, hot sauce, guacamole, rice and beans, or a blanket of extra melted cheese.

As far as frozen Mexican entrées go, the burrito bowl can be controversial. Why? Unlike a tamale, chimichanga, or taquito, a burrito bowl doesn't require the same finesse, so customers generally expect a higher quality product. Surprisingly, many reliable brands struggle to execute what seems like a simple dish. But not Kevin's Natural Foods. If unfamiliar, Kevin's positions itself as a better-for-you frozen meal. Ingredients are clean, non-GMO, and gluten-free. These restrictions are far from a hindrance. On the contrary, the quality of Kevin's frozen meals, especially its burrito bowls, is top-notch.

At least, that's what the customers say. "Absolutely loaded with cheese, chicken in every bite, rice perfectly cooked, veggies still have a bit of a snap to them, and the chipotle sauce has a ton of flavor," one Redditor commented. Others shared a similar opinion. With Kevin's, there's a general appreciation for the quality and portion size. The brand doesn't skimp on proteins or portion size, and whatever ingredients are included are thoughtfully prepared — no mushy rice, sad veggies, or gloopy cheese. The result keeps customers happy.

Many culinary historians consider mole the national dish of Mexico. The sauce's dynamic flavors mirror the country's history: dark and complex, with an underlying sweetness and grounding depth. Mole requires dozens of Mexico's most prized indigenous ingredients — chocolate, chiles, nuts, seeds, spices — to create something that's, frankly, unmatched in any other cuisine.

Does Trader Joe's version capture that centuries of Mexican culinary tradition? That would be impossible. However, given its price and unbelievable convenience, it does a pretty good job of imitating the sweet-and-savory balance of a traditional mole. With just one package, you can make multiple meals. And while a traditional mole takes hours — if not days — to prepare, TJ's frozen chicken mole takes just minutes in the microwave.

The taste is bold, but the texture of the chicken — not overly mushy or stringy — makes it ideal for several applications. Customers say they use it to fill enchiladas and tacos, but you can use it for everything from pizza to nachos to an omelet.

Known for its better-for-you ingredient profile, Amy's has been supplying organic frozen entrées for decades. While some frozen tortilla-wrapped items suffer from a textural breakdown, the tortillas housing Amy's enchilada filling hold up well. All of Amy's frozen entrées are vegetarian-friendly, but where they lack in meat, they make up for it in texture, flavor, and composition. Amy's cheese enchiladas have been a hit with shoppers for decades, with the blend of cheeses, toasty corn tortillas, and savory tomato-based enchilada sauce, and they still earn online praise today.

"I find these to have an incredible depth of flavor for a frozen meal," began one Redditor's review. "I would be 100% happy if this was served to me in a restaurant." Others say that the product is the best in frozen food. And while there's no question that shoppers have enjoyed and appreciated the taste of Amy's foods for years, they are a bit disgruntled about the ever-increasing price point. And while that might be on the higher end of the price spectrum, anything in the $7 or $8 range for a frozen meal does seem a little steep. But they're still beloved, despite that.

The taquito was created as a thrifty way to use up leftover tortillas and filling. Little did its creators know that the fried, toasty snack would become widely beloved happy marriage of taste, form, and texture. Taquitos are almost always made from corn tortillas and filled with everything from chicken and cheese to beef, veggies, and beans. But the best part of a taquito is its tubular shape that's seemingly designed for dipping.

Frozen taquitos aren't new, and their basic composition hasn't changed much over the years. You'll find a plethora of options in the freezer aisle, but customers are most fond of El Monterey's Extra Crunchy Southwest Chicken variety. Fans say it's hard to eat just one... or two. Shoppers also love its unique exterior crust.

The only downside to El Monterey's taquitos is the quantity of filling. There's a collective sigh at how the interiors have grown increasingly barren over the years, an observation noticeable in the chicken taquitos, where the chicken and vegetables retain their individual shapes, unlike the beef and bean option, which is more of a paste. An easy solution is to prepare a few different dips — sour cream, guacamole, queso — to add extra moisture and unctuousness.

If a taquito and a burrito had a baby, it would be a chimichanga. It's deep-fried, which ups the richness but also gives the tortilla a crispy shell that's the ideal foil for any smooth refried bean or cheese filling. Deep frying at home doesn't require the same precision as making a chocolate soufflé, but it still means more work and cleanup than simply reheating on a hot frying pan. That's why a good frozen chimichanga is crucial if you suddenly get that itch for this fried Tex-Mex specialty. And, again, the El Monterey brand delivers.

Online, there was a lot of love for El Monterey, saying it has some of the best frozen burritos and chimichangas on the market. Unlike the taquitos, the chimichangas have a generous filling that, as one person put it, is comparable to the reference photo on the bag. The key to executing these properly is in the preparation. The chimichanga is inherently greasy, so a quick thaw in the microwave followed by a crisp-up on a hot pan usually does the trick. An air-fryer works, too. Some people just throw them in the deep fryer because, well, why not?

The burrito is the quintessential frozen Mexican food item, and over the decades, dozens — if not hundreds — of brands have battled each other for category supremacy. Many have faltered, but a handful have held their ground. Like Red's. Founded in 2009, Red's focuses on one thing: burritos. Sure, it'll try a breakfast sandwich or a "Snacker," but its product lineup is mostly just an array of tortilla-wrapped goodies.

Customers are big fans of the steak and cheddar burrito. While other cheaper, mass-market frozen burritos rely on mushy, indistinguishable fillings, Red's has made a name for itself by using higher-quality ingredients. Although shoppers are big fans of Red's entire line of burritos, they do gripe about the prices. Heating the burrito is another issue entirely, but one Red's expert has a foolproof method: Run it under water in the sink for a few seconds before microwaving. The extra moisture makes the tortilla a bit springier and more pliable. Wrapping it in a wet paper towel will have a similar effect.

The Mexican Style Street Corn Triangles from Tastiez are far from traditional Mexican cuisine. Instead, they are a frozen food product inspired by the classic Mexican street food esquites, which is like a bolder, saltier version of creamed corn. Another way to think of it is like elote, that yummy, cheese-dusted ear of grilled corn, stripped off the cob.

This product is a major departure from the original dish, but if you're craving an easy appetizer inspired by an authentic Mexican dish, it's not such a bad substitute. And, most importantly (at least for this article), customers have given these fried, corn-filled triangles their seal of approval. Whereas your average stuffed, fried snack oozes its insides after a hard bake or big bite, these stay pretty much intact. The crisp outside and gooey center make for a satisfying finger food, but it's the taste that brings it home, with reviewers gushing over its delicious street corn flavor. They come out best when deep-fried, but the air-fryer is an excellent alternative that still yields a crisp exterior.

Today, you can find the most elaborate culinary delights in your supermarket freezer. But while brands can execute everything from frozen kimbap to cioppino, many still struggle to nail a good frozen taco. What has customers complaining is the ratio of filling to shell: not enough of the former and too much of the latter, resulting in a product that's dry and brittle. Maybe tacos are difficult to seal, so manufacturers are forced to under fill? Whatever the reason, some brands miss the mark, while others, like Don Miguel, lead by example.

The Don Miguel Chicken Chipotle Mini Tacos have an active and passionate Reddit community that few items on this list can match. But why? What makes this 50-pack of frozen tacos from Costco so special? The price and crave-worthiness. However, while the consensus is generally positive, long-time customers have commented that the tacos are a bit spicier than they used to be. For this issue, the main workaround is a cooling condiment, like Greek yogurt or sour cream. Still, although people do love these things, it seems like the perfect mini taco has yet to be discovered.

The churro is one of the most globally recognized Mexican desserts. It's basically a twist or extruded wand of fried dough that's tossed in cinnamon sugar and served with, if you're lucky, a smooth chocolate sauce or caramel. Churros come in many shapes and sizes. Costco, famous for scaling everything to the max, sells a foot-long version in its food court for just $1.49. But if you want to scratch that churro itch at home, you can pick up a pack of Hola Churros.

No, these aren't as long as the ones you'll find in the food court, but some regular Costco customers think they're just smaller versions of the same make and model. But how do they taste? Overall, shoppers are pretty pleased. Are they as crisp and pleasantly oily as the ones you'll find at a street fair or Mexican restaurant? Probably not. But they do the job. The key is finding ways to deliver that extra punch of flavor. Although they come with individual packs of cinnamon sugar, some Redditors recommend drizzling chocolate on them. They're also good as a topping for an ice cream sundae. For heating, skip the microwave and go for the air-fryer or toaster oven. No matter how you make them, they are an unbelievably good value: around $14.49 for a box of 30.

When you see a chile relleno on the menu at a Mexican restaurant, it's worth getting. This dish is pretty special, integrating all the different tastes and flavors that make cuisine from this part of the world so interesting and seductive. It's essentially a cheese-stuffed pepper that's battered, fried, and served on top of a rich tomato sauce. It takes some work to prepare, but it's a real stunner when it hits the table.

Somehow the team at Trader Joe's has managed to capture all these dynamic tastes and textures in one convenient, easy-to-heat frozen package. The batter is tempura-style, somewhat spongy but not in a soggy way, providing heft and unctuousness to the otherwise bare, roasted pepper wrapper. The cheese is pleasantly rubbery, and the sauce is savory and delicious. On Reddit, customers are surprised by how good it tastes, even despite the soft, slightly mushy texture. Of all the foods on this list, this one had the most overwhelmingly positive score for flavor. The consensus is that you should reheat the peppers in the air-fryer, not the microwave. Overall, people think the flavor is fantastic, and for just $5.99, you really can't go wrong.

Although this brand of Trader Joe's frozen rice is called "Spanish-style," its flavor profile is decidedly Mexican, drawing on spices and ingredients indigenous to Central America. Tomato gives it its mellow orange color, and a savory spice blend and quick toasting add an umami undertone that makes it easy to pair with dishes from across the culinary landscape, from madras lentils to gyros.

On Reddit, Trader Joe's faithful say it's an excellent accompaniment to many of the store's frozen and refrigerated Mexican entrées, like the birria, mole, green chili and cheese tamales, and even the salad kits. Others use it as a quick and flavorful foundation to a dish. Its utility is what makes this product so special. With dozens of comments on one Reddit thread dedicated to the Spanish-style rice, you can find dozens of interesting and inventive use cases. And while it's marketed as a microwavable grain, some shoppers are convinced you get a better result when you toast it in a pan.

Ethereum Privacy Network Aztec Rolls Out Major Security and Speed Upgrade

Aztec, the privacy-focused Ethereum layer-2 network, has rolled out a major upgrade that fixes a critical vulnerability from its previous version, doubles the speed of private transactions, and ushers in a wave of applications built to keep user data hidden.

The Aztec Foundation said Tuesday that the upgrade, called Aztec Tierra and designated Alpha V6, delivers stronger security, formal verification of its proving system and faster deposits from Ethereum, alongside a refreshed brand.

Aztec is a decentralized layer-2 with more than 3,000 validators and fully programmable private smart contracts, and it pitches itself as having no privileged viewing keys or centralized sequencer, meaning no single operator can peer into or control user activity.

The upgrade resolves known vulnerabilities from earlier versions, including a critical issue discovered in V5, and adds extensive hardening. Notably, that includes a new AI-powered security testing harness and formal verification of core cryptographic components, a mathematical process for proving code behaves as intended.

The move reflects a broader scramble across crypto to harden code as AI tools make vulnerabilities easier to find.

On performance, Aztec said private transactions now generate client-side proofs in about one second on a standard device, twice as fast as before, while a new architecture cuts the time for Ethereum-to-Aztec messages from an average of two minutes to as little as 12 seconds, speeding up deposits and cross-chain activity.

“Privacy shouldn’t be something you have to request or trust someone else to protect,” said Zac Williamson, co-founder and CEO of the Aztec Foundation. “It should be built into the network.”

The upgrade arrives with a crop of private applications spanning payments, trading, AI and social.

Leading them is zk.money, a private payments and savings app built by Aztec Labs that the team revived this year. With Tierra’s faster messaging, users can deposit directly from any Ethereum wallet without complex bridging.

Others in the lineup include Galactica for private AI inference, Nemi for private perpetuals trading, a private browser wallet called Nulo, and Rolling Chat, a private message board based on Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin’s own Aztec experiments.

The launch lands amid a renewed push for privacy on Ethereum. Buterin has laid out plans for a “cryptographic world computer” and pressed to slash the cost of quantum-safe privacy, while a separate team spun out a for-profit firm to drive institutional privacy adoption.

Aztec, which raised $17 million in an earlier funding round to build out its network, is betting that privacy becomes a default feature of Ethereum rather than an add-on.

Robot lamp maker Syncere raises $11M pre-seed led by a16z Speedrun

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The $2,699 Lume is a floor lamp that folds laundry, and every unit shipped trains the company’s AI.

The Lume robot lamp handles household chores.

Syncere, the Palo Alto startup behind the Lume robot lamp, has raised $11m in pre-seed funding led by a16z Speedrun, Andreessen Horowitz’s programme for early-stage founders.

Draper Associates, Physical World, Sequel and SeaX Ventures also took part in the round. The company plans to use the money to deliver its first Lume units, expand manufacturing capacity and adapt its robotics technology for commercial applications.

Lume combines a sculptural floor lamp with a mobile home robot. During the day, it serves as a light. It can also move around the house to handle everyday chores, including folding laundry, tidying rooms, and making beds.

The lamp starts at $2,699, and owners can teach it how they want specific tasks done, while Syncere is developing an onboarding process that will help it learn household preferences and adapt to the layout of each home.

“Home robotics has a deployment problem disguised as a data problem,” said Aaron Tan, Syncere’s co-founder and chief executive. “Robots need real-world data to become reliable, but they can’t get into people’s homes until they’re reliable.”

Lume is designed to break that cycle. By selling the robot as a household light first, Syncere hopes to get its machines into homes earlier, gather data from real-world use, and improve their capabilities through software updates.

The company says customers should see their robots become more capable over time without having to replace the hardware.

Robotics researchers Aaron Tan and Angus Fung founded Syncere less than a year ago. Since then, the company says, Lume has moved from concept to production and into customers’ homes.

The founding team’s backgrounds include Stanford, Harvard, the University of Toronto and UC Berkeley, alongside engineering and design experience at Nuro, AMD, GM and Apple.

Interest has already built ahead of the first deliveries. TIME named Lume one of its Best Inventions of 2026, and Syncere says it has secured millions of dollars in pre-orders.

“Getting robots into real homes earlier creates a fundamentally different path to improving household autonomy,” said Andrew Chen, general partner at a16z Speedrun.

Chen said Syncere stood out because it had designed its sales channel and learning system together, while demonstrating demand before shipping its product.

The home robotics market is attracting a growing number of companies. Norway’s 1X opened orders earlier this year for NEO, a $20,000 humanoid designed to handle household chores, while other manufacturers are developing cheaper machines for specific tasks.

Syncere is taking a different route, betting that a robot designed to look like furniture will be easier to bring into people’s homes.

Christina Bowlby Finds Inspiration Close to Home for Bentley Mills

For textile designer Christina Bowlby, inspiration can come from an unexpected place: the warm, heathered coats of her two Aussiedoodles, Birdie and Maggie. Christina Bowlby with her Aussiedoodles, Birdie and Maggie. Photography courtesy of Bentley Mills.

Bowlby and her two dogs relocated to Los Angeles nearly a year ago, trading Chattanooga, Tennessee, for the West Coast. It’s a homecoming for Bowlby, who grew up in California. Although she quickly fell in love with the South after a day trip and a ghost tour, she always imagined returning west.

Groundcover turns its Wand acquisition into a Kubernetes rightsizing beta

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Observability company groundcover Inc. today added Autonomous Infrastructure to its platform in beta to rightsize the processor and memory requests automatically on customers’ Kubernetes workloads based on how each workload behaves in production.

The technology comes from Kubernetes resource optimization startup Wand Cloud Ltd., which groundcover acquired in September.

The release is aimed at the processor and memory requests Kubernetes has engineers set for each workload up front, usually before it has handled any real traffic, and groundcover said most teams seldom return to those numbers. When one is set too low, the first sign is often a service throwing out-of-memory errors. Padding that goes unused ends up on the monthly cloud bill.

Because the groundcover sensor has been collecting production history all along, a customer on a supported version can open Autonomous Infrastructure for the first time without installing anything and find the data the feature needs already in place. Requested processor and memory for each workload appear next to actual usage. Estimated monthly savings sit beside a count of under-provisioned workloads, and a chart tracks over time how much of the potential saving has been realized.

Dollar figures in the beta are based on a price per processor and memory unit that customers enter themselves. Public cloud pricing is the next step, according to groundcover, and billing data such as the Cost and Usage Report from Amazon Web Services Inc. will come after that.

Most resource optimization tools size each workload in isolation, groundcover argues, so a request that looks right for one service can still starve its neighbors on a crowded node. Stability is the first constraint in groundcover’s own software. Before Autonomous Infrastructure raises a request, it checks how tightly the node is already packed so one workload’s increase does not leave the rest short of resources.

Shahar Azulay, groundcover’s co-founder and chief executive, said customers can check what their clusters stand to save, from data already held in their own cloud accounts, before they let Autonomous Infrastructure “change anything.” The feature stays off until an engineer turns it on and picks the deployments, namespaces or clusters it manages.

During the beta the software touches requests only and leaves limits where they are. Each policy carries minimum and maximum bounds for processor and memory, and an audit log records who assigned each workload to a policy and when. Groundcover suggests starting with lower-risk workloads such as development namespaces and leaving alone something like a production database that needs room to grow.

The beta is available now at no additional cost to every groundcover customer running its sensor on Kubernetes, and the company is also recruiting design partners to work with its product team while it runs. Demonstrations are scheduled for KubeCon + CloudNativeCon North America in Salt Lake City Nov. 9-12.

Groundcover is a venture capital-backed startup that has raised $160 million to date, including a $100 million Series C round led by One Peak in July.

In addition to allowing a bigger screen in the same form factor, the narrower bezels also modernize the iPad mini’s overall look and feel. The bezels are noticeably slimmer than the iPad Air and base model iPad, and slightly thinner than the iPad Pro.

With smaller bezels, a thinner chassis, and a lighter weight, the new iPad mini feels significantly more premium than before. It reminds me less of the base model iPad or iPad Air and more of the iPad Pro, just shrunken down.

The big news about the display, though, is the switch to OLED. This marks Apple’s first non-Pro iPad to offer an OLED screen. It’s not the fancy tandem OLED display of the iPad Pro, but it’s a huge improvement over the previous model’s LCD panel.

The new iPad mini offers SDR brightness up to 600 nits, up from the previous model’s 500 nits max. The iPad mini also supports HDR content, with up to 1200 nits peak brightness.

The new iPad mini’s screen is undeniably an improvement over its predecessor. All of the inherent benefits of OLED apply here, including better colors, improved contrast, higher brightness, and more.

Unfortunately, the new iPad mini’s screen has one limitation: it doesn’t support ProMotion, or a 120Hz refresh rate of any kind. Instead, it’s still locked to 60Hz. This is an incredibly frustrating decision that I can’t quite wrap my head around.

Apple touts the iPad mini’s OLED screen as offering significantly faster pixel response time than the previous generation’s LCD panel, leading to improvements like smoother scrolling and reduced motion blur.

I’ve noticed that things like swiping between Home Screen pages, scrolling social media, and system animations are noticeably smoother. They aren’t, however, as smooth as an Apple device with a ProMotion display, and they’re not a substitute for a higher refresh-rate display.

I’m sure Apple knows the iPad mini’s market, and it seems to have decided not to prioritize ProMotion based on that information. However, as someone who has longed for a so-called “iPad mini Pro” for years, I’m disappointed by the decision. The new iPad mini’s screen is so much better … I just wish Apple had gone that extra step and added ProMotion. We’ve reached a point where ProMotion should be table stakes, especially after Apple brought it to the base iPhone 17 last year.

For an extra $100, you can upgrade the new iPad mini to have a nano-texture display (on the 256GB and 512GB models).

I’m a huge fan of Apple’s nano-texture display and have it on my 14-inch MacBook Pro. On the iPad mini, I think it makes even more sense.

The iPad mini is ultra-portable, and you naturally carry it around and use it in different lighting environments. The nano-texture glass helps reduce reflections and glare in a wide variety of lighting conditions. I also really like the combination of the iPad mini’s book-like size and the nano-texture finish.

There is a slight tradeoff in color vibrancy compared to a glossy OLED screen, as on other Apple devices with nano-texture, but it’s a very minor difference in my experience. The screen still looks fantastic.

One of the more interesting changes with the new iPad mini is something Apple calls the “Acoustic Display.” Instead of traditional speakers with visible grilles, Apple has developed a new system that uses the display itself to produce sound.

Essentially, Apple has designed special woofers that vibrate the display to produce low and midrange frequencies. For higher frequencies, custom tweeters are hidden between the glass and aluminum enclosure. Apple says this new system delivers louder audio, “punchier” bass, and clearer highs, despite the iPad mini’s significantly thinner design.

In practice, I think the new iPad mini sounds great. I’d choose headphones or an external speaker for serious music or video consumption, but day-to-day the iPad mini’s built-in speakers are perfectly adequate. (I’d bet that this Acoustic Display technology shows up in future iPhones too, and the iPad mini is serving as a test bed of sorts.)

The Acoustic Display also plays an important role in another major upgrade: water and dust resistance. The new iPad mini is IP67 rated, which means it can be immersed in 1 meter of freshwater for up to 30 minutes. It’s the first iPad to have an official water-resistance rating.

Adding water resistance to the iPad mini is particularly notable given its size and portability. I naturally find myself wanting to take it everywhere, and water resistance makes that more feasible.

One use case where all these improvements really come together is reading. The thinner and lighter design, nano-texture glass, and water resistance make the new iPad mini the most compelling iPad yet to serve as an e-reader.

The new iPad mini is powered by Apple’s A20 Pro chip, which I already covered in detail in my iPhone 18 Pro review. The short version: the A20 Pro chip gives you plenty of power for anything you can throw at the iPad mini today.

One detail worth calling out is that Apple uses two different versions of the A20 Pro in the iPad mini. The 128GB and 256GB models feature a five-core CPU and 16-core Neural Engine, while the 512GB model gets a six-core CPU and Dual 16-core Neural Engine. All three configurations feature the same seven-core GPU.

I don’t think anyone should buy the 512GB version of the iPad mini just for those extra cores. You should pick your iPad mini configuration based on how much storage you need, not the version of the A20 Pro chip. You don’t need to worry about performance with the new iPad mini. And perhaps most importantly, the A20 Pro chip ensures the iPad mini has enough headroom for the future.

There’s been a lot of consternation about where the iPad mini fits in a world where the iPhone Duo also now exists. It’s a fair question. After all, when the iPhone Duo is unfolded, the two devices share a similar form factor … but that’s really where the similarities end.

The iPad mini offers the full-fledged, unapologetic iPadOS experience. It supports the full multitasking prowess of an iPad Pro, including windowing and the menu bar. The new iPad mini also supports extended display mode, so you can plug it into an external display and extend your iPad experience there.

The iPad mini also gets iPadOS app layouts, and most importantly, a desktop-class browsing experience in Safari. It’s a remarkably capable device in a remarkably compact form factor. It fits where other iPads can’t, yet it runs the same version of iPadOS as other iPads.

The new iPad mini does all of that, but better than before. The thinner, lighter design paired with smaller bezels gives the new iPad mini a meaningful visual refresh. The added water resistance means you can do even more with the compact and pocketable iPad mini design.

But all of this comes at a higher price of $699. This marks the second price increase for the iPad mini this year. It started the year at $499, rose to $599 as part of Apple’s broader price increases in June, and is now $699 as of this refresh.

The new iPad mini also still starts with 128GB of storage. Increasing the base storage to 256GB would’ve helped ease some of the pain of that price increase. It’s also getting increasingly hard to manage a device with 128GB, particularly with the storage needs of Apple Intelligence and Siri AI. The lack of ProMotion and Face ID also make the price increase sting more.

Ultimately, the iPad mini form factor is a joy to use. The design feels great in the hand, and having the full iPadOS experience in such a compact device remains impressive. It’s not quite the “iPad mini Pro” I’ve always wanted, but it’s big upgrade to a form factor that I’ve always loved.
