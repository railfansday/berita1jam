Reviews have dropped for How to Rob A Bank, starring Nicolaus Holt, Zoë Kravitz, Pete Davidson, Anna Sawai, and John C. Reilly, and they aren’t pretty. The movie, directed by David Leitch (Deadpool 2, The Fall Guy), hits the 2026 movie schedule this week. The reviews are not as uniformly negative as they were for another Amazon MGM movie, 2025’s War of the Worlds (How to Rob a Bank is hovering just south of 50% on Rotten Tomatoes), but they are giving me the same vibes. That’s not a good thing.

Even in some of the more positive reviews, writers aren’t impressed by the heist movie’s seemingly ham-fisted attempt to bring the classic heist movie genre into the 2020s in its presentation (and political message), just like War of the Worlds attempted (poorly) to update that classic sci-fi story with Amazon drones and Ice Cube responding to text messages for an entire movie. Caroline Siede, writing for Decoding Everything, says:

How to Rob a Bank wants to be a rallying cry for social revolution via internet community building, but it winds up feeling more like a mindless TikTok scroll.

Even the more positive reviews, like Cortlyn Kelly's 2.5-star review for Roger Ebert, call attention to this:

Breaking the silver-screen barrier, the ringleader, Ryan (Hoult), addresses his audience by stating how important they are; almost like a double entendre, the movie speaks to both its fictional audience and those in the theater. This focus on the audience is intriguing, adding depth to an otherwise standard money-heist flick. Pandering to a younger generation by contentifying the plot makes it easy for ‘How to Rob a Bank’ to one day be screened as a 212-part TikTok series.

The same can be said for the positive-ish review from The Hollywood Reporter, which is pretty harsh on the social media aspects of the film. Writer David Rooney says:

While the robberies are diverting enough and the YouTube inserts splashed with pop-up graphics, the fun wears a bit thin, which makes the shift into trauma and pathos a welcome one, even if the transition might have been smoother.

The Globe and Mail’s review of How to Rob a Bank also questions the presentation of the movie in social media form, with film critic Radheyan Simonpillai writing:

This is not a heist movie. It’s the online hack to a heist movie, curiously made for the big screen, seeking the path of least resistance to constant dopamine hits. We’re foregoing all the labour of a heist movie because it would likely be oh so tedious for the movie’s target audience, presumably the generation under 30, whose work ethic is constantly called into question.

The fact that How to Rob A Bank also tried to make an anti-establishment, anti-banking movie while being financed by Amazon isn’t helping its case, per some reviews.

For example, in his 2-star review for The Guardian, Benjamin Lee wrote that not only does the movie feel like it was put together by someone trying to nail the “right now” of social media and missing badly, but the political message is also lost:

It’s a little hard to stomach an anti-capitalism screed not only funded by Amazon but featuring several scenes of lame, please-aim-logo-towards-camera, product placement (after one dismantles the system, a refreshing can of Mountain Dew really does hit the spot).

Writing for The Daily Beast, Nick Schager puts the film’s attempt at a political statement even more succinctly:

Their film attempts to rage against the machine from within the (Amazon/MGM) machine.

In fact, Amazon and its CEO, Jeff Bezos, find themselves at the heart of a lot of the negative reviews. William Bibbiani at The Wrap writes:

It’s an Amazon-MGM production, which makes the film’s efforts to sell anti-capitalism as entertainment hypocritical, laughable and insulting. It’s a film that says you should stand up to Jeff Bezos, but only after you pay Jeff Bezos. No matter how cool “Atomic Blonde” director David Leitch tries to make bank robbing look, this is the opposite of cool.

I would like to note that not all of the reviews are scathing, but none are overwhelmingly positive, either. The movie seems like it delivers on action for many reviews (though some didn’t like it), but the great action sequences don’t seem to save it from itself, even if some reviews found How to Rob a Bank fun enough.

The movie also doesn’t look like it’ll be all that big of a hit at the box office, meaning it’ll likely be available to watch with a Prime subscription pretty quickly. We’ll have to see on that.

Final Fantasy is a series absolutely defined by its music, with truly legendary composers and artists stemming from every title. Sadly, Final Fantasy 14’s Twitter announced earlier today that Eikichi Iwai, bassist for the band made for the game THE PRIMALS, has passed away. After recently performing at the most recent Japanese Fan Fest, his passing is a sudden low blow that has staggered fans across the globe.

THE PRIMALS are the standout musicians of Final Fantasy 14, far from the only incredibly talented maestros making the music happen, but definitely the flashiest. Often brought out to live events for both their own original tracks and covers of some of 14's other greatest hits, THE PRIMALS helped forge some of the greatest tracks to grace Final Fantasy 14, and Iwai-san's absence will be deeply felt in future events.

While the rest of the musicians behind FF 14 and his bandmates on THE PRIMALS will feel the most potent loss, fans across the globe are aching as well, already holding in-game mourning vigils throughout Eorzea. Sitting by fires and playing different pieces of music Iwai worked on, fans are lamenting his passing the best way they know how, by celebrating all he gave to them.

The future of the band is currently uncertain, especially with how sudden Iwai’s loss truly was.

Eikichi Iwai passed at age 57, a titan of his craft and an incredible musician across his pursuits, charming millions who played Final Fantasy 14 and all who followed his long history with the band Chirinuruwowaka, of which he was a founding member. Never one to slow down or stop making music, with his recent showing at the latest Japanese FF14 Fan Fest being cited as one of THE PRIMALS best ever showings.

Close in the Distance, A Long Fall, and Rise, all written by Masayoshi Soken, show a mastery of tone and emotion across an incredible range, perfectly putting players in the mindset of the current moment. THE PRIMALS’ own coverage of tracks like Footfalls and Dawntrail gives each song life, fueled by THE PRIMALS’ incredible energy.

I genuinely believe you could find someone who cites almost every song in Final Fantasy 14 as one of their favorites, and that’s amazing. The incredible display of artistry throughout the title is a large reason why it has lasted as long as it has with the massive fanbase it has cultivated.

With live service games and MMOs like Final Fantasy 14, it can be all too easy and lucrative to fall into the trappings of consumerism, focusing on the product rather than the art. FF really doesn’t have that issue. FF14 has always felt like a title guided by the artists who create it, and after the loss of such a powerful artist, it’s up to the rest of us to keep on that path. Rest in peace, Eikichi Iwai.

Popcorn at The Paris Theater and Cocktails at Bergdorf’s: Inside Netflix’s Avedon Screening and After-Party

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Richard Avedon, the revered photographer whose work has helped shape the legacy of Vogue, had a choice way of introducing himself: “I can’t write, I can’t type, I can’t read my own handwriting, but I can see like a son of a b*tch.” These are the very words uttered by the late lensman as he introduces himself in a new Netflix documentary chronicling his life.

Ahead of its official streaming release on November 11, Ron Howard’s highly anticipated Avedon premiered at the Paris Theater, Manhattan’s longest-running arthouse cinema. Guests dutifully lined up upon arrival at the 58th Street institution, eager to pick up butter-topped popcorn and sour candy before securing their seats.

Greatly influenced by film and dance, Avedon’s genre-defining artistic direction saw him remove models from the confines of studio backdrops and instead photograph them in everyday settings. “He was smart and he was also the right talent and individual for the right time, as it relates to the mediums of photography and magazines,” shared Howard while discussing the project with The New Yorker’s Adam Gopnik on stage.

Attendees settled in to watch the 106-minute documentary which premiered at the Cannes Film Festival earlier this year. During his six-decade career, Avedon lensed seminal ads for brands like Calvin Klein and Versace, and captured cultural figures of the day including Andy Warhol, Marilyn Monroe, Audrey Hepburn, the Beatles, and Jacqueline and John F. Kennedy. Later, he pivoted from fashion to hard news, capturing images of the Black Panther Party and the Chicago Seven, and even traveled to Saigon to document the impact of the Vietnam War.

“The process really did become a character study of this artist,” Howard said of the documentary. “Although Richard didn’t care passionately about art museums, he was unbelievably passionate about movies and theater. Overall, he really saw himself as a director.”

Following a heartfelt round of applause, the crimson-colored curtains closed, signaling it was time for the after-party to get underway. Thankfully, moviegoers only had to traipse as far right as next door to Bergdorf Goodman, sauntering past the newly installed winter wonderland decor.

On the department store’s second floor, Avedon photography books brimming with famous portraits of Liza Minnelli and Brooke Shields were given pride of place in between displays of Jimmy Choos and Christian Louboutins. Amid the leopard print carpets and chartreuse velvet sofas, guests swapped their favorite moments from the documentary over petite cones of tuna tartare and tangy apple-ginger cocktails.

Deutsche Bank Goes Live With Crypto Custody After a Five-Year Buildup

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Deutsche Bank Goes Live With Crypto Custody After a Five-Year Buildup

Five years after it first floated the idea, Germany’s largest lender is now holding Bitcoin for clients.

Deutsche Bank said Wednesday that its Digital Asset Custody service has gone live for paying clients, giving institutional and corporate customers in Europe a regulated way to store and transfer Bitcoin, Ethereum and three stablecoins: Circle’s USDC and EURC, plus EURAU.

The bank manages wallets and private keys on clients’ behalf. For now, the service covers custody and transfers, with tokenized financial instruments on its roadmap.

The service is aimed at clients of Deutsche Bank’s corporate and investment banking units, including corporations, asset managers, hedge funds, custodians, brokers and sovereign institutions, according to the bank’s September announcement of the plan.

Its setup includes hardware-based key protection, multi-person approvals and separate warm and cold storage, the bank said.

“In the medium term we aim to deliver a fully integrated, regulated ecosystem that supports the custody, tokenisation, and settlement of digital assets,” said Paul Maley, Deutsche Bank’s global head of trust and securities services.

Deutsche Bank also signed a memorandum of understanding with Deutsche Asset Management, the majority-owned money manager formerly known as DWS, to explore working together on tokenized money market funds, crypto exchange-traded commodities and stablecoins.

The asset manager oversaw about 1.22 trillion euros as of Sept. 30. The bank has also said it plans to expand the custody service to Asia.

The launch caps a long road, going back to at least 2021 when Deutsche Bank began readying a crypto custody and trading platform. Then, in 2023, the bank applied for a digital asset custody license in Germany. Last year, reports said the bank planned to build the service with technology from crypto exchange Bitpanda, alongside its longtime partner Taurus.

Deutsche Bank received authorization from German regulator BaFin under the EU’s Markets in Crypto-Assets regulation, or MiCA, which requires crypto service providers to be licensed. The bank cautioned that its custody service doesn’t protect clients against swings in the value of digital assets, fraud or failures by other market participants.

The custody launch isn’t Deutsche Bank’s only crypto bet. The bank is among a group of global lenders, including Goldman Sachs and Citi, backing a $270 million challenge to crypto’s dominant stablecoin issuers.

Why is crypto crashing today? Put simply, cryptocurrency markets are under pressure largely because of stronger-than-expected U.S. inflation data. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) for October rose 0.4% month-over-month, exceeding the forecast of 0.3%.

This, in turn, has pushed Treasury yields higher and dampened interest in riskier assets. As a result, Bitcoin has crashed to around $73,500-$73,800, and Ethereum and several major altcoins have also declined significantly over the past 48 hours.

Adding to the selling pressure are ongoing outflows from U.S. spot Bitcoin and Ethereum ETFs, as well as significant liquidations of leveraged long positions amounting to hundreds of millions, per CoinGlass data.

The combination of anticipated tighter monetary policy and reduced institutional demand has contributed to the prolonged downturn in the cryptocurrency market.

New emergency rules force banks, pension funds, and regulated investment vehicles to reduce or eliminate Bitcoin exposure within months. Spot ETF outflows accelerate into a self-reinforcing spiral, while large corporate treasury holders and early institutional adopters begin liquidating positions to comply.

The “digital gold” narrative fractures under forced selling and negative headlines, creating a prolonged risk-off environment that few current holders are prepared for. As a result, Grok AI predicts the Bitcoin price will fall to approximately $40,000 by January 1, 2027.

Denis Villeneuve Talks ‘Dune: Part Three’ and Rebooting James Bond: ‘We Cannot F*ck It Up.’

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

“I’m out of a job,” Denis Villeneuve said, jokingly. Hours earlier, Villeneuve had signed off on the IMAX 70-milimeter print of Dune: Part Three, officially wrapping post-production on that film—and in turn, a decade-long chapter of the Quebecois writer-director’s working life.

Fresh off this milestone, Villeneuve and GQ had come to the Lucas Museum of Narrative Art in Los Angeles for a conversation and a private screening of Dune: Part Two, presented by OMEGA, in honor of his anointment as GQ’s Director of the Year. Global Editorial Director Adam Baidawi introduced Villeneuve before the director and GQ’s Zach Baron dove into a conversation that covered his original Dune plans, where Dune: Part Three breaks from its predecessors, and Villeneuve’s feelings about taking over the James Bond franchise.

GQ’s Adam Baidawi and Zach Baron with Denis Villeneuve

“I feel light,” the director of the year told Baron, then added, “Numb.” He was still processing Dune’s end, he said—while also coming to grips with closing the book on “the most rewarding, inspiring, and best ten years of my life as a filmmaker.”

Villeneuve’s first two Dune films have raked in more than a billion dollars at the box office. Part Three appears likely to nearly double that when it hits theaters (and locks up most of the world’s IMAX screens) a week before Christmas. But at one point during his conversation with Baron, the director pushed back gently on the notion of these movies as a “franchise.”

“There was nothing industrial in the way they were made,” Villeneuve said. They were the work of “a bunch of friends”—a familial crew, out in the desert putting things together.

“In French,” the Quebecois director continued, “there is a word—artisanal. They are very personal. Made with a lot of care. They’re little big things—but in a way, they are delicate.”

After all, as sprawling and cosmically grand as they are, these movies are also a deeply personal project for the director, born of images that took root in his head when he first read Frank Herbert’s thick-as-a-brick, notoriously adaptation-resistant 1965 novel Dune at age 13. Later, as Villeneuve racked up credits and clout, people would ask him what his dream project was; the answer was always Dune.

“I kept saying to myself, Someone else will do it one day,” he told Baron. “Another filmmaker will adapt this book again. And I kept waiting, and I kept waiting, until I felt ready to do it myself.”

Herbert wrote five escalatingly bonkers Dune sequels before his death in 1986, but Villeneuve intended to walk away from the table after adapting the first book across two films.

“Trilogies are very dangerous,” he told Baron. “The first movie is a promise, and if you fulfill the promise with the second one, then there’s a feeling of security that comes with that, and you feel that you have a recipe…That’s the trap. Because then you repeat yourself.”

But after wrapping Part Two, Villeneuve found himself unable to stop thinking about Dune Messiah—the second Dune novel, which Herbert supposedly wrote in frustration, believing the audience had missed the point by embracing Paul Atreides as a hero.

Villeneuve said his take on Messiah is “a different beast” than the first two films, built it around the tension between Timothée Chalamet’s Paul and his Fremen consort Chani (Zendaya), who Villeneuve has re-imagined—in a pivotal departure from Herbert’s version—as a nonbeliever who takes a critical view of Paul’s rise to power.

Neel Shah, Timothy Simons, and Michael Lasker

Of course, the specter (S.P.E.C.T.R.E.?) of another franchise hung over the proceedings at the Lucas Museum. Villeneuve, after all, is only technically between jobs; he’s a self-described “monomaniac” who insists that he can only hyper-focus on one project at a time and has been locked in on Dune for a decade, but the as-yet-untitled 26th Bond film is in motion, with Villeneuve signed on to direct.

GQ’s Adam Baidawi, Denis Villeneuve, and OMEGA’s Lucien Jornod

He could say almost nothing in public about the project, or even when he’d begin shooting it—he joked about having “the target of the sniper” on his chest—but spoke of feeling the responsibility not to fail: “We cannot fuck it up. We do not have the choice.”

Over the past week, the casting rumors around Villeneuve’s Bond reboot have reached what our UK colleagues accurately termed the “fever-dream” stage. It might be Callum Turner or Slow Horses’ Jack Lowden, but it might be Robert Pattinson, who’s emerged as a surprise front-runner after months of supposition about the part being earmarked for a relative unknown. At some point, toward the end of Tuesday’s conversation, the subject of Bond 26’s lead could no longer be avoided.

“Has the role of James Bond been cast?” Baron asked the director.

“It’s—it’s in the process. In the process,” Villeneuve said with a smile. “I am pushing the gas.”

“Blink once for Robert Pattinson,” Baron suggested, “and twice for Callum Turner.”

Villeneuve, for the record, did not blink. ✦

NASA's secret weapon in the lunar space race may be ... procurement reform?

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

“The pace that’s occurring, and the nature of the procurements, is unprecedented.”

Senior NASA officials held a lengthy news conference last weekend, laying out a series of lunar contract awards, new contract opportunities, and mission decisions. They followed one after the other: landers, nuclear-powered rovers, scouting drones, power generation, storage, and communication satellites.

“Hopefully, we’re keeping industry on their toes trying to catch up to all the solicitations we’re doing,” said Carlos García-Galán, manager of the Moon Base Program, more than 90 minutes into the news conference. “This is what it takes to do the Moon Base in the timeline we have set forth.”

The rapid-fire announcement of awards tells us a few things about NASA’s efforts to return to the Moon and establish a durable settlement there during the next decade.

First, the agency is serious about this effort—the Artemis Moon Base program represents the most credible effort to establish a permanent outpost on another world in NASA’s history. Second, NASA is reaching out to all corners of the US commercial space industry, casting an extraordinarily wide net to reach companies that have yet to fly any hardware into orbit. Third, NASA is getting creative about how it’s buying these services, and this may matter most of all.

There was a lot packed into the announcements this weekend, so to make sense of it all, I called Mike Gold, a lawyer and former NASA associate administrator who set up the Artemis Accords, who is now the president of Redwire Space. He was at NASA at the beginning of Artemis and understands what it takes to get the government moving. He said he was blown away. During a period of seven days, Gold said, NASA put out five proposals for lunar contracts.

“The pace that’s occurring, and the nature of the procurements is unprecedented,” Gold said. “You are seeing an all-hands-on-deck approach. Big companies, small companies, new companies, old companies.”

What has impressed Gold most about NASA’s strategy since announcing the Moon Base in March, at the Ignition event, has been the agency’s evolving procurement strategy. The space agency has adopted a flexible procurement strategy similar to one used by the US Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency.

There are two components. NASA has largely used a mechanism called Broad Agency Announcements, which ask for a capability such as providing power on the surface of the Moon. This allows NASA to ask industry for ideas without being too prescriptive and levying a bunch of requirements. It’s rather like instead of telling industry precisely what it wants, NASA asks industry what it can do.

Critically, Gold said, the space agency has also used a DARPA-style preliminary proposal process. In response to a traditional procurement, interested companies would prepare a detailed proposal, often running dozens, if not hundreds, of pages. This required an extraordinary amount of time.

With the Moon Base, NASA is employing a two-step process. After NASA announces the need for a service, interested companies submit a short white paper describing their solution. After a short review, NASA identifies the concepts it wants to pursue further, and only then do companies invest the time and resources to prepare a detailed proposal.

Gold said several lunar opportunities interested Redwire, and the company submitted initial proposals in response. But it only had to prepare a full proposal for opportunities that NASA wanted to pursue, including using a variant of its roll-out solar arrays on the Moon to generate power.

“We were able to focus the lion’s share of our resources in what the agency really wanted and what we were best at,” he said.

From this shift, it seems clear that NASA is trying to move away from micromanaging every individual lunar-base subsystem and instead seeks to coordinate independently developed commercial capabilities. The space agency is sourcing them from everywhere, from major contractors like Lockheed Martin, SpaceX, Blue Origin, and Northrop Grumman to relatively new startups such as Impulse Space, ispace-US, Voyager Technologies, Quantum Space, and more.

With questions emerging about how committed SpaceX is to NASA work in the future, the space agency appears to be seeking to diversify the way it gets cargo to the Moon. For example, it is leaning into Relativity Space and its large (but unproven) Terran R rocket, as well as orbital transfer vehicles built by Impulse Space. NASA is prioritizing the creation of a whole ecosystem of potential contractors rather than relying on one or two primes.

In doing so, NASA is taking a big risk. It is attempting to accelerate its lunar program by buying services from companies whose hardware is not yet operational and, in some cases, exists only as advanced engineering designs. For NASA, ultimately, it is a high-risk, high-reward approach. But in a demanding contest with China to reach and establish settlements on the Moon, NASA’s post-Apollo timidity is no longer tenable.

So how will NASA pay for all of the new stuff? Some of the funding is being repurposed from the previously shuttered Lunar Gateway program, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said. For example, as part of the “One Big Beautiful Bill” act passed in 2025, NASA received $2.6 billion for the Gateway. Some of the funding was already appropriated through the agency’s Commercial Lunar Payload Services Program.

Moreover, Isaacman said, some of these contracts are relatively small and will enable NASA to invest more down the road in successful services.

“By doing a lot of littles, and learning from our approach and also putting some good wins on the board, we’re demonstrating that we can be very efficient and responsible with taxpayer dollars,” he said. With this approach, he added, “You give future administrations the option of how they can dial up or down missions. Some of these missions in the, call it, $200 million range between launch, lander, and payload; potentially, you can add those or drop those inside a $25 billion approximate budget per year without putting the entire program at risk.”
