This Is the Minimum Amount of Strength You Need in the Offseason to Get Ready for Your Next Training Block

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Scale back your running between races while using your time in the weight room to build a stronger foundation.

After months of long runs and tough workouts, you’ve finally crossed the finish line of your goal race. Now, it’s time to recover—and figure out what’s next.

While you can scale back your running in the offseason, it’s worth making room for strength training. Even a short time in the weight room can help you build a stronger foundation for your next training block.

The good news? You don’t need to spend all your newfound free time in the gym to make progress. By hitting the following minimum amount of strength work each week, you set yourself up to handle more mileage and run more efficiently when it’s time to chase a new goal.

In the first two weeks after your race, you should focus mainly on rest and getting back into easy jogging. After that, aim to add at least two strength sessions per week to your routine, exercise physiologist Heather Milton, C.S.C.S., previously told Runner’s World. Milton says these offseason strength sessions should focus on heavier weights (just make sure you work up in weight!) and a low-to-moderate number of reps.

During these heavy lifting workouts, athletes should aim to complete four to six sets of one to five reps of each exercise, pausing for two to four minutes in between each set, strength coach and physical therapist Lisbeth Hoyt, D.P.T., C.S.C.S., previously told Runner’s World.

While running is your main focus during marathon training (or any race distance, for that matter), strength sessions often help you maintain the strength you already have, Milton explains. But between training blocks, you can put more emphasis on building strength.

“Plus, when you start training for your next marathon, you’ll be in a stronger position to increase your running volume again because you’ve strengthened all of the areas of your body that were impacted by your previous race, which can help prevent injuries,” Milton adds.

You might associate heavy lifting with short sprinters or football players—athletes who focus on quick, explosive movement, rather than steady endurance. But building stronger muscles through heavy lifting supports long-distance running, too.

For starters, lifting heavier weights helps you build muscular strength, which translates to improved running economy, New York City-based strength and run coach Yusuf Jeffers previously told Runner’s World. Meaning, heavy lifting can help you use less energy to run at a given pace.

Research backs this up. A review published in Sports Medicine in 2023 examined several types of strength training in middle- and long-distance runners, including lifting heavy, submaximal lifting, isometric exercises, and plyometrics. The researchers found that lifting high loads—at least 80 percent of a person’s one-rep max—had the greatest effect on improving running economy at faster speeds. (Plyometrics worked particularly well for improving economy at slower speeds.)

The benefits extend beyond your muscles, too. Hoyt explains that strength training can support connective tissues and bone health.

It’s important to remember lifting “heavy” looks different for everyone. If you’re new to strength training or you’re unfamiliar with a particular movement, start by learning proper form and gradually work up in weight, Hoyt says.

Hoyt suggests focusing on movements such as squats, deadlifts, lunges, and rows, encouraging athletes to move slowly and with control.

Jared McCain loves a routine—several routines, actually, which is why there’s a whiteboard in his bathroom, covered with a bulleted list of his daily to-dos (journaling, affirmations, bone broth, and lymphatic jumps, among them). That’s all separate from the 22-year-old’s bedtime and wake-up regimens (silk eye mask, silk pillowcase, silk bonnet, magnesium, and mouth tape, then a multistep skincare process, respectively). And this doesn’t even include many of the things his 7.7 million social media followers have come to love: Jared hanging out in de-puffing eye patches. Jared wandering around wearing Gen Z’s favorite pimple patches. Jared flashing his latest bright-colored manicure.

As he moves through his Oklahoma City home, where he lives with three of his best friends, it’s clear that none of the above is purely performative, as is so often the case with internet celebrities. “I do a lot of skincare because I sweat a lot,” he says as he works his way through what looks like the entire Hailey Bieber–founded Rhode skincare line. “I really like to take care of my face.”

Click through the videos on his TikTok or Instagram page and you’ll likely see that face illuminated by LED lights. “One of my favorite parts about my house is my red light showerhead,” he says. “Red light just creates this vibe and gives me health benefits. It’s number one in my life.” It’s also on his whiteboard three times.

Jared first broke into virality as a starting guard on Duke University’s basketball team. And while his talent on the court would lead him to the NBA—he now plays for the Oklahoma City Thunder—it was something else that captured the wider world’s attention. “I started painting my nails during COVID, and that’s when I also started on TikTok,” he explains. “I played with the polish on and I had a really good game, so I thought, Hold on, this might be the new thing.” (He now has brand partnerships with Sally Hansen and Ulta Beauty.)

I’ve gotten used to it by now. It’s a blessing to even be talked about.”

Not that everything Jared does is 100 percent zen. When he’s not indulging in self-betterment practices, he’s posting TikTok dances and lip syncs from the Thunder’s locker room, sometimes with special appearances from his teammates.

“Entertaining has always been so fun to me,” he says. “That’s kind of what basketball is, too. Growing up, I thought I was going to be on the Disney Channel or a pop star. He launches into a rendition of Olivia’s “A Couple Minutes” with surprisingly impressive vocals. “The song could make me cry right now,” he confesses. One of his roommates later jokes, “We need a mute button”—something Jared’s fans have never asked for and likely never will.

Alex Prager Has Hollywood Dreams

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

With a feature film and major solo shows, the artist is experiencing a moment like no other.

Alex Prager was in her cubicle at an internet startup, staring at the endless rows of other desks, when she realized that something was terribly wrong. “The rest of my life flashed in my mind,” she says. She began to cry. “It was like that Talking Heads song: And you may find yourself in a beautiful house / With a beautiful wife / And you may ask yourself, / ‘Well…how did I get here?’” she says. “This was not my life.” Soon after, she attended a show of William Eggleston’s work and left certain that her future was in photography. Within a week, she’d bought a used Nikon N90s camera and darkroom equipment. “I just knew, the way that you know you have a face and you know who you love. I knew from that moment forward what my path was,” she says.

It didn’t take long for Prager to start getting noticed in art circles. And this winter will be one of the most significant seasons of her career, with both a major solo museum show at the Crystal Bridges Museum of American Art in Bentonville, Arkansas, and an exhibition at the New York gallery Lehmann Maupin. DreamQuil, her first feature film, was released in theaters this fall and will be available to stream in December.

Prager’s color-saturated works capture cinematic scenes that bring to mind classic Hitchcock films. She creates full worlds for her photos, with sets, casts, and impeccable period costumes. Her pieces gained notice with her 2007 show “Polyester” at L.A.’s Robert Berman Gallery. The series showed women, perfectly attired in ’60s-era looks, posed in evocative ways. A heavily made-up girl clutches a Big Gulp, a flaxen braid thrown over one shoulder; four young women huddle together in swimsuits, a plane flying across the perfect blue sky above. An ominous feeling pervades.

Her work sparkles on the surface but explores something darker underneath. “The first color photograph I took was staged and a play on The Wizard of Oz and death, but it was very colorful and beautiful. I was interested in the way to explore something that felt layered. Underneath it, you find there are very uncomfortable things or darker stories and emotions,” Prager explains.

Prager steadily built up a following through a series of gallery shows, but things changed markedly in 2010 when her work was selected to be shown in the elite “New Photography” exhibit at MoMA. She had her first solo show at the Corcoran Gallery of Art in Washington, D.C., three years later. She’s also done work for magazines and brands like Cartier, Disney, and Apple.

“My work has always been about my personal challenges and things that I’m not really understanding in the world, but I think more and more it becomes less about me and my emotions. That’s probably also me becoming a mother and growing up, but the world has changed,” says Prager, whose son Francis is 9. The scale of her work has grown—bigger audiences, more ambitious concepts, a greater ability to stage elaborate photography—but the approach hasn’t. It’s still about the questions she needs answers to.

The idea for DreamQuil came to Prager during the pandemic (she was inspired by a meme that advertised YearQuil, a fictitious product that would allow people to sleep through the misery of 2020), and she wrote the script with her sister, Vanessa Prager. Elizabeth Banks stars as a woman who’s living in a depressing future world where people rarely leave their homes and finds herself dealing with her robot clone.

Opening two major shows within months of the release of her film wasn’t something Prager anticipated—each alone would have been a high point in her career—but she often does her best work amid a frenzy of activity. “I thrive in a bit of chaos,” she says. “I like to try to mold it and wrestle with it and attempt to control it and bend it to my will. It doesn’t always happen exactly the way I want, but it infuses me with inspiration and energy.”

The Maya People Loved the Color Red. Scientists Say It May Have Helped Bring Them Down.

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The reddish pigment cinnabar was so popular that it seeped into the water supply around Maya settlements, setting off a disastrous downstream effect.

The Maya people had a toxic relationship with the color red—quite literally, according to fresh research. The new analysis of the Yucatán Peninsula during the Maya era identified astronomically spiked levels of mercury, which might have contributed to the collapse of Maya culture.

“The mercury data was crazy,” Matt Waters, an environmental sciences professor and paleolimnologist at Auburn University, said in a university statement about the unexpected discovery on the peninsula. “Off the charts. These mercury levels aren’t normal.”

Matt Waters led a study published in Science Advances that explored sediment samples collected at two lakes—Lago Coba and Cenote Xcach—showing the intense spike in mercury (Hg) levels ranging from around 40 C.E. to 800 C.E.

“We show that Classic Period Hg concentrations in sediment cores from two lakes on the Yucatán Peninsula exceeded modern sediment Hg toxicity thresholds by orders of magnitude for centuries during ancient Maya occupation,” the authors wrote, adding that the heightened pollution coincides with aquatic food collapse, “confirming that Hg toxicity was an additional environmental stressor for Classic Maya populations.”

While Waters isn’t saying mercury caused the collapse of the Maya civilization, he also doesn’t want to ignore its impact. “It’s been confirmed that drought was an issue, deforestation was an issue, erosion was an issue, overpopulation was an issue, and feeding the population was probably an issue,” he said. “This study adds the impact of water quality. Let’s put that in the list. That’s what our study does. It establishes that list and moves it forward.”

The study actually began as part of a National Science Foundation project focused on harmful algal blooms, but a then-graduate student and study co-author, Savanna Wooten, suggested also testing for heavy metals. Sediment coring from the lake bottoms pulled up mud from thousands of years ago that contained the intense mercury spikes during the Classic Maya Period, which were then followed by an abrupt decline.

The powerfully high mercury concentration lasted for hundreds of years, and Waters believes the sudden change at the base level likely moved up the food web, impacting fish and other larger creatures. Not only would eating fish or drinking water have exposed humans to the toxic levels, but there may have even been fewer fish to catch. “They would have seen a strange color change in the water,” Waters told Live Science, “and a drop in its productivity, such as fish for protein.”

Waters linked to a study he’d worked on with a team from the University of Florida in the late 1990s that found a gap in ostracod shells during a specific period from Coba, correlating the missing shell timing to the rise in mercury. Further investigation into the cores found that declines in biological markers occurred throughout. “The algae was killed,” he said. “The cyanobacteria, they tanked. Purple sulfur bacteria, vanished. The biological community completely changed.”

The people “experienced this for decades and, in one case, for centuries.”

The source of that mercury was cinnabar, they believe, a vibrant red mineral made up of 86 percent mercury that Maya culture viewed as a sacred pigment. “This was not like mercury raining down from the sky,” Waters said. “This was not a regional thing. It was concentrated.”

Maya loved cinnabar, using the bright red in pottery, murals, temple walls, and burials. To keep up with demand, they heavily imported it, especially around Coba and Xcach. “It’s common knowledge that there was trade in obsidian, in jade, and beautiful stones, and weaponry,” Waters said. “I think this could start a conversation that cinnabar could have been a major trade commodity.”

It may also show a highly toxic love affair that contributed to Maya struggles.

<i><em>Blade Runner 2099</em></i> Is a Beautiful Slog Through a Grim Future

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The new Prime Video series picks up way, way after Denis Villeneuve’s sequel. Not that it matters.

Crowded streets. Long, cool coats. Flying cars darting through smog and neon skyscrapers. An abundance of Japanese aesthetics that imply America’s lost superpower status. Ridley Scott’s seminal 1982 masterwork Blade Runner (itself adapted from Philip K. Dick’s Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?) laid the foundation for almost all of cyberpunk science fiction, so much so that everything else feels undeservedly derivative. Even a new Blade Runner. The new series Blade Runner 2099 looks the part, but it lacks the soul of its predecessors, leaving its perspectives on what it means to be more human than human hollow, like a Replicant without memories of its own.

Streaming now on Prime Video (and just in time for a long holiday weekend) is Blade Runner 2099. A stand-alone continuation of both Ridley Scott’s towering original and Denis Villeneuve’s remarkable legacy sequel, the series takes place in 2099 (decades after Villeneuve’s Blade Runner 2049). An unseen revolution has elevated Replicants to the ruling class of Los Angeles, rendering flesh-and-bone meat-bag humans as the new discriminated minority.

Hunter Schafer flexes some of her budding leading-lady chops as Cora, a real human posing as a Replicant to smuggle herself and her brother out of Los Angeles. Her ruse leads her to Olwen (Oscar-winner Michelle Yeoh), a Replicant Blade Runner spending the final days of her artificial life span seeking out someone from her past. A humorless buddy-cop mystery, Blade Runner 2099 is mired in a plot-centric narrative that cares less about the why of all things—an underrated and beautiful feature of its predecessors—than the what and how.

In other words, it’s the first time a piece of Blade Runner media actually zeroes in on the plot, instead of anything else more interesting. Try not to think of this as an eight-hour Blade Runner series but rather an eight-hour series stretching at the seams to feel worthy of its branded title.

Is it because streaming TV in 2026 has become so plastic and hollow that even Blade Runner 2099 feels like an unengaging slog? Or were the direction of both Scott and Villeneuve so immaculate that we were distracted from how turgid they might have been all this time? Regardless, 2099’s lack of auteurship is its most deafening shortcoming. The series has been steered to the screen by executive producer Silka Luisa, whose warmer color palettes—based on her choice of more sunshine, which glistens off surfaces—lend 2099 some identity. Some.

But beyond even that minor distinction, Blade Runner 2099 is still just a plot-y crime drama that checks off boxes and goes through the motions of its genre, with nary a real thing to say. About us, about our fragile humanity, about the demise of all we’ve built through artificial intelligence. Blade Runner 2099 is a dystopian sci-fi in all the ways you expect it, but it’s hardly a good one. Sure, the original movies were oppressive, dreary, and morbid too. But they possessed a knowing glare into our fears about the future and our imminent collapse as a civilized species. The new series lacks this same insight, or indeed any of its own, all in the name of goosing up engagement numbers for the closest thing to a real-life Tyrell Corp. (Do shareholders dream of electric quarterly reports?)

Despite surface-level similarities to the movies, plus a moody noir-like motif with a sexy saxophone that, admittedly, completely and unapologetically rips, the plodding Blade Runner 2099 is a needless walkabout through a world that never begged for one. Needless is a good word, actually. The show’s strong visual execution is negated by its inability to rise to the occasion as a worthwhile or enlightening probe of the human experience, or even just as an entertaining cop show. It may be smart, but what good is intelligence if there isn’t an ounce of grace?

The 2027 Toyota GRMN Corolla Is Almost Worth the Squeeze

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Revised suspension, new gear ratios, and more torque make the GRMN Corolla more of a blast on track.

Toyota’s GR Corolla has valiantly filled the gap left by the deceased Subaru WRX STI and Mitsubishi Lancer Evo as a rally-inspired compact that combines everyday usability with performance. Combining a torque-rich turbocharged three-cylinder with a manual transmission and all-wheel drive in a practical four-door shell still sounds like a winning formula to us.

But how does it fare if practicality is reduced to sharpen the driving experience? When Toyota announced a limited run of track-focused GR Corollas with no back seat and a $65,655 price tag, alongside some choice mechanical upgrades, the first reaction was skepticism. Why dilute the approachable, jack-of-all-trades appeal of the existing GR Corolla with a more expensive version? To answer that, Toyota took Road & Track to Eagles Canyon Raceway on the outskirts of Dallas, Texas, for the chance to give the GRMN Corolla a proper romp around the track. I’m happy to report that the special edition offered a compelling defense.

Limited to 730 units, the 2027 Toyota GRMN Corolla’s upgrades are primarily focused on improved dynamic response. The suspension, tuned at the Nürburgring and Fuji Speedway, gets shorter, stiffer bump stops up front and longer bump stops in the rear to better deal with circuit curbing and midcorner bumps. Toyota has also firmed up the steering weight, especially at high speeds, and revised front-end aerodynamics to better vent turbulent air through new flaps in the wheel wells and carbon-fiber hood. Wider Michelin Cup 2 tires are also standard, replacing the GR’s Pilot Sport 4s. Driven hard on the elevation-heavy road course, the GRMN felt much more confident and able.

Part of the GR Corolla’s handling charm has historically been its rally-car rowdiness—a tendency to roll and then set into a corner, plus some degree of rear-end playfulness under power and braking—and the revised suspension setup manages to maintain this character. Approaching Turn 12, a descending, off-camber sweeper where the correct line takes the front end over an elevated curb, the GRMN Corolla refused to be unsettled. Similarly, the tendency to swing out the lightened rear end under braking I remember well from the GR Corolla seemed largely negated and easier to correct when it did happen.

The front end of the GRMN Corolla was also sharper than ever, with a rapid turn-in and heavier, confidence-inspiring steering weight. Leaning into Turn 6 at wide-open throttle, the front end didn’t waver as I asked for more steering angle at nearly 100 mph. There was even steering feel, which is a treat on any modern performance car. However, rapid steering changes were sometimes accompanied by a binding feeling, a trait we’ve experienced in previous GR Corolla variants. It was hard to say whether the aerodynamic upgrades contributed to the solid front end, but the addition of the grippier tires no doubt played a significant part in the improved confidence. Back-to-back laps in a standard GR Corolla on Pilot Sport 4s tires proved the GRMN was more planted and offered much better communication of its position and limitations.

Toyota has only made small changes to the 1.6-liter turbocharged inline-three in the GRMN, adding just 7 lb-ft of torque to take the total to 302 lb-ft, accompanied by an unchanged 300 hp. Response felt improved off the line, and there was more top-end grunt too, although the biggest reason for the perception of additional surge was likely the new revised-ratio six-speed manual transmission, which is exclusive to the GRMN.

With a longer first gear and shorter third gear, the car found a good middle ground between the slightly-too-shortness of the STI and the excessive length of something like a Porsche 718. For reference, fourth gear runs out at 105 mph, a good compromise between the thrill of changing gears and having to do so too often. The new, slightly smaller shift knob also felt more ergonomic in my hand, and the updated shifter-cable mount offered more satisfying action.

Of course, a core part of the appeal of the regular GR Corolla is its tunable differential, and this is carried over unchanged on the GRMN. Track mode offered the most rear-biased power split, allowing 70 percent to be sent to the back axle, while Gravel mode split power evenly between both axles. Even in the rear-biased setting, the GRMN Corolla was more liable to push the front end on power than rotate the rear end around.

I actually preferred the GRMN Corolla in Gravel mode around Eagles Canyon Raceway, as the front-wheel-biased performance best practice of trail braking and on-throttle patience more easily led to a clean lap. Brake feel was also improved on the GRMN Corolla, with the GRMN exhibiting no fade over more than half a dozen laps, and there was always more power available through the pedal when asked.

Ergonomically, the GRMN Corolla remains a sneakily competent sports car trapped in a four-door hatchback shape. With new bucket-style seats in red and black upholstery, the GRMN Corolla offered more lateral support at the expense of comfort. For me, the new seats felt thinner and decidedly less comfortable than the set in the standard GR Corolla. Even so, the driving position is low enough and well placed for pedal and shifter access. The pedal box was also spaced for easy heel-toe downshifts, though the intelligent throttle-blipping downshift assistance system means a driver doesn’t need to do this. Similarly, the lack of rear seats didn’t add any obvious level of noise intrusion, although the 66-pound omission came at obvious cost to carrying capacity. All told, the GRMN Corolla improves on the standard GR Corolla formula in nearly every dynamic way, but does that make it worth the premium?

At $65,655, the GRMN Corolla is a hard sell for the average enthusiast buyer. Sure, it is the best-driving GR Corolla yet—they should all come from the factory in this configuration—but it doesn’t have particularly inspiring acceleration nor mind-numbing on-track performance. Its best feature is pure agility, but even rear-driven sports machines like the venerable $65,395 Cadillac CT4-V Blackwing or the $69,550 BMW M2 offer plenty of hustle these days. Sure, there are few modern performance road cars that could outrun the GRMN Corolla on a gravel track, but that’s not a strong enough use case to sway the typical enthusiast buyer away from more horsepower and bigger engines.

Regardless, the GRMN has already proved itself well priced considering the warmth of the response it has so far received from enthusiasts, many seeking to upgrade from the GR Corolla. They will likely snap up every unit, regardless of markup. And they will be happy.

Overview: This first-person essay follows one skier’s years-long search for ski boots that fit, with bootfitters in seven states and two countries, a visit to an orthopedist, three acupuncturists, and a stranger’s garage. It’s a funny account of what hard-to-fit feet can drive a person to do, and a reminder that the right bootfitter can change everything.

In my quest for ski boots that fit, I’ve seen bootfitters in at least seven states and two countries. I’ve visited Evo and REI, small independent shops, local chains, and garages-turned-ski shops. I’ve consulted boot designers, brand reps, professional athletes, and gear reviewers; tried Intuitions, ZipFits, and custom footbeds. I’ve seen one orthopedic doctor, three acupuncturists, and two physical therapists. I’ve stayed up at night thinking about boots, and developed recurring stress dreams about them.

Last season, I was beginning to consider looping in a tarot reader or astrologer. Maybe I needed a psychotherapist, or a priest. Maybe I’d have to just quit skiing. And then, finally, I found my bootfitter. But not before resorting to some questionable behavior along the way. Here are seven desperate things I did in my search for a bootfitter who could solve my ski boot issues.

Every pair of ski boots I wore from ages two through 22 were hand-me-downs from my older brothers. Then I started working in the ski industry. While skiing around Big Sky during a media event, a kind man working for Nordica looked at my feet with a frown.

“What are those?” he asked. I was sliding around in some decade-old size 26.5 monstrosities. He shook his head and invited me to swing by the Nordica headquarters in New Hampshire. There, he measured my feet and sent me off with a pair of 25.5 Nordica Promachines.

Everything changed. Suddenly I was in an aggressive stance, my shins met real resistance, my power transfer improved dramatically. I got a taste of what a good boot can do, shortly before developing a wicked case of tendonitis in my ankles and shins.

After two seasons of excruciating, day-ending pain, I booked an appointment with an orthopedic foot and ankle specialist. I knew my boots were the culprit, and couldn’t be sure a doctor would understand the whole foot-encased-in-rigid-plastic thing. I lugged a giant bag with both my alpine and touring boots into the orthopedic practice and pulled them out for show-and-tell.

The physician kindly assured me he knew what ski boots were, and that I wasn’t dealing with any serious medical issues. I’d have to take my boots back where they belonged.

The owner of Santa Fe’s one dedicated ski shop sized me down yet again, into the stiffest 24.5 he had on hand—a 110 from Fischer. I felt like I was skiing in socks and a vise-grip at the same time, and I lost feeling in my feet on the first lap. Mercifully, he let me return them.

I’d hit the central problem: boots big enough to be comfortable gave me tendonitis, and boots small enough to prevent it gave me bunions and nerve pain. So I took my boots to Taos Bootdoctors, where a whole team of bootfitters worked patiently to expand the forefoot. I’d ski for a day or two and come back, over and over, looking for just a few more millimeters. I brought six-packs and did my best to be charming, and the lovely staff never made me feel less important than the tourists dropping thousands of dollars on gear. Still, I couldn’t finish a day without pain.

I soldiered through the season, eyeing an upcoming hut trip with dread. I could manage shorter tours, but a week of daylong missions posed some real problems. Once we were in the mountains, I knew no amount of bunion donuts and anti-inflammatories would save me. I had to instate boots-off breaks in the middle of eight-hour ski days, carefully pulling my socked feet out of jail and propping them up on a backpack in the snow while my friends took an extra lap. Sweet, stupid relief.

Finally, I heard a rumor about a man in Seattle working out of his garage who was willing to do unorthodox things to ski boots. He had a history of custom-fitting plug boots for ski racers, which seemed like a good sign. While visiting family in Washington, I brought him both pairs of boots.

He looked at my feet, listened patiently, and rattled off a comprehensive plan that involved several devices I’d never seen before. Overnight, he molded both pairs into a completely new shape. I never had to touch either boot again, and proceeded with great pleasure to ski 12-hour days in the backcountry and endless resort laps with happy feet. I had found him—the bootfitter I was looking for. Then, he retired.

When my touring boots started to go, I bought a replacement pair and nobody could make them fit. The experience began to take on mythic proportions. I felt like the princess with a pea beneath her many mattresses, or an ugly stepsister trying to cram her foot into a crystal slipper: all discomfort and wrong fit.

It seemed my trusty Tecnicas were the problem. I tried on every touring boot at ski shops in three states. I ordered over a dozen pairs online, obsessively double-checking return policies and momentarily racking up a very scary credit card bill. Eventually, I came crawling back to my old Tecnicas with no plan, two full seasons into the search.

I hesitated to say yes to ski trips, afraid I’d have to sit out ski days because of pain or busted gear. Eventually, it happened: I took my tired old touring boots to Rogers Pass in B.C. and, on day three, broke the upper cuff clean off the bottom section of the boot. Thankfully we were just a few hundred yards from the truck. I spent the entire day sitting at the trailhead.

In the meantime, my alpine boots had begun to warp. I found a replacement pair and was back at square one: home in New Mexico, with two pairs of ski boots that didn’t fit, rapidly dwindling patience, and no bootfitter.

I considered that this might be the end of the road for me as a skier. Then, I learned that one of the best skiers I know had been contemplating quitting the sport because of foot pain. He’d even gone so far as to teach himself to snowboard. But then he found exactly what I’d been looking for: yet another fit-obsessed industry veteran working out of his garage.

I visited another home workshop in western Washington and the bootfitter listened carefully as I explained the issue. Then, he overhauled both pairs of boots, tirelessly stretching the forefoots to give my feet room to breathe. They took on a completely new shape: generous and wide. I took both pairs out for a spin and sighed in relief.

There are a lot of great bootfitters out there. But for a freak like me, I’ve learned I need something of a mad scientist. If you’re plagued with bone spurs, high insteps, wide feet—there’s hope, I promise. With a little faith, a lot of persistence, and maybe something like divine intervention, I trust you’ll find the bootfitter you need. Let’s just hope they don’t retire.

China’s homegrown memory comes largely from two companies, CXMT for DRAM and YMTC for 3D NAND, and both are booming. CXMT’s Oct. 30 quarterly report shows a nine-month net profit of ¥132.0 billion ($19.6 billion), against a ¥5.28 billion ($785 million) loss a year earlier, on revenue more than eight times as high, at ¥269.0 billion ($40.0 billion). YMTC’s numbers, just for Q1, are at ¥47.04 billion ($7.0 billion) in revenue and ¥33.38 billion ($5.0 billion) in profit attributable to shareholders, more than double 2025.

Both are expanding and adding fabs, and each is edging into the other’s products. No Chinese manufacturer can yet produce high-bandwidth memory (HBM) in volume, though. HBM is the stacked DRAM that AI accelerators depend on, and currently the U.S. bars Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron from selling it into China. CXMT’s prospectus lists no HBM project, though its HBM3E is reportedly in risk production, an early run before the process is fully qualified. Huawei designs its own HBM but doesn’t say who makes it.

Memory is a commodity, so the story is in the wafers. We cover the companies running memory fabs in China: CXMT, YMTC, a handful of smaller Chinese fabs, and Samsung and SK hynix, which run some of their biggest plants there. Huawei makes the cut for its HBM program, though it has no memory fab. Fabless designers, module makers, and NOR flash producers are left out, except XMC for its HBM line. Fab counts, products, and financials come from filings and company statements; capacities, planned fabs, and the HBM progress that remains the big question mark rest largely on anonymous reporting. Let's dive into the current state of the Chinese DRAM industry.

CXMT, short for ChangXin Memory Technologies, is China’s biggest homegrown DRAM maker. It operates two 12-inch fabs in Hefei and one in Beijing, each able to produce around 100,000 wafers a month. It is also building fabs in Shanghai and Hefei and is in funding talks for a second Beijing fab, which together could take it past 600,000 wafers a month. The Shanghai fab is expected to have two to three times the capacity of the Hefei base, with tools going in during the second half of 2026 and volume in 2027.

CXMT is now the fourth-largest DRAM maker worldwide, with 9.5% of DRAM revenue in Q2, according to TrendForce. CXMT says LPDDR6 is in mass production for Xiaomi, and its president says the first 4F² product, a server DDR5 RDIMM, is due by year-end. This comes after record first-half revenue of ¥150.3 billion ($22.4 billion), net profit of ¥77.6 billion ($11.5 billion), an 84.84% gross margin on its main business, and a listing on Shanghai’s STAR Market on July 27. Third-quarter profit alone was ¥54.4 billion ($8.1 billion).

The primary products are DDR5 rated up to 8,000 MT/s with 24Gb dies, and LPDDR5X rated up to 10,667 MT/s. CXMT says its fifth-generation G5 platform reaches 11.95nm active-area half-pitch with quadruple patterning for 24Gb LPDDR5X, and gets at least 50% more dies out of each wafer than G4, counted as 8Gb dies. CXMT says a DRAM product on its sixth-generation platform has also completed engineering-sample validation. Jeongdong Choe, Senior Technology Fellow at TechInsights, told Tom’s Hardware Premium that the firm measured the smallest active half-pitch on CXMT’s G4 at 14.8nm and hasn’t analyzed G5 yet.

CXMT lists no GDDR or 3D NAND among its products and is limited to nodes made without extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography as it lacks the tooling. CXMT’s second Beijing fab, however, is planned to include a 3D NAND R&D line, which could shake things up. The growth picture for now is capacity and price, with the tests of scaling without EUV lying with the RDIMM and Shanghai fab.

YMTC, short for Yangtze Memory Technologies Co., has two fabs of its own in Wuhan, which can produce about 200,000 wafers a month together. A third fab in the Xinzhou district, registered in September last year, is expected to produce about 50,000 wafers a month by 2027 and eventually 100,000 at full capacity. While YMTC is known for its NAND flash, roughly half of Fab 3’s output is earmarked for DRAM. The company also makes enterprise SSDs and UFS and eMMC parts, and sells consumer SSDs under its ZhiTai brand. YMTC filed its own STAR Market prospectus, to match CXMT’s, on Aug. 21.

NAND flash was, until recently, the star of the show for Chinese memory, and YMTC’s Xtacking technology is one reason why. Its fifth-generation Xtacking 4.0 die, used in 1Tb TLC and 2Tb QLC parts, has 294 gates, and Choe told us 267 of them are active word lines. Most of what YMTC ships, however, is still 160- and 232-layer NAND. Samsung licensed YMTC’s Xtacking bonding patents in February 2025, so the technology could be considered both proven and valuable.

Omdia data put YMTC at 38.5% of China’s NAND market in Q2, ahead of Samsung’s 33.5%. Globally, YMTC ranked third in NAND shipments in Q2, according to Counterpoint Research, and its share of NAND revenue rose from 9% to 14% between Q1 2025 and Q1 2026. YMTC’s stated long-term targets are about 20% of global NAND supply and about 500,000 wafers a month, DRAM included.

For DRAM, YMTC’s Fab 2 is running about 5,000 wafers a month of 16Gb LPDDR5, still in R&D and testing, with no plan for mass production. However, a 9,600 MT/s part on a vertical channel transistor (VCT) 4F² cell is slated for mass production at Fab 3 if it passes qualification. XMC, YMTC’s former foundry unit, left its control in July.

Two smaller fabs worthy of note are Fujian Jinhua and SwaySure. The former has a 12-inch fab in Jinjiang, which produces custom DDR4 on 25nm, at 40,000–50,000 wafers a month, with about 90% going to Huawei. DDR4 prices have surged this year, by as much as 50% in the first quarter alone, as the big suppliers wind DDR4 down in favor of server and HBM parts and enterprise buyers stock up.

SwaySure, a Shenzhen company founded in March 2022 with state-owned investment, is building a 28nm 12-inch DRAM line and is reportedly backed by Huawei. Analyst Jim Handy of Objective Analysis doubts the 28nm line could make HBM. Neither company responded to our requests for additional information.

Not every AI chip needs HBM. Inference hardware can get by on LPDDR5X, as Axera’s AX8860P does in Sipeed’s MaixStation, or on DRAM bonded straight onto the processor, the approach PieceMakers is betting on. Training-class accelerators like Huawei’s Ascend series still need HBM, though, and since the U.S. banned the export of HBM as of Dec. 2, 2024, China can’t buy it from the big three memory makers.

Even those who can get HBM are seeing heat and yield issues with larger memory stacks, specifically 12-high. SK hynix said on its Q3 2026 earnings call that some customers’ spec changes show “demand is indeed overwhelming supply,” and that lowering the stack is “a strategy to increase the number of cubes that can be obtained for the same capacity.” This mirrors other indicators, including potential Nvidia Rubin Ultra decisions, and it suggests that HBM is in short enough supply that even easier-to-build stacks are in high demand.

CXMT has sampled HBM with Tongfu, and HBM3 built on its 16nm-class G4 node to Huawei. Its HBM3E has now gone to several domestic AI chip designers as samples, with limited production due by the end of 2026, and an expansion expected in 2027. Yields are only about 20% to 30%, though, against about 70% at SK hynix, and in testing the chips performed closer to SK hynix’s HBM3 than to its HBM3E. An HBM packaging plant in Shanghai worth at least ¥17.1 billion ($2.5 billion) is targeted for year-end, with capacity for about 30,000 units a month.

Tongfu reportedly sampled HBM2, but its own filings mention only DRAM and flash packaging. Huawei said last year that “we will offer two proprietary HBMs.” It launched the Atlas 350 card in March, built on the Ascend 950PR, which carries 112GB of HBM. XMC has had a 3,000-wafer-a-month HBM line under construction since early 2024, but no production has been confirmed. At this time, YMTC, Jinhua, and SwaySure don’t appear to make HBM, though reports say YMTC is developing the through-silicon via (TSV) stacking it would need, and Jinhua was named in a Huawei-led HBM group in 2024.

Huawei said in 2025 that the 950PR would use HiBL 1.0, its own “low-cost HBM”; TechInsights told us it hasn’t analyzed HiBL 1.0 yet. However, Huawei’s 2025 roadmap specced out the 950PR's 128GB of HBM at 1.6TB/s. Huawei said that its 950PR is “optimized for the prefill stage of inference,” which is compute-heavy with “lower demand for memory access bandwidth,” and that using HiBL 1.0 is “more cost-effective than HBM3E and HBM4E” for that workload. The higher-end HiZQ 2.0, for the 950DT, is due in Q4 and, by Huawei’s figures, brings 144GB at 4TB/s. In both cases, Huawei’s supplier remains in doubt, and the company did not respond to our requests for comment.

Of the companies from outside China, Samsung and SK hynix both run large memory fabs on Chinese soil, and both are now upgrading their NAND lines there. Samsung’s Xi’an handles about 40% of the company’s NAND, including 236-layer, or V8, in volume, with X2 converting to 286-layer, or V9, this year, slated to reach 5,000 V9 wafers a month in December and 40,000 by December 2027. A Samsung spokesperson told Tom’s Hardware Premium that the company “has been transitioning its existing manufacturing operations in Xi’an to 9th-generation V-NAND in compliance with U.S. export regulations and in close communication with the U.S. government.”

SK hynix’s Wuxi produces 30–40% of its DRAM and has moved from 1z to 1a, with the EUV layers done in Korea. Dalian turns out about 100,000 wafers a month of 192-layer floating-gate NAND and is moving to 240 layers, the most SK hynix is authorized to make there.

SK hynix, which already sells NAND with up to 321 layers elsewhere, told us it needs a U.S. license every year and declined to give product details or capacities for individual fabs. The company said that it intends for its 321-layer flash to be “over 50% of domestic (Korean) capacity in the fourth quarter,” which puts the Dalian lag into relief.

SK hynix also has “Mod B,” a second fab at its Dalian site that was completed in 2023. The company has reportedly tied a new U.S. NAND fab to Washington, letting it ship more U.S. tools there: Dalian’s 2026 license capped its equipment, no 2027 license had been issued as of mid-October, and its U.S.-based Solidigm unit has applied for an export license to buy more tools for Mod B. SK hynix told Tom’s Hardware that “no capacity expansion is being sought for the Dalian Mod B” and has been installing equipment at its Dalian factory.

Solidigm is now reportedly in talks with the U.S. Commerce Department over a $45 billion chip plant in the U.S., but wants Washington’s approval to ship equipment to upgrade the Dalian plant first, arguing that the upgrade would help finance the new site. However, SK hynix denied an earlier report of such a deal and says no specific plans have been finalized.

As for its Chongqing packaging plant, SK hynix told us it is “exploring various strategic options to enhance our packaging competitiveness,” though nothing is final. Elsewhere in China, Samsung’s Suzhou plant handles assembly and testing, and Micron’s Xi’an site handles packaging and testing.

All of this plays out inside a U.S.-China fight over chips that AI has only sped up. CXMT isn’t currently on the Commerce Department’s Entity List, which bars U.S. suppliers from shipping to listed companies without a license; YMTC, Jinhua, and SwaySure are. Jinhua has been on the list since 2018; SwaySure joined in December 2024 over its Huawei links, and YMTC was added in December 2022.

CXMT is, however, on the Pentagon’s 1260H list of Chinese military companies, which it is suing over. It’s been on the list since January 2025 and stayed on with the June update. For Samsung and SK hynix, the validated end-user status that let both firms ship in U.S. tools was revoked as of Dec. 31, 2025. Annual licenses are now required, and the Commerce Department has said it doesn’t intend to license capacity expansions or technology upgrades. Meanwhile, both companies say upgrades are under way, which sits uneasily with that policy.

China, for its part, has been weighing broader technology export controls and has now proposed adding 40 technologies, reportedly including some memory-chip design technologies, to its Catalogue of Technologies Prohibited and Restricted from Export. The draft, Announcement 48 from China’s Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology, is open for comment until Nov. 15, and anyone exporting a listed technology must register first.

Currently, China’s homegrown memory hinges on two manufacturers that are, admittedly, booming at the moment, although the memory industry worldwide has seen a massive surge since spring 2025. SK hynix said that “our DRAM and HBM volume for the next three years through 2029 are effectively sold out” with supply failing to keep up with demand as “a constraint projected to continue through 2030.” Its average selling prices rose from Q2 by mid-20% for NAND and high-20% for DRAM.

There may be some signs of at least temporary cooling in China’s consumer channel, though, as China Flash Market reports that SSD prices fell across the board this week, with most spot NAND wafers trading more than 30% below suppliers’ official prices. It looks like supply will remain a hot topic and China’s production capabilities are front and center.

CXMT and Huawei both have HBM milestones due by year-end: CXMT’s limited HBM3E production and Shanghai packaging plant, and Huawei’s 950DT with HiZQ 2.0. YMTC’s Fab 3 is due in late 2026 or 2027, and CXMT’s HBM3E expansion is expected in 2027. That leaves little time to ramp within 2027, especially at CXMT’s current yields, so we’re skeptical of volume HBM from a Chinese fab, whether measured against domestic demand or global supply, before 2028. Samsung and SK hynix, meanwhile, still need U.S. licenses for next year to keep tooling their Chinese fabs.

The supermassive black hole, known as Dún Aonghasa, is unlike any other seen at a similar period of the early universe.

The Euclid space telescope has spotted the most distant and ancient quasar ever seen, a bright cosmic event powered by a supermassive black hole with 360 million times the mass of the sun.

The supermassive black hole is seen as it was just 630 million years after the Big Bang. 40 million years further back in time than the previous record holder for the most ancient quasar.

The discovery further deepens the mystery of how supermassive black holes grew so massive so soon into the history of the universe. That's because previously, scientists had theorized it should take 1 billion years of feeding and merging for black holes to reach supermassive status of millions or billions of times as massive as the sun.

Quasars occur when supermassive black holes are surrounded by vast amounts of gas and dust on which they feed. The immense gravitational influence of the black hole causes this material, in the form of an accretion disk, to glow brightly and to blast out bright jets of plasma. That means quasars at the heart of galaxies in regions called active galactic nuclei (AGNs) like this one can often be brighter than the combined light of every star in the entire surrounding galaxy, but only briefly.

The newly discovered quasar, designated J1504+6252 and nicknamed Dún Aonghasa, is in this brief phase of its existence. It was discovered among millions of objects in 18 months of data from Euclid.

Euclid's initial observation of Dún Aonghasa was followed up by an array of ground-based and space-based telescopes, including the W.M. Keck Observatory, the Large Binocular Telescope Observatory, the Hubble Space Telescope, and ultimately the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). It was the JWST that determined the mass of this supermassive black hole.

What is interesting about Dún Aonghasa is that it is unlike other supermassive black holes seen at a similar period of the early universe. That's because this black hole is feeding relatively slowly. That means if it engaged in rapid feeding to reach its massive size, that period of gorging on gas and dust has either ended or it has paused.

That is something that further complicates our picture of supermassive black hole growth.

By staring back 13.1 billion years in time to see Dún Aonghasa, Euclid is also observing a crucial time in cosmic history when the first stars and galaxies were taking shape, and the clearing of cosmic gas was ending the dark ages of our universe by allowing light to travel freely.

Euclid's primary mission is improving our understanding of dark energy, the mysterious force accelerating the expansion of the universe, and dark matter, the most mysterious "stuff" in the cosmos. Collectively, these aspects of the universe account for around 95% of its matter-energy budget and have come to be collectively referred to as the dark universe. Hence Euclid's nickname as the "dark universe detective."

This research, along with Euclid's discovery of at least 12 that date back around 13 billion years, shows that this pioneering space telescope launched in 2023 could also make a major contribution to our understanding of black holes and their growth.

The team's research was published on Tuesday (Nov. 24) in the journal Astronomy & Astrophysics.

So says the official tagline for the Prime Video series "Blade Runner 2099," which is set (self-evidently) 50 years after the events of Denis Villeneuve's "Blade Runner 2049" movie. The world has undergone some seriously seismic changes since then, as viewers will quickly realize in the opening moments of the show. Where both Ridley Scott's classic 1982 film "Blade Runner" and Villeneuve's 2017 sequel took place under very traditional confines (with humanity ruling over bioengineered Replicants as second-class citizens), "2099" has flipped the script entirely. Instead, the Replicants have orchestrated a full-scale uprising and seized control.

Not that we'll actually see any of that go down within "Blade Runner 2099," mind you. The series premiere opens well after this revolutionary turn of events has become old news. It's a bold and decisive creative choice, and one that I had to ask showrunner Silka Luisa about during a recent press junket. Why set this story in the aftermath of such a development rather than during it? As she tells it, it had to do with telling a story we haven't already seen in pop culture before:

"To be honest, I feel like I've seen that before. Not with Replicants, but definitely the robot revolution is definitely something I can close my eyes and picture. It's been in the zeitgeist and people have done different takes on it, so it didn't seem like I was going to be putting anything out into the world that people couldn't imagine for themselves."

Reading between the lines, it's true that properties like "Terminator" have already covered this ground before. And, in fairness, a wartime drama wouldn't quite mesh with the neo-noir vibes of "Blade Runner." But, for Luisa, her reasons extend even deeper than that.

Considering the timeline established in "Blade Runner" and "2049," the Replicant uprising in "Blade Runner 2099" has been almost a century in the making. The Denis Villeneuve sequel even took steps to directly integrate this possibility into its plot, keeping a Replicant rebellion simmering in the background of its story. In the Prime Video series, this powder keg has been detonated, and now the various characters (including Michelle Yeoh's Replicant Blade Runner Olwen and Hunter Schafer's incognito human Cora) have to live with the consequences.

According to Silka Luisa, this was a challenge that she embraced from the start. It's fairly easy to tell a straightforward story about revolution, but it's much harder to depict what comes next. As she told me over Zoom, "Whereas, what I really wasn't sure about myself at the beginning and that was interesting, was what happens afterwards. What does it look like to lose? And if you have been disempowered for your entire existence, what do you do with power?"

It's a fascinating question to pose to a group of sentient beings frequently described as "more human than human." They make look, walk, and bleed like humans ... but Replicants have always been designed to be far stronger, physically. When the oppressed throw off their shackles, do they then become the oppressors in turn? According to Luisa:

"Those were just, at least to me personally, much more interesting questions than just a simple idea of which side is gonna win. When, in truth, you kind of know which side is gonna win [laughs]."

All eight episodes of "Blade Runner 2099" are currently streaming on Prime Video.
