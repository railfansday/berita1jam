Senate passes first-ever prediction market bill as Democrats turn up heat on upstart industry

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Prediction markets are facing new limits on Capitol Hill. The Senate passed a bill Thursday evening that would bar platforms from offering contracts tied to terrorism, assassination, an individual’s death, war or U.S. military operations–the first legislation targeting the burgeoning industry to clear either chamber of Congress.

Sens. Adam Schiff (D-Calif.) and John Curtis (R-Utah) introduced the bill, formally called the DEATH BETS Act, to prevent exchanges and clearinghouses overseen by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission from offering or processing those contracts. It would also ban contracts whose outcomes are reasonably expected to depend mainly on someone’s death, even if the wording does not mention death.

The bill passed the Senate by a voice vote and must still pass the House before heading to the president’s desk.

“They are offered with no regard for national security and in far too many cases, even basic morality,” Schiff said on the Senate floor. “These markets are operating in a wild West, desperately in need of regulation and guardrails.”

As platforms like Kalshi and Polymarket have grown in prominence over the past two years, they have offered contracts on seemingly anything, drawing scrutiny over bets tied to war, violence or human suffering. They have also raised concerns about insider trading, as users can trade without publicly revealing their identities, making it harder for outsiders to spot potential conflicts of interest.

Several cases have drawn politicians’ attention to these issues. One involved a Polymarket trader who made more than $400,000 betting on Nicolás Maduro’s removal from power before U.S. forces captured the Venezuelan leader in January. Kalshi also drew scrutiny over a market on whether Iranian Supreme Leader, Ali Khamenei, would leave office, which sparked a dispute after his killing when Kalshi declined to pay traders who had bet he would leave office, citing a rule that excluded death as a qualifying outcome.

In his speech, Schiff pointed to a Polymarket bet on whether NASA’s Artemis II mission would explode, raising concerns about wagers on astronauts’ lives. He also cited a bet on whether Russian forces would capture the Ukrainian city of Myrnohrad.

The companies’ leaders have previously pushed back on these criticisms. “We don’t list markets directly tied to death,” Kalshi CEO Tarek Mansour wrote on X in February. Speaking at a conference in March, Polymarket CEO Shayne Coplan called conflict-related markets a “complicated question” but defended their value as a source of useful information.

Kalshi and Polymarket did not immediately respond to Fortune’s request for comment.

Other platforms, such as Novig, avoid these contracts altogether, limiting their offerings to sports markets and restricting trading to users 21 and older.

The bill’s passage comes amid growing political scrutiny of prediction markets, particularly after Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress in this month’s midterm elections. Over the past week, several Democratic lawmakers have criticized the platforms on social media.

Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-Mass.) pointed Wednesday to Kalshi’s decision to pause trading on contracts tied to President Donald Trump’s public remarks, which have drawn concerns about insider trading. “The CFTC needs to do more to stop insiders from cashing in,” she wrote on X.

Rep. Ro Khanna (D-Calif.) focused on younger users, calling Wednesday for an investigation and a ban on sports prediction markets. He criticized Kalshi for allowing 18-year-olds to bet on sports and said users under 21 had wagered $3.9 billion on the platform. “They call it a ‘prediction market.’ It’s gambling,” he wrote.

Though Democrats have been the most vocal, the scrutiny has crossed party lines. Besides cosponsoring the bill that passed Thursday, Curtis joined Warren last month in questioning Kalshi and Polymarket about potentially improper election-related trading. The Utah Republican has also backed separate legislation to ban sports and casino-style prediction contracts, citing concerns about young users’ exposure to gambling.

Sen. Ted Cruz (R-Texas) also announced plans to introduce legislation that would bar prediction market platforms from offering sports contracts without state or tribal approval, according to Bloomberg Law.

Mathematical judgment is the ability to distinguish a result that is correct, but boring, from one that might fundamentally change how we think about the problem or even the whole area of mathematics surrounding the problem.

AI could generate hundreds of thousands of complex variations on a theorem, but these variations might add very little to mathematical knowledge, each one ultimately proving a dead end. A truly consequential result might settle a long-standing unsolved problem or connect to apparently unrelated areas. It is mathematical judgment that allows us to tell the difference between the two.

Earlier this year, an OpenAI system produced a counterexample to the long-standing planar unit distance problem, overturning a conjecture of Paul Erdős that had resisted mathematicians for decades. More recently, OpenAI announced an apparent solution to the Navier-Stokes problem, a celebrated question about whether equations describing fluid flow can develop singularities, or infinite values. It is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems — a set of seven well-known and complex math problems, each with a $1 million prize attached.

Whether OpenAI's result ultimately stands up to scrutiny remains to be seen. But what is clear is that AI systems are now operating at a level that many mathematicians did not expect to witness for years, if not decades. OpenAI has since released hundreds of further mathematical results, leaving mathematicians scrambling to assess their correctness and significance.

This does not mean that mathematicians are about to become obsolete. But it does mean we need to carefully reconsider what their precise purpose is.

Traditionally, expertise has been closely associated with production: Experts solve difficult problems, prove theorems, construct models and write papers. If AI can generate proofs, conjectures and probe research areas at unprecedented speed and scale, mathematical production is no longer necessarily the bottleneck. Instead, many pure mathematicians would argue that it is mathematical judgment that will become the scarcer resource.

Human judgment remains necessary because proofs generated by AI are not necessarily illuminating, comprehensible or even reliable. Some proofs may be so long or opaque that checking them requires substantial work by specialists and even more work to reinterpret them in a way that is meaningful to human mathematicians.

A proof can be valid without offering insight into why it is true or how its ideas might be employed elsewhere. The job of human mathematicians will be to become arbiters of the correctness of proofs, but also to scrutinize the reasoning and to extract the underlying ideas, determining whether the result says anything that meaningfully advances the field.

Mathematics, as a research community, needs to react rapidly to this change. Mathematicians and journals must prioritize significance over output. Mathematics involves deciding which results are important as well as generating them. A proof can be correct without being insightful. To give a simplified example, AI might "prove" that the square of a number divisible by two is divisible by four and then prove that the square of a number divisible by three is divisible by nine, and so on with ever larger integers. However, each new "proof" isn't an example of insightful mathematics, just a variation on the same elementary idea.

AI can create arguments faster than the community can verify, interpret or absorb them. Existing safeguards were not designed for that imbalance.

In contrast, a significant proof might settle a long-standing question, reveal an unexpected connection between disparate fields or develop a technique which is useful in the solution of many other problems.

Journals should therefore require authors to explain clearly what is genuinely new, why it matters — and what role AI played. Reviewers should judge whether a result deepens understanding, rather than merely verifying that the argument works. That does not mean rejecting work whose eventual importance is uncertain. The history of mathematics is full of ideas whose significance became apparent only decades later, so journals must continue to make room for foundational, exploratory and curiosity-driven research.

But there is a difference between a result with as-yet unknown implications and one that is unlikely ever to be insightful. Mathematical judgement cannot guarantee that nothing valuable is ever overlooked. It can, however, distinguish work that contains genuinely new ideas or possibilities from the automated production of uninteresting dead ends.

Universities and funders should also stop treating publication counts as a convenient proxy for quality. Careers are built around papers, citations, grants and prizes, but these measures were developed when producing sophisticated mathematics demanded substantial human time and effort. In the age of AI, work that might previously have taken years of careful thought could now be produced in weeks or even days.

Consequently, as the cost of generating mathematical results falls dramatically, those measures of quality become less useful. A decreased reliance on traditional metrics may be no bad thing, but institutions need credible alternatives: assessments of a researcher's most important contributions, evidence of intellectual leadership and explanations of how their work has changed understanding. This is urgent because mathematicians are already struggling to evaluate the sheer volume of material being produced.

Finally, mathematical training must continue to reward understanding. Written work alone will become a less reliable guide to individual capability when papers, reports and applications can be written mostly using AI. Seminars, discussions and collaborations can reveal whether someone understands an argument, can defend it and can connect it to other ideas. Demonstrating understanding has always been a more important quality in university students than simply presenting the answer; education and recruitment should now reflect that principle more explicitly.

Critics might argue that this is an overreaction. Mathematical claims still require proof, peer review already evaluates significance, and AI is simply another tool, like a calculator or computer algebra system. There is a degree of truth to each objection. Yet the explosion in the number of published papers changes the nature of the game. A calculator does not generate hundreds of research claims that specialists must spend precious time checking. AI can create arguments faster than the community can verify, interpret or absorb them. Existing safeguards were not designed for that imbalance.

Recent advances have understandably generated both excitement and anxiety. The right response is neither to reject AI nor to unguardedly celebrate every finding it produces. Journals, universities and funders should adapt their incentives now, before quantity overwhelms judgment completely.

In an environment increasingly capable of automatically generating research-level mathematics, the most valuable experts will be those who know which questions are worth asking, which results deserve trust and which ideas are worth our attention.

The value of combining legal knowledge, technology, workflow design and project management in e-discovery

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ari Kaplan recently spoke with Chantelle Jalland, the associate director of advanced e-discovery and digital services for Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the United States at Ashurst Perkins Coie; and Satinder Soni, the managing director for the United Kingdom at Cimplifi.

Ari Kaplan: Tell us about your background and your role at Ashurst Perkins Coie.

Chantelle Jalland: I qualified as a lawyer in Australia almost 20 years ago, and when I moved to the U.K., I quickly moved into the world of e-discovery. At that stage, what attracted me to e-discovery was predictive coding, which meant the machine would learn from those decisions and reduce the need for humans in that really boring manual review phase. When we fast-forward to the release of gen AI, what was incredibly exciting for me was that you could use LLMs to code the documents from a prompt instead of that initial training round. In my current role, I feel incredibly fortunate to work with lawyers who want to use the latest technology to do things differently, so I don’t have to fight like I used to. My remit now at Ashurst Perkins Coie is much broader than e-discovery. I have a team that also builds custom AI solutions across transactional and advisory practice groups, so I very much appreciate the industry recognition moving toward this legal data intelligence.

Ari Kaplan: Tell us about your background and your role at Cimplifi.

Satinder Soni: I’ve been in the industry for over 20 years. My career started on the right side of the EDRM model, focusing on courtroom technology, transcription services and evidence presentation in court. E-discovery was just surfacing at that point. It really wasn’t a thing. I then moved into an e-discovery software company, which was acquired by several conglomerates. I then moved into a global risk consultancy, Control Risks, working with the investigations team on cyber breach investigations, digital forensics and e-discovery. I then moved to Ankura, which took a different approach to e-discovery, focusing more on analytics and subject-matter expertise in construction and turnaround restructuring, forensic accounting and white-collar crime investigations. Then I moved to Cimplifi, a U.S.-based company focused on e-discovery, investigations and contract analytics. They moved to the U.K. almost two years ago. Our U.S. clients had cross-border matters that needed U.K.-based expertise to be compliant with GDPR. We have grown our team here. We have a project management team with diverse professional skills, ranging from consultancies and accountancy firms to technology companies. It’s a great company to be in.

Ari Kaplan: What’s making e-discovery more complex today, and how is that changing the way you approach each new matter at the outset?

Chantelle Jalland: One of the biggest challenges is the size of new datasets. We had over 45 terabytes of data for a recent matter. I’ve handled some really large cases before, so this wasn’t the biggest, but given the facts of the case and the scope of the material issues in the dispute, I expected the volume to be much smaller. It was a surprise, based on the facts, that it was that large, and it was simply the product of more data being created today. We also have platform switching. This morning, I was on a call with a colleague about a particular client matter, then received an email on the matter to an external party, then I had some internal Teams chat messages, and in one of those messages, they also shared a live document with status updates. That was all within a few minutes, so everything was moving so much faster, and there were four different sources of information on one particular matter or issue. If you think about that and expand it, if every single person is just adding this tiny bit of additional information, it’s going to lead to a data explosion. That’s also the biggest opportunity because the large matters are the ones that are interesting and exciting. They’ve got things that will go wrong or problems, and you want those differences to make it more interesting and something new to solve.

Ari Kaplan: What e-discovery challenges do your clients most often ask you to solve?

Satinder Soni: Everything has a different variable, and each e-discovery matter is a challenge because it is a reactive industry. Our clients come in quite hard and fast, and we have to take a step back to understand the data scoping. I have a separate challenge where they underestimate how much data there is, so you have to ease the shock of what it’s actually going to cost. But if you apply the right technology, phase it out, structure the workflow and talk them through it, the challenge then is pacing it, being realistic and providing timelines. The latest challenge really is applying AI into their workflows and driving the mindset shift they need to use a different type of analytics. They’ve just gotten used to CAL, and there is a bit of fatigue with it being more efficient and more cost-effective. We say it with every new tool we bring in, and the challenge is always the mindset shift.

Ari Kaplan: The Ashurst Perkins Coie Advanced Legal Solutions offering combines legal knowledge, technology, workflow design, project management and flexible resourcing. How does that combination enhance client service, and how do you define success before the work begins?

Chantelle Jalland: There’s tremendous value in having multiple service offerings under one roof. We also have our risk advisory or consulting arm, which enables us to undertake deep data analytics tasks, and this gives us what we refer to internally as our triple threat. We have Advance, our new law arm, risk advisory and our core services team. Before the work begins, much of the success comes back to those foundational principles, and it builds on what Sati mentioned about managing expectations on timelines and being realistic. Historically, a partner will promise a client that we will deliver something in a certain amount of time for a certain cost, and that promise may not be realistic because the scope fundamentally changes or the expectation isn’t technically possible within the timeframe. They didn’t understand the basic principle that if you don’t understand the component, you can’t advise in that area, and that’s where we bring the right people into the conversation earlier. We have that open conversation and set small milestones or goals, so we’re meeting our client’s expectations along the way. And like everyone, I take great pride in an accurate cost estimate. But really, that data can expand at a different rate compared to a previous matter that you might have done an estimate on, or there’s a subset of data that’s got a processing error. There are so many things that can vary from what you might expect. As it shifts, it’s about having that conversation and letting everyone know it’s different from the original ask, and what I’ve found is clients appreciate that openness and willingness to have the conversation. You are advising, and there’s a lot more respect for the advice and the experience that can be offered in this area.

Ari Kaplan: Where are clients rethinking their approach most often, understanding their data earlier, managing review, coordinating providers, or generally adjusting their litigation strategy?

Satinder Soni: All of the above. E-discovery is a complex process. I’ve seen it where you’ve got your litigation strategy set, then you do the data scoping piece, and then you look at the early data and assess it. And when the review is happening, that’s when they realize we’re missing a custodian or this data source. Then the litigation strategy has to be readjusted. Then we go back to the data assessment. It can expand into other litigation support service lines, like asset tracing, or you need a forensic accountant. Chantelle put it really well. Communication is key. Understanding your stakeholders is part of that. Setting realistic timelines is part of that. It flits between the litigation strategy and understanding your data and that feedback loop.

Ari Kaplan: What needs to happen earlier in a matter for a legal team, the e-discovery team and a service provider to work most effectively together?

Chantelle Jalland: We tend to manage matters in-house with our legal team, but we also work with service providers on some matters. Sometimes a client has a preexisting relationship with a preferred provider, or we choose to engage a service provider due to their expertise. We recently had a matter involving historic offline databases that were in the format of a decommissioned product, so we needed to convert them for a current document review platform. It was more efficient to use an existing solution, rather than script something custom for that matter. What needs to happen to work effectively together is to get on a call early with all the right people. You can cut through so much with a phone call, making sure everyone is working toward the same plan from the outset.

Ari Kaplan: When your team is engaged by a client under pressure, what distinguishes a trusted adviser from a provider that simply executes instructions?

Satinder Soni: You should never be an order taker. Just because the client says it, doesn’t mean it’s always the right thing. They come to us because we are experts in the field. They trust us to guide and support them through the process. Often, it goes wrong when the client calls, and everyone’s excited, and they dive right in, applying the same methodology from the previous project. Take a step back. Standard information to ask for at the beginning includes e-data, what has happened, the discovery objective, the goals they’re trying to achieve, and the timeline they’re working toward. Review data types, nonstandard files, foreign languages and whether the data is structured or unstructured. Really get into the details that will influence the e-discovery project and help it achieve its goals. We advise gathering as much information as possible in advance, so we can choose the right technology, manage timelines and predict accurate costs. In those instances, accurate cost prediction is also key. Our job as a trusted adviser is to say, “We can save costs if we do it this way.”

Ari Kaplan: Where is AI making the most meaningful difference in your work today, and where is its promise still a bit ahead of its practical value?

Chantelle Jalland: AI has meaningfully changed my work. From a personal productivity perspective, AI call summaries make follow-up emails and meeting organization much easier. When I check a draft email, there are several small examples I use every day. On a larger scale, for high-impact matters, we have seen really effective use in due diligence, whether it’s legislative reviews for businesses or from an advisory perspective. The area where I see the most promise is large-scale document reviews. We are using AI for document review in a way that replaces CAL. So you filter down, you get a set of, say, 20,000 documents, and run it through. It is more effective than traditional manual review. It’s more effective than CAL. However, I see huge leaps ahead when it comes to document review. Most excitingly, I think, coming back to what Sati said earlier about working in a reactive industry, we are doing everything later. It’s actually about bringing those AI use cases further forward. Within a corporation, it’s about tracking themes, using the data more effectively, so you can identify the last 10 litigation matters or regulatory investigations that are actually costing you money, analyze that data, and then try to prevent future matters. That’s where it’s really exciting.

Ari Kaplan: When would you choose not to use AI in a litigation support matter?

Satinder Soni: We should all be using it in some way, shape or form. Traditionally, it was suggested that not all matters needed AI or analytics, either because it was cost-prohibitive, the dataset wasn’t compatible, or there wasn’t enough data to perform analysis. Recently, we’ve seen a shift toward using AI on small matters, such as DSARs. I have seen clients use AI on DSARs, and it seems effective. It frees up time for project managers to do more thoughtful work because DSARs don’t really go anywhere. They’re weaponized most of the time. So if they’re using it on investigation and litigation matters, their time is better spent. This is where AI can be used thoughtfully. There are still alternatives. CAL is being phased out, but it’s still there and still serves as a bridge to advising against linear review. But if people are not yet comfortable using AI, which hopefully they are, it’s in your everyday work now, so I just think, piece by piece, they’re trying it. I can’t think of any instance of not using AI.

Ari Kaplan: As we leverage technology to perform more work, which human skills become increasingly valuable, and how do you develop them within your team?

Chantelle Jalland: Communication is hugely important, so it’s something I’m focusing on from a recruitment perspective. We have to think about which skills can be taught and which are harder to teach. There’s analytical thinking. We need to make sure that the advisory stage isn’t so automated or process-driven that it’s replaced by AI. It’s about understanding that judgment is still needed and how to apply it. AI will be useful in many contexts, but how you use it will vary by use case. The risk is quite high if you don’t check the outputs, for example. It’s also about making sure people understand that risk. When it comes to training, it’s not a one-and-done. It’s continuous, and the same is true for communication. In a global law firm, different teams adopt different styles, and what works for one team doesn’t work for another. It’s about continuous learning without people feeling fatigued by constant learning.

Ari Kaplan: What are the hallmarks of great client service in an AI-enabled environment?

Satinder Soni: Great client service is all about communication, even when it’s bad news. Hopefully, it’s never bad news, but when it is, pick up the phone and have a conversation. We’ve gone crazy with short-form messaging, and what can be said in 10 emails can be said in a five-minute conversation. Then, with AI, be thoughtful about how we’re using it. Don’t fall into the trap of using AI for everything, and keep learning. There is a job you need to do: Put clients first, break down tasks, add structure to your work, and don’t just let AI take control.

Ari Kaplan: How do you see e-discovery and legal tech evolving?

Chantelle Jalland: I am very positive and optimistic about the future of e-discovery. There are many opportunities because, essentially, all the button-clicking will be gone soon, which is a great opportunity for people who work in e-discovery. You can apply a wide range of tools. It’s not just one technology. There are MCP connectors that you can use to adapt to certain platforms. So you are going to have so much more flexibility in the options. It does create more risk, potentially, if you’re not following a standard protocol, but what is exciting is that you can differentiate yourself in the market by specializing in solutions for certain use cases. Going back to first principles: understanding the problem, how you’re going to solve it, and considering all your options. It’s not going to be as linear. In the next five years, I would even say in the next year, we will be having a very different conversation about workflows in particular.
