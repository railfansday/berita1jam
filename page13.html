Peatland ecosystems like bogs and swamps perform an important balancing act: Their vegetation takes up huge amounts of carbon dioxide, while their soil microbes produce methane. Although methane is a potent greenhouse gas, peatlands exhibit a net cooling effect on the planet because of the sheer scale of carbon dioxide they remove from the atmosphere, which has amounted to hundreds of gigatons globally over thousands of years.

The December full moon reaches peak illumination at 8:28 p.m. EST on Dec. 23 (0128 GMT on Dec. 24) as the lunar disk shines opposite the sun in Earth's sky. It is often called the "Cold Moon" in reference to the frigid temperatures that prevail in the Northern Hemisphere around the time of its rising, according to the Old Farmer's Almanac.

December's full moon will also occur close to perigee — the moon's closest point to Earth in its elliptical orbit — giving rise to a spectacular supermoon. A supermoon can appear 30% brighter and 14% larger than a full moon at its farthest point from Earth (also known as a micromoon)..

According to the late, great astronomer Fred Espenak, the Cold Moon will be just 221,667 miles (356,740 kilometers) from Earth when it turns full, making it the closest supermoon of 2026!

The full Cold Supermoon will make for a spectacular sight when it rises over the eastern horizon at sunset on Dec. 23, mere hours before reaching maximum illumination. Remember to use Time and Date's moonrise and moonset tracker for timings specific to your location.

Earth's satellite may appear larger than normal while close to the horizon. This is partly because it is near perigee, when it appears subtly larger in the night sky, but also because of a phenomenon called the moon illusion, which tricks us into perceiving the moon to be bigger than it actually is while in the presence of foreground objects.

The moon may also adopt a striking golden or orange hue around moonrise and moonset. When the moon is low on the horizon, its light travels through more of Earth's atmopshere, scattering shorter, bluer wavelengths of light and allowing longer, redder wavelengths to reach our eyes.

The December full moon is often called the Cold Moon in reference to the often freezing temperatures that can descend this time of the year. It's also known as the "Long Night Moon" by the Mohican tribe of North America, because of its proximity to the winter solstice, which marks the shortest day of the year in the Northern Hemisphere.

Old English peoples knew it as the "Moon Before Yule", according to Time and Date, while the Celts of Europe referred to it as the Oak Moon after traditions that centered around the hardy trees.

The December full moon also coincides with the Hindu festival of Datta Jayanti, when followers gather to celebrate the birth of Lord Dattatreya, who embodies the Hindu holy trinity of Lord Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva, according to the Government of Maharashtra. The December full moon also rises close to the Christian celebration of Christmas on Dec. 25, when followers celebrate the birth of Jesus Christ.

The Cold Moon will shine in the center of a majestic "Winter Hexagon" of stars bright enough to easily cut through the glare cast by the lunar disk after it rises on Nov. 24.

Begin by locating Sirius, the brightest star in our night sky, which rises into the southeastern sky shortly after 8 p.m. local time for viewers in New York. Next, trace an imaginary line to the blue supergiant star Rigel in the nearby constellation Orion, before hopping to the orange glow of Aldebaran, which represents an eye of the great bull depicted in the constellation Taurus.

Next we move to Capella, the jewel of the constellation Auriga, before jumping down to Pollux in the constellation Gemini and nearby Procyon, before closing the loop with Sirius once more.

Jupiter and the red light of Mars will shine low in the eastern sky in the hour before midnight, while Saturn will lurk close to the western horizon. Look to the southeast just before dawn to spot Venus appearing as a brilliant "Morning Star", with the red giant Arcturus shining higher in the predawn sky close to the constellation Bootes.

The December full moon presents a stunning opportunity to photograph the lunar disk as it shines in Earth's sky entirely free from shadow. Blade Runner 2099 Adds A Major Twist To The Classic Voight-Kampff Test

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

I've seen spoilers you people wouldn't believe. This article discusses major plot details from all eight episodes of "Blade Runner 2099."

What's a worse fate in the world of "Blade Runner," to be human or to be a Replicant? Throughout both films, organics in this post-apocalyptic setting were able to "enjoy" their time at the top of the food chain. That all changes in Prime Video's "Blade Runner 2099," the live-action story taking place after the world order has been upended and our supposed underlings have gained all the power for themselves. While Replicants were built to be "more human than human," the subtle differences between the two kinds are all but impossible to detect — on the surface, at least. But, as we know, looks can be deceiving; what's underneath is another matter entirely.

Leave it to "Blade Runner 2099" to flip yet another classic franchise staple on its head. Traditionally, the purposefully off-putting questionnaire known as the Voight-Kampff test was meant to detect whether an individual is a Replicant or not. In the sci-fi series, the rise of the Replicants and the 50 years that have passed since "Blade Runner 2049" have introduced even more changes. Enter the Index Test, an inverted form of the established quiz. But rather than differentiate between the two beings, this test is meant to detect whether the subject has developed feelings of empathy towards humans.

In Episode 5, titled "My Lord," we learn far more about this strange new setup than the show had previously revealed. All we ever saw was the unusually young human girl administering the test, the odd non sequiturs she posed to (supposed) Replicants like Hunter Schafer's Cora, and the results of whether they passed the baseline or not. But that, too, was only scratching the surface.

Of all the differences between humans and Replicants, empathy is the biggest of them all. "Blade Runner 2099" draws fairly stark lines in the sand between the two, with flesh-and-blood people now completely helpless before their bioengineered creations. But despite their physical advantages and killer instincts, Replicants' lack of a childhood means they're deficit in that crucial, compassionate emotion separating humans from all other living things. As we soon learn, however, Replicants can feel these strong feelings ... and actually fear it, too.

This is all summed up in the storyline involving Greta (Katelyn Rose Downey), the young human girl we learn has been sold off into indentured servitude and indoctrinated along with many others into administering Index Tests. Episode 5 peels back all the horrifying layers, showing us Greta's training under awfully unsettling circumstances. First, she's taken through a museum exhibit explaining the purpose and history of the Voight-Kampff test, used by human Blade Runners to hunt Replicants. Now, the new status quo means Replicants use this new Index Test to police their own kind — and, as one instructor explains, prevent any "emotional contagion" crossing over from humans to their Replicant overlords.

It's a fascinating idea with some pretty serious implications for our two main characters. Michelle Yeoh's Olwen is currently undergoing some serious emotional turmoil herself, as her search for the missing Replicant and her secret lover Marek (Johnny Harris) ends in his tragic death. Meanwhile, Cora's own emotional attachments makes it only a matter of time before her human identity is made known. As a result, every time they sit down for the Index Test makes for must-watch TV.

All eight episodes of "Blade Runner 2099" are streaming on Prime Video.

His storyboarding credits span Toy Story shorts, Cars 2, Dr. Seuss’s The Lorax and Inside Out, to name a few, but his first creator credit came with Cartoon Network’s Emmy-nominated We Bare Bears. DEADLINE: How did you and Jesse talk through making the script?

CHONG: Without a script and just an outline, we had a sense of where we wanted to go with it, but we worked very fluidly with how we created the story. That’s why the movie is so freeform. We wanted it to be very organic and unexpected. We didn’t want the audience to feel the structure of it. And if you think about it, Mission: Impossible and those bigger action-type movies are built that way. They are built around crazy set pieces, unexpected twists and turns. So, I just knew that one of the last things that I wanted to do was work on this in a linear way.

When Jesse came on, he had the tough task of working the way I wanted to work, which was just out of order. He would write something in Act 2, then go to Act 1, then jump to Act 3. We went around and around, putting up reels in a hodgepodge way, and then started shaping it to make sense. It’s madness to work that way, but it made the movie what it is because I think that was the only way we could go so wild and take so many swings in so many parts of the movie. CHONG: There’s an inherent charm in seeing animals anthropomorphized that has always appealed to me. And doing this in animation adds a level of surrealness that everyone can still easily connect and relate to. The humor and silliness come pretty easily from there, but it will also allow me to venture into some weightier topics while still keeping things fun, because at the end of the day, it’s a stack of bears and a beaver with a crown.

DEADLINE: And what about the robotic portion of the premise? This could have just been about regular animals saving their habitat.

CHONG: I think that came from my interest in robot animals. I was reading a lot about animatronics, and A.I. wasn’t quite what it is today, but it was definitely building its way into the zeitgeist. So, I was reading a lot about robotics. I learned in some documentaries that they actually put robot animals into nature to spy on them, and they have cameras in their eyes. If no one has seen them, they are up on YouTube, and you can watch them. They are incredible and a little cheesy looking, and I don’t mean that in a bad way, because robotics are amazing, but they don’t look like real animals [laughs]. When you see them, you look and say, there’s no way an animal is going to buy this, but then the animals poke and prod it, probably questioning whether it’s a friend or a foe. So, I was imagining: What if that technology was so good, the animals could not tell the difference?

CHONG: I grew up with a lot of pop culture. I was a kid raised on movies, TV, and comic strips. I loved Garfield, Calvin and Hobbes, Far Side–I read that a lot as a kid, and I owned every book. It’s very absurd. And it just felt like that language was really what I grew up on.

And because I’m a kid of the ’80s, I love early Tim Burton, just really weird, strange, and tonally off-kilter movies that you can’t even put in a box. Is it horror? Is it comedy? Is it action? And that’s the kind of mind meld that my brain was exposed to growing up with all these things. So, I just brought that into Hoppers, thinking about all these genres. DEADLINE: I want to break some of these comedic beats down. You’ve got Diane, the shark, who is hoisted up on a highway by the avian creatures to help squish the mayor to death. In a post-credits scene, you’ve got the animals playing with another character’s face mask. There’s so much. What was the thing you had to fight for the most or stick to your guns about?

CHONG: Listen, we had to fight for everything [laughs]. I want to be clear that the studio was behind a lot of it, but we did get a lot of notes. And because Pixar has been around for a very long time, it was a lot of, “Is this a Pixar movie? Is this what we are going to make?”

CHONG: I think that’s because there’s a sense of humor that’s a little bit chaotic and illogical. Pixar movies are built so much on logic in a lot of ways. There was a freeness in the humor that many people were unsure about. For example, when they saw the animals with crowns, even if you start with something as simple as that, I think an older Pixar might have noted the situation to death. It would have been something like, “OK, why do those animals have crowns? Explain it to me. So, they just have them? Well, we have to know where they got it from.” So, you would get questions to logic out all these decisions. But now, it’s kind of about explaining that something like that would ruin the joke, and it shouldn’t be explained where the crowns come from. So, if we liked something enough, the work was on us; we just had to figure out how to get it through so it didn’t trip up the audience when they watched it.

So, instead, we came up with that joke where one of the scientists goes, “Where’d they get those little crowns?” And this other scientist goes, “Is that your question?” And that was our way of just going like, “We know this is crazy. We know it doesn’t make sense, but it’s all crazy, so go with it.”

And I think once you’re starting to train the audience to go on this ride of craziness and absurdness, I think then you get them to a place where a shark gets raised into the sky with some birds, and you just go, “OK, I guess that’s the natural conclusion to this movie.” And faces getting ripped off. I think [the reception of Hoppers is] because there’s a sense of humor that’s a little bit chaotic and illogical. Pixar movies are built so much on logic in a lot of ways. There was a freeness in the humor that many people were unsure about.

Antihypertensives Still Worth It for the Healthiest 10%, Large Study Suggests

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

— Drop in major cardiovascular events evident across spectrum of predicted risk

Across hypertensive individuals with varying predicted cardiovascular risk, blood pressure (BP) reduction was associated with a lower occurrence of real major cardiovascular events, according to a meta-analysis.

Using individual participant data from randomized trials, investigators found that major cardiovascular events were significantly less likely with every 5-mm Hg reduction in systolic BP from pharmacotherapy over 5 years (11.4% vs 13.0% with placebo or other control; HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.88-0.92).

This arguably covered people across the whole spectrum of cardiovascular health, as indicated by a study-specific risk model that put people with 5-year predicted risk <4% in the lowest decile and those with estimated risk >27% in the highest decile, reported Kazem Rahimi, DM, MSc, of the University of Oxford in England, and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine.

"BP lowering was associated with a decreased risk of major cardiovascular events across the risk spectrum, including a predicted 5-year risk below 4%," the researchers concluded.

The study adds to the limited literature on BP-lowering treatment in low-risk populations. Without better evidence, guideline recommendations for BP reduction in these individuals had been based on the assumption that the observed benefits of BP-lowering therapy in higher-risk individuals may be extrapolated to peers at lower risk.

"Many of the hypertensive trials, such as the SPRINT trial, have noted cardiovascular risk reduction in a high-risk group of patients with antihypertensive therapy. This meta-analysis shows that antihypertensive therapy reduces cardiovascular events across the risk spectrum and notably low-risk patients have a similar relative risk reduction as higher-risk patients," commented Matthew Sorrentino, MD, of University of Chicago Medicine.

Nevertheless, Rahimi's team found that the greatest absolute reduction in cardiovascular events occurred in the highest-risk group. The number needed to treat to prevent one major cardiovascular event over 5 years was 38 in the highest decile of predicted risk versus 255 in the lowest decile.

This is to be expected, according to Sorrentino. "The number needed to treat in a low-risk population will be significantly higher than in a high-risk cohort because of a lower incidence of events in the low-risk group, but the important finding is that risk reduction can be demonstrated in the low-risk cohort."

"Antihypertensive therapy will benefit anyone with consistently elevated BP regardless of calculated risk," he stressed.

Sorrentino said that the study supports the American Heart Association and the American College of Cardiology guideline on BP treatment, which does not suggest holding treatment in lower-risk individuals unable to achieve BP target with a lifestyle program.

Starting in 2025, the American groups said that low-risk individuals with an estimated 10-year cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk <7.5% (based on the PREVENT-CVD calculator) should initiate antihypertensives if average systolic BP remains at least 130 mm Hg (or average diastolic BP remains at least 80 mm Hg) after 3-6 months of lifestyle intervention, a class I recommendation.

Of note, Rahimi's team had an internal risk estimator for the meta-analysis, not PREVENT or another calculator used in clinical practice.

"This study is helpful to help us understand how BP lowering can be important even in lower-risk groups. While not all risk calculators are comparable, the overarching message is the same," maintained Sadiya Khan, MD, MSc, of Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine in Chicago.

"The key thing we know is that treating BP may be one of the most important things we can do to prevent cardiovascular disease even if someone may be at lower risk," she told MedPage Today. "If they are at higher risk, they will get much more absolute benefit from the treatment, so for individuals who have already had a heart attack or stroke, this could not be more important."

For their meta-analysis, Rahimi and colleagues relied on individual participant data from 51 randomized trials comparing antihypertensive treatment with placebo, an active comparator, or usual care.

The 357,440-person cohort had a mean age of 65 years and was 42% women. Mean baseline BP was 152.4/87.3 mm Hg, and the median predicted 5-year risk of a major cardiovascular event was 12.3%.

The primary outcome of interest was a first major cardiovascular event (i.e., stroke, ischemic heart disease, or heart failure resulting in death or hospitalization). Studies reached a median follow-up of 4.2 years in this report.

As for safety, the investigators opted for a narrower evidence base of 19 trials that compared antihypertensive treatment with placebo or usual care. The three prespecified safety outcomes consistently trended higher with the former:

Patients of all predicted risk levels shared an increase in the risk of safety events with antihypertensive treatment.

"Because the absolute reduction in the 5-year cumulative incidence of a major cardiovascular event in lower deciles was smaller than in higher deciles and the relative risk of adverse events was similar across the risk spectrum, the balance between benefit and harm narrowed as the predicted risk decreased and is unlikely to be captured by any single risk or BP threshold," Rahimi and colleagues noted.

"Quantifying this balance would require methods that account for differences in the severity, duration, and irreversibility of the outcomes concerned, which was beyond the scope of this analysis," they cautioned.

Rahimi's group also acknowledged that event counts under trial conditions may differ from those in routine practice, where the duration of treatment is often shorter and typically characterized by lower adherence and less protocolized care.
