Their conversations combine real-life criminal cases with personal experiences and comedic commentary. The podcast quickly garnered a massive following and, a decade after its debut, it has more than two million listeners per episode.

Now, HBO is bringing the most popular true crime podcast to TV with a new documentary series. My Favorite Murder will star the podcast's hosts and is set to premiere on Thursday, December 3, 2026, at 9:00 PM ET and 600 PM PT. The four-part series will air on the network and its streaming platform HBO Max, with new episodes arriving every Thursday until December 24, 2026.

The four episodes follow Kilgariff and Hardstark to different cities, where they discuss murders, historical events, and local stories. The documentary will begin in Chicago with Kilgariff and Hardstark live at the Chicago Theatre. The debut episode investigates the murders of a hairstylist and a cosmetic surgeon. The second episode will be at the Pasadena Civic Auditorium in California. During the performance, the duo will revisit a 1980s insurance incident involving a business called Just Sweats. They also talk about the 1963 kidnapping of Frank Sinatra Jr., the son of the iconic musician.

The documentary moves to Boston for its third installment. Filmed at the Boch Center Wang Theatre, the episode examines the mysterious 1961 disappearance of Joan Risch, whose fate remains unknown to this very day. Kilgariff and Hardstark also discuss the Blizzard of 1978, a winter storm that left residents to face dangerous circumstances.

The final episode will take place at Brooklyn's Kings Theatre. The last chapter features the murder of Scarsdale diet doctor Herman Tarnower before discussing a strange event involving red bees that led investigators to a maraschino cherry warehouse.

Kilgariff and Hardstark serve as creators, hosts, writers, and executive producers of HBO's adaptation of My Favorite Murder. My Favorite Murder will premiere on December 3, 2026, on HBO and HBO Max.

HBO Max is a subscription-based streaming service offering content from HBO, Warner Bros., DC, and more. In 2025, the service re-branded itself as HBO Max after having previously cut "HBO" from its name.

Michael Connelly’s Bosch is a tough act to follow, but one perfect next read is a 10-book crime series that feels like a combination of Harry Bosch and the Nordic noir subgenre. The bestselling books and the subsequent Bosch TV adaptations on Amazon are among the most celebrated entries in the crime genre. The series is 26 books and going strong, with the most recent book, The Hollow, holding 4.5 out of 5 stars on Goodreads based on 12,488 ratings, and the novel has maintained its place on the New York Times Bestseller list a month after release.

The Bosch books shine because of their complex protagonist, gritty realism, and sharp systemic critique. What’s more, Connelly carried his crime journalism experience into the books, making them feel like they could happen in the real world. It can be difficult to find books that scratch the same itch. Michael Connelly’s style and characters are just so distinctive. However, that doesn’t mean the perfect next book series doesn’t exist.

The Joona Linna and Bosch book series share narrative DNA that makes the Nordic Noir books a natural next choice for fans of Michael Connelly or the Bosch TV shows. What’s more, the Joona Linna series, also called the Killer Instinct series, has just been adapted for the screen in the Apple TV show Nocturne, making it the perfect time to pick them up.

Detective Superintendent Joona Linna is cut from the same cloth as Harry Bosch. The two men are haunted by trauma in their past, which changes the way they engage with the world around them. Joona worked as an elite Swedish paratrooper, while Bosch was a Vietnam tunnel rat. As a result, both have tactical skills and stay calm under pressure. Neither has many personal relationships, and they have uncompromising moral codes. Both have an almost obsessive focus on solving cases. They both put the case above all personal comfort, and they cross the bureaucratic lines to get victims justice.

The Joona Linna books also take a similar approach to procedure as the Bosch series. Alexander Ahndoril and Alexandra Coelho Ahndoril, the husband-and-wife duo who write under the pen name Lars Kepler, do extensive research for their novels, visiting crime scenes, interviewing police officers, and reading up on forensics and psychology. They told The Real Book Spy, “To us, crime fiction stands with one foot in reality and the other in the world of fiction, where anything is possible.” This is extremely similar to Connelly, who was a crime reporter and grounds his writing in real investigative and police work.

While the Bosch and Joona Linna series align in big ways, they also diverge significantly, as Lars Kepler’s books carry the hallmarks of the Nordic noir genre.

The Michael Connelly books are known for including social commentary, and the Joona Linna books are just as critical when it comes to social issues and politics. It’s a cornerstone of the Nordic noir genre. That said, the focus differs because Connelly grounds his criticisms in major US issues, like gun control and the eroding trust between police and the public, while the Joona Linna books focus on significant problems in Sweden, including the international weapons trade, youth alienation, and gender-based violence.

Anyone who thinks the Bosch books are bleak is in for a surprise about how much bleaker crime stories can be. Nordic noirs, like Lars Kepler’s books, frequently include graphic descriptions of violence and psychological terror. The Joona Linna stories include descriptions of the cold, unforgiving Scandinavian landscape, forcing readers to feel the same isolation and hopelessness as the main character. The environment is claustrophobic, and the killers are nightmare fuel.

Unlike other Nordic noirs, the Kepler books aren’t nihilistic, though, carrying a quiet optimism that the world can get better. The authors explain this change in the Goodreads notes for The Sandman, saying this:

“Our books are quite scary, but at the same time optimistic, in our opinion. It is a positive journey from riddles to answers, from chaos to order, from injustice to justice. In fiction, the world can become good again in a way that one wishes also could happen in reality.We love happy endings, not idyllic or cheesy, but in some way happy.”

This core belief will feel familiar to Bosch readers, as it’s very similar to the way Michael Connelly writes. Neither book series pushes the unrealistic idea that everything will immediately get better; however, they still believe justice is possible. Ultimately, the shared thesis makes Joona Linna books the best Nordic noir series for fans of Bosch.

10 things you should never do on your Android phone

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Avoid major mistakes that make your Android phone less secure and less convenient to use.

If you've used Android for a while, you probably know some of its most important rules. But even with an OS that's been around for more than 15 years, myths and outdated assumptions still persist. Or you might be new to Android and not totally sure how to best take care of your device.

Either way, Android doesn't come with a complete user manual, so we've compiled some of the mistakes you shouldn't make on an Android phone. They include ignoring security features and trying advanced functions without understanding them. Follow these, and your Android phone should last years in good condition.

Why it's bad: Software updates fix known vulnerabilities. Patches in a software update are often responsive, meaning they protect against attacks that are happening, not just theoretical threats. Also, if you use high-security apps like banking and wallet tools, those services won't work if your phone gets too behind on security updates.

Who says so: Security vulnerabilities in Android are reported to Google, and Google shares this information confidentially with Android device makers. You can read about what threats Google considers critical on its Android Security page.

Google doesn't share this information publicly for 90 days, giving vendors time to create proper patches and preventing would-be attackers from taking advantage of the details before people have a chance to install patches. There are a lot of players here: Google is responsible for Android software, then every phone maker adds software to Android devices that needs to be patched. Chip makers like MediaTek and Qualcomm also need to patch the drivers that allow those chips to function properly.

What's the best practice: Install every security update as soon as it appears. Also, open the Google Play Store and make sure to update apps regularly — automatic updates are best.

Why it's bad: When your phone is running too many processes at once, it slows down. That seems obvious, but what's actually happening is your phone is shuttling apps between three internal components: storage, RAM and a special partition of RAM called ZRAM made to quickly store and recall active processes.

When your RAM fills up, it starts compressing processes so it can cram them into the ZRAM partition. When there's no more room in ZRAM for compressed data, your phone enters a state called memory thrashing, where it repeatedly loads data from the ZRAM and then compresses it back in an ongoing cycle. This causes your phone to slow down and heat up as the CPU drains power.

Who says so: The Android Open Source Project offers technical details on the so-called "low memory killer daemon" (the software process that stuffs data into ZRAM), and the problems caused when you let that memory fill up with extraneous data pages.

What's the best practice: Restart your phone regularly to clear the RAM and ZRAM. When you're done with a heavy app like a game, swipe to close it from the app switcher menu (though you don't need to do this constantly for all apps). Finally, uninstall apps that you no longer use to make sure they aren't running processes you don't need. On Android, even if you don't open an app, it may still run and use your RAM in the background.

As another part of core maintenance, make sure to keep enough space free on your Android phone. Running it with maxed-out storage means it has no room for temporary caches and other "helper" data.

Why it's bad: There is no easier way for bad actors to take advantage of security flaws than from apps that come from outside Google's Play Store garden. One common practice is for bad apps to take advantage of your phone's Accessibility controls, which grant privileges for copying your phone's screen and running apps in the background. Google is cautious about approving accessibility access on the Play Store, which is why bad apps are often found on third-party sites.

Who says so: According to Google's security blog, Google Play Protect identified more than 13 million malicious apps from outside the Play Store in 2024 — and those are just the ones the service was able to detect. The standards for Android app security are set by the App Defense Alliance, part of Linux, which forms the foundation of Android's code.

What's the best practice: If you must install apps from outside Google Play, make sure you 100 percent trust the source. A first-party developer you trust, or a security-conscious third-party app market like F-Droid, is better than a random forum or Reddit page. Always use caution outside the Google Play Store.

Why it's bad: If you're asked to disable Google Play Protect for any reason, that's a major red flag. Usually, a scammer will try to convince you to download a phony app by pretending to be your bank or another trusted source. When you try to install the new app, you get a warning from Play Protect, which the scammer tells you to disregard. Never ignore this warning.

Who says so: Play Protect has been a part of Android for almost 10 years, and it gets constant updates that make it more stringent. It's the primary line of defense against dangerous apps you try to install.

What's the best practice: Never disable Google Play Protect, but don't trust it blindly either. Researchers like Bitdefender claim to have found apps that made it to the Play Store and still pose a risk, so you still need to be careful about what you install.

Why it's bad: In the early days of Android, there were good reasons to obtain root access to your phone. You could enable features that were inexplicably turned off, like network tethering on Verizon's early Android devices. It allows you to remove bloatware installed by your mobile carrier, or even load an OS like CyanogenMod that was better than what Samsung or Google provided. Those days are over.

Rooting a phone triggers failures in Google's Play Integrity API, and that's a break that can't heal. You won't be able to use banking or financial apps or any apps that rely on your phone's Strong integrity, a tag that disappears when you obtain root access to your system. Having root access means mistakes or malicious apps can cause much more damage.

Who says so: Even the US government says this is a bad idea. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) warns against rooting your mobile device, listing rooting and jailbreaking a phone as a threat in its Mobile Threat Catalogue.

What's the best practice: There is not enough reason to root your primary Android phone today if you aren't a software developer. Phone features are no longer restricted based on which carrier sold you the phone. And phone makers let you remove (or at least hide) the bloat — you can even remove Google apps that used to be permanently stuck in your app drawer.

If you have an old device that no longer gets updates, rooting and installing a custom ROM can bring new life to it, but the trade-offs aren't worth it on an in-support daily phone.

Why it's bad: Say your new Android phone came with a bunch of apps you don't want. The Settings menu lets you disable them so they disappear, but they're still taking up space and you can't let it go.

So, you found a YouTube video that shows you how to turn on USB Debugging, which lets you manage your phone's files from your PC. You figure this isn't a problem, since you aren't installing anything from a third party or handing your phone to a stranger.

Except those instructions often skip an important final step: remember to turn USB Debugging off. Otherwise, your phone is more vulnerable to attacks using a computer. While Android requires you to authorize the connection when you connect to any PC for the first time, you might not realize what you're doing, or someone could trick you into handing over access. That's why it's important to watch the prompts when connecting to an unknown USB port (more on this below).

There are lots of features on Android that can give you advanced access to your system without being dangerous, as long as you're careful to turn them off when you're done.

Who says so: Google is enthusiastic about Android users trying Developer Mode, but admits "it's essential to understand the implications of each setting." That's a massive understatement, since these options can cause problems if you don't know what they do.

What's the best practice: If you dabble in the Android cauldron, be sure to put out the fire. Disable USB Debugging and any other features that you turned on once the task is done. Follow instructions closely, and don't mess with features you don't truly understand.

Why it's bad: When you access an open Wi-Fi network, you have no guarantees of privacy. Anyone else who joins the network can use free analyzer tools to see what's happening. Almost every modern website uses HTTPS, meaning information you send to a site (like login credentials) is encrypted. But someone snooping on the network would still be able to see which sites you're accessing.

Even if the network seems trustworthy, it's hard to be sure. For example, say you use the password on the wall to connect to a cafe network. Someone could have set up a fake network with the same name and password combination, and your device won't know the difference. It looks for the strongest connection, which might be the person next to you with a sketchy hotspot and Wi-Fi sniffer, not the router mounted above the ceiling.

Who says so: The US Government's Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency said public Wi-Fi networks are one of the biggest threats for high-risk security attacks, though this was in 2012 before HTTPS was as common as it is now. Technology departments at universities like Vanderbilt continue to warn of the risks and how to mitigate them.

What's the best practice: Use a VPN for another layer of encryption on untrusted networks. If you must use public Wi-Fi without a VPN, avoid working with sensitive info like bank details. And if a site doesn't use HTTPS, leave it for when you're at home.

Why it's bad: When you plug into a USB port, you are often connecting your phone to both a power source and a data connection. Your phone will prompt you to trust a new device for data connections, which is where the risk comes in. If you grant that access, an attacker could steal your photos, load malware onto your phone or intercept communications, among other harmful actions.

Who says so: The FCC has issued warnings against plugging your phone into unknown USB ports. While no reports of so-called "juice jacking" attacks have happened in the real world, the technique has been demonstrated multiple times at hacking events (first at the Black Hat security conference in 2013). There's even a USB cable called the O.MG sold by an InfoSec testing company, Hak5, that contains the necessary hardware to pull off a juice jacking attack — for testing purposes, of course.

What's the best practice: When you plug your phone in at an airport, don't accept any pop-up notices that appear. If possible, use a proper wall outlet or a portable charger to eliminate the small theoretical risk of these attacks.

Why it's bad: Apps often ask for more information than they need. Does a calculator app need access to your photos? Does a flashlight app need to use the microphone? Those are obvious examples of permissions you should be critical of. More important are Accessibility permissions, which allow an app to monitor everything on your screen, monitor your SMS messages (for 2FA codes, for example) and even control other apps without your knowledge. That sketchy to-do list app could open your banking app, change your password and have its way with your accounts, if it has proper accessibility access.

Another permission to watch is Display over other apps. This lets Facebook float Messenger chat heads on top of your home screen and other apps, but could also let a bogus app overlay a fake OK button that gives permission to further abuse. It may look like you're tapping Agree in the Google Play Store, when the button is actually a separate app that will install a nasty payload.

Who says so: Android takes permissions seriously: the latest versions of Android revoke all permissions from apps you haven't used in a long time, just to be safe. Google tells developers to ask for permissions incrementally, and only when a feature is needed, so you have context for why the app is asking.

What's the best practice: You know to be careful with microphone and camera access, but also be aware when an app asks for any unusual permissions. Regularly review your permissions and remove any that are unnecessary (or uninstall apps you never use).

Why it's bad: Lithium batteries hate extremes. When one is always kept at 100 percent, it increases the wear on internal components and reduces the total charge the battery can hold over time, according to Samsung. That's why modern iOS and Android versions come with battery management tools that keep your phone around 80 percent while you charge overnight, only topping up to 100 percent just before you wake up.

You should also avoid using cheap, off-brand USB cables for charging. Your phone came with a high-quality USB cable that includes a controller chip to make sure the device gets a steady voltage. Cheap USB cables often lack that chip, which can cause your battery to overheat. At worst, it can cause internal electrical damage, though any excess heat is bad for your battery's chemistry. It speeds up reactions and can cause the battery to degrade faster.

Who says so: Most phone makers have a warning page about batteries, with battery utilities recommending that you only charge your phone to 80 percent under normal circumstances. This will extend the longevity of the battery, which is a good idea if you plan on keeping your phone for many years.

What's the best practice: Let Android do its job. Your phone has software that helps manage battery, both for day-to-day life and overall longevity. Don't force your phone to charge past Android's recommended limit all the time.

Choose an SBA loan over an online loan if you can wait weeks. The SBA’s guarantee comes with a capped rate and terms up to 10 years for most uses, 25 for real estate. Expect to pledge collateral on loans over $50,000, sign a personal guarantee, and, as a startup, put in at least 10% of the cost.

Owners turn to online lenders for speed. In the Federal Reserve’s 2025 Small Business Credit Survey, 60% of firms that borrowed from online lenders said their borrowing costs ran higher than expected, against 32% of large-bank borrowers.

An SBA loan is slower and cheaper. The U.S. Small Business Administration guarantees up to 85% of most 7(a) loans, which banks and other approved lenders make. In return for that backing, SBA rules cap a variable rate at the banks’ benchmark prime rate, now 7.00%, plus 3 to 6.5 percentage points, depending on the loan’s size.

Say a landscaper owes $120,000 on online term loans repaid over two years at 30% interest, about $6,710 a month. A 10-year 7(a) loan at a fixed 11% cuts that to about $1,650. The longer term costs more interest in total, about $78,000 over 10 years against $41,000 on the online loans. Extra payments shrink that, and a 7(a) loan shorter than 15 years carries no prepayment fee.

A 7(a) loan of up to $5 million can cover working capital, equipment, real estate, debt refinancing, or starting or buying a business. A 504 loan, made through a nonprofit Certified Development Company, finances buildings, land, and long-lived machinery at a fixed rate. SBA microloans of up to $50,000 come from nonprofit lenders.

Under the SBA’s lending rules, every owner of 20% or more personally guarantees the whole loan, and a loan over $50,000 needs collateral, though a lender can’t refuse you for weak collateral alone. A business open a year or less must put in at least 10% of the total cost, and so must most buyers of a business.

To get an SBA loan, start with a bank that knows your business, and ask whether it’s an SBA Preferred Lender, one allowed to approve loans without the SBA’s review, which can take up to 10 business days. Or describe your needs in the SBA’s Lender Match tool, which lists interested lenders two business days later.

Apply once you know a big cost is coming, and keep online loans for needs that can’t wait weeks.

Moises Caicedo was forced off in the first half of Chelsea’s thrashing by Leeds as Xabi Alonso’s injury problems continue to mount.

The Ecuadorian lynchpin suffered a bad landing after meeting James Justin’s aerial challenge, which saw him twist in midair and land on his tailbone.

Caicedo was initially able to continue but after being troubled by his back in the following minutes, repeatedly holding the problem area, he went down off the ball, covering his seemingly tearful eyes with his shirt in dejection.

He was replaced by Jordan Henderson in the 22nd minute, but did not go straight down the tunnel for further treatment, instead taking a seat on the bench with an ice pack.

Alonso said after the 4-1 defeat that Caicedo had picked up a “knock” and that Chelsea would have to wait and see following further assessments on Sunday.

“He was in pain and let's see how bad it is,” Alonso said.

The circumstances in which Caicedo’s injury was dealt with left the Chelsea dugout unhappy with referee Michael Oliver.

Caicedo agonised on the deck for a considerable period before the Blues eventually sent their medical team on to treat him, with club doctors delaying their entry onto the pitch to try and prevent him having to come off for a minute, as per a new rule introduced in the Premier League this season.

Seemingly for the same reason, Caicedo leapt up and refused treatment from Chelsea medics when they approached him, but Oliver still ordered him to the touchline, reducing Chelsea to 10 men for a minute.

Caicedo also suffered a fitness scare during the international break when he was replaced at half-time of Ecuador’s 2-0 friendly win over Bolivia in midweek after feeling unwell.

The Blues are already without a number of key members due to injury including Reece James, Maxence Lacroix and Joao Pedro, with the club struggling for form following four defeats in five.

Stock market correction? Whatever — Wall Street is bullish on 2027, and one chief investment officer sees the S&P 500 soaring 45% to 10,000

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Dow Jones Industrial Average and the S&P 500 joined the Nasdaq in correction territory as AI stocks plunged further, but 2026 is also winding down, and Wall Street is already putting out predictions for 2027.

Analysts see solid gains ahead, with the U.S. economy expected to continue chugging along on the back of massive hyperscaler spending and resilient consumers. Some also welcomed the recent decline for taking froth off markets that might otherwise start looking overheated.

Even this week’s selloff, which marked the worst weekly loss since the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic, was largely dismissed as a move driven more by emotion than fundamentals.

“This does not mean stampedes are uninformative or palpitations never presage heart attacks, but today’s panic does not feel like an AI bust,” Viktor Shvets, head of global desk strategy at Macquarie Capital, said in a note Friday.

Michael Monaghan, portfolio manager and partner at Founder ETFs, quipped that the market is “selling first and asking questions later.” And Jamie Cox, managing partner for Harris Financial Group, predicted investors will “return to rationality” and look instead to consumption-boosting GDP.

Meanwhile, Morgan Stanley chief U.S. equity strategist Michael Wilson said earlier this week that the S&P 500 would finish 2027 at 8,700, representing a 26% jump from current levels and a 15% rally when he made the call.

“We see the mid-cycle transition now ending with a more stable mid-cycle backdrop evolving in 2027,” he wrote.

Goldman Sachs also sees the broad market index soaring to 8,700 next year, “albeit with higher volatility than in recent years.”

Peter Oppenheimer, the bank’s chief global equity strategist, said that while corporate earnings growth will slow, inflation, bond yields, and oil prices will also moderate. “We expect the equity bull market to continue.”

Elsewhere, estimates were less bullish but still upbeat. UBS Global Wealth Management put the S&P 500 at 8,200 next year, implying 19% upside from Friday’s close.

Although it sees more earnings gains in 2027, Wall Street is focusing on whether the investment cycle can support profits into 2028, UBS explained.

“Cannibalization, financing, and execution risks around AI have increased while expectations and index concentration remain high,” it added. “The market is therefore more sensitive to earnings disappointment or a further rise in discount rates.”

Wells Fargo’s S&P 500 outlook in 2027 is also for 8,200, which assumes slower profit growth as well.

But then there’s Steve Chiavarone, chief investment officer for equities at Federated Hermes, who has a year-end view of 10,000—or a 45% surge from current levels.

He brushed aside this month’s midterm elections, which saw Democrats sweep Congress and several state capitals. While Wall Street fears the blue tsunami will slow the AI infrastructure boom, Chiavarone said risks to the buildout are overblown. Even a slight retreat would be positive.

“A modest pullback in the data center buildout could give cash flows time to catch up, easing inflation pressures and making the investment boom more sustainable,” he argued.

Slower prices could prevent additional rate hikes from the Federal Reserve, which has grown more hawkish as policymakers become impatient with inflation exceeding their 2% target for over five years.

Although Wall Street sees more tightening in the coming months, some analysts also anticipate rate cuts later in 2027 as inflation cools. But that assumes the Iran war will be over some time next year.

For now, Gaurav Mallik, chief investment officer at Modera Wealth Management, described the market slide as a “garden variety correction,” with stock leadership rotating from tech to industrials and financials.

“The pullback in stocks sets the market up nicely for 2027, which we believe will be a high-single-digit year,” he added. “Multiples are shrinking, and total returns are a function of earnings growth, which so far looks robust for 2027.”

Jason Ma is the weekend editor at Fortune, where he covers markets, the economy, finance, and housing.
