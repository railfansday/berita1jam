Amazon teams with Uber Eats for new Prime perks

Order on Amazon’s platforms and (sometimes) skip the delivery fees.

Amazon has a pretty long list of perks for members of its Prime subscription program, and today brings a new addition. The company now supports placing Uber Eats orders directly on its Amazon.com website and in the Amazon Shopping app in the US. The partnership with Uber will also grant $0 delivery fees to Prime Members on their Uber Eats orders of $25 or more placed via Amazon, as well as a promise of "Prime-exclusive offers available only on Amazon." No separate Uber account is needed to place the delivery orders and nearly 10,000 cities and towns in the US will support the integration.

According to the release from Amazon, the Uber Eats integration could save Prime users as much as $175 a year, although the exact amount will obviously vary depending on how much you rely on delivery for meals.

Amazon offered a year of free GrubHub+ access along with Prime starting in 2022. This subscription program, which includes $0 delivery fees and other perks tied to GrubHub orders, became a permanent benefit of Prime in 2024.

How Norway flipped Trump’s name-calling into destination marketing

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

After President Donald Trump called Norway a “shithouse country,” its tourism agency turned the insult into a marketing opportunity to promote its designer bathrooms.

Trump criticized Norway at a rally in Michigan on October 25 because he didn’t receive the Nobel Peace Prize, which is awarded by the Norwegian Nobel Committee. Disparaging the Nordic nation with a term he’s previously used for Haiti, ​Libya, Somalia, and Yemen, Trump said he didn’t want immigrants from Norway. Now its tourism agency has turned the attack into a badge of honor with a website for the world’s best toilets that’s drawing eyes from across the globe.

While Visit Norway has had a web page about its public toilets for several years owing to their dramatic views and better than average design, the group changed the headline of the page to reference Trump’s comments and reshared it last week. It’s currently Visit Norway’s most popular page in most countries and has had 200,000 page views since Monday, while related social media posts have generated 5 million organic views so far, Visit Norway tells Fast Company.

The updated web page says “Norway makes shithouses great again!” and shows off some of the country’s most scene-stealing public accommodations across Norwegian Scenic Routes, a collection of some of the 18 prettiest drives in the country.

Among them are the Stegastein Lookout restroom, which looks out over a fjord, was designed by the architects Todd Saunders and Tommie Wilhelmsen, and won a Norwegian National Construction Prize. The restroom at Ureddplassen, a seaside memorial designed by Haugen/Zohar Architects that honors those killed on a Norwegian submarine by a German mine during World War II, is housed inside a wave-shaped building made from poured concrete with frosted glass windows, and it’s been called “the world’s most beautiful public loo.”

“Along the Norwegian Scenic Routes, architecture and design are used deliberately to enhance the experience of traveling through some of Norway’s most dramatic landscapes,” says Visit Norway’s head of consumer marketing, Anne-Signe Fagereng. “Leading architects have designed rest stops, viewpoints, and public toilets that fit into the landscape. Many have won international architecture awards, and several have become attractions in their own right. Travelers come to see them, photograph them and, yes, use them.”

A well-designed stop takes pressure off nature, makes remote areas accessible to everyone, and turns a necessary break into part of the experience, she says.

What Visit Norway’s selected restrooms have in common is thoughtful architecture and prime locations, like the rest area at Flesefossen waterfall or the restrooms at Skjervsfossen, which has glass floors and windows that look out over a river and was designed by the architectural firm Fortunen. The Oslo-based firm Snøhetta designed its service building at a hill called Kvalhausen as if it’s coming out of the hill.

Americans marvel at the cleanliness of bathrooms at truck stops like Buc-ee’s or Maverick, but in Norway, the best public restrooms show what’s possible when a government really invests in their services. Norwegian Scenic Routes is run by the Norwegian Public Roads Administration, or Statens Vegvesen.

Visit Norway says the updated site isn’t a political statement or broader criticism of anyone specifically—it’s just destination marketing. And it seems to be working. The tourism agency says it’s received many positive messages from Americans who say they want to visit. Now thanks to Trump’s comments, rest stops are on their to-do list.

Agentic AI Is Shipping Code. Who Owns The Risk?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Andrea Scaduto is co-founder and CEO of SecureFlag, a secure coding training platform.

Not long ago, much of the discussion in software engineering circles was about whether developers should use AI to write code. Now, the focus is on agentic AI and agents that run commands with minimal human direction.

Gartner put agentic AI at the top of its 2026 cybersecurity trends, warning that the technology is creating new attack surfaces as employees and developers adopt it, and that no-code platforms and vibe coding are speeding up the spread of unmanaged agents, unsecured code and potential compliance violations.​​

Before AI agents, development gave organizations a fairly clear path to accountability. A developer makes the change, a reviewer checks it and the service team owns what reaches production.

The chain is now more complicated when an AI agent takes a feature request and turns it into code for a developer to review. For example, a product manager provides the instructions, the agent generates the change and the developer approves it. If that change exposes customer data, who owns the decision to ship it?

The most practical answer, I’d argue, is the person who approves the merge. Agent-written code becomes the organization’s code once a developer approves it, so that approval can’t be a formality. If the person signing off can’t explain what a change does and why it’s safe, it shouldn’t be deployed.

Customers won’t accept “the AI wrote it” as an explanation, and neither will auditors or regulators. The EU’s Cyber Resilience Act makes this clear by placing responsibility on the organizations that put products on the market.​

It’s certainly beneficial that teams can now produce more software faster, but they don’t automatically gain the capacity to assess it all. More output also means more changes to approve, and less time to give each one proper attention.

Research from The Consortium for Information & Software Quality estimated that the cost of poor software quality in the U.S. was approximately $2.41 trillion in 2022, with accumulated technical debt tracked separately at around $1.52 trillion. As teams produce more code, even a low rate of flawed code can create more technical debt than they can keep up with.

Some of that debt is also security debt, as security vulnerabilities found in production can become entry points for attackers and cost more to fix in the long run.​

​Any approach to governing agentic AI has to start with knowing where it is being used. Gartner’s research mentioned above recommends identifying both sanctioned and unsanctioned AI agents, establishing controls for each and preparing incident response plans for agent-related risks.

It’s harder than it sounds, as agents work in integrated development environment extensions, command-line tools, CI/CD pipelines and business-user platforms. Calling them all “AI coding assistants” can obscure how differently they operate. An agent that proposes a change for review has a very different level of access and autonomy from one that can modify a repository or deploy code. These differences should determine how each agent is governed.

For each agent, security and engineering leaders should know who uses it and what it is allowed to do. ​

Agents should have only the permissions they need, with human approval reserved for higher-risk actions, such as merging changes to protected branches or modifying authentication and sensitive data.

Routine changes can use the same security checks as human-authored code, with agent-generated changes clearly identified so teams can see where they came from. Some of these checks have limits, though. In my experience, scanners are effective at finding known patterns but much less reliable when a vulnerability depends on business logic or application context.

As co-founder and director of SecureFlag, a company that provides hands-on developer security training, I’m particularly interested in how developers build the skills to work securely with AI agents and review the code they produce. This focus informs my views on agentic AI, though I recognize that training is only one part of governing how these agents are used.​​​

It’s developers who need to find the issues scanners miss, but general awareness training doesn’t necessarily equip them to do so. Instead, Gartner recommends adaptive programs that include AI-specific tasks, and I’ve found hands-on practice in real development environments can help to build the skills to do this.​​

​Organizations can only take responsibility for agents if they know whether the controls around them are working and developers have the skills they need.

The most useful measures come from the code itself, such as comparing vulnerability rates in agent-authored and human-authored changes, and tracking that difference to see if risk goes down. Remediation time and recurring vulnerability classes can also show where problems continue to occur.

It’s also worth tracking how often teams catch problems in agent-authored changes before merge. A rising catch rate could mean controls are working better or agents are producing more problematic code, and it’s important to know which. These measures give a better view of how the organization is managing the risks of agentic development.​

Securing agentic development has two sides. The agent itself needs to be secured, with limits on what it can access and change. The software it produces also needs to be secure, which depends on someone understanding what the agent was asked to do and whether the result matches that intent.

Developers are at the center of both. In many organizations today, agents operate with some of the same access as the developer using them, meaning developers often influence what agents can access without making a deliberate choice. They are also accountable for the changes they approve.

Leadership is responsible for the conditions around that work, including setting boundaries for what agents can access and making sure developers have the skills to configure agents securely and review what they produce. The organization carries the risk either way.

As agents take on more of the development process, organizations need to define where human responsibility lies. Waiting for an incident to establish those boundaries is a costly way to find out.​

Gaming hardware really has come on leaps and bounds in recent years, with console players finally able to keep up with those on PC for the most part. As well as first-party releases, both Xbox and PlayStation have plenty of partners who bring out additional products for their consoles, and it's a much healthier ecosystem with quality products all over the place.

That said, no piece of hardware is arguably as important as the controller, which provides our only method of getting the characters in games to do what we want. Particularly if you're a fan of fast-paced shooters and other genres that require high precision, a good controller really can be the difference between life and virtual death.

With all of that in mind, a new release is about to hit the market that really could be the very best example of a controller anywhere, though be warned that it comes with a price tag to match those lofty ambitions.

As first reported by GameRant, official PlayStation partner Razer has revealed its most premium PS5 controller to date, the new Raiju V3 Pro. Made from magnesium alloy and with plenty of bells and whistles to keep things interesting, the controller is priced at a very hefty $349.99, though you do get a lot of mileage for that outlay.

In sharing the news of the release with potential players, Razer shared the following insight:

"Razer’s most ambitious officially licensed PlayStation® 5 controller to date, uniting premium materials, a one-of-a-kind design, and high-level performance in a single collector’s piece — debuting in a limited, individually serialized drop of 1,337 units. Razer, the leading global lifestyle brand for gamers, today announced the Razer Raiju V3 Pro Signature Edition, its flagship controller officially licensed for the PlayStation® 5 console, built for players who want the highest level of performance and craftsmanship that has defined Razer engineering.

"Introduced in 2016, the Raiju controller line has been refined over the past decade evolving into Razer’s most advanced PlayStation® controller ever. The Razer Raiju V3 Pro Signature Edition distills that decade of experience into a single form, balancing design, weight, and performance for players on the PlayStation® 5 console who refuse to compromise. Limited to an initial release of 1,337 individually serialized units, each Raiju V3 Pro Signature Edition is produced through a demanding, precision-driven process – from its magnesium alloy exoskeleton to its finely detailed anodized aluminum accessory tool."
