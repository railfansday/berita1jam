In 1974, the University of Melbourne's chemistry professor Richard Robson had the idea of replacing the wooden balls and metal rods used in structural models with molecules and chemical bonds. He began experimenting with copper ions linked by a four-armed organic molecule, creating a structure with a cavity. His work laid the foundation for metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), a class of porous materials formed by linking metal ions with organic molecules. Their pores can capture pollutants, support chemical reactions and store gases, giving MOFs a wide range of potential applications.

Warhammer Survivors Review: A Brutal New Bullet Heaven Game

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Warhammer Survivors is a brutal spin-off of Vampire Survivors, pitting heroes from Warhammer 40,000 and Age of Sigmar against endless hordes of enemies while maintaining the addictive gameplay loop.

Few games have a title as accurate or descriptive to its core content as Warhammer Survivors. In a nutshell, itt’s Vampire Survivors gameplay, but with Warhammer 40,000 and Age of Sigmar characters, monsters, and levels, but with the difficulty turned up to eleven.

Well, that’s a bit of an unfair introduction. If Warhammer Survivors was just a cosmetic upgrade, they would have just released it as DLC in Vampire Survivors, rather than giving it its own dedicated game. After all, Vampire Survivors has invited Castlevania to its roster and added Contra too, so surely Space Marines wouldn’t look out of place?

Thankfully, Warhammer Survivors shakes up the gameplay and adds new tweaks to the familiar formula that makes it a worthwhile purchase for people who aren’t familiar with the tabletop game. Long-time Vampire Survivors fans just need to be aware that its Warhammer equivalent is a fair bit harder than the content they’ve beaten before.

At its core, Warhammer Survivors has the same gameplay loop as Vampire Survivors and its many clones. The player picks a hero, who then has to survive against endless hordes of enemies. The main gameplay gimmick is that the player cannot control how the character shoots: just how they move across the battlefield.

Throughout each level, the player earns experience points from killing enemies, and when they level up, they are given new weapons and upgrades that make it easier to destroy more foes. As time goes on, the enemies get stronger and grow in numbers, meaning careful movement becomes even more important.

There are also bosses that appear throughout the stage, which are outlined in a neon green aura. Slaying these bosses results in crates being dropped with level upgrades. These are important, as if a weapon is sufficiently levelled, then a crate could carry its improved version, such as the Boltgun transforming into the Heavy Boltgun, which has a drastically improved fire rate.

Dying on a run isn’t the end of the world, as the player can find coins strewn throughout each stage that can be spent on static buffs and upgrades. These make future runs easier, so the player has a greater chance of surviving later stages.

Warhammer Survivors keeps this gameplay loop intact, though it splits everything across two worlds. On the main menu, the player selects the “Universe”: Warhammer 40,000 and Age of Sigmar, the latter of which is the modern replacement for Warhammer Fantasy (RIP). They have their own stages, characters, and enemies, with no direct crossovers between the two.

Technically, there is content that connects the two, as the upgrades unlocked are shared between the two, meaning working hard in one helps out the other. There are also non-franchise-specific levels that they share, like the Hobby Table, but for the most part, the Warhammer 40,000 and Age of Sigmar parts of the game are separate.

So, if Warhammer Survivors is just Vampire Survivors with Ork Boyz, then is it worth buying? Well, there are some key differences that justify it existing as its own game, rather than DLC for Vampire Survivors.

In Vampire Survivors, all the weapons are tied to the player character. Warhammer Survivors changes this with its Follower system, as characters are now unlocked alongside their weapon when the player levels up. This means they follow the selected hero around, and their attacks will originate from them.

The Follower system might not seem like a big addition, but needing to account for the changing starting position of attacks makes the gameplay considerably more challenging. This is especially true for the Garlic-type weapon users, which have a static area-of-effect centred on their hero. In Warhammer Survivors, the party members move around after the player, meaning the player has to guide them, like a kite, into the enemy hordes.

The other big difference in Warhammer Survivors is how it handles end of stage bosses. In Vampire Survivors, if the player spends too long in a stage, they’ll eventually be attacked by the Grim Reaper and quickly killed. Warhammer Survivors replaces the manifestation of death with the Extremis Bosses: foes that appear at the twenty-five minute mark for an epic fight.

The Extremis Bosses can’t be faced without preparation, however, as the player needs to unlock a specific item in each stage to be able to damage them. If the player doesn’t have this item, then the boss will be invincible, and it will automatically kill the hero in a cutscene.

The enemies the player faces on each stage can also have unique gimmicks, usually tied to their bosses. It’s possible for Tyranids to buff their weaker units, making them considerably faster, while the followers of Nurgle can summon massive poison-spewing chimneys, creating damaging areas for the player to walk through.

The Orks of Warhammer 40,000 easily have the best gimmick, as they can call a mighty “Waaagh”, bringing endless waves of units against the player until it ends. Surviving a Waaagh can be one of the trickiest parts of the Ork stage, unless the player prepares lots of AoE attacks to survive the horde.

The new additions to Warhammer Survivors make the game considerably harder than the source game. And make no mistake: I adore Vampire Survivors and have purchased it on multiple platforms. I have endured countless runs with my boy Poe, facing the creatures of the night with nothing but a piece of Garlic and a dream, and came out the other side.

Warhammer Survivors ramps the difficulty up in a few ways. The Follower system means the origin point of your weapons keeps moving around, making it harder to accurately fire at enemies. This isn’t as much of a problem with targeted weapon users, like grenades, but AoE and shooter characters are harder to predict, as they keep moving while shooting.

The changes to bosses are also a point against the game, because Warhammer Survivors does an extremely poor job of conveying exactly what items are needed to be “Prepared” for facing the Extremis Bosses. The best clues the player gets is seeing what items are always available in each stage, as well as blanked out menus that fill out if they happen to use the correct upgrades.

There’s far too little information about what items are needed to face the Extremeis Bosses, especially as the blanked out menu only appears after a twenty-five minute run, which is hard to reach on a good day. Most players aren’t going to bother doing trial and error: they’ll just Google the answers as soon as the game launches, so why bother obfuscating it?

It also bears mentioning that the Age of Sigmar characters are way less useful than their 40,000 equivalents. The Warhammer 40,000 roster includes gun and grenade users, while the ranged Age of Sigmar characters tend to be much slower. Thankfully, the upgrades menu stays the same between rosters, so it’s possible to grind with the Warhammer 40,000 cast for stat upgrades, in order to help out their fantasy equivalents.

There are also times when the game will just kill the player with no recourse. The Age of SIgmar stages are particularly bad for this, with enemy cavalry units randomly appearing and rushing across the stage, pushing the player into a wall and instantly killing them in a way that’s impossible to avoid.

The sub bosses fought in each stage also have far, far too much health. I’ve sat there for what felt like an eternity with maxed out weapons, stunlocking a boss into place, while damage numbers just rack up, and they won’t die. It’d be fine if their gameplay made them challenging foes to match their mighty health bar, but many of them are just regular enemies with bigger sprites.

The changes to Warhammer Survivors do provide an extra layer of challenge for those coming from the source material, and I’d be lying if I said I didn’t enjoy the new additions and wrinkles: it just needs a few more tweaks and balance checks for the game to be as good as it can be.

The Warhammer franchise has a reputation for being extremely dark in tone, especially 40,000. The Imperium of Man isn’t exactly a lovely place to exist, between the constant threat of Xeno invasion, the corrupting influence of Chaos present in the souls of mankind, and the rampant corruption within society itself.

Warhammer Survivors is a rare example of Warhammer being tongue-in-cheek and referential. This can be seen through its many nods to the actual game and its history, especially the weapons, such as exploding d6s hitting the board, Nuln Oil slowing foes down, the measuring tape increasing damage AoE, and the experience point booster being called White Dwarf.

The highlight of this approach is the Hobby Table stage, where the player fights off monsters on a crafting table, the kind used by many real-life fans of the tabletop game. The developers had mentioned in the past that the Hobby Table stage is a nod to Micro Machines, and it shows, being easily the most fun level in Warhammer Survivors.

The visuals and overall aesthetic of Warhammer Survivors are close to that of Vampire Survivors, and like the Boltgun series, the approach is mired in retro goodness. Few Warhammer games have managed to cram as many foes onto the screen as this one, making Warhammer Survivors one of the few titles to ever capture the essence of the beloved artwork from the series, save shown through a 16-bit filter.

Warhammer Survivors doesn’t try to tell a story: it’s about throwing iconic heroes from the franchise against familiar foes. It’s clear the people who made the game are huge fans of the source material, and that love shines throughout the experience.

The new layer of difficulty in Warhammer Survivors can be frustrating at times, but it also feels necessary for helping distinguish it from Vampire Survivors. If the player can soldier through the hard moments, then they’ll get another massive dose of Survivors gameplay and content, which is likely a huge selling point on its own for people who are still addicted to the original.

Warahmmer Survivors justifies itself as a separate entity throughout plenty of new characters and stages, with enough gameplay gimmicks to ensure it’s not just a reskin. The difficulty can be frustrating at times, but people don’t sign up for a Survivors-like to have an easy time. Warhammer Survivors will satisfy people looking for more of the familiar but different enough to not be a rehash, while still paying tribute to the universes that won over fans worldwide.

Managers can turn on staff when they're afraid of losing their own jobs

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Executives count on team leaders to keep everyone steady through cuts. But some bosses fear for their own roles and start belittling employees

A version of this article originally appeared in Quartz’s Leadership newsletter. Sign up here to get the latest leadership news and insights straight to your inbox.

Plenty of managers say little to their teams when job cuts are rumored. Some already know a layoff is coming and aren't allowed to discuss it. Others think the conversation will get out of hand, or would rather not face their own fear of being replaced, executive coach Dina Denham Smith explained in an article for Harvard Business Review last December.

With nothing to go on, staff tend to imagine the worst. Employees keep telling Smith, "I'm just putting my head down and laying low." They speak up less, avoid risks, and look after themselves before solving problems. An executive can easily mistake a quiet meeting for agreement when the people in the room are scared.

Many managers fear for their own jobs, too. If senior leaders leave them guessing about cuts, some start insisting on making every decision, belittling their teams in the process. Not only are staff worried about losing their jobs, but their bosses may begin to treat them worse too.

Executives tend to blame stress when managers snap at their team ahead of a layoff. Bank managers in China, for example, get more frustrated on days they're more afraid than usual for their jobs, according to a study by Fu Yang and colleagues in the Journal of Business Ethics. They also want to make more decisions themselves. The more managers care about being the one in charge, the likelier they are to turn rude and nasty with staff.

Managers are used to giving orders, according to Yang and colleagues. They stand to lose their say over a team, along with their paychecks, so they push their own staff around to prove they still run things. Executives can tell them to calm down, but the managers will still fear for their jobs.

Many managers get through rounds of cuts without bossing their staff around. They tend to say they bounce back quickly after hard times. Even on their worst days, they run their teams as usual, without insisting on making every decision or snapping at their employees. Before a layoff, a company needs its managers to take the news without turning on their staff. Yang and colleagues suggest checking how quickly job applicants and promotion candidates recover from setbacks before putting them in charge of a team. Current managers could get training to bounce back faster, with follow-ups every week or so.

Some managers take longer to get over bad news. They can still treat staff decently on days they're worried about their job. They do best when their minds stay on what's happening at work instead of racing ahead to what might come next. Two managers can hear the same news about a restructuring and run their teams very differently. The same manager can also handle it well one day and badly the next. A short meditation session or a daily guided recording of about 10 minutes may help managers keep their minds on what's in front of them, according to Yang and colleagues.

Staff tend to absorb some of their manager's strain, often without noticing, according to researchers at the University of Southern Denmark. Employees read the boss's face and voice for clues about how worried to be. Managers under strain may also plan the work less carefully and offer less help, making the job harder for everyone below them. So when senior leaders put dozens of middle managers under pressure at once, they're also putting dozens of teams under pressure.

Staff feel less strained when their bosses listen, fix practical problems, understand their jobs, and show them respect. How they're treated each day matters more to them than their managers' own stress. A CEO can't keep every manager calm through a round of cuts but can still ask managers to hear their staff out and fix the everyday problems that slow their teams down. Senior leaders can bring up both in every meeting with managers until the layoffs are over.

A manager can even calm a team down without any good news to give. Smith advises telling staff what's settled, what's still open, and when they'll hear more. A fixed update schedule helps too, even in weeks with nothing new to report. Smith said managers can admit they don't know yet whether jobs will be cut without making staff more afraid. Employees then spend less time swapping rumors, the source of much of their fear.

Smith reminds managers that their teams are "likely even further removed from information, power, and clarity than you are." With so little else to go on, staff take their cues from their manager. Often, the person they're watching is worried about losing a job, too.

10 flu myths about vaccines, treatment and what influenza really is

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 flu myths cover vaccines, treatment and what the flu really is, from whether the flu shot gives you the flu to whether antibiotics can cure it

The word "flu" does a lot of work it was never meant to do. People use it for a 24-hour stomach bug, a heavy cold or almost any winter illness that keeps them home. Influenza is something more specific. It is a respiratory illness caused by influenza A and B viruses, and it infects the nose, throat and lungs.

That loose vocabulary is one reason flu myths persist. Another is the vaccine itself. Many shots are given once or a handful of times in a lifetime, but the flu vaccine is offered every year. Each new season brings a fresh round of questions about whether it is safe, whether it works and whether a given person needs it.

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends an annual flu vaccine for everyone aged six months and older, with few exceptions. That short line of guidance still leaves people with questions about the details.

Treatment generates its own confusion. Some people expect antibiotics for a fever and body aches, even though influenza is a viral infection. Others assume a healthy adult can shrug off the flu, or that a vaccine without a perfect record is not worth getting.

The 10 myths below are a selection, not a complete catalog. They cover three areas: what the flu actually is, what causes it and how it is treated, and how the vaccine works for different groups of people. Those groups include pregnant women and people with egg allergies. Each entry names the misconception and explains what is accurate instead. Where guidance applies, the entries name the organizations that set it, such as the CDC, the World Health Organization and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists.

Every slide is written to stand on its own. A reader who wants to know whether a flu shot can cause the flu, or how influenza differs from a common cold, can go straight to that entry. The answers draw on established medical guidance rather than anecdote.

A flu shot cannot give you influenza. Injected flu vaccines contain either inactivated virus or a single protein from the virus, and neither one can cause the illness.

An inactivated virus has been treated so that it can no longer cause infection. It still carries the features the immune system needs to recognize. That combination lets the vaccine prompt protection without making anyone sick.

A vaccine built around a single viral protein contains no whole virus at all. It holds only one piece of the virus for the immune system to learn.

Reactions after an injection are a separate matter. A sore arm, a low fever and aches can follow a flu shot. Those effects come from the immune system responding to the vaccine. They are not influenza.

The nasal spray vaccine, FluMist, works differently. It contains a weakened live virus rather than an inactivated one. That virus has been adapted to grow in the cool conditions of the nose, not in the warmer lungs.

FluMist can cause mild symptoms such as a runny nose. It cannot cause the flu.

The illness most people call "stomach flu" is usually not influenza. It is typically gastroenteritis caused by germs such as norovirus or rotavirus. Those germs are separate from the influenza viruses behind the flu.

Influenza is a respiratory illness. Its main features are respiratory rather than digestive. The shared nickname ties together two illnesses with different causes.

Everyday language drives much of the mix-up. In casual speech, "flu" can mean almost any short illness that leaves a person miserable for a few days. A bout of vomiting and diarrhea picks up the label because it feels flu-like. That happens even when no influenza virus is involved.

The nickname does not change the diagnosis. An illness caused by norovirus or rotavirus is gastroenteritis, whatever a household chooses to call it.

The label also blurs personal history. Someone who says they "had the flu" after vomiting and diarrhea has most likely had gastroenteritis. That illness reveals little about how influenza itself would affect them.

The line between the two is not perfectly sharp. Vomiting and diarrhea can occur with influenza. They show up more often in children than in adults. Even so, they are not the main features of the disease.

That pattern matters when reading symptoms. A child with influenza may vomit. The vomiting does not turn the illness into gastroenteritis, and the underlying infection is still influenza. An adult whose illness is mainly vomiting and diarrhea, with no respiratory symptoms, fits the profile of gastroenteritis more closely than influenza.

The naming confusion also shapes how people judge the flu shot. The influenza vaccine is made to protect against influenza viruses. It is not designed to prevent gastroenteritis from norovirus or rotavirus. A stomach bug in the weeks after a flu shot is a different infection. It is not a sign that the vaccine failed.

Influenza is caused by influenza A and B viruses, not by chilly air, a winter draft or stepping outside with wet hair. A person develops the flu only after one of those viruses gets into the body.

The myth sticks partly because of timing. In temperate regions, flu activity peaks in fall and winter. Those are also the months when people are most likely to feel cold. Seeing the two together makes the weather look like the cause.

The seasonal peak is tied to other factors. Flu surges in fall and winter are linked to conditions such as indoor crowding and drier air. In colder months, people spend more time inside and closer together. That gives an infected person more contact with others.

Feeling cold and catching influenza are separate events. Someone who walks outside in freezing weather with damp hair, and never encounters the virus, does not come down with the flu. Someone who stays warm indoors but spends time with an infected person can still get sick.

That distinction shapes what helps. A scarf, a hat or a dry head may make a winter day more comfortable. None of them stops an influenza virus from reaching a person who is exposed to it.

Antibiotics do nothing against the flu. These drugs act on bacteria, and influenza is caused by viruses. The medicines made to treat influenza are prescription antivirals, such as oseltamivir and baloxavir.

The gap comes down to the type of germ. Bacteria and viruses are different kinds of microbes. A drug that acts on bacteria has no effect on a virus, no matter how sick the patient feels.

Oseltamivir is sold under the brand name Tamiflu. Baloxavir is sold as Xofluza. The generic and brand names refer to the same drugs. Both target influenza itself, and a doctor has to prescribe them.

Timing shapes how well these antivirals work. They work best when a patient starts them within 48 hours of the first symptoms. That makes an early visit to a doctor more useful than waiting to see whether the illness gets worse.

The 48-hour mark is not a strict cutoff. Doctors may still prescribe an antiviral after that window to high-risk patients. They may also give one to people who are already in the hospital with influenza. In those cases, the decision rests with the treating doctor rather than the calendar.

Antibiotics do have a role in some flu cases. A bacterial complication can develop on top of the viral infection. Bacterial pneumonia is one example. In that situation, a doctor may prescribe an antibiotic.

Even then, the antibiotic treats the bacterial infection. It does not act on the influenza virus that came first. The flu and the complication call for two separate kinds of treatment. The reverse also holds. An antiviral such as oseltamivir targets influenza and will not clear a bacterial pneumonia.

For someone with flu symptoms, an antibiotic will not treat the illness. Asking a doctor whether an antiviral such as Tamiflu or Xofluza fits the case addresses the virus directly.

Being young, fit and rarely sick is not a reason to skip the flu shot. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends a flu vaccine every year for everyone aged six months and older, with few exceptions.

That guidance covers healthy adults on the same terms as everyone else. Good health is not one of the exceptions.

The myth rests on the idea that influenza threatens only people who are already frail. Healthy people can also become severely ill with the flu. A strong baseline of health does not guarantee a mild case.

The shot also matters for the people around a healthy adult. Someone who catches influenza can pass the virus on. Those on the receiving end may include infants, older adults and others whose age or health puts them at higher risk of severe illness.

A healthy adult may live with a baby, visit an older relative or work alongside someone at higher risk. In each case, that adult is one route the virus can take to reach them.

A flu shot from last season is not designed to protect you through this one. Influenza viruses change from year to year. For that reason, the strains included in flu vaccines are reviewed and updated before each new season.

That gradual change is called antigenic drift. As influenza A and B viruses circulate, they accumulate small changes. A vaccine built around earlier versions of the virus may not match the versions spreading now.

The result is a vaccine that is reformulated for each season rather than one product offered unchanged year after year.

The strain review takes place at a global level. The World Health Organization makes separate vaccine recommendations for the Northern Hemisphere and the Southern Hemisphere. In temperate regions, flu activity peaks in fall and winter. Those seasons arrive in different months on either side of the equator. Separate recommendations account for that difference.

Strain change is not the only reason an older shot falls short. Protection from flu vaccination also wanes over time. A shot given a year ago offers less protection now than it did in the weeks after it was given.

Both problems point the same way. A previous season's vaccine may target strains that no longer match the viruses in circulation. The immunity it produced has also faded. That combination is why the flu vaccine is treated as a seasonal shot rather than a one-time dose.

Influenza is not a stronger version of the common cold. The two illnesses are caused by different viruses, and the flu can lead to serious complications such as pneumonia.

Colds are commonly caused by rhinoviruses. The flu is caused by influenza viruses. Because the germs are different, a cold is not an early or mild stage of the flu. A bad cold does not turn into influenza.

The flu tends to start abruptly. Fever, body aches and exhaustion are typical early symptoms, and they often arrive together.

Complications set the two illnesses further apart. Influenza can lead to pneumonia, an infection of the lungs. Some people with the flu become sick enough to need care in a hospital.

Flu vaccine effectiveness varies by season, but the shot still cuts risk. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that vaccination reduces the risk of flu illness by 40% to 60% in the overall population when circulating viruses are well matched to the vaccine.

That range describes a reduction in risk across a population. It compares vaccinated people with unvaccinated people. It does not mean that 40% to 60% of people who get the shot are protected while everyone else gets nothing from it.

The condition attached to the estimate matters. The range applies to seasons when the vaccine is well matched to the viruses that are spreading. In seasons with a weaker match, protection can differ.

The estimate also measures one specific outcome: flu illness. It describes how much vaccination lowers the chance of getting sick with influenza. It is not a promise that any one person will avoid infection.

A vaccinated person who still catches influenza is not evidence that the vaccine failed. One case cannot show what a vaccine did across a whole population. The CDC figure is built to answer a population question, not an individual one.

Preventing illness is not the only benefit. Vaccination can make illness milder in people who still get sick. Someone who catches influenza after vaccination may have a less severe course than they would have had without the shot.

The CDC and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists both recommend flu vaccination during pregnancy. That guidance runs against the myth that expectant mothers should wait until after the baby is born to get the shot.

The first reason concerns the mother. Pregnancy raises the risk of severe influenza. Putting off the vaccine until after delivery leaves her unprotected through the months when that risk applies.

The second reason concerns the baby. Infants younger than six months cannot receive a flu vaccine. Newborns therefore have no vaccine protection of their own during their first months of life.

Vaccination during pregnancy helps close that gap. The mother develops antibodies in response to the shot. Those antibodies are passed on to the baby during pregnancy. They help protect the infant until the child is old enough to be vaccinated.

The recommendation comes from two different kinds of institutions. The CDC is the national public health agency of the U.S. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists is the professional organization for obstetrician-gynecologists, the doctors who provide pregnancy care. Their shared position means public health guidance and specialist medical guidance point the same way.

One flu shot during pregnancy reaches two people at once. It addresses the mother's higher risk of severe flu. It also helps protect a baby who is too young for a vaccine of its own.

An egg allergy does not rule out a flu shot. The CDC advises that people with an egg allergy can receive any licensed, age-appropriate flu vaccine. No extra precautions are needed beyond standard vaccine safety practice.

That guidance covers every licensed flu vaccine, including those produced using eggs. A history of egg allergy is not, on its own, a reason to skip vaccination. Nor is it a reason to hunt for a particular brand.

The guidance places someone with an egg allergy under the same safety practice as any other vaccine recipient. An egg allergy does not determine which licensed vaccine that person can receive.

The age-appropriate part of the guidance still matters. Each flu vaccine is licensed for specific age groups. The right product depends on the age of the person receiving it, not on an egg allergy.

Egg-free options also exist. Flucelvax is a cell-based flu vaccine. Flublok is a recombinant flu vaccine. Neither is made with eggs.

Those products are an alternative, not a requirement. People who would rather avoid egg-based vaccines can ask about one of them, as long as it is licensed for their age group. Under the CDC guidance, a person with an egg allergy who receives an egg-based vaccine is also following the recommendation.

Most flu care eases symptoms: 15 things that help, don't help or need a doctor

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A guide to what helps when you have the flu, from antivirals, rest and fluids to myths like vitamin C and leftover antibiotics, plus signs that need urgent care

Most of what people do for the flu treats how they feel, not the infection itself. Only a short list of prescription antiviral drugs acts directly on the influenza virus. Everything else — rest, fluids, fever reducers, honey, a warm salt water gargle — works on symptoms while the immune system does the rest of the job.

That distinction shapes the choices a sick person makes in the first few days. Some home remedies make the illness easier to get through. Others do nothing against the virus. A few carry real risks, especially for babies, young children and teenagers.

This list sorts out what helps when you have the flu, what doesn't and when home care stops being enough. It draws on established public-health guidance from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the Food and Drug Administration. Where the evidence for a remedy is thin, the list says so instead of filling the gap with claims that cannot be checked.

Timing runs through much of the advice. Antiviral medicines work best when started within 48 hours of the first symptoms. That makes the first two days of illness important. The decision about returning to work or school follows a rule tied to 24 hours without a fever. Some warning signs call for medical care right away, whatever stage the illness has reached.

Common mix-ups add another layer. Influenza is a respiratory illness, yet the label "flu" gets attached to stomach bugs caused by entirely different viruses. Antibiotics act on bacteria, not on the virus behind influenza. Popular supplements carry reputations the evidence does not support. A quick test result can look more conclusive than it is.

The 15 entries that follow cover medicines, home care, common myths, testing, emergency signs and prevention. Each one stands on its own. A reader can jump straight to the question that matters most. The aim is practical: to explain what each option does, what it cannot do and who should avoid it.

Flu returns every season, and many people end up caring for a sick partner, parent or child as well as getting sick themselves. Knowing which measures carry weight and which do not helps avoid wasted effort on remedies that do nothing. For people at higher risk, it can also prevent a dangerous delay in getting help.

Prescription antiviral drugs are the only medicines that act directly on the influenza virus. They work best when started within 48 hours of the first flu symptoms, according to the CDC.

Four antivirals are recommended for the flu in the U.S. Oseltamivir is sold under the brand name Tamiflu. Zanamivir is sold as Relenza. Peramivir, sold as Rapivab, is given intravenously. Baloxavir marboxil is sold as Xofluza.

Each of these drugs requires a prescription. That sets them apart from the pain relievers, cough syrups and home remedies people usually reach for during the flu. Those products can change how a person feels. None of them targets the virus behind the illness.

The benefit of an antiviral is modest. On average, treatment shortens the illness by about a day. That figure describes the typical result across many patients. It is not a promise about how any one person will recover.

Timing drives much of that benefit. The 48-hour window opens when symptoms first appear, not when someone decides to call a doctor. A person who waits several days to see whether the illness passes may miss the period when these drugs work best.

That makes an early call to a doctor, clinic or telehealth service the practical first step. A clinician can judge whether an antiviral makes sense and write the prescription.

The 48-hour mark is not a hard cutoff for everyone. People who are hospitalized with the flu may still receive antivirals after that point. So may people at high risk of serious flu complications. For these patients, the window is a guide rather than a barrier to treatment.

For otherwise healthy adults past the window, the choice to prescribe rests with the clinician. An antiviral also does not replace the rest of flu care. A patient taking one still needs the same rest, fluids and attention to warning signs as anyone else with influenza.

Rest is a core part of treating the flu at home. No over-the-counter product cures influenza. Recovery depends on the body's own immune response, and rest frees up energy for it.

Fatigue is one of the standard symptoms of influenza. The CDC lists tiredness alongside fever or chills, cough, sore throat, a runny or stuffy nose, muscle or body aches and headaches. The agency also notes that not everyone with the flu runs a fever. The exhaustion comes from the illness itself and is an expected part of having the flu.

Flu symptoms tend to come on suddenly. The CDC describes them as usually more intense than the symptoms of a common cold. Colds are generally milder and build more gradually.

Rest covers more than a night in bed. It includes daytime naps, time on the couch and a break from errands, workouts and housework.

No single number of sleep hours fits every patient. Sleep needs vary by age and by person. Fever, chills, coughing and body aches can also break the night into short stretches. Sleeping whenever tiredness hits, day or night, helps make up for that lost rest.

Tiredness in a sick child is expected, but how the child behaves when awake matters. The CDC lists not being alert or interacting when awake among its emergency warning signs in children. A sleepy child who wakes up and responds normally is in a different situation from one who does not.

Rest is supportive care. The CDC notes that most people with the flu have a mild illness and do not need medical care or antiviral drugs.

Rest helps the body recover, but it does not act on the influenza virus the way prescription antivirals do. It also does not prevent complications. Trouble breathing or chest pain calls for medical care, not more sleep.

Drinking plenty of fluids helps prevent the dehydration that the flu can cause. Fever, sweating and sometimes vomiting or diarrhea all drain water from the body and raise that risk.

The flu pulls fluid out through several routes at once. A raised body temperature increases the water lost through the skin. Sweating, which often comes as a fever breaks, adds to the loss. Vomiting and diarrhea remove fluid faster still when they occur alongside influenza.

Plain water is the simplest option. Broth counts too, and it supplies some salt along with the liquid. Oral rehydration drinks are made to replace both water and the minerals lost through sweat, vomiting and diarrhea. They contain a measured balance of salts and sugar that helps the gut absorb fluid. Pharmacies and supermarkets sell them ready to drink and as powders to mix with water.

Small, frequent sips can be easier to manage than large glasses for someone who feels nauseated. A queasy stomach is more likely to keep down a little liquid at a time.

Fluids do not shorten the flu or act on the influenza virus. Their job is to keep the body supplied with water while the immune system clears the infection.

Urine is one of the clearest everyday signals of hydration. Dark yellow urine is a common sign that a person needs to drink more. Passing very little urine is a warning sign that the body is running short of fluid. Not urinating at all appears on the CDC's list of flu emergency warning signs. A person in that situation needs medical care, not more home treatment.

Children with a fever may drink less than usual, especially if their throat hurts or they feel sick. Parents unsure whether a child is getting enough fluid can call a pediatrician or their family doctor for advice.

Acetaminophen and ibuprofen can bring down a flu fever and dull the muscle aches and headache that come with it. Neither drug acts on the influenza virus. They make the illness easier to get through, but they do not make it shorter.

The two medicines belong to different drug classes. Ibuprofen is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, or NSAID, sold in the U.S. under brand names such as Advil and Motrin. Acetaminophen is not an NSAID. It is best known in the U.S. as Tylenol. In the U.K., Europe and much of the rest of the world, the same drug is called paracetamol.

That naming difference matters for travelers and international households. A box labeled paracetamol holds the same active ingredient as one labeled acetaminophen. Taking one product of each means taking a double dose of a single drug.

The dose printed on the package is the one to follow. That covers the amount per dose, the time between doses and the maximum allowed in a day. Children's versions come in different strengths from adult tablets. Each should be measured with the device packaged with that product.

The larger hazard with acetaminophen often hides in products people don't think of as pain relievers. Many multi-symptom cold and flu remedies, including nighttime formulas, combine acetaminophen with a decongestant or cough suppressant. Adding a separate acetaminophen tablet on top of one of these can push the total past the label limit without the person realizing it.

Too much acetaminophen can cause serious liver damage. The FDA requires a liver warning on over-the-counter acetaminophen labels. In the U.S., every over-the-counter medicine lists its active ingredients on the Drug Facts panel. Checking that panel before adding a second product is a direct way to avoid stacking. Some prescription pain medicines contain acetaminophen as well, sometimes abbreviated as APAP on the pharmacy label.

Ibuprofen carries its own cautions. NSAIDs can irritate the stomach and, in some people, cause stomach bleeding or affect the kidneys. People with ulcers, kidney disease or heart conditions should check with a doctor or pharmacist before taking it. The same applies to anyone on blood thinners.

Children and teenagers who have the flu should not be given aspirin, and the caution continues while they recover. The reason is Reye's syndrome, a rare but serious condition that affects the liver and the brain.

Reye's syndrome causes swelling in the liver and the brain. It can be fatal. Early signs can include persistent vomiting, confusion and listlessness. Chickenpox, another viral illness, is also linked to the condition.

The advice covers the recovery period as well as the worst days of the illness. A child or teenager who seems to be getting better still falls under it. Improving symptoms do not mean aspirin has become safe to use.

Aspirin's chemical name is acetylsalicylic acid, and it belongs to a group of drugs called salicylates. Some other medicines also contain salicylates, and the same caution applies to them. Bismuth subsalicylate, the active ingredient in Pepto-Bismol, is one example. A product does not need the word aspirin on its front label to fall under this advice.

For that reason, the ingredient list deserves a close read before any medicine goes to a sick child or teenager. Parents and caregivers can scan the active ingredients for aspirin or another salicylate. A pharmacist can confirm whether a specific product contains one.

In the U.S., aspirin packaging carries a label warning about Reye's syndrome in children and teenagers. The label tells them not to use the product if they have or are recovering from flu-like symptoms or chickenpox.

The warning is aimed at children and teenagers. It does not set out a separate rule for adults with the flu. Adults with questions about aspirin and their own health conditions can raise them with a doctor or pharmacist.

Families looking for a safe way to manage a young person's flu symptoms can ask a pediatrician which medicines suit the child's age. That conversation also covers any other products already in the medicine cabinet.

Honey can soothe a cough in older children and adults who have the flu, but it should never be given to a baby younger than 12 months. In infants, honey carries a risk of botulism, a rare but serious illness.

Coughing is among the most common flu symptoms. Influenza infects the nose, throat and lungs, and irritated airways can keep a cough going. A spoonful of honey coats the throat and can calm that irritation for a time. It can be taken straight from the spoon or stirred into warm water or tea.

Honey is a comfort measure. It does not act on the influenza virus, and it does not shorten the illness. It eases a symptom that often breaks up sleep, both for the sick person and for anyone sharing the room.

The age limit is firm. Honey can contain spores of Clostridium botulinum, the bacterium that makes botulinum toxin. In older children and adults, a mature digestive system usually stops those spores from growing. A baby's gut is not developed enough to do the same. The spores can germinate in an infant's intestine and release toxin there. That is infant botulism.

The toxin attacks the nerves that control muscles. Early signs in a baby can include constipation, poor feeding, a weak cry and reduced muscle tone that makes the infant seem floppy. Infant botulism requires hospital treatment.

The caution covers more than a spoonful from the jar. Both raw and processed honey can carry the spores, and ordinary heating does not reliably destroy them. Honey also turns up as an ingredient in some foods and drinks. Parents of infants should check product labels before giving those foods to a baby.

For a baby under 12 months who has the flu and a cough, the safer step is a call to a pediatrician. Infants face a higher risk of flu complications than older children. A doctor can judge whether care beyond comfort measures is needed.

After the first birthday, honey becomes an option for a coughing child. Honey is mostly sugar. It raises blood sugar like other sweeteners. People with diabetes should count it the way they would any other sweetener.

A warm salt water gargle or a throat lozenge can make a sore throat from the flu feel better for a while. Neither one shortens the illness or acts on the influenza virus.

Sore throat is one of the standard symptoms of influenza, along with fever, cough and body aches. It can make swallowing food and drinks uncomfortable. That discomfort is what these two measures target.

Influenza infects the nose, the throat and sometimes the lungs. A gargle touches only part of the throat. It cannot reach most of the places where the virus is active.

A salt water gargle is a comfort step, not a treatment. The water is held in the throat, gargled and then spit out rather than swallowed. Evidence that it does more than soothe the throat is limited. Any value lies in how the throat feels afterward, not in an effect on the infection.

Lozenges work in a similar way. Sucking on one keeps the throat moist and can ease the soreness for a time. Some lozenges contain medicated ingredients, such as menthol, which produces a cooling sensation. Their labels should be read and followed like those of any other over-the-counter product.

Lozenges carry a specific risk for young children. They are small and hard, and a child can inhale one by accident. That makes them a choking hazard. Parents of young children can ask a pediatrician about safer ways to ease a sore throat.

A gargle or a lozenge will not prevent flu complications. Their role is limited to easing one symptom while the infection runs its course.

Stay home from work, school and other shared spaces until your symptoms are improving overall and you have gone at least 24 hours without a fever. That 24-hour count only applies if no fever-reducing medicine was used during it.

This is the return-to-normal-activities standard in current CDC respiratory-virus guidance. The guidance covers respiratory viruses as a group. The same rule applies to influenza and to other common respiratory infections, such as COVID-19 and RSV. It has two conditions, and both must be met before going back out.

The first condition is about symptoms. The wording asks for overall improvement, not a complete recovery. What counts is the direction the illness is moving in. A person whose symptoms are getting worse, or holding steady at their peak, has not met it.

The second condition is about fever. Acetaminophen and ibuprofen both lower body temperature. A normal thermometer reading taken while one of them is still active can hide a fever the infection is still producing. Measuring the 24 hours without those drugs means the reading reflects the body, not the medicine.

A fever that comes back resets the clock. The guidance also covers relapses after a return. Anyone whose fever reappears or whose symptoms get worse after going back to normal activities should stay home again until both conditions are met once more.

Meeting both conditions does not mean the risk to other people has disappeared. The guidance pairs the return with a set of protective habits for that reason. Wearing a mask around others indoors helps catch the droplets that coughs and sneezes send into the air. Washing hands often with soap and water limits what passes along through doorknobs, keyboards and shared surfaces. Cough etiquette means covering a cough or sneeze with a tissue or the inside of the elbow, then throwing the tissue away and washing up.

These steps work alongside the stay-home rule rather than replacing it.

Antibiotics do not work against influenza. The flu is caused by a virus, and antibiotics act only on bacteria. Taking them for an uncomplicated case of the flu will not shorten the illness or ease its symptoms.

The mismatch comes down to biology. Bacteria are single-celled organisms with their own cell walls and their own machinery for making proteins. Antibiotics are built to attack structures like these. Penicillin-type drugs, for example, interfere with the way bacteria build their cell walls.

Viruses have no cell wall. An influenza virus is a far simpler particle that enters human cells and uses them to make copies of itself. An antibiotic finds nothing in the virus to target. The medicines that do act on influenza belong to a separate class of prescription antivirals.

Antibiotics still have a role in some flu cases. The flu can leave the body open to a secondary bacterial infection. Bacterial pneumonia is among the most serious of these. Sinus infections and ear infections can also follow a bout of flu. When a doctor diagnoses one of these complications, an antibiotic treats that bacterial infection. It does not treat the flu underneath it.

That call belongs to a clinician who has examined the patient. Thick or colored mucus is common in viral illnesses. On its own, it is not proof of a bacterial infection.

Leftover antibiotics from an earlier illness should not be taken for the flu. A bottle in the medicine cabinet may hold the wrong drug for a new infection. It may also hold too few doses to make up a full course. For the flu alone, it will not help at all.

Unneeded antibiotics carry their own costs. They can cause side effects such as diarrhea, rashes and allergic reactions. Each course also gives bacteria a chance to adapt. Overuse drives antibiotic resistance. Resistant infections are harder to treat when an antibiotic is actually needed. Resistant bacteria can also spread from person to person.

Vitamin C, zinc and echinacea have not been shown to cure influenza. They are popular flu-season purchases, but none of them acts directly on the virus the way a prescription antiviral drug does.

Vitamin C, the nutrient also known as ascorbic acid, is the best known of the three. Humans cannot make it and must get it from food. Citrus fruits, peppers, strawberries and broccoli are rich sources. Vitamin C has not been shown to prevent colds in the general population. Taking it once flu symptoms appear has no proven record of curing the illness.

Zinc is a mineral the body needs for normal immune function. Oysters, red meat, beans and nuts supply it in the diet. Evidence on zinc for influenza specifically is thin or mixed. Most zinc lozenges, gels and nasal sprays on pharmacy shelves are marketed as cold remedies rather than flu treatments.

Echinacea is a group of flowering plants in the daisy family that are native to North America. Extracts from their roots, leaves and flowers are sold as teas, tablets, capsules and liquid tinctures. Products differ in which species and plant parts they use. Two echinacea products can therefore contain quite different ingredients. Research on echinacea for the flu has not produced consistent results. People allergic to related plants, such as ragweed, can have allergic reactions to it.

In the U.S., all three are sold as dietary supplements. The FDA does not approve dietary supplements for safety or effectiveness before they go on sale. Federal rules also bar supplement makers from claiming that a product cures, treats or prevents a disease such as influenza. Labels instead tend to use broader phrases such as immune support.

Supplements are not automatically harmless. High doses of vitamin C can cause diarrhea and stomach upset. Zinc lozenges can cause nausea and a lingering bad taste. Some supplements also interact with prescription drugs. People who take regular medication can check with a pharmacist before adding one while sick.

The FDA advises parents not to give over-the-counter cough and cold products to children younger than two. For a baby or toddler with the flu, these remedies are off the table. A pediatrician is the right person to ask about any medicine for a child that young.

The advice covers a broad shelf of products. Over-the-counter cough and cold medicines often contain one or more of several ingredient types. These include decongestants such as pseudoephedrine and phenylephrine, antihistamines such as diphenhydramine, the cough suppressant dextromethorphan and the expectorant guaifenesin.

Many products combine several of these in a single bottle. A parent who reaches for a familiar brand may be giving a child several active drugs at once. The Drug Facts label on U.S. packaging lists each active ingredient and what it is meant to do.

The FDA has warned that these medicines can cause serious and potentially life-threatening side effects in very young children. Infants and toddlers process drugs differently from adults. A dose that looks small can still be too much for a child under two.

None of these ingredients acts on the influenza virus. At most, they ease symptoms such as a stuffy nose or a dry cough. They do not shorten the illness or prevent its complications.

Adult products should never be split or measured out in smaller amounts for a young child. Adult formulas are not labeled with dosing for small children. Guessing at a fraction of an adult dose is not a substitute for a clinician's advice.

Children older than two fall outside the FDA cutoff, but that does not make every product suitable for them. Parents of preschoolers and school-age children should check with a pediatrician before giving any cough and cold product. A pharmacist can also help read a label and spot overlapping ingredients. If a doctor approves a liquid medicine, the FDA advises measuring it with the dosing device packaged with the product rather than a kitchen spoon.

"Stomach flu" is a common name, not a medical diagnosis, and it does not refer to influenza. The illness most people mean is gastroenteritis, an inflammation of the stomach and intestines usually caused by other viruses, such as norovirus.

Influenza is a respiratory illness. The virus infects the nose, throat and lungs. Its typical symptoms include fever, cough, sore throat, a runny or stuffy nose, muscle or body aches, headache and fatigue. They often come on suddenly.

Gastroenteritis looks different. Its main features are nausea, vomiting, watery diarrhea and stomach cramps. Some people also run a mild fever or feel achy, but the illness centers on the gut rather than the airways.

The overlap is where confusion starts. Vomiting and diarrhea can occur with influenza, and they show up more often in children than in adults. Even then, they appear alongside the respiratory symptoms rather than replacing them. A child with flu who vomits will usually also have a fever and a cough.

Norovirus is the best-known cause of gastroenteritis. It spreads easily through contaminated food and water, contaminated surfaces and close contact with a sick person. Outbreaks are common where people share space and meals, such as cruise ships, schools and nursing homes. In the U.K., the NHS calls it the winter vomiting bug.

Other germs cause similar illness. Rotavirus is a leading viral cause in young children, and many countries vaccinate infants against it. Bacteria and parasites can also inflame the stomach and intestines, often through contaminated food or water.

The distinction changes what helps. Flu antivirals such as oseltamivir target the influenza virus specifically, and they do nothing against norovirus or rotavirus. The yearly flu shot is built around influenza strains. It offers no protection against the viruses behind stomach bugs.

Seasonal timing adds to the mix-up. In the Northern Hemisphere, both influenza and norovirus circulate most heavily in the colder months. A household can catch both in the same winter, one after the other.

A negative result on a rapid flu test does not prove you are free of influenza. Rapid antigen tests are fast but less sensitive than molecular tests, and they can miss an infection that is present.

The two kinds of test look for different things. A rapid antigen test detects proteins from the influenza virus in a sample. The sample is usually collected with a swab from the nose or throat. A molecular test, often called a PCR-type test, looks for the virus's genetic material instead.

This difference explains the gap in sensitivity. Molecular methods copy viral genetic material many times over. That lets them pick up smaller amounts of virus. An antigen test has no copying step. If a sample carries only a little viral protein, the test can come back negative even though the person is infected.

Speed is the main advantage of the antigen approach. Results arrive within minutes, often during the same clinic visit. Molecular tests generally take longer, although some faster molecular versions are available.

A negative rapid result is weaker evidence than a positive one. A positive rapid test supports a flu diagnosis. A negative one leaves the question open, especially when the symptoms fit the flu. A clinician may follow up with a molecular test. A clinician may also judge the case on the symptoms and on whether flu is circulating locally.

Testing carries the most weight for people at high risk of complications. For them, a result can shape whether a doctor prescribes an antiviral drug. Antivirals tend to work best when started early in the illness. A false negative that delays that decision can matter.

For otherwise healthy adults with mild illness, a test result changes less. Home care looks much the same either way. Rest, fluids and staying away from others while feverish apply whether or not a swab confirms the virus.

Some flu symptoms mean home care is no longer enough. The CDC lists emergency warning signs in adults that call for prompt medical attention. They include trouble breathing, ongoing chest pain, persistent dizziness, confusion and not urinating.

Difficulty breathing or shortness of breath is one of the most serious signs. Influenza infects the airways and lungs. Breathlessness can mean the infection has moved deeper or that pneumonia has developed.

Pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen that does not go away is another red flag. So is dizziness that persists. Confusion, or difficulty waking someone up, is also on the list. A person who cannot think clearly may not be able to judge how sick they are. Someone else in the household may need to make the call.

Seizures are on the CDC list too. Other signs on the list are less dramatic but still serious. They include severe muscle pain and severe weakness or unsteadiness. A chronic medical condition that gets worse during the flu is another warning sign.

One pattern is easy to miss. Symptoms sometimes improve for a while, then return with a worse fever or cough. The CDC includes that rebound on its warning list. It can signal a secondary infection, such as bacterial pneumonia, on top of the flu. A second downturn after an apparent recovery is a reason to see a clinician.

Risk is not spread evenly. The CDC identifies groups more likely to develop serious flu complications. They include adults 65 and older and children younger than five, especially those younger than two. Pregnant people are in this group too. So are people with chronic medical conditions such as asthma, diabetes and heart disease.

The CDC advises people in these higher-risk groups to contact a health care provider early when flu symptoms appear. Early contact gives a clinician the chance to decide on treatment while it can still help.

Children can show warning signs differently from adults, and the CDC publishes a separate list for them. Parents who are unsure should call a pediatrician. In the U.S., anyone with severe breathing trouble, chest pain or a seizure should call 911 or go to an emergency room.

A yearly flu vaccine is the main way to reduce the chance of a serious case of influenza. The CDC recommends annual vaccination for nearly everyone aged six months and older.

The shot does not guarantee that a person will avoid the flu. Some vaccinated people still catch the virus. Its main benefit is lowering the risk that an infection turns into severe illness.

Vaccination is needed every year. Influenza viruses change from one season to the next. The vaccine's makeup is updated to match the strains expected to circulate. Protection from a previous year's shot also fades over time. A dose from last fall is not a substitute for this season's vaccine.

A common worry is that the flu shot can give you the flu. It cannot. The injected vaccines contain either inactivated virus or recombinant material. Inactivated vaccines use virus that has been killed. Recombinant vaccines are built around a flu protein made in a lab, without live virus. Neither type can cause an influenza infection.

Some people feel unwell for a short time after the shot. Soreness, redness or swelling where the needle went in are typical. A low-grade fever, headache or muscle aches can also follow. These are signs of the immune system responding to the vaccine. They are not signs of influenza.

The nasal spray vaccine works differently. It contains a weakened form of live flu virus rather than an inactivated one. It is not known to cause the flu in healthy people who receive it. The spray is not suitable for everyone. A doctor or pharmacist can say which version fits a given person.

The vaccine is a preventive tool, not a treatment. It does not cure a flu infection that has already started. Someone who is already sick with influenza is managed with rest, fluids and, where a doctor advises, prescription antivirals. Each season's vaccine is designed to cover more than one flu strain. Getting the shot after recovering can still protect against other strains that circulate later in the season.

On Jill and Peter Kraus’s almost 400-acre property in Dutchess County, New York, the rolling hills add to the serenity and also create drama. Standing in front of any one of the 18 commissioned outdoor sculptures here, you can’t see any of the others. Sabine Hornig’s 2010 concrete work, Entrance With Floating Stairs II, for example, stands atop a hill. “What I love about this piece is the siting of it,” Jill Kraus told me as we pulled up to the work on a Polaris utility terrain vehicle this past May. “You come across it, and as you drive toward it, it starts to disappear—and then it comes back. I think this is the most perfectly sited piece we have. For the past two decades, the Krauses have worked with artists to conceive of site-specific artworks for the expansive property. “Most people who collect outdoor sculpture just buy and plop it—they have ‘plop art,’” Jill said. “People are surprised when they come here because they think that’s what we’ve done. They’re always shocked at how we work.”

To see all of the outdoor commissions, from a Roxy Paine tree to a set of colorful abstract sculptures by Franz West, plus the Art Barn, which is like a mini museum, requires about six hours, with at least two of those having to be well past dusk to accommodate three video projection pieces. The works differ greatly in scale, materials, and approach, but the connective tissue between them all, Jill said, is “they’re all dealing with this place.”

The tour typically begins with Sol LeWitt’s Dome Structure (Four Domes and A Sphere), his only spherical work ever realized. The concrete sculpture, which stands atop a 10-foot foundation so it doesn’t slide down the hill, holds a tender spot for the Krauses, whose children were friends with LeWitt’s children at boarding school; the couple asked the artist to make them a work before his death in 2007. In the two decades since, the piece has weathered the elements, though not without fissures.

When commissioning an artist to make a new work, Jill opts for a collaborative approach, which she attributed to having gone to art school for both her BFA and MFA, and working as a design director for 20 years. “I like to discuss ideas and push artists out of their comfort zone to get them to think about things that they might not normally think about,” she said. “We want them to look at the natural habitat and then react to it.” The couple doesn’t work with an adviser, and instead sources any engineers or fabricators needed to realize a project themselves. “It’s all about talking things through. We work through ideas, and I think that’s what makes what we’re doing very different from what most other collectors do.”

Their one requirement is that an artist visit at least twice, “once when the leaves are on the trees, and once when the leaves are off the trees,” Jill said. “Then we say to them, ‘You can drive all over the property. We’ll show you every square inch, and you find a spot where you want to work.’”

One of the Krauses’ most collaborative pieces is Between sky and earth by Tatiana Trouvé, which was first commissioned in 2012 and has been adjusted at least yearly ever since. “I warned them that it was going to take time, and that this slowness was part of my work,” Trouvé told me. “They said, ‘We have all the time in the world!’”

The work imagines the Guardian, a hermit who lives atop a wooded mound about 65 feet in diameter, situated in a clearing on the property. He’s not in at the moment, but you can peruse his belongings—books, chairs, a patent leather shoe, suitcases, blankets, a rolled-up cot, paint cans, a collection of ropes—all fabricated in concrete, copper, or bronze. The work is in a constant state of flux, in dialogue with its natural environs and the seasons. “We’re trying to decide, how much do we let nature take over the piece?” Jill said, noting that moss has begun to grow over parts of it, while some elements have had to be moved after proving too heavy for the branches they once hung from. “It’s not an object,” Jill added. “It’s a world.”

“Jill and Peter have that touch of madness that belongs only to passionate people driven by their deep love for artworks and artists,” Trouvé said. “Alongside art and artists, they live an adventure built on sincerity, audacity, and determination.”

The Krauses started collecting art in 1980, their first purchase being a print by Pierre Alechinsky, a birthday present for Jill, shortly after they married. Works on paper would be their focus for the next several years because that was all they could afford at the time. “What we really liked was sculpture, but that’s hard to collect when you live in a New York City apartment,” Jill said. They put off buying a summer home for a few decades until finally they decided that if they bought a substantial piece of land, they could commission artists to make site-responsive sculptures there. They began searching within a two-hour radius of Manhattan, at first thinking that Connecticut might be right.

“What we didn’t realize is that the difference in the geography from Connecticut to New York is huge,” she said. “A glacier came down the Hudson River Valley and took the tops off the mountains, so everything is rolling hills on the New York side. The Connecticut side is very tight, peak to valley, so there are no big, expansive views. The difference visually is huge.” In 2001, their broker recommended they see a piece of land that wasn’t on the market, with rolling hills where the previous owner, actor James Cagney, grazed his long-horned cattle. “We came to look at it, and we said, ‘Absolutely, this is it,’” Jill recalled.

The Krauses are unlike their neighbors, many of whom own this much land so they can ride horses or hunt. After buying the property, when asked if they were interested in either of those, Jill replied with a twist on agriculture: “No, we’re growing art.” Their modestly scaled modernist house, nestled in a secluded spot, is also an anomaly up here: They were able to persuade the Dutchess Land Conservancy that such a structure had “historical precedent”—a requirement of their purchase agreement—because Harvard Five architects John M. Johansen and Marcel Breuer both had connections to the area.

The brightly colored walls in the home are filled with the Krauses’ collection, including works by Tacita Dean, Raymond Saunders, Rachel Harrison, Richard Tuttle, Rudolf Stingel, and Franz Ackermann, the latter a commission. Upstairs is To Count: Intransitive (1972/2009), a site-specific piece by Mel Bochner, who inscribed 10 windows with hundreds of numbers, starting with 0, in different arrangements. In the late afternoon, the setting sun sends their shadows onto the floor and walls.

On the other side of the property is the Art Barn, which holds a rotating display of artworks. When I visited, there were pieces by Meriem Bennani, Alvaro Barrington, Lauren Halsey, and Helen Marten on view. In one corner was Ericka Beckman’s mesmerizing video Cinderella (1986), with an original set piece installed nearby; around another corner was Alex Da Corte’s video Rubber Pencil Devil (2018), which they saw when it debuted at the Carnegie International that year. “When it was time to install it, I said, ‘I can’t put this in a white box—it won’t work,’” Jill recalled. Peter suggested calling the artist for his thoughts, to which Jill replied, “I’d rather ask for forgiveness than for permission.” She painted the walls yellow to match the playful spirit of the work and added her colorful dining chairs.

If there is one thing that characterizes the Krauses’ approach to collecting, it is the sheer investment of time. Due to a tricky structural issue, their home took eight years to complete; but that’s nothing compared to the outdoor sculptures. Matthew Monahan’s Second Nature (2023) took around 14 years, though the first eight or so of those involved the Krauses trying to convince the artist to take on the commission. It’s not unusual for that stage to take some time; for example, at the moment, they are in the ideation process with Max Hooper Schneider and Meriem Bennani. “Jill and Peter are aware of the conventional solutions that artists use to get the job done,” Monahan told me by email, “and I suspect they work to positively sabotage these conventions by selecting artists with little or no experience in outdoor work, and by insisting on site specificity.”

Monahan was intrigued by the Krauses’ aversion to “plop art.” “They invited me to pick the site. There was no deadline and no discussion of cost,” he said. He spent several months considering where he would place his piece, ultimately choosing one area, only to find out that Trouvé had already gotten dibs on it. He then submitted a proposal for a plot of land about 200 yards from the main house, but on a subsequent visit, Peter drove him out to yet another site, one with a slope, hoping that Monahan would agree to that instead.

Alongside Peter, Monahan began to rethink the modeling of his sculpture, with artist and patron determining the distance and grade of the slope by walking away from each other. The resulting artwork is double the size of Monahan’s original model: a 6-foot-high, 50-foot-long wall, the front half mostly buried in the ground, the back half topped off with a 6-foot-tall sculpture of a figure that has been cut up and then welded back together. The wall, constructed to resemble chicken wire, has been filled with rocks from the property as well as other remnants of the figure (a hand here, half a nose there). A true sculpture in the round, the work shifts as you circle it: A black metal screen bisects the wall, hiding the site’s slope and its dwarfing scale when seen from downhill.

“The sloping site made the work better, [creating] a dynamic viewing experience that has to be walked through [and] around, so that at one end the viewer is standing face-to-face with the figures, and at the other end, the figures stand high above them,” Monahan said. He sees the Krauses as “friends and collaborators,” adding that “the years were not spent on aspiring to vertical grandeur but on a complex, layered negotiation with each other and nature.”

Negotiating with nature is a hallmark of the works the Krauses have commissioned. Michael Van Valkenburgh, the property’s landscape architect, introduced the Krauses to a student of his, Jon Piasecki, in Harvard’s Graduate School of Design. On Piasecki’s first visit to the property, he ran into a forested area and called out that he’d found the remnants of an old stone wall, assembled haphazardly back when farmers cleared the field. He told Peter that he wanted to build a path along the wall. Soon, using stones from nearby Connecticut, Piasecki began laying out the almost unnoticeable pathway—beginning atop the wall, continuing inside the wall, emerging atop the wall—in the Fibonacci sequence. Working with only a hammer and chisel, he spent three years, from April to November, on the project, finishing in 2009. “Everything here is placed here for a reason,” Jill said. “At one point, I was walking through while he was working, and I said, ‘Look, a snail.’ And he replied, ‘I know. I put it there.’”

Further along in the woods is a Jose Dávila sculpture fabricated to look like cut-out sections of a blue shipping container—“a riff on if a Donald Judd stack had fallen over,” Jill joked. Beyond that is a labyrinth-like installation of mirrors by Jeppe Hein that reflects the forest back onto itself. In a nearby field is a series of sculptures by Katja Strunz that imagines the spreading of a mother dandelion’s seeds. A towering piece by Monika Sosnowska is, as Jill put it, “a staircase that has collapsed and has become a tree in the forest.”

After dusk, Jill took me to see the three video installations. The late Ján Mančuška’s short film The Invisible Exists (2009) was his only animation work, an enigmatic story rendered in ballpoint pen. Tony Oursler’s installation, titled +/- (2023), sends three projections onto different tree lines and relays an idiosyncratic history of the Hudson River Valley, where the artist grew up, bringing in everything from technological advances like the telegraph and early filmmaking to references to West Point, IBM, and the cultish religion of the Oneida Community.

But the tour de force is Doug Aitken’s Lighthouse (2012). Aitken visited before the house was built. “It really struck me—the power of the landscape,” Aitken told me, recalling that he told the Krauses, “If you ever do build something here, I think we should make an artwork that makes it disappear, because this impression that we’re having today is so profound.”

Aitken visited several times over the course of a year—in all four seasons and at all hours of the day—to capture video footage of the natural landscape. Lighthouse projects those images onto the house. “We found all these moments of the changing entropy of the landscape: the melting snow, the sun moving across the forest,” the artist said. In the resulting piece, “as day turns into night, the structure turns into this image of the landscape around it.”

“It was profound finding collaborators who were willing to take that journey,” Aitken said. “As far as collectors go, it’s so important to see things beyond the [object], and instead look to actually push where art can go, what it can be, and what forms it can transform into. They really believe in the true potential of art—the idea of art as an agent of change. They’re open to challenging works, provocative ideas, and works that are outside the box. We need more of that.”

Oh, and it's basically two games in one. On the main menu, you can pick between Warhammer's dark sci-fi 40,000 setting, and its high fantasy Age of Sigmar setting. Each comes with its own character roster, maps, enemy factions, objectives, upgrades, and everything else, making for a seriously robust package.

Gameplay-wise, the title offers a slight twist on the absurdly addictive Survivors structure. Stages have a 25-minute time limit instead of 30, and I'd say there's a larger emphasis on tactical thinking.

That's because specialised opponents are more prevalent, forcing you to weave your way through the hordes in a more methodical manner. At least, until you rack up so many super-powered upgrades that anything in your path just evaporates anyway.

Yep, that same progression curve that makes Vampire Survivors so insanely moreish is alive and well here, although I do think it takes a little longer to hit those truly chaotic highs because of the aforementioned tweaks.

All in all, Warhammer Survivors perfectly melds Games Workshop's property with poncle's timeless formula, resulting in another exceptional roguelike experience.

No one knows how to dress for work anymore—even executives

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Companies spent years dragging employees back to the office, but forgot to tell them what to wear. The result is confusion stretching from junior employees to the executive suite.

One of Grant Alexander’s longtime clients is the COO of a big private company. He recently lamented that a few of his vice presidents had asked him what they should wear to an event.

“He looked at me, and he’s like, ‘When was the last time a VP had to ask the COO what they should be wearing for something?’” Alexander, the founder of Grant Alexander Studio, an executive image and styling consultancy, tells Fast Company. “A lot of the executives I work with have never talked to their employees about this.” 

When the COVID-19 pandemic began, people still wore their office attire for the first few weeks. But as the lockdowns went on, smart trousers were replaced with sweatpants, and shirts were put away to make room for T-shirts and hoodies.

“Now there’s a gap between what people believe they should be wearing at the office, and what their wardrobe actually has become,” says Alexander. “So they’re just like, ‘I don’t know what I should be wearing right now’.”

The uncertainty is far reaching. A 2025 survey of 1,000 hybrid workers in the U.S. by International Workplace Group (IWG) found that workers reported feeling stressed or anxious about what to wear to the office around seven times a month. Almost half (46%) said this happened at least weekly, while 14% experienced it daily. 

Zoom dressing is no longer an option

Samantha Harman, a former newspaper editor turned personal stylist, tells Fast Company that knowing what to wear to the office has always been complicated. But return to office (RTO) has made it even harder, with so much ambiguity around what’s expected. 

For people whose work wardrobes have been neglected for the past few years, remembering and updating their personal style can be a daunting prospect.

“I am working with quite a few people who’ve got return to office mandates for the New Year, and they’re now looking at their wardrobe thinking, ‘I couldn’t go into the office like this’,” Harman says. “It’s all very well ‘top dressing,’ or Zoom dressing—where you are presenting yourself in a certain way on screen, but on the bottom you’ve got your pajamas on. But you can’t do that face-to-face.”

In one recent conversation, a manager told Harman that people either came to the office looking like they’d just rolled out of bed, or were so put together they could have been going to the opera. When she asked if he’d spoken to them about it, he replied: “Are you kidding? I don’t even know what the rules or the guidelines are.”

The problem is compounded by the fact that workplace dress codes haven’t always kept pace with changing expectations, Harman says. Companies continue to rely on generic terms such as “business casual” without explaining what they mean in practice. 

The confusion also can add to workers’ mental load. Harman recently overheard two men debating whether or not an invitation specifying “smart casual” meant they needed to wear a tie. “You’re giving your employees more work to do,” she says.

Clarity is particularly important for juniors

Francesca O’Connor, cofounder of the workplace culture, leadership, and communications consultancy HappyHQ, tells Fast Company that leaders need to explain what dressing appropriately actually means for their specific organization. They also must distinguish between expectations for an ordinary office day and something that requires stepping it up, such as a client pitch.

“That doesn’t mean everyone needs to wear a suit,” she says. “It means having the judgment to understand your audience and what the situation calls for.”

This is particularly important for younger workers entering the office for the first time, who may have had little opportunity to observe traditional workplace style.

“If there’s an expectation that people dress differently for certain occasions, explain it,” O’Connor says. “Don’t leave them guessing, particularly when they’re starting out in their careers.”

In the IWG survey, 94% of Gen Z respondents said they wanted guidance on what to wear to work, compared with 84% of millennials, 70% of Gen X and 61% of baby boomers. 

“Gen Z entering the workforce have no idea what to wear and nobody’s talking about it,” Alexander says. “So they’re just like, ‘OK, I have joggers. I’m just going to wear them.’ And it usually takes them getting yelled at and something happening and somebody correcting them.”

Guidelines can help build confidence

Beyond avoiding awkward conversations, clearer expectations could help employees feel more confident at work, Harman says. 

“If you are wearing something that makes you feel really good, you will behave in a different way than if you’re wearing something that makes you feel a bit like a potato,” she says.

The best way for leaders to have this conversation with their employees is to get their buy-in, Harman adds, by asking what they actually love to wear and what makes them feel confident.

“Even if you just host some workshops or forums where you have people talking about this stuff, [it] can be really helpful,” Harman says. “People often tell me, ‘this is not ever something that we ever speak about at work, and yet it affects me every single day—because every single day, the one thing that I have to do is get dressed’.”

Alexander believes that focusing heavily on getting people back to the office without considering how they present themselves is shortsighted. It’s also easily fixed, he says, with a simple email or Slack update.

“You can say, ‘Hey, a few people have asked us lately about what we should wear.’ Nobody’s going to question it,” he says. “Executives can make it easier on the entire organization by just talking about it.”

That’s if the executives themselves know what protocol should be, of course.
