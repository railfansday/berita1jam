powerhouse parramatta’s steel-latticed museum opens with art by es devlin and james turrell

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

behind its intricate white exoskeleton, the sydney museum welcomes its first visitors with an ambitious program of exhibitions.

Powerhouse Parramatta, the new museum designed by Moreau Kusunoki and Genton in Western Sydney, Australia, will officially open to the public on November 7th, 2026. Following years of construction along the Parramatta River, the monumental cultural building, with its lattice facade, is ready to welcome its first visitors, with five inaugural exhibitions including immersive installations by Es Devlin and James Turrell.

The opening marks a significant moment for Western Sydney, bringing the region its first state cultural institution and the largest museum ever built in New South Wales. Spanning more than 18,000 square meters of public and exhibition space, the building introduces a new home for the Powerhouse’s collections of art, design, science, and technology. More than 3,000 objects will be presented across the opening exhibitions, with one designed by OMA/AMO.

The museum‘s distinctive steel exoskeleton has already established a striking presence along the Parramatta River. Designed by French and Japanese architecture studio Moreau Kusunoki with Australian practice Genton, the building comprises two large volumes enclosed by a white structural lattice. Its outward-facing framework allows for expansive, column-free interiors, giving the museum flexibility to accommodate installations of varying scales.

Beyond the galleries, the project introduces new public spaces along the waterfront, including a landscaped square connecting the museum to the river. A rooftop garden, observatory, and spaces for educational programming extend its use beyond conventional exhibition halls. With the building now complete, attention turns to the artists and collections that will occupy its vast interiors.

Among the opening presentations is The Turning, the first major Australian exhibition by British artist Es Devlin. Known for her large-scale installations and stage designs, Devlin brings together five works exploring the relationships between human perception, collective memory, and the natural world.

The exhibition includes Forest of Us, a mirrored labyrinth that draws parallels between the branching structures of human lungs and trees, and Memory Palace, a three-dimensional landscape tracing moments that have shaped human understanding across 75,000 years. In Collective Portrait, visitors are invited to sit opposite strangers and draw one another, contributing to an evolving installation. Other works explore drawing and the biodiversity of the Sydney region, inviting audiences to take an active role in the exhibition.

American artist James Turrell also contributes to the opening program with Shangri La (Over The Hump), a monumental light installation presented as part of Task Eternal, an exhibition examining humanity’s fascination with flight and space exploration.

The work belongs to Turrell’s celebrated Ganzfeld series, which uses light and color to alter the viewer’s perception of space. Visitors enter an environment of shifting hues, where the horizon appears to disappear and familiar boundaries become difficult to distinguish. The installation draws on Turrell’s longstanding research into human vision and sensory experience, offering an encounter with the uncertainty of depth and distance.

Elsewhere, the opening program includes The Mall, developed with OMA/AMO to examine the cultural history of shopping centers, and Overflow, a First Nations-led exhibition exploring relationships with water across New South Wales. An immersive children’s exhibition, The Dark, completes the five inaugural presentations.

The museum’s public opening also begins a 23-day festival of performances, workshops, and conversations, bringing activity into the galleries and surrounding spaces. Together, the exhibitions offer the first opportunity to experience Powerhouse Parramatta as a functioning cultural institution, with its architecture now serving the artists, collections, and communities it was designed to accommodate.

9 players Fergie decided not to sign for Man Utd: Lampard, Silva, Zidane…

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Sir Alex Ferguson signed some wonderful players during his 27 years in charge of Manchester United – but he missed out on a fair few, too.

Ferguson didn’t get too many things wrong at Old Trafford. But as well as several inspired decisions among around 100 signings, there were also a few duds.

However, as much as Fergie may regret some of the players he signed over the years, he surely regrets some of those he didn’t sign just as much.

No United fan would criticise Ferguson for putting Eric Cantona above any potential new recruit, but the prospect of Zidane at Old Trafford is too tantalising not to regret.

Martin Edwards, in his book, lifted the lid on Ferguson’s thinking when it was put to him that United should be signing the Frenchman from Bordeaux in 1996: “Alex said that Eric had also mentioned Zidane to him, but Alex felt Zidane played in the same position as Eric.

“Having gone over to France to persuade Eric to re-sign for us, after the Crystal Palace incident, he felt that, if he had brought Zidane in, it may have affected Eric’s position, so he stuck with Eric.”

As it turned out, Cantona retired just one season after Zidane left Bordeaux for Juventus.

Liverpool don’t have much to thank Ferguson for, but passing up the chance to sign John Barnes from Watford in 1987 is certainly one reason to be grateful.

Edwards explains in his autobiography, Red Glory: ”Graham Taylor rang Alex Ferguson to see if he wanted to take him before he signed for Liverpool.

“Alex passed on him because it was his first season at United, he had Jesper Olsen in his squad and he wanted to see whether Jesper would make it.”

Olsen showed glimpses, but the Denmark winger had nothing even remotely like the impact Barnes made on Liverpool.

Even the staunchest City-hating United fan will admit – privately, at least – that it is impossible to find any reason not to love David Silva. But Ferguson managed it.

Before the Spanish genius joined Manchester City in 2010 for £24million, United had their eye on him.

“We watched Silva a lot when he was at Valencia,” said Ferguson. “My problem with that was you have to be very, very good to play in that position, because there’s no defensive duties for them.

“So if you’re wanting to be really successful, everyone has to do their turn, has to work, you know?”

Roberto Mancini, Manuel Pellegrini and Pep Guardiola all managed to get Silva to do the dirty work. Maybe all you had to do was ask?

The likes of Bryan Robson, Roy Keane and Paul Scholes all starred in midfield for United and famously had an eye for goal.

Ferguson also saw that in Lampard before he moved to Chelsea in 2001, and the ex-United boss acknowledges he might have made a mistake by not following through with his initial interest.

“Lampard has been unbelievable,” said Ferguson. “I don’t think a midfield player will be able to do that figure again. It’s quite phenomenal.

“Like Bryan Robson, he has had a great knack of timing his runs into the box at the right time. John Wark was another one at Ipswich.

“The guy has had a great career. I must say we looked at him when he was at West Ham as a young player and I maybe regret not having done it.

Not from Juan Sebastian Veron, who moved to Old Trafford at the same time Lampard went to Chelsea, that’s for certain.

Despite signing Peter Schmeichel, Edwin van der Sar and David de Gea during his reign at Old Trafford, Ferguson occasionally had a blind spot when it came to goalkeepers.

Replacing Schmeichel took six years, and during that time, Cech was making a very positive impression in Ligue 1.

“I went to see him in 2003 for Rennes,” Ferguson said. “We thought Petr was a bit young for the bruisers in the Premier League.”

So he persisted with Tim Howard and Roy Carroll passing the gloves between them and all-too-often dropping them.

Cech went to Chelsea instead in 2004, where he had over a decade of success, but Van der Sar arrived a year later and he didn’t do too badly either.

Cech wasn’t the only Chelsea legend who Ferguson dithered over. Drogba was also catching the eye in France with Marseille, but the United boss’s uncertainty allowed the Blues almost a free run.

“We went to check him out [at Marseille] but the club wanted £25million and Chelsea moved in for the kill before we had made up our minds,” Ferguson said.

A month later, Ferguson splashed £27million on Wayne Rooney, and that worked out okay in the end…

“His agent was demanding a price we were not prepared to pay,” said Ferguson when asked about United’s interest in Sergio Aguero.

Whatever amount it was, it would almost certainly have been worth it.

Less than a year after Aguero joined Manchester City for what turned out to be a total of £31.5million, the striker scored the last-gasp goal that denied Ferguson and United the title in 2012.

The Cervélo Soloist—The Race-Inspired Road Bike That Makes Sense for the Way Most Riders Actually Ride

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A race-inspired road bike built for riders who want one fast machine for climbs, descents, and pacelines.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

Over the last few years, many bike brands have tried to combine the benefits of their aero bike platform and climbing bikes into an all-arounder. When I managed a Trek store, the brand launched the Madone Gen8. Trek intended it as the next logical step in its road bike lineup by combining the Madone’s speed with the Émonda’s low weight. Other examples of this trend include the Specialized Tarmac and Cannondale’s SuperSix Evo.

Cervélo touts the Soloist as its do-everything road platform. While you’ll see its S5 and R5 at races like the Tour de France, the Soloist probably won’t show up at the starting line of any Grand Tours. That’s because the Cervélo-sponsored Visma-Lease a Bike team doesn’t need a third road bike; it already has race-winning aero bikes and dedicated climbing bikes for its athletes. Most days those riders use the S5, and on the steepest alpine climbs they can go with the R5.

But most of us don’t have the money, space, or desire to own multiple road bikes to choose between depending on the route we’re riding that day. One road bike that flies up climbs, inspires confidence on descents, and holds well in a paceline is the key for many riders. After almost three months of riding, I found Cervélo’s Soloist fits right into that sweet spot.

The Soloist mixes the pure speed of the S5 with the uphill float of the R5. It sits between both models in weight—about a pound heavier than the R5 and about two pounds lighter than the S5. Visually, the Soloist looks like a midpoint between both bikes. It brings the lines of the R5 while tying in the aero aesthetics of the S5, but the geometry charts paint a different picture. The Soloist leans toward the S5 when looking at the geometry charts for each model. When shopping the Cervélo lineup consider the pricing. The Ultegra Di2 model of the S5 and R5 are both listed at $10,100. The Soloist gives the benefits of each bike, and saves you almost $2,000 for the Ultegra Di2 model.

The Soloist lineup ranges from $6,800 for the SRAM Rival AXS model up to $11,000 for the SRAM Red AXS–equipped bike. I tested the Shimano Ultegra Di2-equipped version, which sells for $8,250. This bike comes stock with a 4iii power meter, an integrated carbon bar and stem, and carbon wheels. My 54cm test bike weighed 15.9 pounds without the bottles or the pedals—pretty respectable for an aero-ish bike with Ultegra components.

Straight out of the box, it was almost ready to roll. Although Cervélo equips this bike with many parts I’d choose for my own bike, I swapped out the stock 700x29c Vittoria Corsa Next TLR G2.0 tires after only a few rides. The rubber was okay on flatter rides, but lacked the grip and ride feel I wanted on twisting descents.

Installing 28mm Pirelli P Zero TLR Race tires was exactly what the Soloist needed to elevate my confidence on descents. The tires made the bike feel locked-in when leaning into turns (I even bagged a couple of Strava trophies on our local descents).

The Soloist ships with aero bottles. Cervélo claims the bottles save 4.3 watts compared to standard round bottles. However, that savings doesn’t matter if you’re wasting energy fumbling with your bottles. The aero ones fit so tight in their cages I almost fell on my first ride trying to remove one.

Even after weeks of testing, I didn’t get used to these bidons. I’ve tried other aero bottle setups and haven’t had the same issue. Plus, the stock aero cages that come with the bike don’t pair as well with traditional round bottles as some brands’ aero cages.

For my first ride, I dove right into the deep end by taking the Soloist up Fiddler’s Elbow. This notorious mile-and-a-half climb gains a little over 700 feet with grades that exceed 20 percent near its summit.

I’ve done this climb at least twenty times on my Trek Madone, with gearing that is a bit more favorable for climbing than the Soloist’s 36x34t lowest gear. But even with taller gearing, the Cervélo floated up the climb. The bike’s geometry put me in a comfortable position, and I appreciated the Soloist’s lower weight on steeper parts of the climb.

But the Soloist didn’t only excel on climbs. The backside of Fiddler’s features a twisting, high-speed descent where maintaining your line is critical, and the bike felt stable. Once I reached the bottom, I had rolling farm roads back to the office, similar to where I did the bulk of my daily rides with this bike.

The Soloist shines on undulating rural roads and group rides. Sometimes our office lunch rides lean toward a spicier pace, with a single or double paceline of up to eight riders. The Soloist is calm, collected, and easy to ride in groups, and its aerodynamics helped when I started to fall off the back and had to catch back to the group.

Lastly, since you don’t always ride on butter-smooth tarmac, I also tested the Soloist on roads outside of its typical scope. The bike felt confident, even on some of the chunky gravel roads. However, I don’t recommend doing this too often with the Soloist; it doesn’t have enough tire clearance for actual gravel tread.

The bike proved easy to ride and live with. I wish I didn’t have to send the Soloist back to Cervélo, as I’d gladly spend more hours on this bike.

Bicycling’s Test Editor, Dan Chabanov, felt like the previous model tried to mix the benefits of the R5 and S5, but missed the mark. “I found it pretty unremarkable. Not in a bad way, but compared to other bikes at the time it just kind of split everything down the middle. It wasn’t very aero but it was aero-ish. It wasn’t very light, but it wasn’t overly heavy. It did a lot of stuff well, but it never really wowed me,” says Chabanov.

The new model irons out those wrinkles. Cervélo shaved nearly a pound off the Ultegra model and added more aero features from the S5—notably the integrated handlebar and stem combination.

I’m impressed after a few months using the new-generation Soloist as my primary road bike. It climbs better than other comparable bikes I’ve tried, holds a pocket in a paceline well, and descends like a banshee. Many brands have tried to blend the watt savings of an aero bike and the weight of a climbing bike. Cervélo took a swing with the previous Soloist model, but refined the previous generation to create a well-blended machine.

This bike is the great choice for everyone from the local crit ripper to the Saturday coffee shop ride champion. If you get this bike, then pick up a pair of Pirelli P Zero Race tires or Continental GP 5000 tires to unlock its full potential. This bike is at the top of my list for those of us who want to have one road bike at home that can rip every ride.

DMZ key and locations in Modern Warfare 4

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There are loads of DMZ keys in Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4. Used to open both locked containers and locked doors, keys are your fast-track to getting your hands on some of the best loot the extraction mode has to offer. However, when you receive a key, you're only given a brief description of where to use it, so we're going to outline where you can use every key the game has to offer.

Here are all the keys we've found in Modern Warfare 4's DMZ mode and where to use them, listed in alphabetical order by the name of the key.

Note that this guide is a work-in-progress, so if the key you're looking for isn't listed here, check back soon.

The blackmarket weapons locker key can be used in two different locations in Hajin. Look for the blackmarket weapons store, identifiable by its iron wrought gates and red advert on the roof. One of them is in southern Deadtown, while the other is in eastern Hanrin Village. Go to the basement to find two lockers at each location, so if you have a second blackmarket weapons locker key, it's worth bringing with you.

Along with finding it as loot in Hajin, the coin mining den key can also be obtained by completing the "Operating Shopping Hacks" story mission. It opens a door on the third floor of an otherwise unassuming building directly north of the Netshin Tech Complex and east of the gas station found to the west of the NuriGO Mall. Inside, you'll find a number of lootable containers, including plenty of server racks, which are handy for electronic sensors.

Completing the "Operation Silent Ledger" story mission will net you the counting room vault key, but to use it, you'll need to go back into the casino. Our dedicated guide explains exactly where to go, but briefly, you'll want to take an immediate right-hand turn as you enter through the main entrance then go through the brown door in the following corridor. You'll find a bunch of safes, loose cash, and containers inside.

The executive arms auction key can be used inside the DBN Headquarters building, which is the tallest skyscraper inside The Grid. If you choose the "Operation Shopping Hacks" mission, you'll spawn nearby. This place is highly fortified, so there are loads of enemies around, and to find the locked room, you'll need to use the various elevators and ziplines dotted around to go to the very top floor (but not the roof). Here, you'll find the locked door pictured above.

The Gangbyeong apartment A key can be used on an apartment block at the northeastern end of Hajin River Heights. The building is next to the compound, and is the one that is perpendicular to the river to the south. If you need to get inside Gangbyeon apartment A without the key, then you can descend into the parking lot below and search for the staircase that will take you up inside the apartment block.

The key for Gangbyeon apartment B can usually be found inside Gangbyeon apartment A, which is quite handy. I found it on top of a table in one of the apartments. Gangbyeon apartment B is the building right next to apartment A, running parallel to the river. The key can be used to get inside either the first floor, the basement, or the roof, and if you have the militia arms depot key, you'll need to get inside apartment B first. Apartment B has several apartments to explore, full of decent loot.

Much like the blackmarket weapons locker key, there are two locations for the gun store weapons locker key, and each location has multiple lockers inside. The gun stores can be found in western Cheongun Village and northern Songdo Wharf. Each of them has an arched red brick roof and a sign above the entrance depicting a cartoon character. In the basement, you'll find multiple lockers that the keys can be used on.

The Hanex guard station key is used inside the bank within The Grid, which is the slightly smaller building next to the DBN Headquarters, which is the tallest building there. Go inside and take a zipline to the roof, then go down one floor to find the bank's vault. The locked door inside this room, pictured above, is where you can use the hanex guard station key.

The militia arms depot is located inside Gangbyeon apartment B, which is northeast of Hajin River Heights. Once you've got the key for apartment B, which can usually be found inside apartment A (details on those keys above, including how to get into apartment A without a key), you'll find the locked door for the militia arms depot on the first floor. Inside is an apartment with loads of locked containers and high-tier loot.

You'll receive the Mirae electrical room key upon completing "Operation Critical Extraction," which is the story mission where you must escort a high-value target out of the general hospital. This key unlocks a room in the underground parking lot for the hospital, which can be found by going to the northwestern corner. Enter through a door at the back and follow the signs for parking, which will lead you to a parking lot with purple walls. At the back is a room with iron bars over the windows and "danger" above the door, which can be unlocked with the key.

The police station armory key is one of the most common keys in DMZ, as it opens armories in all five police stations in Hajin. The map above shows all the locations. In each police station, the armory can be found in the basement, where you'll find weapon crates and other locked containers.
