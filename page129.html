Tiny ocean animals reveal a hidden foundation of marine life

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Tiny ocean animals depend on different food sources across the Pacific, revealing important differences in marine food chains.

Microscopic algae form the base of many ocean food chains, but bacteria also play a major role.

In the warm, nutrient-poor tropical Pacific, researchers found that roughly half of the food supporting tiny crustaceans called Calanus copepods could be traced back to bacteria.

Near the Arctic, in the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea, more than half was supplied instead by dinoflagellates, a group of algae.

Calanus is an important food of fish and seabirds, and a change in what feeds it can therefore be passed on to animals higher in the food web.

The study was led by Hyuntae Choi and Kyung-Hoon Shin of Hanyang University ERICA in South Korea; the co-authors include Brian Popp of the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa.

The copepods were collected on cruises between 2015 and 2020.

What the work provides is therefore a comparison among regions at particular moments; it is no record of change in a single place.

Copepods are small crustaceans that drift with ocean currents. They include Calanus, which feed by filtering tiny food particles from the water.

Adults were collected from two research ships, the Isabu and the icebreaker Araon, by means of nets pulled up from a depth of about 660 feet (200 meters).

From the tropical and subtropical Pacific, the sampling sites extended through the subarctic Pacific and the Bering Sea as far as the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea.

Copepod proteins were then broken down in the laboratory into their separate amino acids. Of these, the essential amino acids cannot be made by animals, which must get them from food.

Production of those amino acids by each source at the base of the food web yields its own mixture of carbon isotopes (slightly heavier and lighter forms of the same element).

Because passage up the food chain leaves the mixture almost unchanged, it serves as a fingerprint.

The proportion derived from each of six groups was then estimated with a statistical model; the groups included diatoms, dinoflagellates, two kinds of cyanobacteria (bacteria that use sunlight) and bacteria that feed on organic matter.

Such proportions refer to the origin of the food and not to what each copepod actually ate, since part of it may first have passed through other microbes.

The tropical Pacific is strongly layered: a warm surface layer mixes little with the nutrient-rich water below it. The condition is known as stratification, and relatively few algae grow there.

Satellite measurements of chlorophyll, the pigment with which algae capture sunlight, ranged there from 0.03 to 0.13 milligrams per cubic meter, against as much as 7.86 near the Bering Strait.

For these tropical waters, the model put about 31% of the copepods’ food base down to bacteria that feed on organic matter.

Cyanobacteria that turn nitrogen gas into a usable nutrient added about 20%, and algae about 47%.

Bacteria thus supplied roughly as much of the food base of tropical copepods as algae did.

Farther north, the share from algae increased with latitude, reaching about 74% in the southern Chukchi Sea.

The share of bacteria feeding on organic matter fell to about 16%, and that of nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria to about 7%.

The greater contribution from algae came mainly from dinoflagellates, single-celled organisms that use sunlight and of which some also eat other microbes.

In the tropics, dinoflagellates and diatoms contributed similar shares, about 19% and 18%. In the southern Chukchi Sea, dinoflagellates reached about 52%, whereas diatoms stayed near 15%.

The small share of diatoms is notable, because diatoms were the most common algae counted in the northern water samples (although at some sites dinoflagellates were equally or more numerous).

The ships reached these waters after the bloom of algae had ended, when nutrients were scarce and the sea ice had largely melted.

Dinoflagellates and other small algae tend to become more common under those conditions, the authors noted.

The researchers suggested that diatoms may be digested more slowly, or that copepods may choose other food. Across all sites, the dinoflagellate share increased with chlorophyll, a relationship shown by no other food source.

Nitrogen in the same amino acids was also used to estimate the number of feeding steps between the copepods and the bottom of their food web.

One estimate counts mainly the steps through animals. A second also counts steps through protists, single-celled grazers that eat bacteria and algae.

In the tropics, the second estimate exceeded the first by less than one step. At subarctic and Arctic sites, it exceeded the first by more than one step.

The authors wrote that the larger difference indicates more feeding on protists at higher latitudes during summer.

The difference between the estimates was also greater where the dinoflagellate share was higher.

The team therefore proposed that dinoflagellates able both to use sunlight and to eat microbes may be an important link between microbes and Calanus in the north.

Algae need nitrogen, which here came from two main sources: nitrate dissolved in seawater, or nitrogen gas turned by cyanobacteria into living tissue.

The two sources carry distinct nitrogen isotope signatures, which allowed the contribution of each to be estimated.

In the tropical Pacific, about 29% of the nitrogen in algae was estimated to come from nitrogen gas. Bacteria break down what the cyanobacteria make, and the nitrogen released is taken up by the algae.

In the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea, by contrast, nitrate supplied an estimated 91% of the algae’s nitrogen.

The authors linked the tropical pattern to stratification, which limits the amount of nitrate reaching the sunlit surface where algae grow.

“Importantly, our study shows how warming-driven changes in stratification and nutrient dynamics are reshaping planktonic food webs,” Shin said.

The study nevertheless compared different regions and did not follow a single region as it warmed.

Warm Pacific water flowing north through the Bering Strait in summer has sped up the loss of sea ice in the western Arctic Ocean, the authors noted.

Between 2015 and 2017, no year-to-year differences were found in the diets of copepods from the subarctic Pacific to the southern Chukchi Sea.

Summer surface water in the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea was, however, warmer in 2019 and 2020 than in 2016 through 2018.

An Arctic species, Calanus glacialis, also became less common there, while Pacific species increased.

The authors wrote that food-chain measurements from those warmer years may differ from the ones reported.

Not every copepod was identified to species, and tropical and northern waters were sampled in different months, so that seasonal changes could not be checked.

It also remains unclear whether dinoflagellates that eat other microbes carry the carbon pattern of an alga or that of their prey, an uncertainty that could blur the fingerprints.

The authors called for isotope measurements across more of the North Pacific and over more years, to see how these food chains vary as the ocean keeps warming.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

What Caused The FAA Meltdown? Duffy And Mullin Give Different Accounts

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The most revealing moment in last night’s press conference about the FAA ground stop came when Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin appeared to explain what Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy preferred to talk around: the government’s efforts the day before to update the system seem to have triggered the failure. That deserved a clear explanation. Instead, we got praise for President Trump, blame directed at Congress, and a demand for $50 billion.

The FAA lifted its nationwide ground stop Friday evening after more than four hours of disruption. Getting departures moving again was welcome news. Explaining why they stopped was another matter.

I updated our original story after the press conference last night, but left it neutral, resisting the urge the opine. It is worth watching below, particularly the exchanges with reporters (a full transcript is available here). But I knew I had to address the conference in a bit more depth (as One Mile At A Time has also done so well) .

Duffy described an aging communications system overwhelmed by data:

“Our switches, our routers actually got overloaded with data and they went down. And — I shouldn’t say they went down; they started to give us inconsistency in the data from those routers. And because of that, we shut the system down.”

He emphasized that the newer equipment had not failed:

“And our new IP switches did not go down; it’s the old hardware that went down. And I think the question for the American people is, do you want your Congress to fund this?”

But what changed Friday? Old equipment does not become old overnight. What caused the overload that made a nationwide departure halt necessary?

Mullin offered a much more specific explanation:

“What it appears to be right now is, as Secretary Duffy and FAA was going in and putting patches into the system, they were putting new equipment and new technology, and that new equipment, new technology just wasn’t — it was overwhelming the older system, which is why Secretary Duffy made it very clear the patchwork doesn’t work.”

There it is. According to Mullin’s preliminary account, the process of introducing patches and new technology was overwhelming the older system. And look at Duffy’s reaction:

You can see Secretary Duffy’s reaction as Secretary Mullin says the newer technology was overwhelming the system. pic.twitter.com/IeoW5nM4aY

Those explanations are not impossible to reconcile, with the key being what Duffy left out of his explanation. New equipment can remain operational while changes associated with it overload older equipment elsewhere. But that makes Duffy’s emphasis on the new switches staying online an incomplete answer to the question passengers actually needed answered and my mother always taught me that a half-truth can actually be a lie.

If the upgrade process contributed to the outage, saying the new equipment did not fail does not tell us whether the transition was properly managed. Later, Duffy acknowledged the limits of what officials knew:

“So, we’re still working on the details of what went wrong today.”

“I’ll be happy to give you more information when we have it, I don’t want to give you the wrong information. Our team is working overtime trying to navigate what happened here, what was the cause and how do we prevent it in the future should similar situations arise?”

That is a perfectly reasonable thing to say during an unfolding investigation. It would have been a much better starting point than presenting the outage as an obvious demonstration that Congress needed to send more money.

I am quite certain that replacing decades-old aviation infrastructure is not easy and I expect hiccups along the way as we update our aging air traffic control systems. At the same time, I expect the people responsible for the project to explain a major failure candidly.

Something like: “The government is replacing a sprawling, aging system while keeping aircraft moving and old and new technology must coexist during the transition, but we are investigating whether a change produced unexpected consequences, and working around the clock to keep it from happening again.”

I would have been far more understanding of that explanation.

“Growing pains” cannot become a blanket defense for shutting down air traffic across the country if there is evidence of poorly tested updates, inadequate safeguards, or a failure to provide a workable rollback. But it would acknowledge the difficulty honestly. We can distinguish a hard project that encounters a serious setback from officials trying to sell that setback as proof of their own excellent leadership.

Early in the briefing, Mullin offered this assessment:

“This is what the leadership of President Trump looks like by the organization and the team he’s put together. We’re built to handle crises like this, and that’s exactly what we’re doing. We’re moving at an unprecedented pace as a whole of government to make sure that we are doing the public justice by making sure everybody is safe, which everybody is.”

I am thankful passengers were safe. Halting departures when communications become unreliable is the correct decision and even with this administration I have no fear that the skies are unsafe, at least when it comes to air traffic control.

But why was a nationwide operational failure the moment to congratulate the president and his team?

The public needed a straightforward explanation and some acknowledgment of the disruption. Instead, we got two cheerleaders praising the emperor with no clothes.

The administration’s performance should be judged after we understand what happened, not advertised as a success while officials are still trying to establish the cause.

And there is an awkward comparison with the response to the 2023 FAA outage. Donald Trump Jr., not the president, used that failure to attack then-Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg as incompetent:

Breaking – All flights nationwide have beengrounded – the #FAA is facing a computer outage.

Yet another major blow to incompetent Transportation Sect Mayor Pete and people who get important jobs with no experience to check some identity politics boxes.

— Donald Trump Jr. (@DonaldJTrumpJr) January 11, 2023

If an FAA outage demonstrates incompetence when the other party is in charge, what does an even longer nationwide shutdown under your own administration demonstrate?

“I’ve asked, the president has asked for $30 billion. I want $50 billion.”

I support modernizing air traffic control. This country should have reliable aviation infrastructure, and that requires sustained investment. But a funding demand does not help with root-cause analysis.

Would having the entire amount appropriated have prevented Friday’s failure? Perhaps a completed replacement system would have avoided this particular vulnerability. But if the immediate problem involved introducing new technology into an older network, even a fully funded project would still go through the same transition. Money cannot eliminate the need for testing and competent implementation.

Congress already approved $12.5 billion over five years in July 2025 for air traffic control improvements and controller hiring. Earlier Friday, before the outage, Duffy and White House budget director Russell Vought sought another $30.1 billion for a package covering both air traffic control and airport infrastructure.

Question, though: Republicans have controlled the presidency and both chambers of Congress for nearly two years. When a reporter raised that point, Duffy’s immediate response was to ask which network the reporter represented.

DUFFY: How many times does this have to happen before congress gives the money to fix it?

R: You’ve been in office almost two years now, Republicans controlled both chambers of congress and still do.

DUFFY: What your network are you from? pic.twitter.com/xuX5arTwjl

The answer to a question about your record should not depend on whether the person asking works for NBC or Fox…

I think this press conference helps illustrate a larger problem for the administration.

Democrats have won control of both chambers of Congress. My reading of that result is that Americans are becoming impatient with a government that demands credit constantly while treating accountability as someone else’s responsibility.

There were many reasons people voted as they did, but I see a common thread between the Republican victory in 2024 and the Democratic wave in 2026: voters want results, and they are willing to punish the party that fails to deliver them.

Most people are fairly pragmatic about whether their government works. They want competence. They want officials who can distinguish between explaining a problem and trying to talk their way out of responsibility for it.

My view is that the election was a rejection of the self-dealing and incompetence that have characterized this administration, along with a Republican Congress that had two years of unified government and far too little to show for it.

It would be inaccurate to say Congress accomplished nothing. It certinlay pushed through a massive tax cut. But the Congressional Budget Office estimated that law would add $3.4 trillion to deficits over 2025–2034, excluding additional debt-service costs.

Congress could find the votes for another enormous deficit-financed package. Now the administration wants to explain an aviation meltdown primarily by pointing to money Congress has not provided.

Sorry, but that is not a serious argumentfrom people who controlled the entire process.

I still want Congress to fund necessary aviation improvements. Passengers should not suffer because lawmakers want to score a point against Duffy. But providing money and demanding accountability should happen together.

The aging air traffic control system needs replacing. Friday’s ground stop makes the urgency painfully clear.

What the press conference did not establish is that an additional appropriation would have prevented this particular failure. Mullin’s account instead raised the possibility that the modernization process itself contributed to it, while Duffy focused on the old equipment and the need for more money.

An honest explanation about a difficult transition would have earned far more understanding from me. A clear account of what failed, followed by a credible plan to prevent a repeat, would be even better.

Instead, we got an administration congratulating itself before it could clearly explain what went wrong. After nearly two years in charge, Americans are entitled to expect better.

This Is How Fast The B‑2 Spirit Bomber Can Fly

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is an aircraft that still looks unusual, even today, decades after it first entered service. Its smooth, triangular shape looks more like a shadow than a conventional airplane, setting it apart from the familiar outlines of wings, tails, and vertical fins.

That distinctive appearance is the most visible sign that the B-2 was designed to operate in a very different way from earlier bombers. Understanding the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber means looking at the reasoning behind its design rather than just its specifications.

Nothing about the B-2 Spirit Bomber is accidental, from its overall form to the systems hidden beneath its surface. Instead of relying on speed, the aircraft combines efficiency, stealth, and long-range capability, demonstrating how deliberate design choices can fundamentally change how a strategic bomber is used.

At its core, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is a pure flying wing. There is no traditional ​​​​​​fuselage, no tail, and no vertical stabilizers, meaning that the entire aircraft is essentially one continuous lifting surface. Flying wing concepts had been explored for decades before the B-2 Spirit Bomber, including earlier Northrop designs.

However, none had ever reached operational service at this scale or level of sophistication. This configuration offers major aerodynamic benefits. With fewer protrusions and less structural clutter, drag is reduced, and lift efficiency improves. The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber can stay airborne for long periods while carrying heavy payloads, which is exactly what a strategic bomber needs to do.

However, the trade-off is stability. Flying wings are naturally less stable than conventional aircraft, but the B-2 Spirit Bomber solves this problem with advanced flight control computers, making thousands of adjustments every second to keep the aircraft flying smoothly and predictably.

The B-2 is famous for its stealth, but how much punch does it pack? Let’s find out.

Stealth is the defining characteristic of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber, and it starts with the aircraft’s shape. The smooth, continuous curves of the flying wing reflect radar energy away from the source rather than back toward it, while sharp angles and right-angle intersections, which are common radar reflectors, are carefully avoided wherever possible.

The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber uses radar-absorbent materials across much of its surface, turning incoming radar energy into heat rather than reflected signals. Even the engine intakes and exhausts are designed to reduce radar and infrared signatures.

Air enters through serpentine ducts that hide the engine faces, while exhaust is spread and cooled to make the aircraft harder to detect with heat-seeking sensors, resulting in an aircraft that can penetrate heavily defended airspace in ways that would be unthinkable for older bombers.

This brings us on to the key question: how fast can the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber fly? It is important to note that the B-2 Spirit Bomber is not a speed-focused aircraft, but it is far from slow. Its maximum speed is generally cited at around 628 mph (1,010 km/h), with a typical cruise speed closer to 560 mph (901 km/h).

This puts it just under the speed of sound, operating at high subsonic velocities that balance efficiency, long range, and survivability. These figures place the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber in an interesting position among bomber aircraft. It is slower than the supersonic Rockwell B-1B Lancer, which can exceed 900 mph (1,448 km/h).

However, it is broadly comparable to the venerable Boeing B-52, which has a top speed of around 650 mph (1,046 km/h). Compared to stealth-focused contemporaries like the Lockheed F-117 Nighthawk, the B-2 Spirit Bomber offers similar subsonic performance but with vastly greater range and payload. In practice, the B-2 Spirit Bomber relies on stealth and planning rather than raw speed to survive, making its top speed more than adequate for what the aircraft is designed to do.

One of the most impressive aspects of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is how far it can fly. With a published range of around 6,900 miles (11,104 km), the B-2 Spirit Bomber was designed from the start for intercontinental services, and when paired with aerial refueling, its endurance becomes extraordinary, allowing it to stay airborne for well over 40 hours.

This endurance has real operational consequences, and Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber crews have flown combat missions directly from the continental US to targets halfway around the world and back again. Long endurance reduces reliance on forward bases, which may be politically sensitive or vulnerable in a conflict, and it also allows planners to approach targets from unexpected directions, further enhancing the aircraft’s survivability and strategic flexibility.

Despite its sleek appearance, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber can carry a substantial weapons load, and internally, it can accommodate up to 40,000 lbs of ordnance, all carried inside weapon bays to preserve stealth. This includes a mix of conventional and nuclear weapons, making the B-2 Spirit Bomber a key component of strategic deterrence as well as conventional strike operations.

The emphasis is on precision rather than volume, and the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is capable of deploying GPS-guided bombs, allowing a small number of aircraft to strike multiple high-value targets with remarkable accuracy. This precision reduces the need for large formations and minimizes collateral damage.

The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is flown by a crew of just two (a pilot and a mission commander), which is a striking contrast to older bombers that required crews of four or more. Advanced avionics and automation make this possible, handling navigation, flight management, and many defensive functions that once demanded dedicated crew members.

Inside the cockpit, the emphasis is on situational awareness and workload management. Long missions mean crew endurance is just as important as aircraft performance, so the cockpit includes provisions for rest and careful ergonomics. The aircraft’s systems are designed to be highly reliable, but also flexible.

This allows crews to adapt to changing mission requirements while airborne. This means that flying the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is less about aggressive maneuvering and more about precision, patience, and trust in the systems that quietly keep the B-2 Spirit Bomber invisible.

The operational history of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber is unusually straightforward, largely because only one country has ever flown it. Unlike many combat aircraft that are exported and adapted by multiple air forces, the B-2 Spirit Bomber has remained exclusively in the hands of the USAF since it entered service in the late 1990s.

That exclusivity reflects both its cost and its strategic importance. The Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber first saw combat during the 1999 Kosovo conflict, when it flew missions directly from the US to strike targets in the former Yugoslavia. These demonstrated its ability to deliver precision-guided weapons over intercontinental distances while bypassing sophisticated air defenses.

A small number of B-2 Spirit Bombers were able to hit a significant portion of high-value targets early in the campaign, highlighting the efficiency of stealth-based operations. In the years that followed, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber was used by the US in Afghanistan and Iraq, again flying long-range missions supported by aerial refueling.

Its ability to carry large numbers of precision weapons made it particularly useful in the opening phases of these conflicts, when defended targets needed to be neutralized quickly. The B-2 Spirit Bomber also took part in strikes in Libya in 2011, where it was used to attack aircraft shelters.

While no other country has operated the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber, its influence has been global, and the aircraft has shaped how other nations approach long-range strike and stealth technology, even if none have fielded a direct equivalent. In practice, the B-2 Spirit Bomber has been used sparingly, but when it has been deployed, it has typically been at moments where its unique capabilities mattered most.

The aircraft might be the Air Force's deadliest weapon.

When comparing the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber to other aircraft in a similar role, it quickly becomes clear that it was never meant to compete on the same terms. Rather than focusing on speed or payload alone, the B-2 Spirit Bomber was designed around survivability in heavily defended airspace, and that choice sets it apart from both earlier and contemporary bombers.

Against legacy platforms like the Boeing B-52, the contrast is especially sharp. The B-52 offers greater payload capacity and has proven remarkably adaptable over decades of service, but it relies on escort support to operate safely in modern threat environments. The B-2 Spirit Bomber, by comparison, is built to fly directly through advanced air defenses, using stealth rather than distance as its primary layer of protection.

While the two aircraft share similar subsonic performance, their operational philosophies are very different. The Rockwell B-1 Lancer provides another useful comparison: with its variable-sweep wings and supersonic capability, the B-1 emphasizes speed and flexibility. However, its radar signature is significantly larger than that of the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber, limiting where it can safely operate without support.

In practice, the B-2 Spirit Bomber trades outright speed for the ability to reach targets that would be inaccessible to faster but more detectable aircraft. Compared to stealth-focused designs like the Lockheed F-117 Nighthawk, the Northrop B-2 Spirit Bomber operates on a much larger scale.

While both rely on low observability, the B-2 Spirit Bomber offers intercontinental range and a vastly greater payload, allowing it to carry out strategic missions rather than precision strikes on a limited number of targets. In this sense, the B-2 Spirit Bomber occupies a unique space, blending stealth with global reach in a way no other operational bomber has fully matched.

Air Rifles Are Still Underrated for Big Game Hunting. Here’s Why They Shouldn’t Be Overlooked

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 14, 2026 10:00 AM EST

We may earn revenue from the products available on this page and participate in affiliate programs. Learn More ›

The latest for gun hunters and competitive shooters.

I lived in Europe for several years and was introduced to quality airguns for competitive shooting and hunting while there. In the late 90’s I obtained my first PCP rifles, a .177 Daystate Huntsman followed by a .22 Webley Raider. It did not take long to appreciate the advantages these guns offered for small game hunting. When I returned home I found there was a small but enthusiastic community of airgun hunters testing the limits of the existing technology.

Eventually, a growing number of airgun manufacturers released larger caliber and more powerful rifles to market. It was only a matter of time before we started to think about using air rifles for larger game like deer and hogs. It was about this time that I first heard of Dennis Quackenbush and the big bore airguns he was producing, initially in .308 and .50 calibers, but eventually the .457 became his flagship model. I purchased the .308 in early 2002 for predator hunting, using it to bag fox and coyote. About the same time the idea of using these rifles for larger game really took hold with me.  But why would anyone want to use an airgun, rather than a conventional firearm?

Quite simply, because it’s fun, challenging, and effective. In the following overview, I’ll give a brief history of the big bore airgun, the motivations for hunting with them, the power they are capable of generating, hunting regulations, ammunition used for hunting, and how to keep the rifle filled with air and ready to hunt.

There are several advantages to airguns in general: they are safer to use in more developed and densely populated areas, they are far quieter than even a rimfire, and ammunition is less expensive. But in my opinion these advantages are less significant when discussing large caliber rifles. Why use them then? For many it comes down to the challenge of the hunt, the requirement for the hunter to exercise the discipline to get closer, be more selective in choosing the shot, and be more precise in shot placement. At least for me, hunting is more about how you do it, than just the outcome. Many hunters find this challenge through hunting with handguns, archery, or muzzleloaders.

In this respect hunting with a big bore airgun is closer to the ethos and field craft of bowhunting but based on the core skill set of the rifleman. And this is what many enjoy most about hunting big game with an airgun, it forces a hunter to close the range and be very precise in shot selection, with the discipline to work for the optimal shot and hold back until that is achieved.

The first commercially available rifles that were applicable for larger quarry, were the Quackenbush big bores. With the advent of this new generation of big bores, the power outputs that could be achieved with an airgun jumped from the 30-40 foot-pound of energy (fpe) range obtained with a standard .22 to over 400 fpe. And just like that, the potential for using them on deer or other large game became a reality.

If like me, you grew up hearing that a deer rifle needs to generate over 1,000 fpe to be effective, this 400 number seems very low. Well, It turns out the 1,000 fpe threshold is simply wrong. I have killed many hogs with airguns generating 100 fpe, and hogs are harder to kill than deer, in my opinion. And many times, when hunting alongside others using centerfires, my kills have been cleaner and more decisive. This is because I get in closer and place the shot more precisely. The best air rifles produce a wound similar to an arrow in that there is no hydrostatic shock generated, but rather direct injury to the vital organs. The simple fact is that 300 fpe is plenty of power to kill deer-sized game but you have to be selective with your shooting and be capable of hitting your target. You cannot be a sloppy or lazy in airgun hunting.

Today’s big bore airguns can be very powerful, and there is a wide selection of calibers and power outputs to choose from. An important factor to understand, is that the caliber is only one element contributing to the power an air rifle can generate. A .50 caliber slug in one rifle design may generate 250 fpe, but that same slug in another design can produce more than 800 fpe. The power is defined by the gun, the pressure it charges to, the pressure and volume of air it releases, and the efficiency of air flow and the guns valving system.

When talking about the more powerful rifles such as the Hatsan Piledriver, Umarex Hammer, Air Force Texan, and Western Bushbuck, you should expect to get over 600 fpe in a .457 and over 700 in the .50 caliber versions. One of the newer players in the big bore space is the AEA Zeus, which is a .72 caliber that comes in various barrel lengths and can generate between 1,000 fpe to 1,500 fpe.

Getting the rifle optimized for a hunting application is a balancing act between power, accuracy, and shot count. Some big bore airguns are regulated, for instance the Umarex Hammer has a factory set regulator adjusted to operate at 3,000 psi, and since every shot uses the same air pressure and volume it is very consistent. Some rifles, such as the AirForce Texan allow the user to adjust the power, which is usually achieved by increasing or decreasing the tension on the hammer spring. And others, such as the Western Bushbuck, have a two-step cocking position that allows either a full power or reduced power shot to be achieved. In many ways, a power adjustment on an airgun is an analog to hand loading for a powder burner.

Initially, the problem big bore airgunners faced was finding game that could be legally taken with their guns. Regulations were the barrier, and in the early days there were very few places that allowed airguns to be used for anything other than small game and pest control. Most jurisdictions have separate deer seasons for firearms, muzzleloaders, and archery that are very specific in defining the criteria for each method. And up until a few years ago, none of them specifically allowed airguns for big game. But there were a few workarounds, Texas for instance did not allow any (big or small) game animal to be taken with an airgun. However non-game species such as feral hogs, predators, and exotic species, could be taken in any number, at any time, and by any means.

Texas became ground zero for early big game airgun hunting in the U.S. The state has large populations of feral hogs, and there are also populations of feral Spanish goats, axis deer, and aoudad that could be hunted. I started flying down to the Texas to meet up with my buddy Eric Henderson, and we would roam the state hunting hogs and exotics during the day, then go after predators at night, with our Quackenbush air rifles.

We experimented with our guns, trying different fill pressures and hammer springs, to optimize power, accuracy, consistency, and the highest shot count. Somewhere along the way, big bore airguns gained visibility and more people started shooting them. This led to grassroot movements lobbying state wildlife management authorities to introduce provisions for airgun hunting, and new regulations came on the books.

At the time of this writing, several states including Michigan, Indiana, Alabama, Missouri, Georgia, Virginia, Tennessee, Kentucky, Idaho, North Dakota, and a growing number of others allow deer hunting with big bore airguns (airgun hunting law resource). Texas and Arizona not only allow deer to be taken with airguns, but most other species of big game can be taken with air powered PCP rifles as well. Some states place a caliber and/or power restriction on the guns that are permitted, so you need to check local regulations. Under these new regulations, airguns have taken whitetail and mule deer, elk, bison, bear, mountain lion, javalina, and pronghorn.

Along with the North American hunts, I’ve been traveling to South Africa for almost two decades to hunt plains game with air rifles, after being granted permission to do the first sanctioned airgun hunts there in 2003. Many of my big bores have been used to take kudu, wildebeest, blesbok, impala, springbok, warthog, bushbuck, and several other species over the years.

Many of the manufacturers producing big bore airguns offer ammunition as well. These may be produced inhouse or on an OEM basis, but in general there is a wide selection of ammo available in all big bore airgun calibers (.308-.72). Hatsan offers their own line of big bore “pellets” in .457 and .50 caliber in a variety of weights. Not surprisingly, they work very well in the Hatsan guns. Established muzzleloader companies such as Hunters Supply have an extensive line of big bore airgun slugs and pellets as well, and even the heavy hitters like Nosler have stepped in with a line of airgun ammo.

One of the companies whose product I use a lot is Mr. Hollowpoint, which is a small operation run by Robert Vogel. He was one of the early big bore airgunners and knows airguns inside and out. The slugs he produces are mostly (but not all) hollow points that are specifically designed around the individual guns.

Airguns can be notoriously finicky about the ammo they prefer, and even guns out of the same manufacturing run might have different preferences. When I get a new rifle, I will try a few slug configurations and weights until I find the one that’s most accurate—then I stick with it. Of course, if you want to use a flatter shooting 86-grain .308 slug for coyote and switch to a heavier 126-grain for better penetration on feral hogs, that’s fine. You just need to find pellets in both weights that work for your gun and commit to those.

Here’s another important consideration for all air rifle hunters: How will you keep the rifle filled? There are a couple options, but in my experience a handpump is not a viable one. The volume of air a big bore requires, the high pressures they usually fill to, and the high-volume air usage all make a handpump ineffective. This leaves you with either getting an air tank that you have filled at a paintball or dive shop, or getting your own compressor.

Tanks are a good approach; you will want to opt for a large-volume carbon fiber tank that can be filled to 4,500 psi. These tanks are connected to the rifles on board storage using a high-pressure hose with either a female quick release connector or a proprietary fill probe. The air flow circuit is opened so that the air flows from the high-pressure tank to the low-pressure onboard storage, and when the gun reaches the required pressure, the tank’s valve is closed, the bleed valve opened, the gun disconnected, and you are ready to go.

Tanks are moderately priced, fast and easy to operate, and are probably the most convenient means of filling a rifle. There is a downside though, first you need to find a place with a compressor that will fill your tanks (look for paintball shops or dive shops, which will usually do this for a fee). A bigger problem is that these shops are often unable or unwilling to charge the tank up to 4,500 psi.

Another option, especially for a high-pressure high-volume rifle is to get a portable compressor. Up until a few years ago this was cost prohibitive, unless you shot a lot or had a group of shooters going in together, as a compressor could easily cost more than $3,500. But there are several compressors (both AC and DC models) on the market today that can fill an individual gun to a 4500 psi, and they can cost less than a carbon fiber tank. The downside is they take longer to charge the gun than a tank does, they are loud, and they require some upkeep. Personally, my preference is to have both a CF tank and a portable compressor to use in combination, using the tank to charge as high as its residual pressure will allow, then topping off with the compressor.

Each of the topics we’ve covered could fill multiple chapters on their own, but now you have an honest overview big game hunting with an air rifle.

I quit hunting with anything but air-powered guns almost 15 years ago, but before that I had moved to handgun hunting and bowhunting to increase the challenge. The airgun is an effective, efficient, and ethical method of take for deer and other big game, but you need to use the gun appropriately. If you just want to go out and shoot a deer at long range and call it a season, a big bore airgun is probably not a good fit for you. However, if you want the challenge of closing the range and dropping the slug precisely on target, you need to give hunting with an air rifle a try. Now more than ever, big bore airguns are readily available. There’s a broad selection of models with a range of features and price points, and there are more places to hunt with them than ever before.

PMDD is a complexcondition that causes severe physical and psychological symptoms that typically occur in the days or weeks leading up to menstruation.

“What distinguishes PMDD from the more common PMS (premenstrual syndrome) is the severity of the symptoms and how significantly they can interfere with daily functioning,” Sari Eitches, MD, dual board certified physician and medical advisor to Terra Mare, told Healthline.

“Another defining feature is the timing: symptoms typically worsen in the week or two before a period, resolve within a few days after menstruation begins, and are minimal or absent during the rest of the cycle,” she said.

Eitches also noted that PMDD is a chronic condition that can affect women at any stage of their reproductive lives, from adolescence through perimenopause, as long as they are having menstrual cycles.

“It is caused by a sensitivity to cyclic hormonal and neurotransmitter fluctuations, so [it] can occur in anyone with a menstrual cycle, though stages of hormonal fluctuation- including postpartum, weaning breastfeeding, and perimenopause- can be a trigger,” she added.

According to research published in 2024, approximately 1.6% of women and girls meet the strict diagnostic criteria for PMDD, equivalent to an estimated 31 million people worldwide.

A higher percentage of 3.2% have a provisional diagnosis, meaning a healthcare professional may believe a person has PMDD, but symptoms have not been measured for a sustained amount of time to meet the criteria for a confirmed diagnosis.

Eitches said that women with a personal or family history of depression or anxiety may be more prone to developing PMDD, as are women with a family history of PMDD itself.

“PMDD is understood to be caused by a combination of heritable biological sensitivities and environmental triggers. These can include a history of trauma, chronic stress, and other chronic medical conditions,” she noted.

Eitches also said that you should consider seeing a doctor if your symptoms are severe enough that they’re interfering with your ability to go to work or school, maintain your relationships, or participate in your life in the same way that you do during the other weeks of the month.

To diagnose PMDD, a healthcare professional will review your medical history and symptoms and may perform a physical exam. They will typically ask you to track your symptoms over at least two menstrual cycles.

A PMDD diagnosis generally requires at least five symptoms, including at least one mood-related symptom, that occur before menstruation, improve shortly after a period begins, and significantly interfere with daily life.
