BYD is launching this 5-seat luxury electric SUV next week with over 1,000 km of range

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

BYD’s luxury brand, Denza, calls it “the king of large 5-seater pure electric vehicles.” The new Denza N8 electric SUV will launch next week, offering an impressive CLTC driving range of over 1,000 km (621 miles), massive digital screens, and plenty of premium extras.

After opening pre-orders for the new 5-seat luxury electric SUV last week, Li Hui, Denza’s general manager, said the results so far “have been excellent.”

According to Li, the Denza N8 is “definitely the most anticipated luxury SUV of the year.” Using BYD’s second-generation Blade Batteries, the Denza N8 EV has a pure electric CLTC range of up to 1,003 km (623 miles) on a single charge.

That’s just as much as the N8 plug-in hybrid (PHEV) it’s replacing, which offered a combined CLTC range of 1,003 km (623 miles) on a full tank of gas and a full charge.

While Li confirmed last week that the new EV variant would go on sale in November, he didn’t give a specific date.

Now, it’s official. Denza announced on its Weibo page on Friday that the launch event for the Denza N8 EV will be held on November 12, 2026.

BYD is still offering a limited-time promo that includes a 5,000 RMB credit for those who place a reservation with a 2,000 RMB ($200) deposit.

The Denza N8 measures 5,150 mm long, 1,999 mm wide, and 1,820 mm high, with a 3,075 mm wheelbase. That’s slightly longer than the Kia EV9 and Hyundai IONIQ 9.

It’s available with a single rear-wheel-drive (RWD) or a tri-motor all-wheel-drive (AWD) powertrain. The RWD version uses a single motor producing either 320 kW (429 hp) or 370 kW (496 hp).

The tri-motor Denza N8 uses BYD’s e3 platform. The setup includes three electric motors: a 270 kW (362 hp) front motor and two 310 kW (416 hp) rear motors, for a combined output of 890 kW (1,193 hp).

Regulatory filings earlier this year revealed it will use either a 130.15 kWh or 105.79 kWh BYD Blade 2.0 battery, offering a CLTC range of up to 1,003 km (623 miles) and 800 km (497 miles), respectively.

The Denza N8 also features BYD’s latest Flash Charging technology, letting it recharge from 10% to 80% in just five minutes. It takes just nine minutes to go from 10% to 97%.

Inside, the five-seat electric SUV features a massive 30″ infotainment screen that spans nearly the entire passenger-side dashboard, plus a new 43″ (1.1 meter) panoramic head-up display (PHUD) at the bottom of the windshield.

Like most of its new luxury vehicles, the Denza N8 is loaded with BYD’s latest software and technology.

Added premium features include zero-gravity seating in all rows, a three-spoke steering wheel, wireless phone chargers, and ambient lighting.

The electric SUV is equipped with a roof-mounted LiDAR to support BYD’s God’s Eye 5.0 intelligent driving assistance system, plus its DiSus-A intelligent air suspension system.

BYD has yet to announce prices for the Denza N8 EV, but it will likely be slightly less than the N8L, an extended version that starts at 299,800 RMB ($45,000).

Rights on the Ballot Alongside US Congressional Elections

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image A voter picks up an "I Voted" sticker after casting their ballot in the US midterm elections on November 3, 2026. © 2026 AP Photo/George Walker IV

Alongside the high-profile congressional and gubernatorial races decided during the United States midterm elections on November 3, human rights were at issue in several state and local ballot measures across the country. 

In Virginia, voters adopted a measure to automatically restore voting rights to people with felony convictions upon release from prison, ending a lifetime ban that had been in place since the Jim Crow era.

Arizona voters rejected and Wisconsin voters approved measures to end diversity, equity, and inclusion practices in education, employment, contracts, and administration, mirroring Trump administration initiatives to dismantle programs to address ongoing impacts of historical discrimination based on race and ethnicity. Vermont voters approved an amendment providing equal protection to individuals regardless of their race, ethnicity, sex, religion, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression or national origin.

Meanwhile, Missouri voters rejected an effort to restrict the right to abortion care and measures to safeguard abortion rights were successful in Nevada and Virginia but failed in Idaho.

Ballot measures on transgender rights also showed mixed results. In Colorado, voters adopted a measure to ban gender-affirming surgeries for youth under 18. In Missouri, one of 27 US states to have a ban on gender-affirming care for youth, voters opted to keep the ban out of the state’s constitution.

Virginia voters approved an amendment formally lifting the state’s obsolete ban on same-sex marriage and affirming that two adults may marry regardless of sex, gender, and race.

Immigration-related measures were also on several ballots. Colorado voters rejected a measure that would have required state and local law enforcement to coordinate with federal immigration authorities in some circumstances. Voters in Honolulu, Hawaii were consulted on a measure requiring their police department to ensure the constitutional rights of all persons regardless of immigration or citizenship status, and work with federal agents only when required by statute or a court order, though the results are still to be decided. And voters in Chicago, Illinois, said they agreed that the city should seek financial compensation from the federal government for harm caused by immigration enforcement operations.

The US Congressional elections may be the biggest story of the week, but voters took up some important human rights issues directly in these initiatives and the impacts will be felt for years to come.

AI is dramatically accelerating AI research

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This post discusses a public METR report on AI R&D acceleration at Anthropic. I did not work on this project or see the unredacted report.1 All opinions are my own and do not represent METR or the report authors.Thanks to Ryan Greenblatt for discussion, feedback, and help with modeling, and Daniel Kokotajlo, Eli Lifland, and Hjalmar Wjik for comments.

Earlier this week, Anthropic stated in their S1 that they “may imminently meet” the threshold of “dramatically accelerated progress due to AI” as defined in their Responsible Scaling Policy. In their latest RSP (effective July 2026), say this threshold will be met when:

They observe or expect overall capabilities progress to be going twice as fast as the fastest sustained rate before AI was contributing much to AI R&D.2

This is plausibly attributable to AI automating AI R&D, rather than acceleration in compute or other inputs.3

For the last few months, some of my colleagues at METR have been working on a deep-access investigation to understand how much Anthropic is being accelerated by its AI systems. From my reading of their report and Anthropic’s RSP, it looks like the “dramatic acceleration” threshold has already been crossed.4

Between October 2025 and October 2026, capabilities progress at Anthropic has been going about twice as fast as it had been over the previous twelve months. The measurement they report is an internal version of the Epoch Capabilities Index called AECI; a wide range of other supporting evidence for acceleration has been redacted. They said that this redacted evidence was "a majority of the evidence [they] found most informative."

While the pace may have initially started picking up for a mix of reasons, AI automation is probably the large majority of the reason why capabilities are still improving at this faster pace today. Their reasons for believing this have been redacted.

This is a much bigger deal than it might sound at first. I think this report is the most important update we have gotten about AI risk in the last couple of years — much bigger than the Hugging Face attack. Naively, it points toward much shorter timelines than most external researchers (including the AI 2027 team) have projected.

Importantly, if AI is roughly doubling capabilities progress overall, it must be speeding up human researchers by much more than that.

Researchers work on improving algorithms and architectures; Anthropic has sometimes referred to these innovations as “compute multipliers.”5 Overall AI capabilities (“effective compute”) is the product of compute multipliers and training compute. Because the compute multipliers are only one input into capabilities, this means that if AI capabilities are advancing twice as fast overall because of software progress, the rate of growth in compute multipliers would need to be more than twice as fast: perhaps ~2.5-3x faster based on reasonable assumptions.6 Since there’s uncertainty about the size of speedup and how much of it comes from AI, I’ll go with ~2-3x.

Similarly, researcher labor is only one input to discovering these compute multipliers: they need to test their ideas, which requires compute for experiments. If we assume a very simple Cobb-Douglas model where experiment compute and labor are similarly important, that means the uplift to labor roughly needs to be the square of the acceleration in compute multipliers.7 This would imply that we would need a (~2-3x)^2 = ~4-9x speed up in labor to make overall AI capabilities go twice as fast.8

However, once capabilities start speeding up significantly, this also means that low-hanging fruit is plucked at a correspondingly faster rate. Each new compute multiplier takes more and more work to discover and implement (i.e., “ideas are getting harder to find”). Before there was much acceleration from AI, labor roughly grew at some roughly fixed exponential rate from hiring more humans. Combined with growth in compute, this compensated for diminishing returns enough to maintain the baseline rate of capabilities progress. But to maintain a new faster rate of capabilities progress without scaling compute faster, we would need a new faster rate of growth in overall labor.

Since the human labor force is stuck growing at more or less the old rate, the uplift from AI would need to get larger and larger over time just to sustain capabilities progress at its new rate. That means the uplift today, after several months of accelerated progress from AI,9 needs to be even higher than ~4-9x, the estimate for what it would take to double progress at the start: maybe the necessary labor uplift is ~10x. This is very uncertain, and substantially more or less is plausible. But it lines up surprisingly well with informal self-reports from Anthropic staff, who say they are now getting a ~12x uplift from the use of internal models.

When you’re picturing a 10x uplift, you should not be imagining that every researcher is now a team of ten researchers — as anyone who has ever done anything knows, a team of ten does not get ten times as much valuable work done per hour as one person. Instead, it’s as if every researcher at Anthropic is in a time warp bubble where they experience ten days for every day that passes in the outside world. As if AI lets them think, read, speak, type, and do everything else ten times faster.10 Anthropic probably wouldn’t have been able to speed up its research this much by hiring any number of people.11

A reasonable default expectation is that as AI capabilities continue to improve, AI researchers will be sped up more and more from automation: if today it takes them a month to get as much work done as they used to in a year, soon it will take a week, and then perhaps a day. If this increasing labor uplift is strong enough to overcome diminishing returns, then in the absence of other bottlenecks it would translate into even larger speed ups in the rate of AI progress: progress will start going 4x faster, and then 8x, and then 16x, and so on.

The report authors were very uncertain about this question, and say that their picture changed several times with new information. But ultimately, they did not find evidence of bottlenecks or steep diminishing returns that would get in the way of compounding acceleration.12 They also report seeing some (confusing) redacted evidence indicating that acceleration could already be above 2x and increasing over time.

If we take it at face value that AI is now nearly doubling the rate of AI capabilities progress and assume acceleration from AI is smooth, this naively implies that we would reach extreme superintelligence within a year. It seems that around a year’s worth of AI progress got us from modest acceleration to the point where AIs now make AI progress roughly twice as fast. If acceleration from AI is smoothly increasing, then at this new rate, we should expect that we will double the rate of progress again within six months. At that point, it would take three months to double it again, then six weeks.13

In theory, these doubling times would add up to infinite capabilities within one year. In reality, at some point we would reach the effective ceiling on software efficiency. But I expect these limits to be very far from where we are now.

If we go through this feedback loop, I expect that the AI systems that come out the other side will be able to very quickly develop novel weapons and very easily overpower human civilization in a conflict. If these systems were seriously misaligned, I think they would likely disempower humanity, and this could lead to human extinction.

The “dramatically accelerated progress due to AI” is such an important and extreme milestone because it is a strong concrete indicator of extreme capabilities in the very near term. Indeed, in an earlier version of Anthropic’s RSP (May 2025), the highest named risk threshold for AI research automation (AI R&D-5) corresponded to a somewhat weaker milestone.14 According to the old RSP, Anthropic would be required to have, at a minimum, security good enough to “protect against model-weight theft by state-level adversaries”15 by the time it reached AI R&D-5.

I agree that very strong security, both for model weights and algorithmic secrets themselves, is a minimum prerequisite for undergoing an intelligence explosion next year without running very large risks of killing billions of people. The AI industry is nowhere near this bar: both OpenAI and Anthropic recently had major security breaches by amateur groups. Beyond strong model weight security, I also think we would need robust logging and monitoring with universal coverage, reliable measurements of monitorability, effective countermeasures against monitor collusion, principled alignment training techniques with very convincing model organisms or interpretability to understand how well they’re working, governance infrastructure for pacing progress if we notice these alignment and control interventions start to break down…and many other measures if we’re going to approach an intelligence explosion in anything like a responsible way.

Unfortunately, most of the important evidence that fed into these conclusions has been redacted from this report. Analytical choices will inevitably be fraught, and the report authors say that the evidence was often confusing.

I trust that my colleagues did a careful and rigorous job navigating making sense of what they saw, and I made a big update based on this report. But I wish much more of the underlying evidence was transparent so readers don’t have to rely on trust. We may be running out of time to get on the same page about timelines and takeoff speeds, and it is much harder to do that when external scientists cannot examine the data and test out their own models.

Because I may have seen other confidential information that might be relevant to timelines and takeoff speeds, I will only discuss implications of public evidence rather than sharing precise bottom line forecasts.

The precise condition is: “We observe or expect double the rate of progress in AI aggregate capabilities compared to both the rate we’d expect and the fastest rate of extended progress we’ve observed in the absence of significant AI contributions to AI R&D.” My understanding is the reason to have both of these conditions (double the rate of progress both compared to the counterfactual and compared to the fastest sustained rate without much AI assistance) is to exclude cases where the pace of capabilities would have slowed down a lot without AI automation, but we manage to keep the old trends going with dramatic uplift from AI.

The precise condition is: “It is plausible that this doubling is significantly attributable to the automation of research and/or engineering (as opposed to other factors, such as increased headcount, compute, or general productivity), such that continuation of the trend in AI progress seems likely to lead to even greater acceleration.”

The definition of the threshold is a bit finicky, partly because two speedup conditions must be met (speedup relative to the counterfactual and speedup relative to the past). But on a plain reading, my best guess is that it has been met. The authors of the METR report estimated roughly a doubling in the rate of progress relative to the past, but they only attribute roughly 80% of the current speed-up to AI, which would mean the central estimate for observed speedup relative to no AI was roughly ~1.8x as of the time of writing their report, in October 2026. But they also found that the contribution of AI has been growing over time, and I would guess that now (as of November 2026) it is appreciably higher. Even if the rate of progress is not fully double the counterfactual right this second, the threshold incorporates reasonable expectations (“We observe or expect double the rate of progress…”).

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei explains the idea of compute multipliers in this essay: “The field is constantly coming up with ideas, large and small, that make things more effective or efficient: it could be an improvement to the architecture of the model (a tweak to the basic Transformer architecture that all of today’s models use) or simply a way of running the model more efficiently on the underlying hardware. New generations of hardware also have the same effect. What this typically does is shift the curve: if the innovation is a 2x “compute multiplier” (CM), then it allows you to get 40% on a coding task for $5M instead of $10M; or 60% for $50M instead of $100M, etc. Every frontier AI company regularly discovers many of these CM’s: frequently small ones (~1.2x), sometimes medium-sized ones (~2x), and every once in a while very large ones (~10x).”

This is a simplified version of the modeling that my colleague Ryan Greenblatt goes through here. His numbers are based on his prior public modeling of the factors like the importance of software vs training compute and the importance of labor vs experimental compute.

Common formulations of Cobb-Douglas often measure labor as the number of humans. In that formulation, there needs to be a parallelization penalty on top of labor and the coefficients don’t add to 1. As I discuss below, I’m choosing to represent labor in terms of serial labor acceleration rather than the number of humans; this formulation was suggested by Ryan Greenblatt in this post.

To make the math simple, I just assumed that labor and experiment compute contributed equally (i.e. compute multipliers = (labor)^0.5 * (compute)^0.5. This means you square the acceleration in compute multipliers to back out the acceleration in labor. The range I gave omits uncertainty on the size of the contributions: for example if labor contributed ⅔, you would instead need to raise it to the power of 3/2.

I say several months rather than a full year because the report authors say that the initial faster progress in late 2025 was in significant part due to other factors, though the current speedup is mainly due to AI.

In practice, the 10x uplift is unlikely to be uniform: some tasks (e.g. more “hill-climbable” tasks) will be sped up much more than others, for a net productivity improvement as big as a 10x uniform acceleration.

That is, any number of people that actually exist. I’d guess it would be sufficient if you could clone all the existing researchers several hundred times, but there aren’t that many strong AI researchers and engineers in the world.

From the report: “A key concern is that acceleration from the use of AI could start to compound, as the development of more capable models leads to further acceleration. As we describe below, we did not identify any clear bottlenecks (limited inputs that would prevent further growth) or steep diminishing returns to further scaling that would get in the way of such compounding acceleration, though our understanding evolved several times over the course of this assessment, and we have significant uncertainty. Because of this, we believe there is a significant risk that further capabilities progress could lead to even faster acceleration over the coming year.”

This heuristic argument is consistent with simple but plausible models where recursive self-improvement makes the rate of AI progress exponential in cumulative AI progress (e.g., log effective compute); here is a derivation of this heuristic argument for one parametrization of this class of models.

AI R&D-5 was defined as: “The ability to cause dramatic acceleration in the rate of effective scaling. Specifically, this would be the case if we observed or projected an increase in the effective training compute of the world’s most capable model that, over the course of a year, was equivalent to two years of the average rate of progress during the period of early 2018 to early 2024. We roughly estimate that the 2018-2024 average scaleup was around 35x per year, so this would imply an actual or projected one-year scaleup of 35^2 = ~1000x.” It is weaker because it compares to the fixed 2018-2024 period rather than the fastest observed pace without significant AI acceleration, and it doesn’t technically require that you do any attribution to AI to meet the threshold.

The minimum commitment was to reach RAND’s ASL-4 security level; my understanding is that this is a level of security that would protect model weights from a routine operation by top state actors (e.g., China), but not from a top-priority operation. This is how Anthropic describes it: “At minimum, the ASL-4 Security Standard (which would protect against model-weight theft by state-level adversaries) is required, although we expect a higher security standard may be required.”

Charter to Cut $2 Billion In Costs Next Year Under “New Operating Model”

That's not counting the $1 billion in its expected Cox synergies.

Charter Communications sees the writing on the wall. It reads: “You need to cut costs.”

Charter, which is the home to Spectrum, calls itself “America’s Internet Company” — and for good reason. Charter is the leading internet and video provider in the nation providing services to more than 70 million homes and businesses across 45 states. In August, Charter Communications combined with Communications.

Now, CEO Chris Winfrey is unveiling a major reorganization.

“With the combination of Charter and Cox, we have the scale and network capabilities to connect more Americans than ever before,” Winfrey said in a statement accompanying the company’s third-quarter earnings results.

“Our purpose is Connecting Gigabit America,” Winfrey said. “Today, we are announcing decisive steps to unlock our full potential.”

Winfrey continued, “We are fundamentally rethinking how we operate and compete, with a renewed focus on delivering the best products and services. We will return Charter to Internet customer growth and transform Charter into a simpler, faster, more agile organization — and reignite our culture to win. We have the assets and competitive advantages to succeed and are moving with urgency to deliver more value for our customers and stronger results for our investors.”

To that end, Charter is “targeting $2 billion in annualized run-rate operating expense savings over the next 18 months,” the accompanying press release read, “in addition to more than $1 billion in anticipated Cox Transactions synergies.”

The cost-cutting program “will focus on eliminating nonessential activities, simplifying organizationalstructures and driving greater efficiencies through vendor partnerships,” per Charter. “Together, these initiatives are expected to strengthen Charter’s competitive position, drive improved financial performance and support significantly higher free cash flow generation over time.”

If only the actual company earnings offered as much optimism. Charter lost $2.1 billion in Q3; the Charter side lost 156,000 internet customers; Cox lost 77,000.

Donovan Mitchell needs to shoot more, not less

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If you've been online recently, you may have noticed that Donovan Mitchell has… pissed off a lot of Cleveland Cavaliers fans.

Twitter is ablaze with criticism. People want him traded. Some regret that he was ever traded for. Mitchell has become the crux of all problems; the shot-chucking 'dictator' who will never be the primary option on a championship team.

But everyone has it backwards; Donovan Mitchell needs to shoot more.

Mitchell's field goal attempts and usage rate are at career lows to start the year.

His assist percentage and shot chart are nearly identical to previous seasons.

That means Mitchell has less control over the offense than ever, despite some insisting that he's grown into a ball-dominant tyrant.

How did we get here? And why are fans feeling such a disconnect?

Mitchell has always been a scorer more than a passer. He currently ranks in the 41st percentile in assist-to-usage ratio — a stat that tracks how often a player is ending their possessions with an assist rather than a shot attempt or turnover.

This means that roughly half of the league's players are more likely to get an assist on any given possession than Donovan Mitchell. That can lead to games where it feels like Mitchell is hogging the ball while leaving his teammates hanging.

That gung-ho play style caused some frustration throughout last year's playoffs, and the complaints have carried into this year.

Fans want to know why Evan Mobley and Jarrett Allen aren't getting the ball. Why do their leads disappear so often? And why haven't the Cavs gotten over the hump despite a roaring regular season?

Some have identified Mitchell's lack of playmaking as the problem.

Which, in fairness, is certainly a factor. Cleveland has lost big games in the past due to Mitchell's shot selection. He doesn't always make the right decision with the ball in his hands — and that can be difficult to watch as a fan.

But we should reframe this discussion. Let's start here.

Donovan Mitchell isn't a point guard. And maybe, he doesn't have to be.

Shai-Gilgeous Alexander was in the 29th percentile for assist-to-usage ratio when the Oklahoma City Thunder won the championship. He's in the 0th percentile now.

The truth is, championship-caliber players are allowed to have weak points. You are not disqualified if you can't do everything on your own.

Giannis Antetokounmpo never learned how to shoot. Nikola Jokic never became an elite defender. And the New York Knicks didn't win a championship last June because Jalen Brunson suddenly became an elite playmaker — they won because they delegated more playmaking responsibilities to Karl-Anthony Towns — leaving space for Brunson to do what Brunson does best… score the ball.

All of those teams identified their star's weakness, and built a roster around them that could insulate them.

The Cavs need a similar solution. They need someone who can take playmaking off of Mitchell's plate.

Darius Garland was the original fit. And, for a while, he would thread the needle beautifully. He sent the defense scattering into motion while Mitchell would catch the ball against a defense already on their back feet. That dynamic led to 64 wins — but unfortunately, Garland couldn't stay healthy.

James Harden was the next option. He's been more available than Garland, which was enough to help bolster the team to an Eastern Conference Finals run.

But Harden's style differs from Garland's. He's slower-paced, favoring a heliocentric style that inhibits stationary spacing around him. The result is fewer chances for Mitchell to attack in motion, nerfing his greatest strength as a slasher who can attack gaps.

All of this has culminated in the Cavs (and fans online) asking Mitchell to be more of a playmaker. But that is a fundamental misunderstanding of who he is — and how you win in the NBA by playing to your strengths.

I want to be clear that I am not advocating for Mitchell to go full Dion Waiters and ignore the other four Cavaliers sharing the court with him at any given time. He still needs to pass the ball — and he needs to choose the right moments to assert himself as a scorer.

You can't win by playing like a bull in a China shop. There has to be a delicate balance between scoring in bunches and releasing control of the offense to someone else. That's the key here.

Mitchell will never be a point guard. Any system that asks him to make reads at that level will learn the hard way. You can't force a square peg into a round hole.

You can, however, build a competitive team that understands who Mitchell is.

We know for a fact that Mitchell can go nuclear as a scorer in the playoffs. We've seen it multiple times.

But who can grab the reins and facilitate those high-scoring performances? Who is the Karl-Anthony Towns to his Jalen Brunson in this scenario?

The Cavs are still hoping that will be Harden. Additional support from Mobley would be welcomed. Whether that will be enough is certainly worth questioning.

But here's another cold, hard truth: Making it to the top of the mountain is hard. If we're being honest, it's damn near impossible. This franchise had the greatest player alive for 11 seasons and only made it to the finish line once.

Cleveland would need a myriad of miracles to win a championship with this group. Even if everything went right, they could still fall short. That's just the way it goes.

That's why sticking true to your identity is more important than anything. Being a bad version of someone else will always be inferior to being the best version of yourself.

I don't think Mitchell will lead Cleveland to a title through his playmaking. That feels like jumping the shark.

The Cavs do need him to be better defensively and to make better reads offensively, but that doesn't necessarily mean relinquishing his role as one of the highest-volume scorers in the league. That's who he is — that's who the team traded for.

Mitchell needs to be a world-class scorer. Nearly all of his attention on offense should be centered on getting his shot. Passing will always come second. Winning or losing comes down to Mitchell taking and making the big shots. That was the primary difference between him and Brunson last season.

Of course, any team would prefer a superstar who is capable of blending elite scoring with masterful playmaking and impactful defense. But, you work with what you have — and the Cavs have a dynamic scorer, not a floor general.

Accepting Mitchell's true nature and having the supporting cast insulate him with their playmaking is the most realistic path forward. It's the only path forward.
