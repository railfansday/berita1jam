XIIlab Approves KRW 10B Convertible Bond to Expand AI Data Center Business

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

South Korean AI infrastructure company XIIlab disclosed in a November 6, 2026 regulatory filing that its board approved a KRW 10 billion privately placed convertible bond for its AI data-center business. Subscription and cash payment are scheduled for November 16, 2026, so the disclosure records an approved financing, not a completed raise.

XIIlab’s announcement connects the proceeds to working capital for GPU-cluster construction and a broader operating model. The company plans to apply its AstraGo software to infrastructure it builds, pursue operations and management contracts, and generate recurring revenue alongside construction work.

The first-series bonds are bearer, unsecured and privately placed, with the full amount classified as operating funds. Both the coupon and maturity interest rates are 0%, no separate interest is paid before maturity, and principal is repayable at 100% of the electronically registered amount on November 16, 2031. Bonds held to maturity therefore retain a full repayment obligation despite the absence of coupon and maturity interest.

Conversion is set at KRW 8,106 per share and would produce 1,233,654 registered common shares if exercised in full. Investors can request conversion from November 16, 2027 through October 16, 2031. The bonds exclude a share-price-decline refixing clause, although separate adjustments apply to specified corporate actions, including below-market share issuance, mergers, capital reductions, stock splits and consolidations.

XIIlab or its designee can call up to 50% of each investor’s original subscription, capped at KRW 5 billion in total, at quarterly dates from November 16, 2027 through November 16, 2028. The company presents that option as a way to reduce potential dilution if its share price rises. Investors hold a counterbalancing put: they can request early repayment at 100% from May 16, 2029 and at subsequent quarterly dates. Taken together, the provisions balance potential equity dilution against a company-side purchase option and preserve a creditor route to seek cash repayment before maturity.

The planned subscriptions comprise KRW 8 billion from National Growth Fund sub-funds managed by Korea Investment Value Asset Management, DB Asset Management, Susung Asset Management and Orion Asset Management, and KRW 2 billion from other Orion-managed funds. XIIlab characterizes the National Growth Fund as a policy vehicle created to foster advanced strategic industries including AI, semiconductors and biotechnology, and places its AI infrastructure business within the fund’s principal investment targets.

The spending schedule assigns KRW 5 billion to 2027 and KRW 5 billion to 2028 and later, all for AI data-center-related business. XIIlab said it is securing construction working capital ahead of expected demand for newer GPUs, including Vera Rubin, and intends to expand GPU-cluster construction in its role as an NVIDIA Elite partner.

XIIlab’s AstraGo product documentation describes a Kubernetes-based platform for allocating GPU resources, running training and inference workloads, and managing resources and workloads in one environment. It supports existing NVIDIA GPU servers and specified Furiosa and DeepX NPUs, multi-server clusters, and on-premises or closed-network deployments. Pricing combines an annual subscription license with a per-node server installation charge, giving the operations plan a subscription-based commercial structure.

XIIlab launched AstraGo 2.1 on April 1, 2026, saying the release partitions one physical GPU across isolated inference workloads, runs training and inference in the same cluster, prioritizes resources for real-time inference, and separates allocation and usage histories by organization or service in a multi-tenant structure. Those are the operational functions XIIlab plans to develop further as it adds data-center management work.

The financing connects XIIlab’s GPU-cluster construction pipeline with an AstraGo-based operations model built around annual subscriptions and continuing management work. The central question for its medium-term direction is whether that project activity becomes recurring operations revenue while the company manages the dilution and cash-repayment choices embedded in the bonds.

Palo Alto Networks Explores Clutch Security Acquisition

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

TechCrunch reported on November 6, 2026 that Palo Alto Networks is in talks to acquire Clutch Security for $500 million in cash plus additional milestone-based payouts. The report describes discussions rather than a signed or completed acquisition, and neither company responded to TechCrunch’s requests for comment.

The person familiar with the discussions said Clutch was approaching $10 million in annual recurring revenue and had most recently been valued at $85 million in January 2025. TechCrunch characterized the proposed cash price as roughly 50 times that recurring revenue.

Clutch’s own January 30, 2025 Series A announcement says SignalFire led the $20 million round, with participation from existing investors Lightspeed Venture Partners and Merlin Ventures, bringing total funding to $28.5 million. The proceeds were designated for go-to-market expansion, research and development including Identity Lineage and Zero Trust controls, and customer-success staffing.

Clutch defines non-human identities as API keys, secrets, service accounts, tokens and certificates used across cloud infrastructure, CI/CD pipelines and AI-driven systems. These credentials give software access to enterprise resources, making their permissions and lineage central to agent security.

Clutch says its discovery product inventories agents across endpoints, SaaS applications, self-hosted infrastructure and cloud environments. It then maps each agent to its deployer, tools, authentication identities and reachable resources, producing a single inventory spanning all four environment types.

Clutch says its agent guardrails extend policies across that chain, covering the person, agent, tools, identities and resources. Its examples include restricting access to production infrastructure, protected branches, MCP servers and stored credentials. Enforcement can allow a tool call in development while blocking it in production, and each policy trigger and enforcement decision is recorded with lineage context.

That control chain closely resembles capabilities described in Palo Alto Networks’ fiscal 2026 annual report. Its Idira platform secures human, agentic and machine identities, while its AI Agents Security offering discovers agents, assigns identity attributes, limits access to task-specific resources, records activity and can suspend or revoke access. Secure AI Agents also scans SaaS, cloud and developer environments, places agents in a central registry, applies gateway guardrails and issues short-lived permissions that are revoked when a task ends.

The annual report also connects that portfolio to a series of acquisitions. CyberArk formed the identity-security platform, Koi added agentic endpoint security and enhanced Prisma AIRS, Portkey enhanced its AI gateway capabilities, and Console is expected to deepen agentic capabilities in Cortex. Clutch’s focus on discovery, identity lineage and context-aware enforcement therefore intersects with an existing product buildout spanning identity, endpoints and AI gateways.

Cyera announced on September 3, 2026 that it had completed its $1 billion acquisition of Oasis Security, combining its data-security platform with Oasis’s non-human-identity access-management technology. Oasis, founded in 2022, developed continuous, policy-driven controls over which workloads and agents can access systems, in what context and for how long. Cyera said the platform would operate as Cyera Identity, its dedicated identity pillar.

The Oasis deal supplies a completed comparison point for consolidation in agent identity; it does not show that Clutch will be acquired. For now, the reported talks pair close technical overlap with no signed agreement. Whether Clutch’s focused controls become part of Palo Alto Networks’ expanding identity portfolio remains the central open question.

Local LLMs vs Cloud AI: Choosing the Right Solution for...

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 6 • Originally published at veduis.com on Nov 6

The explosion of large language model capabilities has created a fundamental choice for small businesses: rely on cloud AI services like ChatGPT and Claude, or run AI models locally on owned hardware. Each approach carries distinct implications for privacy, cost, performance, and operational complexity.

This comparison examines both options through the lens of small business requirements, providing the framework needed to make an informed decision based on specific circumstances rather than general hype.

Before diving into comparisons, clarity on what each approach actually means helps frame the discussion.

Cloud AI refers to large language models hosted by companies like OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Microsoft. Users send queries over the internet to remote servers where powerful hardware processes requests and returns responses. The model itself never touches the user's device.

Local LLMs are AI models that run entirely on hardware the user owns or controls. The model files download to local storage, and all processing happens on local CPUs, GPUs, or specialized AI accelerators. No data leaves the local environment during inference.

For many small businesses, privacy concerns drive the local LLM consideration more than any other factor.

When using cloud AI services, every prompt and response passes through third-party infrastructure:

OpenAI's business tier and API usage exclude data from training by default, but enterprise agreements provide stronger guarantees. Consumer ChatGPT usage may contribute to model training unless users opt out. Similar variations exist across providers.

For businesses handling client confidential information, healthcare data, legal documents, or proprietary processes, cloud AI usage requires careful evaluation of provider terms and potential liability.

Local deployment eliminates third-party data exposure entirely:

A law firm using AI to summarize case documents gains significant advantage from local deployment. Client privilege concerns that might prohibit cloud AI usage disappear when processing happens entirely on firm-controlled hardware.

Cost comparisons require honest accounting of all expenses, not just subscription fees.

Moderate usage with ChatGPT Team: $25 × 5 × 12 = $1,500/year

Heavy API usage (1M tokens/month): Approximately $300-500/month = $3,600-6,000/year

Local deployment requires upfront hardware investment with minimal ongoing costs.

A $2,000 hardware investment for medium-capability local LLM breaks even against $125/month cloud spending in 16 months. After break-even, local operation costs only electricity.

Raw capability differences between cloud and local options have narrowed significantly but remain relevant.

The largest, most capable models remain cloud-exclusive. GPT-4, Claude 3 Opus, and Gemini Ultra offer reasoning capabilities that exceed what runs efficiently on consumer hardware.

Cloud providers operate massive GPU clusters improved for inference. Response times typically range from 1-10 seconds for most queries, with streaming output beginning almost immediately.

Open-source models have improved dramatically. Meta's Llama 3 family, Mistral models, and community fine-tunes offer impressive capabilities:

Local performance depends entirely on hardware:

Larger models run proportionally slower. A 70B model might generate 10-20 tokens/second on high-end consumer hardware.

For businesses ready to examine local deployment, these tools provide the most accessible entry points.

Ollama provides the easiest path to running local LLMs. A single command downloads and runs models with sensible defaults.

Ollama exposes a local API compatible with many applications:

LM Studio offers a graphical interface for users preferring visual model management over command line.

Install Ollama or LM Studio on individual workstations. Each user maintains their own model installation. Simple but redundant.

Deploy models on a dedicated server accessible across the local network. All team members access the same instance:

Use local LLMs for sensitive processing while maintaining cloud access for tasks requiring peak capability. Route requests based on content sensitivity and complexity.

Different business scenarios favor different approaches.

Processing internal documents, contracts, meeting notes, and reports works excellently with local models. No external transmission, consistent performance, and 7B-13B models handle summarization well.

Developers working with proprietary codebases benefit from local code models like CodeLlama. IDE integrations like Continue work smoothly with local Ollama backends.

Parsing information from documents, emails, or databases involves potentially sensitive data. Local processing eliminates exposure concerns while capable models handle structured extraction tasks effectively.

Generating response drafts for customer inquiries allows local AI to help with routine communications while human review ensures quality before sending.

Tasks requiring deep reasoning, multi-step problem solving, or synthesis across broad knowledge domains benefit from the largest cloud models.

Marketing copy, blog posts, and creative writing often benefit from the nuanced language capabilities of frontier models.

Occasional research queries where peak capability matters more than privacy work well with cloud services.

Translation and multilingual content generation typically perform better on large cloud models trained on extensive multilingual data.

Use this framework to guide the cloud vs. local decision for specific business contexts.

The local LLM landscape evolves rapidly, with several trends worth monitoring.

New AI accelerators from Apple, AMD, and dedicated AI chip companies continue improving local inference performance. Hardware purchased today will run future improved models faster.

Research in model compression, quantization, and architecture improvements steadily closes the capability gap between what runs locally and cloud-exclusive models.

Commercial offerings for enterprise local AI deployment are emerging, providing managed solutions for businesses wanting local benefits with reduced operational overhead.

Increasing data privacy regulation may force certain industries toward local processing regardless of capability tradeoffs.

The choice between local and cloud AI is not permanent. Many businesses start with cloud services for immediate capability access, then migrate specific workloads to local deployment as use cases mature and privacy requirements clarify. The key is making an informed initial decision while remaining flexible as both technology and business needs evolve.

How we moved our agents from personal terminals into team channels

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 6 • Originally published at agentconnect.md

In six months, a team of under ten went from editor autocomplete to about thirty agents with standing jobs, from cluster checks and code review to releases, fork maintenance and customer support, plus the permissions and context sharing around them.

Over the past few months, AI has fundamentally changed how our team works. Much of our recurring work is now genuinely automated by agents: the daily cluster inspection and alert analysis, pull request review, maintenance of an upstream fork, release notes, and answering questions in customer groups. We are an engineering team of fewer than ten people.

A year ago, "using AI" meant an editor with AI in it: completions, rewriting a function, explaining unfamiliar code. Everyone used it, and nothing about how the team worked changed. The output was still a diff on someone's machine. Nobody else saw the conversation, and there was nothing in it worth seeing.

We did not build anything around it. The models were not yet at the point where you could hand them a task and walk away.

The turning point was Claude Opus 4.6. It was the first model we used that was worth building around: give it a task and it runs commands, reads the results and keeps going. Within weeks Claude Code and Codex were everyone's daily tools.

Then people started giving them work other than code. A production error: let an agent take the first look. An upstream release: let an agent upgrade our fork. Inspect the cluster every morning. Take customer questions. Within a month every engineer had accumulated a pile of their own glue: shell scripts, cron entries, a bot token in a dotfile, a prompt that stitched together the context the agent needed.

The first was duplication. When we compared what each of us had written, it was nearly the same: how messages get in and out, how to schedule, where credentials live, how to assemble context. Everyone had done it once, and each copy only ran on its owner's machine.

The second was lost context. The most useful part of a session is usually not the final diff but the process in the middle: what was tried, what failed, why this fix over that one, which dashboard had the answer. All of it sat in one person's terminal and was gone when the session ended. When someone else needed it, the only way to get it was a meeting.

In June we tried the most direct approach: a set of Slack bots built on the open-source OpenACP, changed so that each thread in an existing channel became one session.

Someone mentioned an agent in an incident thread, the rest of the team watched what it was checking, and asked follow-up questions in the same thread. Colleagues outside engineering started using the bots too; a mention in Slack is all it takes.

Then came two weeks of patches, all about threads: carrying the earlier thread into a mid-thread mention, backfilling messages the bot had missed, stopping bots from triggering each other in a loop. The software had no concept of "a conversation with several people and several bots in it", and we were bolting one on.

One platform app with several agents behind it, instead of one process, one Slack app and one set of tokens per bot

Separate identities for several users, instead of one shared login on one machine

A real control plane, instead of a bot that ran operational shell commands from the chat

Versioned daemons with controlled upgrades

Sessions that survive a restart, each with its own workspace, instead of one directory shared by every thread

Conversations and threads as core concepts, not adapter patches

We also tried replacing the whole thing with Hermes. Its demo covered most of what we wanted: loops, proactive messages, a kanban board and memory, all built in. We moved a rebase bot onto it and ran it for a day. The biggest problem was that it had no team layer: who may use which agent, who may see its sessions, which repositories and tools an agent may touch. There was no permission model; the whole thing assumed one user. Beyond that, its own harness and Claude Code ran interleaved inside one task, and without the coding agent's cache and resume, every turn re-sent the full context, so latency and cost were both high. When something failed, we could not tell which layer had failed. This describes the version we tried at the time; Hermes has changed a great deal since.

Two decisions from that time have not changed: permissions are part of the platform, not a feature to add later; and we keep the harness engineers already run locally, so that any behaviour seen in a channel can be reproduced in a terminal.

We built the connecting layer ourselves, on ACP, and open-sourced it a month later as AgentConnect.

The production organization was created on a Sunday in July. By Tuesday six agents were running, the same jobs people had been running from personal scripts, now each with a name, a channel and a machine. Today:

About thirty agents: one generalist, the rest specialists

Running on seven of our own machines plus a managed pool

Connected to Slack, Telegram, Discord and Linear

The generalist is connected to the development channel, direct messages and Linear. It can check out the team's repositories on demand, and anyone can mention it. Small changes are done right in the Slack thread: describe the problem, it edits the code, opens a pull request and runs the checks, and a person glances at it in the thread and merges, without opening a terminal.

Each specialist owns one kind of work, with its own repositories, tools, channels and schedules, and permissions scoped to that work.

Design documents and meetings. One agent owns documentation. Design discussions mention it in the development channel; it reads the current system and the existing design documents and proposes a path, colleagues add constraints in the thread, and it revises. Once the discussion settles, it opens a pull request that writes the conclusion into the matching design document under docs/, reviewed together with the implementation. Every meeting is transcribed. We do not use the transcription service's own summaries; the raw transcript goes into a separate repository, the agent writes the summary there, and then updates the related issues and design documents in the other repositories.

The documentation repository is organized by project, and each project has two parts, design documents and meetings:

Inspection and alerts. One agent walks the cluster every morning and posts a report to the alerts channel. A few others each watch one class of service in a business area for latency and versions. When an alert lands in the channel, the on-call agent picks it up in the same thread: it checks Grafana metrics, looks at pods and logs, and compares against recent changes in the repository. Kubernetes and Grafana are MCP services, registered once at the organization level and enabled per agent. How each class of event should be handled is written as a skill in the repository: which metrics to check first, the common causes, what it may handle on its own and when it must call a person. When the procedure changes, the skill changes, and every on-call agent uses the new one from the next run. Whether it is an incident is the engineer's call.

Cross-model code review. Every pull request gets a first pass from the review bot, which leaves inline comments and posts its own check. The same review bot can switch runtime and model per pull request, and different models notice different things. Running on Codex, it reproduced a bug in one pull request: editing a scheduled task's time before saving dropped the saved task's identity. It requested changes, and approved once the fix landed. Running on Claude, it approved another pull request and listed exactly what it had not verified and was leaving to CI. For a deeper look, someone mentions a reviewer on a stronger model.

Releases. Every merge to main produces a release candidate; a stable release points the release branch at a chosen commit. What happens after the release is the agent's job: confirm the release succeeded, read the pull requests between the two stable versions, group related pull requests into one feature entry, call out behaviour changes and upgrade notes separately, write that as the Summary at the top of the release page, and then comment on and close each issue the release fully fixed. The Summary on v2.8.0 came from this; the Features list below it is the generated changelog. The procedure is a skill, so the rules are the same whichever agent runs it.

The upstream fork. We maintain a fork of an upstream runtime adapter: the upstream tag plus a pile of our own patches. The patches carry logic, so when upstream refactors, the job is not resolving a few lines of conflict. It is understanding what upstream changed and what our patch was for, then re-implementing the intent on the new base. The whole thing goes to a coding agent: an upstream release or a schedule starts it, it reads both sides' changes, decides which patches are still needed, which upstream has absorbed and can be dropped, and which need rewriting, makes the changes in its own worktree, runs typecheck, tests and build, opens a pull request, and posts what needs a human judgement to the channel. That repository now has fourteen rebase branches, one per upstream version, with the check results beside each. Merging and publishing are done by a person.

Customer group support. A support agent answers questions in customers' Telegram groups and escalates what it cannot answer to an engineering channel in Slack. The groups get a lot of spam, so each message is scored first; only messages under the threshold reach the support agent, and the rest go to a moderator agent. Replies about a specific account are confirmed by a person before they go out.

The generalist can reach many repositories and sits in public channels, private channels and direct messages at once, so permissions cannot be skipped. We handle them in three layers.

The agent itself: who may change its configuration, which repositories and tools it may touch, who may use it in which channels, and which agents may call it or be called by it, each set separately.

Sessions: who may read a session follows the membership of the place the conversation happened. A session in a private channel is readable only by people currently in that channel; whoever leaves the channel can no longer read it. Direct messages stay private. There is no separate list to maintain.

Memory is layered by where the conversation happened: what was said somewhere is remembered there. What an agent learns in a public channel goes into the shared layer, a private or external group gets its own layer, and a direct message gets that person's layer. When it answers, the agent loads only the memory the current conversation is allowed to read.

Operations that need approval send an approval card by direct message to the agent's editors, rather than letting anyone in the channel approve.

What an agent produces has to be kept somewhere the team and the other agents can find later, with access still controlled by where it came from.

Design documents live in the same repository as the code, under docs/, with implementation status and links to issues and pull requests. The project instructions file says where the documents are and that a change to a design and its implementation go in the same pull request. The product repository has gone from 35 design documents at open-sourcing to 100 now. A reviewer reads the linked design document directly instead of asking the author to explain it in a meeting. Anything beyond a single repository, runbooks and architecture decisions, goes into the organization's knowledge library: owners publish it, it is versioned, and agents look it up on demand.

How to handle a class of alert, how to write a release description, how to rebase the fork: each is a skill file, imported once and enabled per agent. Once, after the agent that checks the price list every day posted its report, a colleague asked in the thread whether it had failed to find the matching skill. It confirmed it had not found it and had instead done a read-only comparison following the channel's earlier practice. The colleague pointed out the skill was in the repository. The agent found it, confirmed its earlier search had skipped hidden directories by default, and recorded the problem in its working conventions for later runs.

An agent's workspace is a Git repository, so what one agent writes, any agent's next session can read; the daemons on colleagues' own machines use the same repositories. Tickets are maintained by the agent doing the work: updated as it progresses, closed when done, linked to the documents and pull requests.

The local Claude Code and Codex memory mechanism is unchanged; we only point the memory directory at a Git repository, with hooks that pull at session start and push at session end, so it is the same memory across machines and harnesses. The platform agents' memory is a separate system, layered by conversation source as described above. Merging the two is something we may do later.

Today's agents are triggered by a mention, a schedule, a pull request or issue event, a webhook, or a judgement (is this spam, which agent should take it), and they do the one thing the trigger specifies.

Proactive agents. Make the agent more like a teammate: it checks the things it is responsible for and proposes before acting. For example, it notices that a just-merged pull request changed behaviour and the matching design document was not updated, and proposes a documentation pull request in the development channel; or the same question has come up three times this week in a customer group, and it proposes writing the answer into the docs. It acts only once a person approves. This is in preview.

Computer use. Some things have no API, only a desktop app or a browser. Register a machine as a Computer, grant it to specific agents, and they can take screenshots, click and type; a person watches the live screen in the console and can take over at any time. Also in preview.

Joining meetings. Today a meeting is recorded, transcribed and processed afterwards by the documentation agent. Further out, the agent joins the meeting as a participant: it can be asked questions during the meeting, and decisions get written back to design documents and issues on the spot rather than afterwards. This has not started.

AgentConnect is open source under Apache 2.0 and deploys with Docker Compose or Kubernetes. If you hit a problem, open an issue; if you find it useful, you are welcome to star the repository.

Two Paths to the Future: The Paradigm Shift Facing Every Developer

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The current conversation surrounding AI and software engineering is dominated by anxiety. We debate when AI will replace us, why our workloads are increasing despite automation, and how the junior developer funnel is changing. However, these discussions often assume one fundamental thing: that our ultimate goal as developers must always be to *remain employees of traditional corporations. * If AI systematically shifts the job market, the lesson we must learn isn't to panic. Instead, we must realize that a major crossroads has emerged. Every technologist must now consciously choose which path to follow and understand the tradeoffs involved.

*Path 1: The Corporate Specialist (Labor Leverage) * This is the traditional route. In this ecosystem, you adapt to becoming a system governor, architect, and debugger-of-last-resort. You focus on managing complex legacy codebases, integrating advanced architectures like WebMCP, and guiding armies of autonomous agents to deliver enterprise objectives.

The advantage of this path is structure, scale, and access to massive corporate resources. However, the macroeconomic reality is hard to ignore. If major enterprises use autonomous agents to massively downsize their engineering departments, systemic employment drops.

This creates a dangerous economic loop: if disposable income vanishes across the workforce, who will buy the products these automated companies create? Relying solely on corporate hiring pipelines means tying your career security to an increasingly volatile and unpredictable corporate lifecycle.

*Path 2: The Sovereign Developer (AI Leverage) * This path represents a shift toward true ownership and self-reliance. Instead of studying solely to fit into a company's pre-existing architecture, you study to build independent systems.

The very same AI capabilities that allow a corporation to run with fewer people also give a single individual the production power of an entire traditional IT department. Historically, solo developers were severely bottlenecked by time and specialization. You could write great backend logic, but you might struggle with UI design, marketing, or cloud infrastructure.

Today, autonomous agents act as the ultimate force multipliers, seamlessly filling those execution gaps. On this path, your goal isn't to look for a seat at the corporate table. Instead, you use AI to build what those companies do, but directly for yourself, your clients, or your local community. You shift your focus from selling your labor to owning your product.

*The Decision Before Us * The future of technology is no longer just about learning the next popular framework or programming language; it is about choosing your leverage model.

Do you want to optimize your skills to remain indispensable to a legacy corporation? Or do you want to use the democratization of AI tools to build your own independent ecosystem? Both paths require deep technical mastery, critical thinking, and intense dedication, but they lead to entirely different destinations.

We don't need to stop working or stop learning. We just need to decide who we are building for. The tools are entirely in your hands, and the choice is yours. Which path are you choosing?
