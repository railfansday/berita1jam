Scientists Build 6,986 Digital Brain Twins to Study Dementia

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An international consortium has created personalized biophysical “digital brain twins” for 6,986 individuals across 16 countries to uncover how aging, neurodegenerative diseases, and environmental conditions shape neural dynamics. The computational models simulate excitation-inhibition balance across 90 connected brain regions, revealing distinct mechanistic signatures for Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal degeneration, and environmental exposures ranging from air pollution to democratic governance.

Standard magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides an invaluable window into the living human brain, yet it records only indirect proxies of neural activity, typically relying on group averages that obscure individual variation and say little about the underlying cellular mechanisms driving those signals.

Now, an international research team led by corresponding author Dr. Agustín Ibáñez (Scientific Director of BrainLat at Universidad Adolfo Ibáñez, strategic director of core scientific assets at the BarcelonaBeta Brain Research Center, and faculty at the Global Brain Health Institute at Trinity College Dublin) and first author Dr. Rubén Herzog (BrainLat and Distinguished Professor at the University of the Balearic Islands) has transformed static brain scans into dynamic, personalized virtual laboratories.

Published today in Nature Biomedical Engineering, the study introduces biophysical “digital brain twins” constructed from resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) scans of 6,986 individuals spanning ages 18 to 100 across 16 countries.

Rather than treating brain scans as passive statistical snapshots, biophysical brain twins apply mathematical equations grounded in neurobiology to simulate how populations of excitatory and inhibitory neurons interact across 90 connected brain regions.

For each participant, the investigators tuned their computational model until its simulated fMRI output closely matched the individual’s actual resting-state brain recordings. Independent validation showed that model-derived regional excitability closely correlated with structural grey matter volume, confirming that the virtual simulations capture real biological architecture.

“Every brain ages in a different context. Our biophysical twins help us study how the variations in our biology and the social and environmental conditions of our lives relate to brain function,” explained Ibáñez. “The importance of biophysical brain twins is that they connect what brain scans record with the underlying processes that could produce those signals. Each twin acts as a personalised virtual laboratory: researchers can adjust how its neural populations interact and examine how those changes affect the whole brain. This opens a route from observing differences to testing possible explanations, mechanisms, potential treatments and interventions.”

“MRI records indirect measures of neural activity. Biophysical twins let us simulate the neural processes that might generate those signals and explore why they differ from person to person,” added Herzog.

Applying these nearly 7,000 personalized models across diverse clinical cohorts exposed clear mechanistic boundaries between normal aging and dementia:

To overcome the historical divide between neuroimaging and real-world lived context, the team linked individual brain models with macro-level environmental indicators across 10 domains alongside personal socioeconomic histories.

Robust associations emerged in seven of the ten environmental domains, most prominently in indicators measuring democratic conditions (free choices, civic participation, political representation, and rule of law), soil and water quality, and air pollution. Complementary analyses of individual life circumstances further linked brain dynamics to healthcare access, childhood experiences, and financial stability. These relationships persisted even after controlling for age, sex, clinical diagnosis, country, and MRI scanner hardware.

By releasing their open-source code and model parameters, the authors have established a global testbed for in silico experimentation. Future studies can overlay neurotransmitter receptor maps onto these digital twins—simulating how targeted modulation of glutamate (excitatory) or GABA (inhibitory) signaling, or public health improvements in air quality and healthcare access, might shift an aging brain back toward healthy functional patterns.

What is a biophysical “digital brain twin”?

A digital brain twin is a personalized computer simulation built from an individual’s fMRI scan using biological equations. By modeling how 90 connected brain regions interact, it allows researchers to estimate underlying cellular processes, such as the balance between signals that excite or inhibit neurons—and test virtual treatments safely on a computer.

How do brain dynamics differ between normal aging and dementia?

In the study of nearly 7,000 people, normal aging primarily changed how regularly or unpredictably brain activity fluctuated over time. In contrast, Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal degeneration disrupted overall activity levels, variability, and the balance between excitatory and inhibitory brain signals all at once.

Can environmental and social conditions really affect how the brain functions?

Yes. Even after accounting for age, sex, diagnosis, and scanner differences, researchers found that environmental factors, especially democratic conditions, soil and water quality, air pollution, healthcare access, and financial circumstances, were measurably linked to underlying simulated brain dynamics.

Biophysical brain twins map the phenotype–expotype architecture of ageing and dementia

Biophysical whole-brain models may reveal causal links between neural dynamics and biological–environmental variation, yet no framework has explained how these factors become embedded in mechanistic dynamics.

Here we develop individualized biophysical digital twins mechanistically linked to phenotypes and expotypes (that is, combinations of exposomal influences) leveraging resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging data (n = 6,986, 16 countries) of healthy controls and participants with mild cognitive impairment, Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal dementia.

Personalized twins improved goodness-of-fit over group-level models. They reproduced structure–function coupling between regional excitability and brain volume, captured age, sex and neurodegeneration, and encoded 68 expotypes.

Associations remained robust after controlling for demographics, data quality, model fit and scanner/site, with the strongest effects for social and physical (pollution) exposomes. We release an open resource (empirical data, code, >3 million simulations) for future research.

These findings ground phenotypic–exposomic variation in individualized biophysical twins, enabling in silico testing of mechanisms and potential model-informed interventions.

When discussing movies from the ‘90s, there are some films that get mentioned all the time. I'm talking about movies like Pulp Fiction, The Silence of the Lambs, and, of course, The Shawshank Redemption (Even though I think it's just “good” and not great).

I'd also be remiss if I didn’t bring up films that came out during the Disney Renaissance, such as The Lion King and Aladdin. Why am I bringing up those movies? You already know them and have probably already seen them. In fact, you can find many of the most beloved films from the ‘90s on our list of the 100 best films from that decade.

However, do you want to know what movie ISN’T on our list? The 1995 masterpiece (Yes, masterpiece) 12 Monkeys. I actually think it’s one of the best films from that decade, and here’s why.

A lot of people like to bring up great opening scenes in films, but do you know what I don’t think gets enough credit? Great opening songs, and look, I get it. Many older, typically black-and-white films (or westerns!) began with opening credits and stirring music before we met the characters. But nowadays, most films jump right into the action (in medias res, as it were).

However, 12 Monkeys probably has my favorite intro from the ‘90s, and it’s entirely because of that accordion-driven song, “Suite Punta del Este” by the Argentine composer, Astor Pantaleón Piazzolla. This is after we get a green text scrawl telling us that 5 billion people will die from a deadly virus, and that the survivors will move underground. Oh, and these are the words from a diagnosed paranoid schizophrenic.

This text is then followed up by the aforementioned, driving accordion song, and then a close-up of a child’s eyes as the kid watches somebody get gunned down in an airport. Absolute cinema!

I love a good time travel story, and 12 Monkeys has one of my favorites of any film. The story concerns a convict named Cole (Bruce Willis) who is sent back in time to investigate what caused the virus that killed 99% of the human population. He’s tasked with learning who the “Army of the 12 Monkeys” are, since they’re suspected of releasing the virus, as there are records of them writing, “We did it!”

Sounds simple enough. But the problem is, Cole’s time traveling gets overshot, and he ends up in 1990 rather than 1996. Because he sounds crazy - talking about the end of the world and whatnot - he gets committed to a mental institution. It’s here that he meets another patient, played by Brad Pitt, but is then yanked back into the future, only to be sent to World War 1.

There’s a lot more wonky time travel shenanigans, but we learn what the message of “We did it!” meant (and it has nothing to do with the virus). We also learn that the child from the beginning who witnessed the shooting is actually Cole witnessing himself as an adult getting shot! Trippy!

Lastly, Brad Pitt is just so good in this movie. He plays a mental asylum patient, and the closest thing I can compare his performance to is Johnny Depp wigging out in Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas.

Pitt’s character, Jeffrey Goines, looks both extremely tired and also hopped up on goofballs at the same time. He’s erratic, unpredictable, and simultaneously both funny and scary. It’s little wonder why he was nominated for Best Supporting Actor for this performance.

In every way, the acting, the storytelling, the music, the everything! just makes 12 Monkeys one of the best movies of the ‘90s, and an absolute gem that more people need to talk about.

That said, he’ll take Chicago Deep Dish pizza over a New York slice any day of the week. Don’t hate. Coldcard reveals how Bitcoin wallet flaw went undetected for over 5 years

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Coldcard maker Coinkite explained why years of security checks failed to catch the flaw and said it has yet to decide on compensation for affected customers.

Bitcoin hardware wallet maker Coinkite has detailed how a security flaw in its Coldcard devices went undetected for more than five years despite several independent security reviews, including those using artificial intelligence (AI).

In a Nov. 23 postmortem report, Coinkite said that while its security checks confirmed the wallets had the correct system for generating secure recovery phrases, the company never verified whether the wallets were actually using it. As a result, a software error caused the wallets to use a weaker system instead, making the recovery phrases easier for hackers to guess.

A Bitcoin hardware wallet is a physical device that stores private keys offline, while a recovery phrase is a series of words that allows users to restore access to their funds if the device is lost or damaged.

Coinkite said at least 15 independent security reviews and research efforts failed to catch the flaw before the thefts began. It added that the weaker system went undetected because the recovery phrases looked normal and could still pass some security tests.

The flaw allowed hackers to steal at least 1,789 Bitcoin (BTC), worth around $114.7 million at the time of the thefts, Galaxy Research reported in August. Coinkite said it fixed the flaw on July 31 and introduced further security measures in August.

The report says that as of Nov. 18, Coinkite had received around 300 loss-related support tickets associated with 242 accounts. The company also said that the actual number of victims was likely higher and that it is still exploring ways to help affected customers but has yet to decide on a compensation plan.

Coinkite said it still does not know who stole the funds or how the hackers found the flaw. The company said law enforcement authorities are also investigating the thefts, and the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security has opened a separate case.

The company continues to urge affected users to update their software, create new recovery phrases and move their funds to new wallets, warning that software updates cannot make previously affected recovery phrases safe.

Cointelegraph contacted Coinkite for comment on its compensation plans but had not received a response by publication.

2 Montgomery County magnet stories raise 1 question about access

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

“Minorities in Magnets” was the winner of the ABA Section of Civil Rights and Social Justice’s 2026 High School Civil Rights Film Competition.

A high school filmmaker saw a problem in Montgomery County, Maryland’s selective programs: Lack of representation of students of color had an impact on their educational experiences. At nearly the same time, a lawsuit challenging admissions to the county’s middle school magnet programs reached the U.S. Supreme Court.

Although these stories involve different programs and raise different questions, they share an important issue: How do we determine whether an admissions system provides fair access to educational opportunities, and what evidence should matter?

The ABA Section of Civil Rights and Social Justice recognized Minorities in Magnets, directed by Toftaa Fedhaa, as the first place winner of the section’s 2026 High School Civil Rights Film Competition. Her film explores the experiences of students of color in the Montgomery County Public Schools’ high school magnet programs and the effects of their underrepresentation.

Separately, on Oct. 19, the Supreme Court agreed to hear Association for Education Fairness v. Montgomery County Board of Education. The case concerns admissions to four selective middle school magnet programs, not the high school programs discussed in the film.

While the court did not rule on whether the district’s admissions policy is discriminatory, it agreed to consider a narrower legal question about the evidence plaintiffs must present when challenging a facially neutral admissions policy as racially discriminatory.

The Association for Education Fairness alleges that Montgomery County’s admissions policies intentionally disadvantaged Asian American students. The school district maintains that its admissions system is designed to provide equitable access to rigorous educational opportunities.

After initially challenging a “field test,” the plaintiffs challenged a revised lottery-based system introduced for the 2021-2022 school year. That system screened students for their potential to benefit from enriched instruction and then used a lottery to assign students to magnet programs or enriched instruction at their neighborhood schools.

The federal district court dismissed the challenge to the revised plan, finding that the plaintiffs had not adequately alleged discriminatory effect or intent. The 4th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals at Richmond, Virginia, affirmed, applying circuit precedent on disparate impact allegations.

The Supreme Court will now consider whether a plaintiff challenging a facially neutral admissions policy must demonstrate that the affected group experienced an aggregate “disparate impact” compared with its share of the applicant pool.

Put simply, “disparate impact” refers to a policy that produces unequal outcomes for a particular group, even when the policy does not expressly classify individuals by race. An equal protection challenge also raises the question of discriminatory purpose.

The court’s decision will address the legal standards governing these types of claims and whether a particular showing of aggregate impact is required before such a claim may proceed, not whether Montgomery County’s policy ultimately violates the Constitution. The court has not yet decided the merits of the case.

The film underscores why the conversation should not only focus exclusively on admissions policies, statistics and legal standards but should also consider the voices and experiences of students.

The file and the lawsuit should not be confused or treated as evidence of one another. They involve different grade levels, programs and legal questions. The experiences described in the film do not establish what happened in the middle school admissions process, just as the allegations in the lawsuit do not resolve the concerns raised by high school students.

Although selective educational programs can open doors to advanced coursework, specialized instruction and opportunities that shape a student’s academic future, simply making a program available to everyone does not necessarily mean everyone has an equal opportunity to participate.

We must also consider who knows these programs exist, how students are identified and encouraged to apply, what barriers may discourage participation, and whether students feel represented and welcome once they are admitted.

These are important questions for educators, families, policymakers and communities to consider. They may also inform legal analysis, although the applicable legal standards determine what evidence is relevant to a particular claim.

For lawyers, the Supreme Court case highlights the importance of understanding how statistical evidence, applicant pools and allegations of discriminatory intent intersect when challenging facially neutral policies. It also reinforces the need to examine the specific admissions process and how that process may have changed over time. The court’s decision may affect how courts evaluate statistical evidence in challenges to facially neutral admissions policies.

For school officials, families and advocates, the broader questions are equally important:

• How are students identified and informed about selective educational opportunities?

• What criteria determine eligibility and admission?

• Are admissions outcomes regularly examined across schools and student populations?

• Are the policies and measures used to assess access transparent and understandable?

These questions deserve attention regardless of how the Supreme Court ultimately resolves the legal issue before it.

The case is also a reminder that we must distinguish between allegations in a lawsuit, a school district’s stated reasons for its policies, procedural court decisions, and a final ruling on the merits. Understanding those distinctions is essential to an informed discussion.

The winning film, Minorities in Magnets, brings student perspectives into a debate that is often reduced to admissions criteria and demographic statistics. The Supreme Court will address a specific legal question about the evidence required in an equal protection challenge.

Neither alone answers the broader question of what meaningful educational opportunity should look like. But they invite a broader conversation about how communities define opportunity, how they measure access, and how they respond when students say the system is not working for them.

As lawyers and members of our communities, we have an obligation to listen, ask difficult questions, and examine whether the systems we create are providing genuine opportunities for all students.

Ultimately, educational access is about more than who gets admitted. It is also about who gets the opportunity to succeed, who feels that they belong, and whether every student has a fair chance to reach their full potential.

What Cloudflare Doesn't Tell You

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cloudflare has arguably done more to push modern internet technologies than any other company. The work they have done to enable TLS, IPv6, post quantum encryption, and other modern standards for millions of websites deserves a tremendous amount of credit.

While this is great, Cloudflare also hides many of the shortcomings of the underlying infrastructure from both users and website owners. Yes, you can connect to a Cloudflare website and it'll allow for IPv6, post quantum encryption, and TLS. But Cloudflare's connection to the origin server might be entirely unencrypted and rely exclusively on IPv4. This creates a huge mismatch between what is being presented to users and the reality of the connection.

One of my biggest problems with this is the fact that Cloudflare doesn't do enough to notify website administrators about these issues. While Cloudflare provides tools to inspect these, comprehensive visibility is lacking. The Cloudflare dashboard should notify you about each of these issues where Cloudflare is following better practices than your origin server, without website administrators having to dig through and specifically look for this information. Clear warnings and proactive alerts to administrators would help greatly.

Not only that, but according to Cloudflare's own documentation, they actively prefer IPv4 over IPv6 when making origin server requests, which is particularly disappointing for a company that has done so much to advance IPv6 adoption:

For proxied DNS records that have both an IPv6 and IPv4 origin address, Cloudflare will prefer the IPv4 address when connecting to your origin server.

Even when website administrators invest in deploying IPv6 on their origin servers, Cloudflare still prioritizes IPv4.
