All noise-cancelling headphones, including earbuds, have some passive noise cancellation. While over-the-ear headphones naturally block out noise by wrapping around the ears, earbuds don't block sound as effectively. Instead of covering your ears, wireless earbuds deliver sound directly into the ear canal to drown out other noises. They rely more on active noise cancellation (ANC) to reduce environmental noise.

ANC is an electronic process within the headphones. Internal microphones listen to your surroundings and then invert the sound, effectively canceling it. Sometimes, with noise-cancelling headphones and earbuds, you'll hear a slight buzzing or humming in the background, and that's the ANC at work. The best ANC earbuds shouldn't have this buzzing, however.

Warhammer Survivors Review - Bringing A Tank To The Mad Forest

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I spent hours carving through termagants with a chainsword and watching their sprites explode into a viscera of gems before Warhammer Survivors finally clicked. It wasn’t until I unlocked the tank and started rolling through the Ork Stronghold, turning pixels into paste beneath the running gear, that I finally got it: this isn’t just a Vampire Survivors reskin. It’s a Warhammer power fantasy that lets you unleash an orbital bombardment on the poor denizens of the Imperial Bastion, doling out the Emperor’s duty while you rummage through chests to turn garlic—ahem, Shield of Faith—into the Emperor’s Wrath.

Warhammer Survivors almost reminds me of those old ‘00s crossover games, like Peggle Extreme, where child-friendly unicorns are coupled by Half-Life headcrabs as blood drips from their horns. It’s unashamedly Vampire Survivors underneath the prettier UI, boltguns, and fusil-cannons, but once you push through and begin to unlock the more experimental Warhammer weapons, it becomes its own beast.

If you’ve played Vampire Survivors or its myriad clones, you’ll know the core gameplay loop: kill enemies, collect gems, acquire weapons, kill more enemies. Warhammer Survivors is familiar but a little more sluggish, with its hulking brutes clad in Space Marine armor. But even as someone who isn’t embroiled in the tabletop side—with passing knowledge thanks to Dawn of War and Boltgun—it just felt cooler. The whip was always a bit flimsy. Serviceable, but you toss it aside the second you get something better. Turn that whip into a chainsword, and suddenly I can’t let go of the thing.

Warhammer Survivors is split into two campaigns with their own progression systems, so it’s not just 40K on offer. Age of Sigmar is more familiar, with its high-fantasy tropes that throw you straight into a stage brimming with rats and other vermin. But this was another concept I just wasn’t sold on, as passing through the realmgate from one universe to the other was a bit of a slog at first. The two campaigns share so much DNA in their starting weapons, and both have the exact same passives, that it took a while to appreciate what each had to offer as I was forced to retrace my steps. Again, once I got past the first few hours, the two began to diverge with truly unique weapons and stages that felt every bit as rewarding to experiment with—it’s just a shame you have to push through to truly appreciate it.

So much of Warhammer Survivors pays homage to Games Workshop’s miniature wargame, such as paint buckets that unlock new characters and a bit box menu to view all your weapons in. But one of the most inventive ways that it captures the feeling of placing tiny figurines onto a board is how unlocking new weapons works. Not every character can use every item, so you’ll gradually build a squad with their own unique arsenal. There are mages brandishing the shield of faith, grenadiers lobbing grenades, Space Marines carving with the chainsword, and a tank. Just a tank. Can you imagine having a tank in the Mad Forest? That’s what this game is.

It’s a crucial part of what makes Warhammer Survivors feel less like a reskin and more like its own distinct entry into the Survivors genre canon, but I’m not sure it lands. Using weapons like the shield of faith, which is Auroch Digital’s answer to garlic, is a lot less satisfying when it’s permanently off-center or lagging behind as your troops get separated by the horde. It turns the satisfying gameplay loop of becoming an unstoppable powerhouse into a fiddlier game of wrangle-the-squad. It doesn’t matter so much when evolutions are brought into the fold, and suddenly you have five unstoppable heretics deleting everything in sight, but it’s another hurdle that slows the pace and makes the early moments of a game less rewarding.

And while Warhammer Survivors does eventually diverge and become its own experience, the early game is so similar to Vampire Survivors, with blatant reskins of weapons and passives, that I was even able to use Vampire Survivors guides to figure out evolutions. So much of the initial crawl is carried by the sheer cool factor of Warhammer’s aesthetic. It’s a bit of a shame that the early game is tantamount to a texture pack, imagining what Vampire Survivors weapons would look like in Warhammer, rather than imagining what Warhammer weapons would look like in a survivor game. Especially given how much better the game gets when Auroch Digital finally starts taking more ambitious swings.

After years of waiting, this forgotten chapter is finally ready for an explosively stylish return.

With that being said, the pixel art, thematic UI, and retro synthwave fantasy music are all a step above Vampire Survivors. Warhammer Survivors Review: Late to the Game But Packed With Bullets

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The budget-priced Warhammer Survivors brings a variety of unlocks and franchise fanservice, but feels a little unevolved for the bullet heaven genre.

Story isn’t a crucial component here, but there is some fun Warhammer lore to collect.

It’s unsurprisingly quite similar to Vampire Survivors with weaker feedback overall, but there’s a lot to unlock and some surprise elements further into the game.

Could have served as a DLC, but larger sprite sizes, greater map detail, and some visual surprises manage to outdo vanilla Vampire Survivors.

OST is repetitive and uninteresting, audio feedback is lacking.

Warhammer can seem intimidating from afar, but the many video games minted from Games Workshop’s tentpole wargaming IP have brought countless new fans into the fold. There have been over a hundred such video games thus far, with few as easy to pick up and play as Auroch Digital’s Warhammer Survivors, a new fully licensed riff on Poncle’s Vampire Survivors, the bullet heaven action-roguelite dynamo that set the standard for the subgenre.

Imprinting Warhammer’s extensive lore, dark oddball humor, and formative tropes onto Poncle’s dopamine-juicing treadmill sounds like an easy swish. And, while perhaps feeling a tad too similar to justify its own SKU, Warhammer Survivors delivers an eminently playable counterpart to its vampiric predecessor. Anyone fond of both the subgenre and the Warhammer brand will find a considerable distraction here, but the game needs another pass for polish, and I wish it simply added more to the formula.

Like Vampire, Warhammer Survivors dispenses with any attempt at storytelling and tosses you into the action with a choice between two campaigns: 40,000 or Age of Sigmar (read as: sci-fi or fantasy). Both “worlds” hold a troupe of 20 unlockable heroes with dozens of weapons and relics to stack and track and synergize. Pick a toon and map, mark up any mods, then head off to war, directing the non-reptilian part of your brain to other affairs, like a low-stakes Netflix series or comedy podcast.

If you’ve somehow managed to sidestep the bullet heaven survivors genre until now, let’s briefly dig into the basics. Each level presents an infinitely scrolling pixelated map, with hordes of spawning enemies swirling into your hero as you work to avoid contact damage and projectiles, all while hoovering up XP, coins, and power-ups. Maxing out the XP bar delivers a choice of various active and passive items, with select pairings producing a scripted synergy that overpowers the basic weapon at hand. You choose where and when to move, but attacks are always automatic and constant.

Elite minibosses materialize every few minutes and drop a treasure chest on defeat. Open it and endure a dizzying Vegas-styled jackpot celebration and stack of cash. Money accumulates over multiple runs and feeds a metaprogression menu that boosts your basic stats, so even failure leaves something behind in the tip jar. You’ll lose early and often, but those buffed stats eventually slacken the resistance and ticks up your win-to-loss ratio.

Ten minutes deep into a run, you'll be eradicating dozens of mobs in a blink with a kill-count into the tens of thousands, multicolored damage numbers snapping like popcorn, utterly incoherent in the fracas.

Warhammer Survivors’ metaprogression and a few of its power-ups are reskinned from Vampire Survivors outright. Still, most bullet heavens crib freely from one another—Vampire Survivors is itself a riff on Korean mobile game Magic Survival—and there are some fun irreverent hat tips to the wargaming pastime here. You can collect paint pot relics, wield Nuln Oil and dice as weapons, and some other cheeky references set the game apart from Vampire Survivors’ 8-bit horror theming.

Beyond that, the hero objectives, Extremis Bosses, and faction traits in Warhammer Survivors are all wholly unique and excellent additions to the format. Some heroes are unusual indeed and tap directly into the lore, like Knight-Commander Pask and his massive Leman Russ Battle Tank, and Commissar Sebastian Yarrick with his Power Klaw. Like any other weapons, these can be unlocked and equipped later on, regardless of your initially selected hero.

That plays into the game’s squad mechanics, though I found them somewhat frustrating in action. Essentially, if your chosen hero cannot equip a granted weapon, someone from the pool will be conscripted and added to your team to wield it, which can get clumsy to control.

For instance, the straight-shooting boltgun is hard enough to aim properly when it’s in your own hands, but having to angle its shots from a teammate trailing behind you is a pain. Additionally, when your screen is packed to the rafters with targets, it's nearly impossible to keep track of your own five-man squad.

I found the game’s two eras to also be unbalanced, with early game progress on the 40k track much slower and more difficult than Age of Sigmar. Maybe that’s due to a better selection of power-ups in the latter, but I’m not entirely convinced, and I’m certain the game will go through a few rounds of balancing updates after launch to account for it.

I enjoy the way some levels feature environmental mechanics, like tunnels that hide items or elevators to carry you between different map layers. There should be many more of these quirks, as it's strange to wander battlefields peppered with massive turrets in the 40k era while none of them can be used, and 90% of the game's background content is mere set dressing; Vampire Survivors’ own Ode to Castlevania DLC featured a map with much more interactivity.

Speaking of that DLC, Warhammer Survivors starts you off with a similar map function, but this one is pretty much a basic pause menu radar for items. You can zoom in or out to get an idea of where relics and pickups can be found and track them with onscreen indicators, but there’s no landscape detail, nor can you even scrub through the map itself. Since most optional level objectives involve collecting a distant hero or relic, you’re forced to use that shoddy map a lot.

Perhaps most importantly, the game's feedback is far inferior to Vampire Survivors, which may largely be due to the sound. Warhammer Survivors’ audio mixdown is a problem, and I couldn't effectively balance out the SFX and music in settings, eventually opting to silence one or the other. That ultimately wasn't a great loss, as most of the music amounts to heavily-quantized militaristic industrial loops that overstay their welcome fast, though the ambient menu themes are decent enough.

Speaking of menus, the UI in Warhammer Survivors could have done with its own corrective pass (not that Vampire Survivors is much better). Controller input is especially scatterbrained; for instance, pressing A starts a selected level, but doesn't if you're checking off a mod, and you swap worlds with the Start button but then quit the game from the worlds menu with B. This all isn't impossible to manage, but peculiar.

I’ve never understood the fullscreen edge transparency in Vampire, but Warhammer Survivors actually blots out those edges with a skulls texture for some reason. The game’s overhead POV also often works against, as enemies love choking up doorways and walls, which renders them functionally invisible. Player projectiles evaporate on the edges of these surfaces, while enemy shots phase right through. Again, not unmanageable, but this will probably lead to a few frustrating deaths.

The game’s many objectives do dangle carrots aplenty, a boon in any bullet heaven. However, I wish there was a way to index and sort the game's 200+ challenges to the 40 characters they relate to. Additionally, the objectives menu provides no additional info or any explanation of the related rewards, which just seems like a missed opportunity. This could have functioned as its own wiki—like the Bestiarium, which is the best collection of Warhammer lore in the game—but it's just a minimalist list to chew through.

We cut bullet heaven games a lot of slack as a matter of course, and Vampire Survivors is a perfect example. As inspiration to countless others, it’s handily been outplayed at its own game time after time; Brotato shrunk the play space and filed down the progression structure, and Everything is Crab re-expanded that map, evolving the genre’s most basic active/passive upgrade design. Megabonk made traversal equally important to DPS, and Boneraiser Minions used a necromancer concept to present a kind of RTS/autobattler/survivors hybrid. And so on.

Considering these examples, Warhammer Survivors is a few days late and dollars short, albeit at a budget price. For those of you who’ve fully rinsed Vampire Survivors… Well, first off, that’s simply an impressive feat; well done. But secondly, here are another 40 heroes to max out, with hundreds of objectives to clear. Consider your bullet heaven buffet fully restocked.

Few games have a title as accurate or descriptive to its core content as Warhammer Survivors. In a nutshell, itt’s Vampire Survivors gameplay, but with Warhammer 40,000 and Age of Sigmar characters, monsters, and levels, but with the difficulty turned up to eleven.

If Warhammer Survivors was just a cosmetic upgrade, they would have just released it as DLC in Vampire Survivors, rather than giving it its own dedicated game. After all, Vampire Survivors has invited Castlevania to its roster and added Contra too, so surely Space Marines wouldn’t look out of place?

Thankfully, Warhammer Survivors shakes up the gameplay and adds new tweaks to the familiar formula that makes it a worthwhile purchase for people who aren’t familiar with the tabletop game. Long-time Vampire Survivors fans just need to be aware that its Warhammer equivalent is a fair bit harder than the content they’ve beaten before.

At its core, Warhammer Survivors has the same gameplay loop as Vampire Survivors and its many clones. The player picks a hero, who then has to survive against endless hordes of enemies. The main gameplay gimmick is that the player cannot control how the character shoots: just how they move across the battlefield.

Throughout each level, the player earns experience points from killing enemies, and when they level up, they are given new weapons and upgrades that make it easier to destroy more foes. As time goes on, the enemies get stronger and grow in numbers, meaning careful movement becomes even more important.

There are also bosses that appear throughout the stage, which are outlined in a neon green aura. Slaying these bosses results in crates being dropped with level upgrades. These are important, as if a weapon is sufficiently levelled, then a crate could carry its improved version, such as the Boltgun transforming into the Heavy Boltgun, which has a drastically improved fire rate.

Dying on a run isn’t the end of the world, as the player can find coins strewn throughout each stage that can be spent on static buffs and upgrades. These make future runs easier, so the player has a greater chance of surviving later stages.

Warhammer Survivors keeps this gameplay loop intact, though it splits everything across two worlds. On the main menu, the player selects the “Universe”: Warhammer 40,000 and Age of Sigmar, the latter of which is the modern replacement for Warhammer Fantasy (RIP). They have their own stages, characters, and enemies, with no direct crossovers between the two.

Technically, there is content that connects the two, as the upgrades unlocked are shared between the two, meaning working hard in one helps out the other. There are also non-franchise-specific levels that they share, like the Hobby Table, but for the most part, the Warhammer 40,000 and Age of Sigmar parts of the game are separate.

Managers can turn on staff when they're afraid of losing their own jobs

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Executives count on team leaders to keep everyone steady through cuts. But some bosses fear for their own roles and start belittling employees

A version of this article originally appeared in Quartz’s Leadership newsletter. Sign up here to get the latest leadership news and insights straight to your inbox.

Plenty of managers say little to their teams when job cuts are rumored. Some already know a layoff is coming and aren't allowed to discuss it. Others think the conversation will get out of hand, or would rather not face their own fear of being replaced, executive coach Dina Denham Smith explained in an article for Harvard Business Review last December.

With nothing to go on, staff tend to imagine the worst. Employees keep telling Smith, "I'm just putting my head down and laying low." They speak up less, avoid risks, and look after themselves before solving problems. An executive can easily mistake a quiet meeting for agreement when the people in the room are scared.

Many managers fear for their own jobs, too. If senior leaders leave them guessing about cuts, some start insisting on making every decision, belittling their teams in the process. Not only are staff worried about losing their jobs, but their bosses may begin to treat them worse too.

Executives tend to blame stress when managers snap at their team ahead of a layoff. Bank managers in China, for example, get more frustrated on days they're more afraid than usual for their jobs, according to a study by Fu Yang and colleagues in the Journal of Business Ethics. They also want to make more decisions themselves. The more managers care about being the one in charge, the likelier they are to turn rude and nasty with staff.

Managers are used to giving orders, according to Yang and colleagues. They stand to lose their say over a team, along with their paychecks, so they push their own staff around to prove they still run things. Executives can tell them to calm down, but the managers will still fear for their jobs.

Many managers get through rounds of cuts without bossing their staff around. They tend to say they bounce back quickly after hard times. Even on their worst days, they run their teams as usual, without insisting on making every decision or snapping at their employees. Before a layoff, a company needs its managers to take the news without turning on their staff. Yang and colleagues suggest checking how quickly job applicants and promotion candidates recover from setbacks before putting them in charge of a team. Current managers could get training to bounce back faster, with follow-ups every week or so.

Some managers take longer to get over bad news. They can still treat staff decently on days they're worried about their job. They do best when their minds stay on what's happening at work instead of racing ahead to what might come next. Two managers can hear the same news about a restructuring and run their teams very differently. The same manager can also handle it well one day and badly the next. A short meditation session or a daily guided recording of about 10 minutes may help managers keep their minds on what's in front of them, according to Yang and colleagues.

Staff tend to absorb some of their manager's strain, often without noticing, according to researchers at the University of Southern Denmark. Employees read the boss's face and voice for clues about how worried to be. Managers under strain may also plan the work less carefully and offer less help, making the job harder for everyone below them. So when senior leaders put dozens of middle managers under pressure at once, they're also putting dozens of teams under pressure.

Staff feel less strained when their bosses listen, fix practical problems, understand their jobs, and show them respect. How they're treated each day matters more to them than their managers' own stress. A CEO can't keep every manager calm through a round of cuts but can still ask managers to hear their staff out and fix the everyday problems that slow their teams down. Senior leaders can bring up both in every meeting with managers until the layoffs are over.

A manager can even calm a team down without any good news to give. Smith advises telling staff what's settled, what's still open, and when they'll hear more. A fixed update schedule helps too, even in weeks with nothing new to report. Smith said managers can admit they don't know yet whether jobs will be cut without making staff more afraid. Employees then spend less time swapping rumors, the source of much of their fear.

Smith reminds managers that their teams are "likely even further removed from information, power, and clarity than you are." With so little else to go on, staff take their cues from their manager. Often, the person they're watching is worried about losing a job, too.

10 flu myths about vaccines, treatment and what influenza really is

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 flu myths cover vaccines, treatment and what the flu really is, from whether the flu shot gives you the flu to whether antibiotics can cure it

The word "flu" does a lot of work it was never meant to do. People use it for a 24-hour stomach bug, a heavy cold or almost any winter illness that keeps them home. Influenza is something more specific. It is a respiratory illness caused by influenza A and B viruses, and it infects the nose, throat and lungs.

That loose vocabulary is one reason flu myths persist. Another is the vaccine itself. Many shots are given once or a handful of times in a lifetime, but the flu vaccine is offered every year. Each new season brings a fresh round of questions about whether it is safe, whether it works and whether a given person needs it.

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends an annual flu vaccine for everyone aged six months and older, with few exceptions. That short line of guidance still leaves people with questions about the details.

Treatment generates its own confusion. Some people expect antibiotics for a fever and body aches, even though influenza is a viral infection. Others assume a healthy adult can shrug off the flu, or that a vaccine without a perfect record is not worth getting.

The 10 myths below are a selection, not a complete catalog. They cover three areas: what the flu actually is, what causes it and how it is treated, and how the vaccine works for different groups of people. Those groups include pregnant women and people with egg allergies. Each entry names the misconception and explains what is accurate instead. Where guidance applies, the entries name the organizations that set it, such as the CDC, the World Health Organization and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists.

Every slide is written to stand on its own. A reader who wants to know whether a flu shot can cause the flu, or how influenza differs from a common cold, can go straight to that entry. The answers draw on established medical guidance rather than anecdote.

A flu shot cannot give you influenza. Injected flu vaccines contain either inactivated virus or a single protein from the virus, and neither one can cause the illness.

An inactivated virus has been treated so that it can no longer cause infection. It still carries the features the immune system needs to recognize. That combination lets the vaccine prompt protection without making anyone sick.

A vaccine built around a single viral protein contains no whole virus at all. It holds only one piece of the virus for the immune system to learn.

Reactions after an injection are a separate matter. A sore arm, a low fever and aches can follow a flu shot. Those effects come from the immune system responding to the vaccine. They are not influenza.

The nasal spray vaccine, FluMist, works differently. It contains a weakened live virus rather than an inactivated one. That virus has been adapted to grow in the cool conditions of the nose, not in the warmer lungs.

FluMist can cause mild symptoms such as a runny nose. It cannot cause the flu.

The illness most people call "stomach flu" is usually not influenza. It is typically gastroenteritis caused by germs such as norovirus or rotavirus. Those germs are separate from the influenza viruses behind the flu.

Influenza is a respiratory illness. Its main features are respiratory rather than digestive. The shared nickname ties together two illnesses with different causes.

Everyday language drives much of the mix-up. In casual speech, "flu" can mean almost any short illness that leaves a person miserable for a few days. A bout of vomiting and diarrhea picks up the label because it feels flu-like. That happens even when no influenza virus is involved.

The nickname does not change the diagnosis. An illness caused by norovirus or rotavirus is gastroenteritis, whatever a household chooses to call it.

The label also blurs personal history. Someone who says they "had the flu" after vomiting and diarrhea has most likely had gastroenteritis. That illness reveals little about how influenza itself would affect them.

The line between the two is not perfectly sharp. Vomiting and diarrhea can occur with influenza. They show up more often in children than in adults. Even so, they are not the main features of the disease.

That pattern matters when reading symptoms. A child with influenza may vomit. The vomiting does not turn the illness into gastroenteritis, and the underlying infection is still influenza. An adult whose illness is mainly vomiting and diarrhea, with no respiratory symptoms, fits the profile of gastroenteritis more closely than influenza.

The naming confusion also shapes how people judge the flu shot. The influenza vaccine is made to protect against influenza viruses. It is not designed to prevent gastroenteritis from norovirus or rotavirus. A stomach bug in the weeks after a flu shot is a different infection. It is not a sign that the vaccine failed.

Influenza is caused by influenza A and B viruses, not by chilly air, a winter draft or stepping outside with wet hair. A person develops the flu only after one of those viruses gets into the body.

The myth sticks partly because of timing. In temperate regions, flu activity peaks in fall and winter. Those are also the months when people are most likely to feel cold. Seeing the two together makes the weather look like the cause.

The seasonal peak is tied to other factors. Flu surges in fall and winter are linked to conditions such as indoor crowding and drier air. In colder months, people spend more time inside and closer together. That gives an infected person more contact with others.

Feeling cold and catching influenza are separate events. Someone who walks outside in freezing weather with damp hair, and never encounters the virus, does not come down with the flu. Someone who stays warm indoors but spends time with an infected person can still get sick.

That distinction shapes what helps. A scarf, a hat or a dry head may make a winter day more comfortable. None of them stops an influenza virus from reaching a person who is exposed to it.

Antibiotics do nothing against the flu. These drugs act on bacteria, and influenza is caused by viruses. The medicines made to treat influenza are prescription antivirals, such as oseltamivir and baloxavir.

The gap comes down to the type of germ. Bacteria and viruses are different kinds of microbes. A drug that acts on bacteria has no effect on a virus, no matter how sick the patient feels.

Oseltamivir is sold under the brand name Tamiflu. Baloxavir is sold as Xofluza. The generic and brand names refer to the same drugs. Both target influenza itself, and a doctor has to prescribe them.

Timing shapes how well these antivirals work. They work best when a patient starts them within 48 hours of the first symptoms. That makes an early visit to a doctor more useful than waiting to see whether the illness gets worse.

The 48-hour mark is not a strict cutoff. Doctors may still prescribe an antiviral after that window to high-risk patients. They may also give one to people who are already in the hospital with influenza. In those cases, the decision rests with the treating doctor rather than the calendar.

Antibiotics do have a role in some flu cases. A bacterial complication can develop on top of the viral infection. Bacterial pneumonia is one example. In that situation, a doctor may prescribe an antibiotic.

Even then, the antibiotic treats the bacterial infection. It does not act on the influenza virus that came first. The flu and the complication call for two separate kinds of treatment. The reverse also holds. An antiviral such as oseltamivir targets influenza and will not clear a bacterial pneumonia.

For someone with flu symptoms, an antibiotic will not treat the illness. Asking a doctor whether an antiviral such as Tamiflu or Xofluza fits the case addresses the virus directly.

Being young, fit and rarely sick is not a reason to skip the flu shot. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends a flu vaccine every year for everyone aged six months and older, with few exceptions.

That guidance covers healthy adults on the same terms as everyone else. Good health is not one of the exceptions.

The myth rests on the idea that influenza threatens only people who are already frail. Healthy people can also become severely ill with the flu. A strong baseline of health does not guarantee a mild case.

The shot also matters for the people around a healthy adult. Someone who catches influenza can pass the virus on. Those on the receiving end may include infants, older adults and others whose age or health puts them at higher risk of severe illness.

A healthy adult may live with a baby, visit an older relative or work alongside someone at higher risk. In each case, that adult is one route the virus can take to reach them.

A flu shot from last season is not designed to protect you through this one. Influenza viruses change from year to year. For that reason, the strains included in flu vaccines are reviewed and updated before each new season.

That gradual change is called antigenic drift. As influenza A and B viruses circulate, they accumulate small changes. A vaccine built around earlier versions of the virus may not match the versions spreading now.

The result is a vaccine that is reformulated for each season rather than one product offered unchanged year after year.

The strain review takes place at a global level. The World Health Organization makes separate vaccine recommendations for the Northern Hemisphere and the Southern Hemisphere. In temperate regions, flu activity peaks in fall and winter. Those seasons arrive in different months on either side of the equator. Separate recommendations account for that difference.

Strain change is not the only reason an older shot falls short. Protection from flu vaccination also wanes over time. A shot given a year ago offers less protection now than it did in the weeks after it was given.

Both problems point the same way. A previous season's vaccine may target strains that no longer match the viruses in circulation. The immunity it produced has also faded. That combination is why the flu vaccine is treated as a seasonal shot rather than a one-time dose.

Influenza is not a stronger version of the common cold. The two illnesses are caused by different viruses, and the flu can lead to serious complications such as pneumonia.

Colds are commonly caused by rhinoviruses. The flu is caused by influenza viruses. Because the germs are different, a cold is not an early or mild stage of the flu. A bad cold does not turn into influenza.

The flu tends to start abruptly. Fever, body aches and exhaustion are typical early symptoms, and they often arrive together.

Complications set the two illnesses further apart. Influenza can lead to pneumonia, an infection of the lungs. Some people with the flu become sick enough to need care in a hospital.

Flu vaccine effectiveness varies by season, but the shot still cuts risk. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that vaccination reduces the risk of flu illness by 40% to 60% in the overall population when circulating viruses are well matched to the vaccine.

That range describes a reduction in risk across a population. It compares vaccinated people with unvaccinated people. It does not mean that 40% to 60% of people who get the shot are protected while everyone else gets nothing from it.

The condition attached to the estimate matters. The range applies to seasons when the vaccine is well matched to the viruses that are spreading. In seasons with a weaker match, protection can differ.

The estimate also measures one specific outcome: flu illness. It describes how much vaccination lowers the chance of getting sick with influenza. It is not a promise that any one person will avoid infection.

A vaccinated person who still catches influenza is not evidence that the vaccine failed. One case cannot show what a vaccine did across a whole population. The CDC figure is built to answer a population question, not an individual one.

Preventing illness is not the only benefit. Vaccination can make illness milder in people who still get sick. Someone who catches influenza after vaccination may have a less severe course than they would have had without the shot.

The CDC and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists both recommend flu vaccination during pregnancy. That guidance runs against the myth that expectant mothers should wait until after the baby is born to get the shot.

The first reason concerns the mother. Pregnancy raises the risk of severe influenza. Putting off the vaccine until after delivery leaves her unprotected through the months when that risk applies.

The second reason concerns the baby. Infants younger than six months cannot receive a flu vaccine. Newborns therefore have no vaccine protection of their own during their first months of life.

Vaccination during pregnancy helps close that gap. The mother develops antibodies in response to the shot. Those antibodies are passed on to the baby during pregnancy. They help protect the infant until the child is old enough to be vaccinated.

The recommendation comes from two different kinds of institutions. The CDC is the national public health agency of the U.S. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists is the professional organization for obstetrician-gynecologists, the doctors who provide pregnancy care. Their shared position means public health guidance and specialist medical guidance point the same way.

One flu shot during pregnancy reaches two people at once. It addresses the mother's higher risk of severe flu. It also helps protect a baby who is too young for a vaccine of its own.

An egg allergy does not rule out a flu shot. The CDC advises that people with an egg allergy can receive any licensed, age-appropriate flu vaccine. No extra precautions are needed beyond standard vaccine safety practice.

That guidance covers every licensed flu vaccine, including those produced using eggs. A history of egg allergy is not, on its own, a reason to skip vaccination. Nor is it a reason to hunt for a particular brand.

The guidance places someone with an egg allergy under the same safety practice as any other vaccine recipient. An egg allergy does not determine which licensed vaccine that person can receive.

The age-appropriate part of the guidance still matters. Each flu vaccine is licensed for specific age groups. The right product depends on the age of the person receiving it, not on an egg allergy.

Egg-free options also exist. Flucelvax is a cell-based flu vaccine. Flublok is a recombinant flu vaccine. Neither is made with eggs.

Those products are an alternative, not a requirement. People who would rather avoid egg-based vaccines can ask about one of them, as long as it is licensed for their age group. Under the CDC guidance, a person with an egg allergy who receives an egg-based vaccine is also following the recommendation.
