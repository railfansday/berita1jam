Hard-luck Rams catch break in Puka Nacua suspension

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

It figures that in a season this sideways, the best break the Rams have caught in weeks came from the people who just suspended their best player.

Somewhere in the NFL offices, somebody signed off on a three-game suspension for Puka Nacua. Somewhere in Los Angeles, somebody had to look at the calendar and think, well, if you insist.

Los Angeles Rams wide receiver Puka Nacua reacts after winning an NFL football game against the Washington Commanders in Landover, Md., Sunday, Nov. 8, 2026. AP Photo/Stephanie Scarbrough

Nacua can serve all three games while he's on injured reserve. The shoulder pads were going to stay in his locker anyway. When he's healthy enough to put them back on, his suspension will already be behind him.

How often this season have the Rams been able to say something worked out about as well as they could have hoped?

Certainly nobody was saying it after the Denver game. The Rams had a game they should have won, the kind you circle when you're trying to figure out how a team with this much talent ended up having to fight this hard just to keep its head above water.

Just look at who has been hit by injuries. Myles Garrett. Nacua. Davante Adams. Cornerbacks Jaylen Watson and Trent McDuffie. That's a whole lot of the blueprint spending time on the injury report. The Rams built this team to pressure quarterbacks, cover receivers and make defenses account for two dangerous targets.

Injuries have reached into every one of those plans. Sean McVay has spent too much of this season figuring out what's left to work with each week.

They've also had more experience than anybody around here wanted finding out what the offense looks like with one of its best players standing next to McVay. There's a reason a coach spends all week drawing up ways to get the ball to somebody. The guy behind him might know exactly where to line up. There's still the matter of what happens once the ball gets there.

With Nacua, sometimes the best part of the play starts right about where it should end. A defender gets his arms around him a yard short of the sticks, and somehow it's first down. Try finding that on the call sheet.

That still leaves the Packers, the Chiefs and the 49ers on the schedule without Nacua. Go ahead and read those names back to McVay and see how lucky he feels.

Head Coach Sean McVay of the Los Angeles Rams looks on during the fourth quarter against the Washington Commanders in Landover, Maryland, Sunday, Nov. 8, 2026. Getty Images

Except the Rams were going to play those games without him anyway. And that's where the calendar did them a favor. They've already been figuring out how to win without Nacua. Now they just have to do it a little longer.

Maybe the catches come from three different guys against Green Bay. Maybe the running backs have to carry them against Kansas City. By the time San Francisco rolls around, nobody will care if the same formula worked twice.

Now imagine getting word that Nacua is healthy again and having to explain to the rest of the locker room that it'll be another three Sundays before anybody gets to see him play. That was the danger. The timing of the suspension took it off the table.

At some point on that flight, everybody involved had to let go of whatever they thought the mission was going to look like. They had duct tape. They had some awfully smart people on the ground. They had a bunch of stuff on board originally intended for other purposes. What could they piece together that would get three astronauts back to Earth?

Get there however you can. Win an ugly one. Ask a backup to give you one good afternoon. Give the defense a lead late and let somebody on that side close out the kind of game that got away against Denver. Then come back Monday and see who can practice.

For all that's gone sideways, the Rams are still alive. And the team an opponent draws in January could look a whole lot more like the one we expected in September than the one McVay is trying to hold together in November.

Puka Nacua #12 of the Los Angeles Rams celebrates a fourth-quarter touchdown with Head Coach Sean McVay in Landover, Maryland, Sunday, Nov. 8, 2026. Getty Images

Nobody asks how much duct tape it took once you land. And if the Rams get there with Nacua healthy, good luck to the defensive coordinator who spent the last month watching film of an offense that had to make do without him.

All the Rams need is a way in. From there, they have a chance to run the table. Funny to think, after everything that's happened to them, who just helped keep the door open.

It's Not 'Car Mechanic Simulator,' But GTA 6 Is Leaning Harder Into Vehicle Repair Than Before

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

More so than any of its predecessors, "Grand Theft Auto 6" leans heavily into realism. You can't just carry your entire arsenal on you anymore — instead, you must rely on a few guns on your person and a stash in your trunk. Building off that simulated realism, the game now puts a greater emphasis on the exciting world of vehicle maintenance directly into the gameplay. There will be times when you have to get your car repaired, repainted, or refueled. Who knows — maybe "Grand Theft Auto 7" will go one step further by including a mission called "Doing Taxes."

To be fair to "Grand Theft Auto 6," it does remember that it is an action/adventure game and not the next coming of "Car Mechanic Simulator." For instance, stops to get your car fixed are generally pretty brief. That way, you can get right back to running heists and stealing a car that looks a lot like a Pontiac Trans Am out of a police impound lot.

Still, the game does feature four different ways to get your car fixed: Pay 'N' Spray shops, mod shops, gas stations, and a guy named Joey who can be called upon to do repairs. Gas stations are limited in the repairs they can do and are more expensive than other repair shops — there's that realism again. For more extensive repairs, you'll need to stop at one of the other shops or call Joey.

Missions can be pretty rough on your vehicles, especially when you're trying to get away from the cops. Your car can get engine damage or cosmetic damage, and fixing it can be important to the gameplay. One of the early missions, "Just a Scratch," will actually make you take your vehicle into Coastal Auto Care to get fixed. That's where you meet Joey.

Throughout the game, there is an engine condition indicator that goes from green to orange to red. It ends off at red with an "X" in the middle, indicating that the engine is dead. Green indicates the engine is in good shape, orange means there is moderate damage, and red indicates critical damage. An engine that is not in good shape can affect how the car performs, which is not something you want to deal with as your wanted level increases.

And don't forget to fuel up, as each car has a working fuel gauge. But don't worry, because you won't have to stop for gas too often; apparently "Grand Theft Auto 6" cars get pretty great gas mileage. EVs are also a part of the universe, which means you'll have to stop for a charge every so often if you grab one.

The cars in "Grand Theft Auto 6" are not indestructible, but they can keep running even after getting pretty banged up. So, why bother to fix body damage? You don't want the NPCs to point and laugh at your car, do you? Even if you aren't worried about the opinions of Vice City pedestrians, the condition of your car's body can affect gameplay as well. Want to sell that hot car that you just stole? Well, just like in real life, damage to the body of the car can affect how much it sells for.

Then there's the matter of getting away from the cops after stealing a car. They will often have the car's description, including its color. There are even Flock-like cameras in GTA 6 that can track your movements. Repainting it another color, even when the body is in good shape, can help hide it from the police. It's like a wardrobe change, but for your vehicle. Pay 'N' Spray is the place to go for a quick paint job, while mod shops will be able to make more extensive changes to your car.

Overall, it seems like Rockstar has found a nice balance between the realities of car ownership and the escapist fantasy that the Grand Theft Auto series has always embodied. We'll just have to wait and see if "Car Mechanic Simulator 2027" will be adding heists.

Why Universal Technical Institute Stock Tanked on Thursday

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Investors were dismayed by the education specialist's final earnings report of fiscal 2026.

By Eric Volkman – Nov 19, 2026 at 7:46PM EST

Universal Technical Institute (UTI -8.48%) released its final set of earnings for its 2026 fiscal year, and probably wishes it hadn't. The vocational education specialist fell short of analyst estimates, and its guidance also left something to be desired. Investors expressed their displeasure by trading the stock down by almost 9% that day.

After market close on Wednesday, UTI revealed that its fourth-quarter revenue was $235.9 million, up more than 6% year over year. That was on the back of a 6%-plus rise in the average number of full-time students over the year, to 26,251.

Net income took a different trajectory, sliding by almost 4% to slightly over $18 million, or $0.32 per share.

That top-line figure was slightly higher than the average analyst estimate of just under $235 million. Pundits tracking UTI stock were also modeling a slightly higher earnings per share (EPS) figure of $0.33.

UTI is in the midst of implementing its multi-year "North Star Strategy," aimed at transforming its historical focus on training for certain vocations into a learning institute for a variety of them. It attributed the dip in profitability to expenses related to this effort.

UTI also proffered revenue guidance for the current fiscal year of $925 million to $945 million.

However, those expenses -- which include spending on four planned new campus openings -- will continue to impact the bottom line. Management is guiding for annual net income of $0 to $5 million ($0 to $0.09 per share), which would be well down from fiscal 2026's $33.6 million. It's also notably below the $0.73-per-share consensus analyst estimate (ditto for UTI's revenue guidance, which whiffs on the $971 million collective pundit forecast).

UTI's expansion plans are ambitious and, in my view, achievable. Demand for vocational education is high given the shortage of skilled professionals in such trades, so this feels like the opportune time to build out an educational business. I think UTI has a brighter future than the Thursday market reaction would indicate.

Anthropic’s 7 founders cashed out nearly $2B in record-breaking IPO

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic became a public company on Thursday, raising a total of $81.3 billion including $1.95 billion for its seven founders. As we previously reported, each of them ponied up about 3.4 million shares apiece to sell in the IPO.

At the upgraded IPO price of $83 per share, that means they each pocketed about $280 million before underwriting fees and taxes. It looked like they had calculated the number of shares they planned to sell to give them each around $250 million if shares hit $74. That was the midpoint price from Anthropic’s original price range. So that’s a cool $30-ish million more for each of them than perhaps they were gunning for.

Anthropic implied in its IPO filings that the founders aren’t entirely in this for the money though. The company said that each of the founders has pledged to dedicate 80% of their personal equity in the company to philanthropy.

However, if any of them struggle to live on their quarter of a billion, they can scale back their charitable commitments. The company points out that these commitments are “voluntary, revocable expressions of each co-founder’s philanthropic intent and do not constitute legally binding obligations.”

With the IPO in the record books, there were a few other interesting numbers worth highlighting.

$104.8 billion. That’s really the amount Anthropic raised for its coffers (with a possibility of $117 billion, if the bankers buy all of their allotment of shares). In addition to selling its Class A common stock, it also sold a previously announced $26 billion worth of a special non-voting Class N stock to some of the biggest tech names in the industry.

Nvidia bought $10 billion of these Class N shares, Google bought $10 billion, and AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft, and TSMC divvied up the rest.

Just for comparison: When Facebook had its then record-breaking IPO in 2012, it raised $16 billion on IPO day.

28% stake to Amazon and Google combined. All told, at the higher IPO share price, Google wound up owning 13.4% of the company. Amazon also now has a big stake in Anthropic because when it previously invested in the AI lab as a startup, it was granted promissory notes that could be converted to equity. It converted a small percentage of them as part of the IPO, leaving it with a 14.6% stake. Had it converted all of them, Amazon could have owned nearly 24%.

The two large cloud providers now own 28% of Anthropic, the AI lab says.

More than $1 trillion. As mind-blowing as it is to raise nearly $105 billion, giving Anthropic nearly $230 billion of working capital available (along with capital it already had on hand), the company’s need for future capital is still far greater.

Anthropic needs about $70.7 billion just to cover its compute and infrastructure spending commitments in the next 12 months as of the quarter ended September 30, 2026. It needs $451.6 billion to cover these commitments before the end of 2030. And it has racked up hundreds of billions of other commitments that are either conditional (and may not happen) or haven’t been priced yet.

All told, the company has disclosed a ballpark of $1.05 trillion to $1.08 trillion in infrastructure commitments in its foreseeable future. This was a huge IPO. But it is a pricey business to run.

Michael Burry Says He Tried to Warn You About Nvidia, CoreWeave and Nebius. Here's What the 'Big Short' Investor Saw

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The famed investor took a victory lap as AI stocks slid again on Thursday.

By Jonathan Ponciano – Nov 19, 2026 at 7:43PM EST

Michael Burry, the investor made famous by "The Big Short," isn't shy about saying "I told you so."

"Coreweave, Nebius, NVIDIA, semis, I tried to warn you all," Burry posted on X on Thursday afternoon. "I didn't say short. But I provided the information one would need to sell."

He picked a good day for it. Nvidia (NVDA -3.30%) fell 3.3% on Thursday, CoreWeave (CRWV -6.11%) dropped 6.1%, and Nebius Group (NBIS -9.39%) sank 9.4%, as tech stocks gave up an early rally.

Here's what Burry saw, and what investors should do with it.

Michael Burry. Image source: Getty Images.

Burry's warnings came in pieces. In mid-October, he shared a Wall Street Journal story arguing that CoreWeave's sell-off showed the pressure from rising borrowing costs, adding his own commentary: "Dumb da dumb dumb da."

CoreWeave and Nebius are so-called neoclouds, companies that rent out Nvidia-powered computing to AI developers. Building their data centers takes enormous amounts of money, much of it borrowed, so higher interest rates hit them hard.

On Nvidia, his case is about the long run. He says he expects Nvidia's competitive edge to shrink over the next five years. In September, he also argued that its chips "may be economically depreciating waiting in warehouses while GAAP depreciation remains at zero," meaning the hardware could be losing value faster than the accounting shows.

The numbers have lent credence to his concern. CoreWeave closed Thursday at $44.91, down 76% from highs last year, and Nebius finished at $96.92, down 66% from its June peak. Nvidia has held up better but still sits 39% below its October high. The damage has spread across the chip industry, too: The VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH -2.62%), a basket of the biggest chip stocks, is down 33% from its June high.

As he laid out his long-term worries about Nvidia earlier this month, Burry said he imagined "the whole market is going to think this is a heck of a trade fairly soon." But since then, the stock has fallen another 16%.

Nvidia's business, meanwhile, hasn't cracked. Its revenue jumped 103% to $115.8 billion last quarter, and management raised its growth forecast for next year. The stock's slide this month coincided with Jensen Huang's sudden resignation as CEO, rather than a drop in sales.

If you own neocloud stocks like CoreWeave and Nebius, watch how much they borrow and what that debt costs them. If you own Nvidia, watch whether customers keep buying at today's pace, and whether the chips they buy hold their value.

The AI stocks that come out of this sell-off in good shape will be the ones with the best answers to those questions. For long-term investors, that's a better guide than any single post on X, even one from Michael Burry.

LSD changes the brain’s rhythm in a way earlier studies missed

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Brain recordings from 17 volunteers show that LSD speeds up two brain rhythms even as it weakens them.

Brain recordings of people on psychedelics have told the same story for years: the brain’s steady electrical rhythms get weaker, as if the drug were turning the volume down.

A new analysis of recordings from 17 people given LSD finds that the volume isn’t the whole story. The drug also turns up the tempo.

LSD pushes two of the brain’s main rhythms to faster beats, and it makes the brain’s electrical activity more detailed and less predictable.

Venkatesh Subramani, a doctoral student at the Université de Montréal, led the analysis with his supervisor Karim Jerbi. Jerbi is a psychology professor there and a researcher at Mila, the Quebec artificial intelligence institute.

Co-authors included Nicolas Farrugia and Giulia Lioi of IMT Atlantique in France and Robin Carhart-Harris of the University of California, San Francisco.

“Imagine a radio station switching its broadcast frequency from 99.5 to 100.5 FM,” Jerbi said.

“If your radio stays tuned to 99.5, the signal may seem distorted or lost. In the same way, overlooking the shift to faster brain rhythms can lead us to misinterpret LSD’s effects on the brain.”

Groups of neurons firing together produce rhythms that a sensor outside the head can pick up. Researchers call them brain waves.

Alpha waves dominate when you rest with your eyes closed. Beta waves are faster and show up during concentration and decision-making.

Under psychedelics those waves look weaker, and the standard reading is that neurons are firing less in unison. Subramani’s team pointed out a catch.

If a rhythm speeds up, its peak can shift outside the frequency range researchers are measuring. That shift can make the wave look weaker even if its strength hasn’t changed.

So the team pulled the two possibilities apart. They first stripped out the brain’s rhythm-free background activity.

Then they located each person’s actual alpha and beta peaks under LSD and under placebo, and measured the power at those peaks.

The recordings were made at Imperial College London by Carhart-Harris’s group. Seventeen healthy volunteers came in twice, two weeks apart.

In one session, they received an intravenous injection of 75 micrograms of LSD, roughly the weight of a grain of salt. In the other they received a saline placebo, without being told which was which.

About four hours after the injection, each person lay in a magnetoencephalography scanner. It records the faint magnetic fields that neurons produce, with timing down to the millisecond.

They rested with their eyes closed for seven minutes, and in a separate seven-minute block they listened to ambient music.

The Montreal team reanalyzed those recordings with newer signal-processing tools and a machine-learning model trained to tell the LSD sessions from the placebo sessions.

Under LSD, alpha waves ran about 2 cycles per second faster than on placebo, and beta waves about 5 cycles per second faster, across large areas of the cortex. The waves did also weaken.

Once the speed-up was accounted for, that weakening held up as real rather than an artifact of looking in the wrong place.

The two effects didn’t fully overlap. The weakening was strongest in regions that handle hearing, language, and emotion.

The speed-up followed its own map, and the regions that weakened most tended to speed up least.

Anesthetics do the opposite to the alpha rhythm, slowing it down as the cortex becomes less excitable. The authors read the faster alpha under LSD as a sign of a more excitable cortex.

They also noted that psilocybin and DMT haven’t been shown to shift the peaks this way, so the speed-up may be a signature specific to LSD.

A fractal is a shape that repeats itself in its own parts, the way each floret of a Romanesco broccoli is a smaller broccoli.

The team measured how fractal the brain signal was over time, and how varied its patterns were. Both rose under LSD.

The increases were largest in areas tied to vision, hearing, memory, and mental imagery, and smallest in the motor cortex.

That split fits a familiar feature of the drug. Perception and inner experience change a great deal, while people can still move normally.

People on LSD often report seeing repeating geometric patterns with their eyes closed. The paper links the two only loosely.

“Our findings lend support to this intriguing hypothesis, but they do not yet confirm it,” Jerbi said. “We measure the fractal structure of brain signals over time, not the fractal structure of the images people perceive.”

Given only the shift in peak frequencies, the machine-learning model identified the LSD session more than 82% of the time.

Models trained on the fractal and complexity measures did almost as well. The brain regions the models relied on matched the ones the conventional statistics had flagged.

“Our machine-learning algorithm can distinguish brain states under LSD from those under placebo using brain signals alone,” Subramani said.

“By examining the clues it relies on, we can identify LSD’s signatures in brain activity.”

Music, which is routinely played during psychedelic therapy, didn’t amplify any of these effects. If anything the trend ran the other way, with slightly smaller brain changes during the music block.

That difference didn’t survive the statistical correction, and the authors called it exploratory.

Seventeen people is a small group, and everyone received a single dose while healthy, so the study says nothing about whether any of this helps patients.

The volunteers rated their experience at the peak of the drug’s effect, while the scanner ran about two and a half hours later.

The authors wrote that this gap may be why brain changes and reported experiences lined up only loosely.

The recordings also included no direct measure of muscle activity, which can contaminate the fastest brain signals. So the authors treated their high-frequency results with extra caution.

The next question is whether the speed-up really belongs to LSD alone.

Subramani and his colleagues are now running the same analysis on recordings taken under psilocybin, the active ingredient in magic mushrooms, and under DMT.

The aim is to see whether the three drugs produce the same signatures in the brain or different ones.

The full study was published in the journal Communications Biology.

Trump Justice Department Drops Attempt to Block Certification of Alaska Senate Election Result in "Voter Confusion" Case

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

I previously wrote about the Trump Justice Department's lawsuit seeking to block certification of the Alaska Senate election results, and the district court ruling rejecting it.  The Trump DOJ argued that blocking the  results was necessary to prevent "voter confusion" caused by the presence of two candidates with very similar names on the ballot. As I  explained previously, Republican candidate Dan J. Sullivan can potentially be confused with Dan S. Sullivan (the incumbent GOP senator), and some voters might have accidentally chosen Dan J. even though they intended to back Dan S.

Today, as reported by prominent election law scholar Rick Hasen, DOJ filed a brief in the US Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit seeking to dismiss the case. They also want the district court ruling vacated for procedural reasons (I will not opine on whether that's warranted).

This brings to an end a lawsuit that was always a turkey of a case (though perhaps calling it that is an insult to turkeys!). As explained in my post on the district court ruling and in an excellent amicus brief filed by Rick Hasen, the case never had any merit, and a court decision ruling in its favor would have set a dangerous precedent.

In addition, the election results show that Democratic candidate Mary Peltola got an absolute majority of the vote (about 50.1%), more than both Dan Sullivans and all other candidates combined. Thus, even if all of the 1.7% of voters who chose Dan J. actually intended to back Dan S., it made no difference. Alaska's system of ranked choice voting (under which, if no candidate gets a majority in the first round, votes get reallocated) might have further reduced any problem. But no reallocation took place here, because Peltola got an absolute majority to begin with. This is likely what led DOJ to finally thrown in the towel.

As noted in one of my earlier posts about the case, Democrats may well deserve blame for seeking to deceive voters in this way, even though it was never likely to change the result (evidence indicates Dan J. may have gotten on the ballot as part of a Democratic effort to siphon off voters from Dan S.). But for reasons outlined in the same post, this kind of thing is far from the worst example of exploitation of voter ignorance.

Peltola's win was mostly just part of the broader "Blue tsunami" which saw the Democratis gain 9 Senate seats, including in several traditional red states. But perhaps Peltola got over the top because of the immense popularity of her pet lizard! We could use more lizards on Capitol Hill.

The offer lets owners have their device examined for physical alterations. For anyone who has already used a suspect device, Ledger advised moving funds to a wallet with a fresh recovery phrase. The company also said in its advisory that the hardware it found was designed to capture those words during setup.

Ledger noted that the German cases it had investigated were localized to Berlin. The company advised concerned customers who bought a Ledger wallet from a third party there from January 2026 onward to contact its support team.

Ledger revealed it is still investigating reports involving two devices in Dubai and is working with distribution partners to learn how they entered circulation. It reported one affected Nano Gen 5 as it continues its investigation into Southeast Asian resellers.
