New R-Rated DC Movie Officially Replaces Bruce Wayne As Batman Next Month

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

DC has a surprisingly rich history of replacing Bruce Wayne as Batman. Famously, Terry McGinnis took up the cowl in Batman Beyond, proving pretty resoundingly that Gotham’s dark future could thrive under the shadow of a new Dark Knight, and he’s far from the only one.

The main DC comic continuity also has seen plenty of familiar faces pull on Wayne’s cowl, too: Dick Grayson, Tim Drake, and Damian Wayne have all taken a turn wrestling with the weight of the bat symbol. Even Jim Gordon and Alfred have temporarily become Gotham’s masked protector, while Jace Fox followed Terry McGinnis as a legitimate heir before shifting his operation to New York City. In other words, the supposed sanctity of Bruce Wayne as Batman has been interrupted a number of times.

The most notorious example, though, came in the wake of Bruce Wayne’s most famous defeat at the muscular hands of Bane in DC’s mid-'90s Knightfall arc. DC has long discussed the impact of being Batman on Bruce, and how heavy the cape really is, but it comes into focus most prominently when he steps aside, and Knightfall showed that cost better than any story.

Following the brutal climax of Batman: Knightfall - Part 1, the upcoming release of Batman: Knightfall - Part 2: Knightquest will officially hand the cowl to Bruce Wayne’s most infamous replacement. And while passing the torch often feels like a triumphant rite of passage, Knightfall is actually a terrifying warning about what happens when the wrong man tries to play god.

The ending of Knightfall: Part 1 delivered one of the most brutal cliffhangers in DC history as Bane systematically tore Batman apart and then snapped his spine. Played with dark relish by Michael Mando, the hulking DC villain was the real deal, and that climax was the perfect way to shatter the illusion of Batman’s invincibility. He did it to prove his superiority, as well as finally overcoming his fear of bats.

That same moment has been shown on screen before, of course, as Christopher Nolan partially adapted Knightfall into The Dark Knight Rises to send Christian Bale’s Batman spiraling to his lowest point of the trilogy. Tom Hardy plays a very different Bane, of course, and the twist in The Dark Knight Rise’s ending changes him further, but the image of the giant brute snapping Batman over his knee was still iconic.

Interestingly, Nolan’s final Batman movie actually used Batman’s vulnerability as the way in to tell a very different story than Knightfall. For Nolan, Bruce passing the torch (to Joseph Gordon-Levitt’s Blake) was important to underline the idea that Batman as a symbol was more important than Bruce Wayne’s destructive commitment to his duties. Nolan chose to give his Batman a happy ending by freeing him after Bane’s victory over him, but in Knightfall, the terrifying inverse of Nolan's philosophy is true.

The original comic storyline - and 2026’s excellent and faithful R-rated animated adaptation - confirms instead that Batman is not an off-the-shelf symbol that anyone can simply step into. The cowl requires restraint, empathy, and psychological discipline, and Bruce Wayne is very much presented as the only viable option to keep Gotham safe. Including from Batman.

When Bruce is paralyzed and forced off the streets, you might naturally expect that Dick Grayson would step up as his natural heir. But Bruce deliberately overlooked him, partly because DC wanted to protect the successful separation of Nightwing, but also for narrative reasons. They were somewhat estranged for a start, but Bruce also saw an opportunity to further redeem Jean-Paul Valley, the former assassin, by giving him the Batman mantle.

Big mistake. Batman’s broken legacy being added to the volatile background of Azrael was easily one of Bruce’s most idiotic moves. He was a brainwashed child killer, conditioned to kill by the System, and Batman’s strict moral code and no-kill rule simply doesn’t fit with how he’s built. He modifies the Batsuit into one of the coolest versions ever, thanks to DC’s desire to parody dark 1990s extreme antihero tropes, and he effectively becomes a monstrous, heavily weaponized mirror of Batman, instead of Valley changing himself.

As Knightfall Part 2 will show, Valley’s extreme measures and aggressive style did deliver results, and he’s able to stand up to Bane in his new suit, after initially suffering a defeat that drives him to make the improvements. But it all comes at a cost, and in contrast to Nolan’s message in The Dark Knight Rises, it becomes clear that Bruce Wayne brings something to being Batman that stops the hero becoming Gotham’s nightmare.

Batman: Knightfall Part 2: Knightquest will release on December 8.

The Strokes rocked Saturday Night Live with two new songs and a surprise appearance by Walton Goggins.

The New York City rock band appeared during the Druski-hosted episode on Nov. 7, performing “Lonely in the Future” and “Going Shopping,” both featured on its seventh studio album, Reality Awaits, which arrived in July.

For their first performance, the Julian Casablancas-led quintet delivered “Lonely in the Future” against a backdrop of psychedelic visuals and elaborate lighting. Following the performance, the band released the song’s official music video, which features a cast of handcrafted puppets created in collaboration with the team behind the British satirical series Spitting Image.

The group closed its SNL musical guest appearance with “Going Shopping,” which featured a surprise appearance by actor Goggins. Dressed in an all-black suit and sunglasses, the Fallout star joined the band on bongos, reprising his role in the song’s music video.

Saturday’s appearance marked The Strokes’ fourth time as musical guests on SNL. The band made its debut on the NBC sketch comedy show in January 2002, followed by performances in 2006 and 2011.

The Strokes are currently promoting Reality Awaits, which debuted at No. 13 on the all-genre Billboard 200 and No. 5 on the Top Rock & Alternative Albums chart.

The nine-track set was recorded in Costa Rica with legendary producer Rick Rubin, who previously worked with the act on its 2020 album, The New Abnormal.

Collision of two trains in Egypt kills at least four

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

President orders prompt investigation after one train derails in collision in al-Ayyat about 30 miles from Cairo

At least seven people were killed and more than 100 others injured when two trains collided on Sunday south of Cairo, authorities have said.

The crash occurred in al-Ayyat district in Giza province, about 30 miles (50km) south of the capital, when a train travelling to the south-western Fayoum province crashed into the rear of a train heading to the southern province of Aswan, according to the transport ministry. It said one of the trains had derailed.

The health ministry said at least seven people had been killed and more than 100 injured. The injured were taken to nearby hospitals for treatment, it said.

The president, Abdel Fatah al-Sisi, said in a social media post that he had ordered a prompt investigation into the crash to hold those responsible accountable.

Emergency teams and cranes were dispatched to the scene to clear the wreckage and restore services to their normal schedule as soon as possible, the Egyptian railway authority said.

A train driver and his assistant were injured in September last year when two freight trains collided in the same district.

Bills who need to keep rolling and those that must step up on MNF vs. Vikings

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Buffalo Bills (6-1) travel to Minneapolis on Monday Night Football to take on a Minnesota Vikings team that is also 6-1 and coming off its first loss of the season, 19-15 at Detroit. The Bills beat the Baltimore Ravens 36-10 last Sunday, but Baltimore lost both quarterbacks in the first half, and I'm careful about using that game as a measuring stick for this one. Minnesota is a different kind of test.

Brian Flores is the main reason. His defense leads the NFL in scoring (13.3 points per game) and blitzes on roughly 70 percent of dropbacks. Carolina is next at 42.6 percent (the Bills themselves blitzed on 64.5 percent of dropbacks last Sunday, second only to Minnesota in Week 8, per Next Gen Stats). Josh Allen completes 65.7 percent of his passes against five or more rushers at 8.4 yards per attempt, so, on paper it's a great matchup but the reality is that nobody is better than Flores sending 5 or more after opposing QBs. Buffalo has the league's best offense (33.3 points per game) and the best EPA per play (0.175), and Minnesota has the best defensive mark (-0.189).

The Vikings' offense is their problem on the other side. It ranks 30th in EPA per play (-0.099), Carson Wentz starts with Kyler Murray out with a hamstring injury, and Justin Jefferson has 335 yards heading into Week 9, a lower pace than his career-low 2025, per Pro Football Talk. I think the game comes down to whether Buffalo's offense can stay on schedule against the blitz while the defense tries to not give Wentz and Jefferson easy completions. With all of this in mind, here are the Bills who need to keep rolling and those who must step up Monday night.

Zay Flowers entered Week 8 second in the NFL in receiving yards per game (101.3) and first in yards after the catch (331). He caught none of his five targets, and four came from rookie Joe Fagnano. Jim Leonhard also changed the plan: the Bills ran man coverage on 54.8 percent of dropbacks, up from 19.5 percent, per Next Gen Stats.

Benford was a big part of that. His coverage EPA of -5.8 was sixth in the NFL in Week 8, he was eighth in catch rate allowed over expected, and his tight-window percentage (50) tied Damar Hamlin for sixth. He played 45 of 51 snaps. He has found his rhythm after a slow start — Pro Football Reference has him allowing a 106.6 passer rating through his first five games (22-of-36, three touchdowns). PFF had him 72nd of 102 corners (55.4) through Week 8. Monday needs the last two games version.

Jefferson has 28 catches for 335 yards and four touchdowns. In games Wentz has played, he gets 31.5 percent of targets and 45.2 percent of air yards, and averages 2.52 yards per route run, per Sharp Football Analysis. Against Detroit, he caught five of nine targets for 33 yards and a touchdown. With the offense 30th in EPA, stopping him goes a long way toward stopping Minnesota.

In his three full games, Moore has a 28.7 percent target share and 42.3 percent air yards share, which Next Gen Stats ranks sixth and fourth in the league. Against Baltimore he caught four of seven targets for 82 yards and a touchdown. Downfield, he caught three of four for 78 yards and a score, including a 43-air-yard completion that was Allen's longest of the game by air yards. PFF had him on the field for 37 of Allen's 42 pass plays (54 snaps).

As stated above, Allen has been successful against five or more rushers. That kind of defense leaves a receiver one-on-one, and Moore is the one the offense looks to first. His AC joint sprain is the variable. He's always one bad fall over his shoulder away from leaving the game and making Josh's life tougher. Keon Colrman has stepped up when it happened and could be another key in this matchup.

Grable allowed two pressures on 42 pass-blocking snaps against Baltimore, per charting-based metrics cited by Buffalo Rumblings. Both came in true pass sets, and he had 12 of those. He played all 82 snaps, and Allen was sacked once. The numbers aren't clean, though: PFF had him at 38.8 in Week 5 after he replaced Spencer Brown, and 55.8 for the season over 133 snaps. His negatively graded run-block play rate was 13.2 percent, against 5.3 percent for Dion Dawkins.

The veteran is questionable with a rib injury and hasn't practiced, though Joe Brady said he could play anyway. If he sits, which I expect, Grable likely moves to left tackle with Brown at right. Minnesota's rush is led by Andrew Van Ginkel (6.5 sacks) and Dallas Turner (six sacks, 19 quarterback hits), per ESPN. The Bills line has allowed the third-fewest pressures (74) and seventh-fewest sacks (13). Grable helps decide whether that holds.

Cook has rushed for at least 75 yards and a touchdown in six straight games, but he got 80 yards on 21 carries (3.8 per carry) against the Ravens. On the season he has 707 yards and seven touchdowns on 138 carries. The Bills rank third in rush EPA (0.013) and second in rush success rate (49.7 percent). Excluding Week 8, Buffalo led the NFL in rush DVOA (16.1 percent).

The Vikings are beatable on the ground. They rank 17th in rush yards allowed (116.7 per game) and gave Jahmyr Gibbs 153 yards and a touchdown on 23 carries last week. Screens have produced +0.39 EPA per play on 12 attempts against Minnesota, per SIS. Cook played 44 snaps (54 percent) against Baltimore and had three targets. He needs to be both the draw and the screen outlet against the blitz. We know very well Joe Brady loves a screen opportunity.

The SIS data points to quick throws, bootlegs, and screens as what has worked against Minnesota (+0.24, +0.37 and +0.39 EPA per play). The Woman Who Said Yes While Shaking Her Head No

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Early on in my career, I was a research manager working on consumer health products for a pharmaceutical company. Part of my job was to develop ideas for new products by testing them in large quantitative surveys to forecast sales. We showed respondents product descriptions I had painstakingly written and asked how likely they would be to buy it.

We tabulated the results to the decimal point, giving the data an aura of accuracy. But there was one problem. What people said in surveys didn’t reliably predict what they bought in market.

I came across a clue as to why sitting behind a two-way mirror. The focus group moderator was reading a product concept we tested in an earlier survey. She asked: “If this was available, would you buy it?” Without hesitating, a woman across the table clearly said yes, while her head was already shaking no.

The woman herself and the moderator didn’t notice. And we all tallied a yes in the backroom.

But her words just didn’t match her gesture. This doesn’t mean a headshake is the same thing as a lie detector. I wasn’t sure what she might do later. But I knew at that time that her answer was less settled than she made it sound.

From then on, I would note verbal and nonverbal responses, and I kept seeing the same split. A yes would arrive with a wince, eye roll, or furrowed brow. Sometimes they would say deadpan they are excited to buy. I would ask them to write down their reasons. The written words seemed well-reasoned and coherent. Over time, the unspoken reactions helped explain what my self-reported surveys were missing. It’s not like the words were useless. But they weren’t the clear window into behavior that the market research industry was built on.

I also had been assuming that honest people could explain their answers. But I started doubting that, and it changed my career path. The pharmaceutical company offered me a paid executive MBA at Columbia. I asked if I could get a degree in psychology. They said no, so I said no. I left to work at a qualitative research firm conducting focus groups and in-depth interviews. The answers weren’t in my spreadsheets, so I’d rather be in the room with people, so I could see the real story show up before the words.

Years later, I found a different kind of research and an aha moment that helped explain the “say versus do gap” that had undermined my earlier self-reported quantitative forecasts.

Neuroscientist Michael Gazzaniga conducted fascinating studies on patients whose corpus callosum, the fibers connecting the two hemispheres of the brain, had been cut to control severe epilepsy. The surgery sharply limited communication between hemispheres, which allowed him to show patients information to one side of the brain without the other side seeing it.

A patient was shown the word “walk” to their right hemisphere and he got up and began walking and the experimenter asked him why. The left hemisphere, which controls speech, hadn’t seen the instruction, so the honest answer should have been "I don’t know." But instead, he said something completely sincere: "I was thirsty and wanted a drink."

Gazzaniga called this left hemisphere function the interpreter: a process that takes something we just did and creates the most coherent explanation based upon whatever information is available.

The woman behind the glass wasn’t a split-brain patient. Her hemispheres communicated normally. We can’t compare the two research studies literally. But what she did share with split-brain patients was the gap: a fluent verbal response that didn’t match her actions. People with intact brains base their explanations on much more information and they often get it right. But Gazzaniga argues that the interpreter is not just a quirk from surgery but something all our brains do as we attempt to make our experiences feel unified and our actions seem like our own doing. The surgery simply made the interpreter more visible; by cutting off information we would normally use.

You can see this in people with normal brains. Researchers at Lund University in Sweden conducted an experiment that let people in a supermarket taste jam. Shoppers were asked which jam they preferred and then asked to taste it a second time. But the experimenters secretly swapped out their preferred jam with another. Most of the participants didn’t even notice the swap, and when asked to describe why they preferred it, they easily explained why, even though they had just rejected it. Despite their brain being fully intact, their reasons seemed like honest reports when they may have been made up.

Have you ever bought something on a whim? You can probably think of a reason why easily. But here’s a harder question: How do you know that is the real reason? Maybe the answer arrived later based on what you know about the product, the moment you bought it, and the person you believe yourself to be?

Or how about bigger decisions, like the job that felt right, the person you just met that you trusted, or that comment from your loved one that made you snap, only to explain it after as: I was just having a bad day. I’m not saying your explanations are fictitious. But maybe it was the simplest story you could put together on such short notice, while leaving out a deeper feeling of being dismissed by them, or some other worry you felt but hadn’t yet named.

Your reasons are not necessarily lies. But alone they’re incomplete. Sometimes there’s a story, and then there’s the real story. When you close a conversation with a quick confident answer you miss an opportunity to dig deeper for a question that deserves to stay open a little longer.

If you ever find yourself doing something surprising, remember your first explanation is just the start not the verdict. Think about what was happening right before and try to parse out if you were moving away or toward something. We tend to avoid bad feelings rather than try to get what we want. We choose a more expensive bottle to avoid looking cheap, agree with someone to avoid a fight, or put off doing something because we fear what might happen. Those reasons are usually not the first things to come to mind. Make sure to check if your story fits everything, not just the things you noticed.

I never learned what that woman finally did. She said yes, but her body said no. And while both responses came from her, neither told the whole story.

Your explanation may be sincere, but it also might be incomplete.

Scientists Discover a Hidden Eye Disease That Can Steal Your Night Vision

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Researchers can spot signs of a newly identified genetic eye disease before light-sensing cells are lost by measuring how vision recovers in darkness.

People with a newly identified genetic eye condition can see well for most of their lives before gradually losing peripheral vision and struggling to see at dusk or at night. The condition is linked to a gene already associated with a different eye disease, one that affects central vision first.

The two conditions arise from different variants, or versions, of the EFEMP1 gene. The newly identified variant, p.Arg140Trp, causes abnormally thick material to build up between cells in the eye. Researchers are calling the condition EFEMP1-associated late-onset retinal degeneration, or L-ORD, although it has not been formally named.

L-ORD begins slowly at the periphery of the retina, the tissue at the back of the eye that converts light into signals the brain interprets as sight. Its gradual progression makes it difficult to detect. One useful clue is unusually slow recovery of vision when a person moves from a lighted setting into low light. This adjustment involves rod photoreceptors, the retinal cells specialized for seeing in dim conditions.

“We can detect a major abnormality in the way rod photoreceptors recover in darkness with retinas that still look structurally intact,” said Artur V. Cideciyan, PhD, an ophthalmology researcher and co-director of the Center for Hereditary Retinal Degenerations. “This gives us a functional marker of disease at a stage when photoreceptor cells have not yet been lost.”

The investigation began with a clinical description of a family in the United States in 1998.

“The different disease expression in this family was thought to be similar to a condition caused by C1QTNF5 gene variants. However, none of the variants associated with that disease were found,” Cideciyan said. “Our collaborators then discovered a new variant in the EFEMP1 gene that normally causes a different disease where the macula, which is tied to central vision, is affected first.”

The genetic finding allowed researchers to expand the investigation to multiple European families. They studied three unrelated families in different countries that shared the p.Arg140Trp variant and compared them with a separate family carrying p.Arg345Trp, the EFEMP1 variant responsible for the already-known condition affecting central vision.

Published in JAMA Ophthalmology, the research involved an international team that included scientists from the Scheie Eye Institute of the Perelman School of Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania. Researchers from the University of Edinburgh, the University of Basel, Charles University, and the University of Bonn also contributed.

The researchers do not yet know how widespread p.Arg140Trp is or how many people have this form of L-ORD. They suspect it may be relatively rare, but larger studies are needed to establish its prevalence.

“Aging is a risk factor for many medical conditions, including retinal diseases such as age-related macular degeneration, the most common retinal condition of aging,” said Tomas S. Aleman, MD, a professor of ophthalmology and the other co-director of the Center for Hereditary Retinal Degenerations. “Lessons learned from these infrequent, late-onset genetic eye diseases offer glimpses at possible mechanisms of disease for much more frequent retinal problems.”

Meatloaf Is So Much Juicier When You Add This Boxed Ingredient

When you want a meal that sticks to your ribs and brings a measure of comfort with every bite, you want a good, homemade meatloaf, one of America's favorite dinners. It's traditionally made with ground beef, though to keep things tender, people often include ground pork or Italian sausage. Some include a cooked-down vegetable mix to help the loaf stay moist, while others employ the use of milk. But there's a boxed (or bagged) ingredient likely already in your pantry that you might bring out next time you make meatloaf and want to avoid a crumbly, cracking mess, and it'll help create the juiciest version you've likely ever had. Instant mashed potatoes, which are great to keep on hand for soups and sauces, are about to be your secret weapon in the battle against dry meatloaf, too.

Instant mashed potatoes might sound like a strange mix-in, but hear us out. They're dehydrated and flaky, which makes them ideal when used in meatloaf because when those flakes get dampened by your other ingredients (meat, milk, egg), they thicken, adding bulk and binding the mixture together. More importantly, though, these hard-working flakes hold onto moisture, locking it inside the loaf inside instead of letting the juices run out quite so freely, so your dinner won't dry out during its long cook in the oven.

While moist meatloaf is reason enough to add instant mashed potatoes to the mix, there are a few other benefits worth considering. First and foremost: They're cheap, and they keep for a long time. You can typically get a box from Walmart for less than $4 (prices may vary), and its bags often start at just a buck apiece. Many brands can last up to a year, so you can keep them in your pantry, and they'll likely be good to go the next time you're in the mood for meatloaf (or shortcut gnocchi, for that matter).

Furthermore, most instant mashed potatoes are gluten-free. While you'll want to be sure to check the labels before using them, these potato flakes can often make meatloaf accessible to our friends and family with celiac disease or gluten sensitivities, since they can substitute for breadcrumbs.

Finally, a few tips when employing instant spuds in your dinner loaf. You might let them sit for a few minutes after combining them with the other ingredients, before you introduce the meat; this gives them the opportunity to "set up." And you're not confined to just plain buttery instant mashed potatoes. Give your supper a major upgrade with different flavors of boxed flakes, like sour cream and chives, four cheese, and more.
