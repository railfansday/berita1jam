What is Muse Ember? Meet Meta’s new low-cost AI model

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Booking a vacation sounds like one thing to hand off to an AI assistant, right up until it has to check the calendar, open a dozen tabs, and figure out whether the cheapest flight is still the cheapest once a carry-on is involved. Every one of those steps takes work, and when an AI agent is doing it, the cost can add up.

Meta has a new model built for that part of the job.

On Nov. 9, Meta's Superintelligence Labs introduced Muse Ember, which the company calls its smallest and most cost-efficient AI model yet. It's designed for the steps an agent might repeat dozens of times in a single assignment, like pulling details out of a document, calling another tool, or running a test after changing a line of code.

Muse is the app with the cute little avatar — named Jolly — that Meta has been promoting since September. The AI assistant can work across (some) apps with a user's permission. The app passed 5 million downloads in 22 days, and has already had many updates; last week, Meta started rolling out voice conversations and animated avatars for the assistant in the U.S. The best tech deal this week The Apple iPhone 18 Pro is free right now with T-Mobile Products available for purchase through affiliate links. Meta also uses the Muse name for a family of AI models. The company positions Muse Wonder as the model for its toughest problems and Muse Spark as a model for coding and other work that involves using tools. Ember is the cheaper option for repeatable tasks. Meta's pitch is that developers can use Wonder to plan a complicated job and hand off the individual steps to Ember. For now, Meta lists Ember as available through its Muse Code tool and developer API.

In early testing shared by Meta, cloud storage company Box said it used Ember to check contracts clause by clause against a company’s negotiation rules and assign an overall risk rating. Meta says the model can take in up to a million tokens at once, enough to work through large sets of documents, and can handle images, audio, and video alongside text. That gives developers room to build an agent that can read a scanned contract or pull details from a meeting recording.

The price is what makes Ember interesting. On Artificial Analysis's benchmark, Ember and Anthropic's Claude Haiku 5.5 both rounded to a score of 36 at their highest reasoning settings. Ember cost about 14 cents per benchmark task, compared with 41 cents for Haiku, even though the two have the same standard rates of 10 cents per million input tokens and 50 cents per million output tokens. OpenAI's GPT-6 Luna scored 28 and cost about 15 cents per task at its highest setting.

In Meta's published results, Ember beat Haiku on a terminal coding test, while Haiku did better on a test that judged whether code changes were ready to merge. Meta added that it already has even cheaper models in training.

As the company continues to bet that AI assistants will take on more everyday tasks, developers will need an affordable way to handle all the little steps behind the scenes. A flight search, for example, can mean sorting through dozens of fares, each with its own rules. If Meta wants people to hand off that kind of chore every day, it has to make checking the carry-on policy cheap on its end, too.

Dinosaurs May Have Eaten Far More Fruit Than Scientists Ever Thought

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

An exceptionally preserved 74-million-year-old forest in New Mexico is revealing unexpected details about ancient plant life.

Dinosaurs may have been eating a much wider variety of fruits than scientists once believed. Fossils from a 74-million-year-old forest in New Mexico show that some flowering plants were already producing surprisingly large fruits millions of years before the asteroid impact that ended the age of non-avian dinosaurs.

The findings, published in the journal Science, challenge the longstanding theory that larger fruits and more complex seed dispersal strategies evolved primarily after the extinction event 66 million years ago. They also suggest that dinosaurs and other prehistoric animals may have helped spread the seeds of flowering plants much earlier than previously thought.

The research centers on an exceptionally preserved fossil forest near Truth or Consequences, New Mexico. James Saulsbury, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Kansas Biodiversity Institute and Natural History Museum, helped collect, classify, and analyze the plant fossils discovered there.

“This paper focuses on a fossil forest in New Mexico and the preserved fruits, seeds and other reproductive structures of the plants that lived there,” Saulsbury said. “These fossils change how we understand the way flowering plants became modern in terms of their reproductive ecology.”

The forest was buried beneath volcanic ash that eventually hardened into rock. The preserved deposit stretches more than a kilometer (about 0.6 miles), capturing plants from approximately the same period and retaining evidence of how they were arranged across the ancient landscape.

“The New Mexico fossil site is exceptional,” Saulsbury said. “My co-authors have referred to it as a ‘botanical Pompeii’ because of its remarkable preservation. The site contains a layer of volcanic ash preserved as rock, known as tuff, that stretches for more than a kilometer. The deposits formed at essentially the same time, preserving many plants where they lived and died. It provides a snapshot of a forest from about 74 million years ago and even preserves aspects of its spatial arrangement.”

Flowering plants first appeared approximately 136 million years ago, but researchers have traditionally believed that their reproductive strategies remained relatively simple throughout much of the Cretaceous Period. Early flowering plants generally produced small seeds and fruits, with wind and other non-animal mechanisms thought to play major roles in seed dispersal.

“For much of their early history, they were relatively small, weedy plants that did not dominate forest canopies,” Saulsbury said. “Although they gradually diversified and occupied more ecological niches, it was thought their seeds and dispersal structures remained small and were primarily dispersed by wind or without fleshy fruit.”

Many early flowering plant seeds were thought to have ranged from dust-sized particles to roughly the size of poppy seeds. By contrast, the New Mexico fossils include fruits approximately as large as modern grapes. Such fruits are much larger than those previously expected from flowering plants of that age.

“The prevailing view has been that this more diverse reproductive ecology emerged only after the end-Cretaceous extinction,” Saulsbury said. “Our study suggests that transition began much earlier. The largest fruits in our fossil flora are about the size of a grape. That may not sound especially large today, but it is remarkably large for flowering plants of that period.”

The team examined hundreds of fossilized seeds, sorting them according to their physical characteristics. Because fossil material often lacks the features necessary to identify an exact species, researchers organized specimens into groups based on their shapes and structures.

“We sorted hundreds of fossil seeds into what we call morphotypes,” Saulsbury said. “These are groupings based on form rather than confirmed species because it is often impossible to determine whether similar-looking fossils represent one species or several.”

The fossil assemblage contains an unusual diversity of fruits, seeds, and reproductive structures. Although the exceptional preservation helped researchers recover this material, Saulsbury believes the forest itself differed from many other known Cretaceous environments.

“However, we don’t believe these findings are simply the result of preservation bias,” he said. “It appears to represent a genuinely unusual forest.”

Fruit size can provide information about the environments in which plants evolved and how their seeds may have spread. Today, many flowering plants produce fleshy fruits that attract animals, which can transport seeds away from the parent plant after eating the fruit.

“Large fruits tend to occur in moist environments with densely packed forests,” Saulsbury said. “Our findings indicate that modern-looking forest structure and reproductive ecology were developing earlier than previously believed. The diversification of flowering plant reproductive strategies did not have to wait until after the end-of-Cretaceous extinction.”

The discovery also complicates an established explanation for the evolution of fruit-bearing plants. Scientists had proposed that large, fleshy fruits became more widespread as mammals diversified following the extinction of non-avian dinosaurs, creating new opportunities for plants to disperse their seeds through animals.

“As mammals expanded into newly available ecological niches, flowering plants were thought to have diversified their seed dispersal strategies in response,” Saulsbury said. “Our findings suggest that story began earlier. If diverse, large fruits already existed in Cretaceous forests, they were likely being dispersed not only by early mammals but also by large dinosaurs and other animals.”

Reference: “Diversification of angiosperm reproductive strategies predated the end-Cretaceous extinction” by Jaemin Lee, Dori L. Contreras, James G. Saulsbury, Garland R. Upchurch and Cindy V. Looy, 25 June 2026, Science. DOI: 10.1126/science.adw9457

Meta introduces Muse Ember to power cost-efficient AI agents

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Meta Platforms Inc. today introduced Muse Ember, an entry-level artificial intelligence model that costs significantly less than the competition.

Notably, it’s the first AI that the company trained using Muse Wonder. The latter algorithm is the Facebook parent’s flagship large language model. Meta didn’t specify which part of the workflow it automated with Muse Wonder.

One possibility is that the company used it to perform post-training, the task of honing a model’s capabilities by training it on additional data. Thinking Machines Labs Inc. recently demonstrated that even a relatively small LLM can run post-training runs end-to-end. Anthropic PBC, for its part, last week revealed that its AI models could soon gain the ability to significantly accelerate machine learning research.

Applications often use frontier LLMs such as Muse Wonder as part of AI ensembles. When a user enters a request, the frontier LLM splits it into smaller tasks and assigns them to agents powered by a lower-cost model. Meta says Muse Ember is specifically optimized to play the latter role.

The company evaluated the model using the Artificial Analysis Index, which aggregates nine AI benchmarks spanning use cases such as coding and financial analysis. Meta says that Muse Ember matched the score of Anthropic’s entry-level Claude Haiku 5.5 model at about a third the price. It also outperformed OpenAI Group PBC’s comparable GPT-6 Luna algorithm.

The same internal evaluation saw Meta test Muse Ember and the two rival models across nine other benchmarks. All three algorithms were in “max” mode, which means they used the maximum amount of hardware at their disposal instead of prioritizing cost-efficiency. Muse Ember outperformed Haiku and Luna across seven of the benchmarks.

The area where the model appears to have the biggest edge is business task automation. Whereas most of Muse Ember’s benchmark results were only a few percentage points better than those of Haiku and Luna, it clocked a 17% lead on AutomationBench-AA. It’s a collection of 657 tasks that span popular cloud applications such as Gmail and Salesforce.

Muse Ember has a context window of 1 million tokens, which enables it to ingest lengthy business documents. Users can also include images, audio and video in their prompts. The model generates responses up to 512,000 tokens in length.

Muse Ember is available through Meta’s Model API for 10 cents per million input tokens and 50 cents per million output tokens. A built-in cache can reuse frequently used prompts to avoid duplicate processing. When the feature is active, Muse Ember’s pricing drops to 0.4 cents per million input tokens.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/healthtap-unveils-virtual-primary-care-program-medicare-advantage-plans" hreflang="en">HealthTap unveils virtual primary care program for Medicare Advantage plans</a>

HealthTap unveils virtual primary care program for Medicare Advantage plans" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

HealthTap is rolling out a new program for Medicare Advantage plans that seeks to help members form a connection with a primary care doctor that carries forward.

The program leans on a combination of proactive outreach and access to a patient's historical medical records to support plans in identifying needs or risks, filling care gaps and coordinating follow-up or other necessary care. It's built to directly support individuals who may not have a PCP on file.

HealthTap reaches out to those members proactively, the company said, and helps them schedule an initial virtual visit with a provider who stays connected for preventive services, chronic condition management and medication concerns.

"The angle that we're taking is: wouldn't it be cool if, in fact, your virtual primary care physician was just like a bricks and mortar primary care physician?" said Rick Cochran, senior vice president of health plans at HealthTap, in an interview with Fierce Healthcare.

In an ideal scenario, a patient who has a consistent primary care physician who they see over the course of multiple years and who they come to trust, he said. HealthTap is looking to build that experience with virtual primary care docs, and reach patients who are disconnected from the health system with critical services.

Cochran said that the team, at times, receives feedback about concern that a program like this could upset in-network practices. However, he said that HealthTap views these service lines as complementary, and the company as offering another way for insurers to reach members who are underserved and in need of help.

"There's a bunch of people who haven't been receiving care that we can work with," he said. "We're not attempting to disrupt anything. We're attempting to complement things."

Through the program, HealthTap can connect with members via text messaging, email, direct mail or phone, at the plan's discretion. The member can then schedule the appointment for themselves and choose a board-certified primary care physician based in their state.

Between video visits, the member can also connect with their physician through secure messaging, HealthTap said.

In aiming to make the experience feel as much like traditional, brick-and-mortar clinics as possible, Cochran said that members are allowed to switch their physicians and find the one that best matches their individual health needs.

And to better support the physicians, HealthTap pulls from health information networks to pull key medical records, including relevant data from before an individual enrolled in their current plan. That set-up also means that the patient can carry their relationship with a HealthTap doc forward should they switch plans, Cochran said.

Cochran said that COVID-19 really kicked down the door to virtual care for seniors, and there's significant opportunity in MA for a program like HealthTap's, as these patients often have multiple comorbidities and may face barriers to seeing a doctor in person, such as the lack of transportation.

The company did an initial deployment with one MA plan and found that 35% of the initial outreach cohort scheduled an appointment, and 19% completed their first visit within six weeks. Satisfaction scores for both patients and physicians are high, Cochran said.

The approach gives doctors more time to spend working "to make people better, not crank through visits," he said.

"Our physician satisfaction score is very high because our physicians have the time to spend with the patient to ensure that the patient's needs are met," he said.
