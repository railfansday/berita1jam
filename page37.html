Nightmare at Dens Park: Dundee come to terms with history as child asks ‘what about last night?’

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It wasn’t quite the morning after the night before. Nevertheless, Dundee players were very obviously still bearing the mental scars on Monday nearly 48 hours after a night of infamy inflicted by Hearts.

An old adage is that it’s best to go again as quickly as possible to get the performance and - most urgently of all – the result out of the system.

“To a point, yes…” agreed Steven Pressley when this theory was put to him at the sun-sprayed Dundee training facility at Gardyne College Campus ahead of Tuesday’s return to action against Hibs.

The counter argument, one the Dundee manager was clearly conscious of, is that three nights later is maybe just a bit too soon. In addition, when it comes to grounds Dundee might choose to visit for their next assignment after losing 8-0 – or should that be 0-8 – then Easter Road would not be high on the list. Dundee have not won there for a quarter of a century. The record books are being well-thumbed just now.

“We obviously don’t have a lot of time to work on elements of our game plan,” said Pressley. “It has been pretty frantic.

“Not only have we had to plan how we have to play and be prepared for Hibernian, I have also had to think a lot about the management of the group and everybody within that. I have had to find a way to stabilise the group.”

The employment of such words as “stabilise” reflect the harrowing nature of the experience. A group of 16 players including substitutes, many of them in their early 20s, endured something akin to a medical procedure.

A rampant Hearts side removed their sense of self-belief with almost surgical precision. Professional sport is often about how quickly such wounds can heal.

But this is new territory for everyone, including those as steeped in the game as Pressley. Owen Goodman, the 22-year-old keeper on loan from Crystal Palace, was perhaps left with most to feel sore about on Saturday night as goal after goal whizzed past him.

Dundee presumably have a duty of care towards him. It’s maybe one reason why Pressley decided against showing a re-run of the match the following morning. “I didn’t think putting them through the agony of watching it again was helpful,” he said.

Goodman was the last Dundee player to leave the field and one of the first in on Monday. He mentioned never having let in eight goals before, even in academy games. This was new territory for so many.

Young men such as Goodman carry a huge burden of responsibility as they attempt to bounce back from a historic defeat. An updated edition of the definitive Dundee FC history book, They Wore the Dark Blue, has just been published. It’s already out of date in one respect. Although it contains every result, competitive and otherwise, dating back to 1893, the Dundee fans' bible doesn’t include what now stands as the club’s biggest-ever home loss.

Such landmark results usually belong in the late 1800s, rendered a curiosity rather than a wounding detail due to the passage of time.

In Dundee’s case, prior to Saturday, their biggest-ever home defeat was relatively recent – 1971, against a Celtic side who had been crowned European champions in recent enough memory for one newspaper to describe them having “conjured the magic of Lisbon” at Dens Park following their 8-1 win.

Aberdeen equalled the margin of victory if not the scoreline in 2017. But Hearts outdid both those results on Saturday night. When Stuart Findlay rose like Alan Gilzean to nod in No 8 with a minute or so of the game left, the old pillars in the Dens Park main stand might have warped a fraction in recognition of the goal’s significance. Even if few, least of all Findlay, were probably aware of it at the time. A historic low dating back to 1893 – 1899 in the case of Dens Park itself – had been recorded.

Extraordinary, unthinkable results do happen. Just ask Hearts. Five goals down to Falkirk at home just a few weeks ago, they at least rallied to 5-2. Rallying was something Dundee conspicuously failed to do, which might have been the most disappointing aspect of the night for Pressley personally. He shouldered the blame himself, citing his suspicion that a poor performance had been in the post. He just didn't realise how poor. When he sought to rectify things at half time by sending on three substitutes there was precious little reaction. If anything things got worse, with Dundee reduced to ten men due to Owen Bevan’s red card.

Often teams who rack up handsome half-time leads cannot maintain such intensity in the second half due to a combination of factors, including stiff resolve from the opposition to avoid further embarrassment. This wasn’t evident on Saturday. Hearts scored another four goals to match their first-half quota and might have scored more, with another chalked off for a handball.

Although he says he did not consider tendering his resignation, it’s safe to assume Pressley endured a long dark night of the soul on Saturday night when he returned home to Edinburgh.

He said he found strength “from I don’t know where” and has been buoyed from a raft of messages of support. Perhaps Drey Wright, the Dundee wing-back, got to the heart of what it all meant on a human level.

“My six-year-old was at home watching the game, getting very upset with every goal that's going in,” said the 30-year-old.

“My eight-year-old is putting his hand on the shoulder of my wife, kind of subconsciously helping her out. So that's not easy to hear.

“I was back in training yesterday and later I was at the dinner table and my eldest says: ‘So Dad, what about that score last night?’ And it’s this kind of a back-to-reality moment. There’s no getting away from it.”

Tarkowski's first Premier League red ruins Moyes' night - was it deserved?

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Janelt scores stunner as Brentford beat 10-man Everton

It had been some run for Everton captain James Tarkowski.

Prior to Monday night, the defender had received 74 yellow cards in the Premier League but had never been sent off - a top-flight record.

But that run came to an end at Brentford as he was given his first red card in his 351st Premier League appearance, although the decision has attracted plenty of debate.

His dismissal crucially means he will miss Sunday's Merseyside derby and also ruined David Moyes' record-breaking night as he took charge of his 593rd game as Everton boss - more than anyone else in the club's history.

The 4-0 loss was also the heaviest defeat Everton have had since Moyes returned to the club at the start of last year.

Tarkowski's dismissal proved a big talking point during and after the match, with opinions split on whether he should have gone. Even Moyes wasn't sure.

He told BBC Match of the Day: "My thoughts at the time, I didn't think it was but when I've looked at it again I think his foot high. Is there enough force for it to be a red card? Not sure about that."

James Tarkowski received his first Premier League red card

Just 15 minutes after Keane Lewis-Potter scored his second deflected strike of the evening, Everton skipper Tarkowski thundered into a challenge on Brentford's Yehor Yarmoliuk.

He won the ball but also followed through with the challenge forcefully. Initially, referee Craig Pawson deemed a foul and a yellow card was enough.

But, after an intervention from the video assistant referee (VAR) and studying the replay, Pawson changed the yellow for a red.

The Premier League match centre said on X: "After VAR review, the referee overturned the original decision of yellow card for Tarkowski for the challenge on Yarmoliuk and issued a red card for serious foul play.

"VAR: 'Point of contact is on the shin. He's got speed and intensity. Studs are high.'

"REF: 'After review, Everton No.6 commits a challenge that endangers his opponent's safety, making contact with the studs onto the shin. Cancelling the yellow card - and a red card.'"

Referees are asked to take into account several considerations for what counts as serious foul play including the speed of the challenge, its intensity, force and what degree of control there was.

But, as always, there was plenty of debate about whether it was the correct decision.

BBC Sport's football issues correspondent Dale Johnson, said: "Really not sure about this as a VAR red card.

"Yes, Tarkowski absolutely ticks the boxes for speed and intensity and leading high with studs showing.

"But the question marks are that Tarkowski made only small contact and didn't have a straight leg when he did so.

"Maybe his reputation played a part? Remember, he wrongly escaped a VAR red card against Liverpool for a challenge, which was along the same lines. He won the ball and then followed through into Alexis Mac Allister. Thought that was a far stronger challenge."

BBC Radio 5 Live's - and former Blackburn Rovers striker - Chris Sutton, said on X: "Come on that's not a redâ¦ the game's gone."

Former Arsenal striker Alan Smith agreed the decision was harsh. He told Sky Sports: "Ten years ago we wouldn't have looked at it once and now we still [frame] it on the follow-through. I just can't see it.

"Only Tarkowski knows if he wanted to leave it high or if it was natural. The follow-through was just natural momentum.

"There's a bit of anger in there from Tarkowski and it's a little high but only he knows if he wanted to leave a little more on Yarmoliuk there."

Opinion was far from unanimous though, with both pundits in the Sky Sports studio adamant it was a clear red.

Former Liverpool centre-back Jamie Carragher said: "It's a red card. Tarkowski has got previous for tackles like that. He is a 90s defender, he tackles like that.

"He follows through with such force, it is excessive. You want to give him the benefit of the doubt but it's a red card."

He added: "You could argue was it a big enough mistake for VAR to get involved but I think we are splitting hairs. He doesn't need to do this."

While ex-Totenham defender Toby Alderweireld added: "It's a red card, he knows what he is doing. 100%."

Before paying extra for premium dog or cat food, find the small-print line on the package that names your pet’s life stage and AAFCO, the group that sets pet food nutrient standards. Any food with that line meets the same standards as pricier brands, so buy the cheapest one your pet does well on.

In each food’s biggest bag at Walmart, the store’s own Pure Balance Chicken & Brown Rice Recipe costs $1.17 a pound, less than half the $2.67 a pound for Blue Buffalo’s Wilderness Grain-Free Chicken Recipe. Both dry dog foods carry that line and have nearly the same calories per pound.

That line is the nutritional adequacy statement, and newer labels add a short version on the front, such as Complete Food for Adult Dogs. The statement says the food was formulated to meet the nutrient standards of the Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO) or passed AAFCO feeding trials. It also names a life stage, such as growth for puppies and kittens, adult maintenance, or all life stages. The FDA says a food has to pass one of those tests to be called complete and balanced, which means it’s meant to be your pet’s only food. One labeled for intermittent or supplemental feeding only isn’t a complete diet.

Premium claims don’t make a food more complete. Natural describes how ingredients are sourced and processed, organic how they’re grown, and human-grade how the food and its ingredients are handled. Grain-free isn’t healthier for most pets either. Veterinary nutritionists at Tufts University call it a marketing concept and say grain allergies are rare.

The FDA has also investigated reports of a heart disease called dilated cardiomyopathy in dogs eating certain foods, many of them grain-free. It hasn’t established that the foods cause the disease, but ask your vet before switching your dog to grain-free.

So compare prices only among foods whose statement names your pet’s life stage, and look at dry food first. In a Tufts price check of cat foods, even the most expensive dry food cost less per day than the cheapest canned one, for the same calories.

If you’d rather cook, have a veterinary nutritionist write the recipe for any homemade dog food or cat food you’d serve daily.

For a grown dog or cat, a store brand whose statement covers adult maintenance or all life stages is a complete diet.

A former Ohio State University graduate student could not convince a federal appeals court to overturn a jury’s verdict in favor of the school in her sexual harassment lawsuit because she failed to show that her trial was unfairly prejudiced, according to a Tuesday decision.

The plaintiff in Huang v. The Ohio State University alleged that a faculty advisor subjected her to unwanted touching and that, when she resisted his advances, he retaliated by manipulating her candidacy exam for an engineering doctorate. She brought claims under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act as well as the 14th Amendment’s due process right to bodily integrity.

A district granted summary judgment for Ohio State on the plaintiff’s Title VII claims, holding that she was not an employee of the university within the law’s definition and therefore could not bring those claims. At trial, a jury held for the plaintiff’s advisor. She appealed to the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, which reversed and ordered a new trial.

At the second trial, the jury determined that the plaintiff was not an employee because Ohio State and the advisor did not have the “right to control the manner and means by which [the plaintiff] accomplished her work.” The jury also ruled in favor of the advisor on the plaintiff’s 14th Amendment claim.

The plaintiff again appealed to the 6th Circuit, alleging that the district court incorrectly instructed the jury on what facts were relevant to its determination of whether she was an employee. She also claimed that the court abused its discretion in excluding certain evidence and that it improperly prohibited her from requesting economic damages in her closing argument.

Each of these claims failed, the 6th Circuit held.

The plaintiff forfeited her challenge to the court’s jury instructions because she failed to raise it, despite the district court specifically asking whether the parties had objections or edits to its instructions. To grant a third trial on this point “would be unfair to the court and the defendants,” the 6th Circuit said.

In her evidentiary argument, the plaintiff claimed that the district court improperly prevented her from submitting portions of a slideshow containing the results of an Ohio State campus climate survey on sexual assault. She argued that the presentation would have made her allegations more plausible.

However, the plaintiff failed to include the relevant slides in her appeal, leaving the 6th Circuit unable to conclude whether they had sufficient weight to affect the jury’s verdict. The appeals court also could not determine whether the slides offered anything “above and beyond” other testimony in the case.

In all, the plaintiff failed to show that any of her allegations amounted to unfair prejudice warranting a new trial.

The case represents one in a handful of recent jury decisions to favor employers facing Title VII lawsuits. In March, a jury held for Haribo of America in a former employee’s discrimination and retaliation case, determining that the plaintiff’s dismissal was a mutually agreed-upon separation between the parties rather than retaliation.

And in a 2025 case, a jury sided with Levi Strauss in a lawsuit alleging that a former executive did not receive a promotion because of her pregnancy, gender and age.
