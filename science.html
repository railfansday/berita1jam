EPA throws in the towel on policing air pollution from factory farms

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Critics say EPA is effectively abandoning Clean Air Act enforcement at factory farms.

The Environmental Protection Agency late last week announced it would end a 20-plus-year effort to develop methods to monitor air pollution from factory farms, effectively shuttering the agency’s stop-and-start attempts to regulate the potentially pollutant-spewing facilities under the Clean Air Act.

The announcement came just days before the midterm elections and was characterized by the agency as a long-awaited reprieve for farmers, a historically important constituency for Trump and the Republican party.

When tens of thousands of animals are packed into one place, pollution from their waste is blown from ventilation fans or emanates from giant lagoons of liquid effluent. Livestock facilities emit fine particulate matter, ammonia, nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide and volatile organic compounds, all contributing to a toxic mix. Research has shown that emissions from these operations are a major cause of air pollution, causing nearly 12,700 deaths in the United States a year.

Strictly speaking, concentrated animal feeding operations, or CAFOs, are required to get permits under the Clean Air Act and report emissions of these pollutants under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation and Liability and Act and the Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act. But through a deal crafted in tandem with the EPA and the livestock industry, they’ve been given amnesty from doing so for more than 20 years.

The arrangement goes back to the early 2000s when the agency attempted to prosecute CAFOs for exceeding limits on some of these pollutants. The industry pushed back, arguing there was no adequate way of measuring them. So the agency worked with industry groups to develop what was dubbed the Air Consent Agreement, through which the EPA granted thousands of CAFOs safe harbor from legal action in exchange for their participation in a study that was funded by the industry.

The goal of the study, the agency said, was to develop tools and models that CAFOs could use to “determine whether they emit pollutants at levels that require them to apply for permits under the CAA or submit notifications under CERCLA or EPCRA” and “produce a scientifically sound basis for measuring and estimating air emissions” from these facilities.

But since the adoption of the agreement in 2005, the agency has failed to produce any air “emissions estimating methodologies,” even prompting a 2017 report from the agency’s Office of Inspector General that faulted it for slow-walking the process.

Meanwhile, the Trump administration and Congress have also gutted reporting requirements for factory farms to report air pollutants under CERCLA, also known as the Superfund Act, and EPCRA.

“As EPA has been saying it’s proceeding with this plan, it’s been dismantling all of its own tools to regulate emissions,” said Tarah Heinzen, the legal director for the advocacy group Food & Water Watch, which has tracked the proceedings from the beginning and filed dozens of lawsuits attempting to push regulators to control pollution from CAFOs. “Now it has announced it has no intention, basically, of ever holding factory farms accountable under the Clean Air Act, either.”

In its Oct. 30 announcement, the agency noted that in the two decades since the study, known as the National Air Emissions Monitoring Study, was conducted, many farms have modernized their barns and manure systems.

“Good science requires good data, and these data are too old and too limited to support methods that farms could rely on,” said Abigale Tardif, the acting assistant administrator for the agency’s Office of Air and Radiation, in a statement. “We would rather tell farmers that plainly than issue numbers we cannot stand behind.”

Rob Brenneman, president of the National Pork Producers Council, which has long pushed for the decision, said in a statement: “This move confirms what farmers have long known: Every farm is different, and there is no one-size-fits-all model to apply to them.”

Heinzen noted that the agency’s decision simply means that this administration has no intention of regulating air emissions from CAFOs, but for now, the Clean Air Act still requires permits for facilities that exceed emissions thresholds. The amnesty granted to these facilities will no longer be in effect now that the agreement has concluded.

“There’s no EPA-issued methodology to estimate emissions,” she said. “That doesn’t mean that there aren’t good studies out there of emissions from different types of animal feeding operations that can be used.”

Heinzen pointed to California, which has models for livestock emissions that it uses to enforce its own air regulations. “We just don’t have one set of national emissions factors from EPA,” Heinzen said. “That does not absolve these factory farms from their obligation to comply with federal law.”

Still, under this administration, it’s clear air emissions from factory farms won’t be measured and monitored any time soon. “No one’s going to estimate anything,” Heinzen noted. “As EPA said in its press release, we’re certainly not about to try to enforce the law.”

The Air Compliance Agreement did not extend to methane. The U.S. livestock industry is the single-biggest source of methane in the country, but Congress effectively blocks livestock facilities from reporting methane emissions in annual spending bills.

Almost 22 years after it was launched, NASA’s Swift Observatory is now about to fall back to Earth on Wednesday after attempts to push it back into a higher orbit failed. NASA announced that the aging space telescope is anticipated to re-enter Earth’s atmosphere at approximately 3:30AM ET (give or take 11 hours), and that it expects most of the spacecraft to burn up, but some components will likely survive re-entry.

The observatory weighs around 3,200 pounds and measures in at 5.6 meters (18.4 feet) high and 5.4 meters (17.7 feet) wide, though much of the latter includes its deployable solar arrays. The risk of surviving components causing any harm is low, according to NASA:

“Re-entry locations of any surviving components can vary from hemisphere to hemisphere and depend on when it re-enters. Due to Swift’s low inclination, re-entry locations of surviving fragments are limited between 20.6 degrees south latitude and 20.6 degrees north latitude. The risk of harm coming to anyone on Earth is unlikely — approximately 1 in 2,100. NASA and Space Force will continue to monitor the re-entry and update predictions.”

The agency gave up on trying to save the falling telescope earlier this year, after the Link spacecraft that was attempting the rescue experienced technical issues with attitude control. Still, Swift far outlived its original two-year prime mission to study the origin of gamma-ray bursts, and went on to serve as a general-purpose multi-wavelength observatory until solar storms pushed it into a lower orbit. You can follow Swift’s descent by monitoring NASA’s blog for continued updates.

Why You Shouldn’t Take Anything Personally

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Posted November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Don’t take anything personally. Easier said than done. This represents the second agreement in the bestselling book The Four Agreements written by Don Miguel Ruiz (1997). In it, he astutely states, "Even when a situation seems so personal, even if others insult you directly, it has nothing to do with you. What they say, what they do, and the opinions they give are according to the agreements they have in their own minds."

Despite this, we find ourselves allowing someone's criticism, disapproval, or lack of responsiveness to determine how we feel about ourselves. Our mood and confidence can instantly shift because of a perceived disapproving tone or sensing that we’re the cause of someone’s irritation or frustration. We may react irrationally, impulsively, and intensely based on someone’s reactions, and our perceptions and interpretations.

Taking things personally is often an automatic psychological response shaped by our insecurities, attachment experiences, learned beliefs, and fundamental desire and need to belong. Research suggests that perceived rejection can activate our sympathetic nervous system, helping explain why interpersonal slights can feel disproportionately painful (Eisenberger et al., 2003).

1. You do not like everyone; therefore, you cannot expect that everyone will like you.

Consider how many people you have encountered whom you did not particularly connect or resonate with. Sometimes we have negative feelings that get evoked solely by someone’s presence or mannerism, even if there isn’t any rational justification for it.

Our personal preferences are influenced by many factors such as another’s personality, values, expectations, etc. When we’re not readily accepted, or outwardly rejected, we assume and interpret it as evidence of our inadequacy or lack of worthiness.

Our brain’s tendency is to seek external approval and acceptance and gauge it as a valid measure of our worth. We’re taught this early on in our development and societal standards reinforce it. We frame it as: “If they think I have value, then I must. If they think I do not, then I must not.”

We do not learn that someone else’s preference or opinion is just that, and that our standards and opinion about ourselves matter most. You can reframe and consider that someone's preference for you doesn't determine your value, and you can be an incredible person, despite not being someone’s preferred person.

2. You cannot read minds. Your triggers, perceptions, judgments, and assumptions are all yours.

We frequently confuse our thoughts, feelings, and interpretations as absolute facts. If someone mistakenly doesn’t acknowledge us, we conclude that they don’t care about us and we’re not important enough to them. We also sometimes fail to own and take accountability for our own triggers and often get defensive, deflect, and blame to avoid facing our own fears and vulnerabilities.

In relationships, mind reading, personalization, catastrophizing, and other cognitive distortions can skew reality and be counterproductive and harmful (Beck, 2020). The inaccuracy is understandable given that our mind constructs explanations based on our past experiences, expectations, and emotional states.

To balance out your thinking, you can ask yourself: "What do I actually know, and what am I assuming? How else can I see this?" This is approaching the circumstance and person with overarching openness, curiosity, and expansiveness.

3. Criticism, aggression, and other harsh responses may reflect someone else's internal struggles.

Individuals can externalize their shame, frustration, insecurity, or unresolved emotional pain through hostility, defensiveness, or criticism. Psychodynamic theory describes projection as a defense mechanism through which individuals attribute aspects of their internal experience to others (Bailey & Pico 2023).

For example, a highly insecure person may be especially critical toward others. To distinguish whether a projection is being enacted, you can consider their delivery, intensity, or emotional reaction.

4. Negative energy is to be observed, not absorbed.

Imagine someone is speaking to you impatiently and with irritability. Within seconds, your chest tightens, your heart rate accelerates, your thoughts spiral, and you feel compelled to relent to protect yourself or actively defend yourself. Their emotional state has comingled and become yours.

Research on emotional contagion suggests that people can influence one another's emotional experiences, sometimes without conscious awareness (Wang, 2024). Self-awareness, self-regulation, and self-compassion don't require emotional absorption.

You can connect with and offer empathy for someone else’s reaction and experiences without absorbing and taking on their negative energy. It requires thoughtfully and mindfully deciphering what is theirs and what is yours, and how you choose to react to it.

5. Someone's negative mood is not automatically about you

You may encounter distance, irritability enacted by a harsh tone or body language. Your immediate thought might be, "What did I do?" We often personalize others’ behavior, as connected to something we said or did, without evidence to support it.

Their mood can be reflective of their disappointment, fatigue, hormones, stress, or concerns that can be entirely unrelated to you. Before jumping to conclusions, approach with curiosity and consider asking, "I wonder if something’s going on for you. Is everything okay?" You can choose to approach with compassion and be attuned to someone's emotions without automatically assuming you're responsible for them.

Rejection can be particularly painful because belonging is a fundamental psychological need. Experiencing rejection can contribute to negative self-confidence, social anxiety, and the ability to sustain healthy trusting friendships and intimate relationships.

When someone rejects us, we often transform a specific experience into a more global conclusion. We make inferences such as “They didn’t choose me" becomes "I'm not good enough." Even though intellectually we know that rejection can reflect differing needs, incompatibility, timing, or circumstances beyond what we can control, we tend to personalize and generalize it.

You can remind yourself that your worth isn't determined by whether someone recognizes, attunes to, appreciates, or chooses you. You can’t avoid rejection, but you can strengthen your self-respect and self-compassion based on how you respond to it.

7. People's reactions provide you with vital information

You can learn a lot from observing others’ reactions. It can give you a glimpse of their expectations, perspective, judgments, and emotional state. Emotional maturity involves vulnerability in noticing and acknowledging each person’s contribution, taking accountability, and repairing and healing when appropriate.

In order to accomplish this, you can identify if there is a difference of opinion, a divergent way of seeing circumstances, or an altered way of behaving based on differing core values. The goal is to evaluate, validate and reach mutual understanding so that future interactions can be healthier and more satisfying.

8. Proactively protect your emotional energy

We can spend hours self-doubting, replaying conversations, anticipating rejection, questioning ourselves, or trying to change someone else's opinion of us. This rumination or perseveration spirals into thoughts about the thoughts, feelings about the feelings, thoughts about the feelings, and feelings about the thoughts. The emotional cost is exhaustion, exasperation, and typically extends beyond the original interaction.

Learning to notice, acknowledge, and interrupt your thoughts allows you to redirect your attention, even though your mind wants desperately to problem-solve. You could more readily focus on what you can directly influence, such as your interpretations, boundaries, responses, and choices.

The greatest emotional freedom comes when you no longer require other people to behave differently to feel secure within yourself. You recognize that you have choices regarding the way you respond.

You can recognize that you can be impacted by someone’s behavior without it defining you or automatically driving your behavior.

It can be challenging to notice parts of yourself that you would rather avoid or rid yourself of. Your activation, which can manifest itself as aggression, distancing, or in other ways, is about you. No one triggers you; you get triggered. Recognizing that can change not only how you experience others, but how you experience yourself.

Workers & Resources: Soviet Republic's next DLC is set to expand the city building sim into the one place not corrupted by capitalism: SPACE

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Following not the inherent liberal capitalistic framework of city builders like SimCity but instead a revolutionary (sorry) interpretation of planning and logistical simulation that prioritises centralisation and minute detail, Hooded Horse and 3DIVISION's Workers & Resources: Soviet Republic offers a tense, socialist look at what a citybuilder could be. Fellow contributor Sin Vega, in her 1.0 review, described it like this: "You have to build things to demolish things. That might be Workers & Resources in a nutshell. You're not managing budgets (money is exclusively for imports/exports), you're organising stuff." Now, having previously expanding out of the Soviet Republic and across the globe with the World Maps DLC, the next step for socialist citybuilding is, of course, space. Our Edwin outright predicted this two years ago, correctly calling that "the logical endgame here is to go to space." Good on him.

The Space Program DLC, set to release on November 23rd, will add a variety of new technologies and types of industry to the builder. It looks like it'll focus on not only the actual rockets that get into space, and the spectacle of it all, but also the huge amounts of science and industry that get to the point of rockets not exploding upon takeoff. For example, while it's all well and good for Space Program's scientists to be able to design rockets that are capable of remaining together for long enough to land safely, the designs they dream of still have to be built, meaning that industrial pipelines must be effectively centralised to ensure that every single component is of high quality and arriving in the required quantity.

The update also specifies the Sputnik and Vostok programs as elements of the expansion, implying a close historical aspect to it — though, elsewhere, "an unprecedented duel with nature" is mentioned, which is referring to either the simple harshness of space or aliens. Jury's out. A 1960s campaign will be a star feature of the DLC, which is described as a place for players to learn the new mechanics "in relative peace, surrounded by a happy, healthy, and economically stable utopia." What an idea.
