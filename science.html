Permafrost Is Thawing From the Inside Out—and It’s Unleashing an Entirely New Kind of Landslide

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Wildfires from decades ago may be secretly melting the frozen ground from below, setting off a disastrous chain of events in the present.

The largest northward-flowing river in North America, Canada’s Mackenzie River carries roughly 78 cubic miles of freshwater out of Great Slave Lake, winds through the frozen expanse and pristine forests of the Northwest Territories, and eventually empties into the Beaufort Sea, a marginal sea of the larger Arctic Ocean. The river represents the second-largest drainage basin on the continent, covering roughly 20 percent of Canada’s total landmass. The river valley served as the ancestral home of the Dene people, who in Slavey (a group of Athabaskan languages) called the river dehcho, or simply “big river.”

For thousands of years before the arrival of industrial revolution, permafrost—soil, rock, and sediment that stays frozen at least two consecutive years—encased this part of the world in frozen beauty. But with rising anthropogenic carbon emissions, that immemorial stability is crumbling away. And in some places in the central Mackenzie Valley, melting permafrost is changing the landscape in surprising ways.

In 2017 and 2018, two gargantuan landslides impacted the area, moving 4.5 and 18 million cubic meters of frozen ground respectively. Landslides, on their own, aren’t necessarily surprising. As the climate warms and permafrost thaws, boreal and tundra ecosystems, whether in Canada, Alaska, or other northern regions of the world, have seen an increasing risk of landslides. However, the leftover evidence of these huge terrestrial deluges raised a few questions, chiefly because they appeared to carry large chunks of frozen ground with them.

In 2019, permafrost geologist Joseph Young, then with the University of Alberta as a Ph.D. student, traveled by helicopter to a remote field site when he spotted slopes “just collapsing with landslides everywhere,” he told Science News. Young admits he was puzzled by these particular landslides because permafrost typically melts from the top down, making a muddy slurry that forms a slow-moving landslide.

However, the Mackenzie Valley landslides contained chunks of still-frozen earth that had moved hundreds of meters, and initial ground observations showed that sliding occurred roughly 20 meters under the top layer of permafrost. To better understand the mechanics of this strange landslide formation, Young and a team of researchers from the University of Alberta set up instruments to track ground temperature from 2020 until 2022. They hypothesized that wildfires—including some that occurred decades in the past—and rising summer temperatures formed tension cracks, allowing water to seep in and cause the permafrost to thaw from below. They called this new kind of landslide “permafrost detachment slides” and reported their observations in the journal Geophysical Research Letters.

“Climate warming and wildfire work together to drive heat to depth over decades, weakening the permafrost base,” the authors wrote. “Exposed ice-rich scarps continue thawing, triggering secondary landslides and irreversibly reshaping the landscape.”

Melting permafrost isn’t just rearranging these long-standing slopes, it's also unleashing previously trapped metals into riverbeds, turning them in some cases a bright, eye-popping orange. Shifting permafrost is also swallowing up villages in Alaska’s remote tundra, and studies estimate that continued thawing could cause upwards of $50 billion in damage to buildings and roads in Alaska by 2050.

The impact of climate change can’t be ignored, from rising sea levels inundating coastal communities to roiling wildfires that blanket world-renowned cities in smoke. And in the coldest regions of the world, climate change is quite literally changing the ground beneath our feet.

The ELT Can Probe Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies and Their Dark Matter Mysteries

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

We've been waiting a long time for the Extremely Large Telescope's first light, scheduled for March 2029. Telescopes this large, powerful, and costly always have multiple science goals, and that's true for the ELT. One of its science goals is to study fundamental aspects of the Universe, including dark matter.

The current model of cosmology is called Lambda Cold Dark Matter (Lambda-CDM or ÎCDM.) It's basically the Big Bang model of cosmology. There are, as every space-head knows, unanswered questions about Lambda-CDM, including the fact that we don't even know what dark matter is. (Some don't think it even exists.)

This is where the ELT comes in. One of its proposed instruments, and telescopes like this always have multiple instruments attached, is called NEXUS@SHARP. SHARP stands for Spectrograph for High Angular Resolution Physics, and NEXUS is a Multi-Object Spectrograph designed to work with SHARP.

New research in the journal New Astronomy outlines how NEXUS@SHARP can probe questions about Lambda-CDM. It's titled "SHARP(ening the) view of unresolved globular clusters in ultra diffuse galaxies," and the lead author is Marco Mirabile. Mirabile is a PhD student at INAF-Astronomical Observatory of Abruzzo (INAF-OAAb) and Gran Sasso Science institute (GSSI) in Italy.

"Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies (UDGs) challenge current galaxy formation models within the ÎCDM framework, particularly regarding their dark matter content and formation histories," the authors write. "Globular Clusters (GCs) serve as excellent tracers for probing the halos of these faint systems; however, current spectroscopic facilities struggle to reach the depth required to fully characterize GC populations beyond the local neighborhood."

UDGs are unusual because they're about as large as the Milky Way, but can have thousands of times fewer stars. The stars are spread out and the galaxies are dim. Basically they're too puffy to agree with Lambda-CDM.

This visualization shows how stars are distributed in a simulated UDG. UDGs are as faint as dwarf galaxies, but are spread out like large galaxies like the Milky Way. Astronomers thought that supernovae explosions could've been responsible for spreading out the stars, but that interpretation hasn't endured. Image Credit: Arianna Di Cintio, Chris Brook, NIHAO simulations and HST

A galaxy's size is shaped by its dark matter halo, one of the cosmos' basic structural units. There's an apparent mismatch between the sizes of UDG halos and the sizes of the UDGs themselves. The expected size of the halos shouldn't produce such diffuse galaxies.

A way to understand this is to examine the globular clusters around UDGs. But the problem is, the spectroscopes astronomers have at their disposal now aren't powerful enough to fully characterize the globular clusters around UDGs. NEXUS@SHARP can extend their reach.

"In this work, we present the scientific potential of NEXUS@SHARP, a near-infrared multi-slit spectrograph proposed on the upcoming Extremely Large Telescope (ELT). We demonstrate that NEXUS is uniquely positioned to overcome current observational limitations by combining high spatial resolution with efficient multiplexing," the authors write. Multiplexing means it's a Multi-Object Spectrograph (MOS) that can observe and capture data from many GCs at once. MOSs are common; Keck, the VLT, Gemini, and the JWST all have them, so do many others. But NEXUS@SHARP would be attached to the extraordinarly powerful ELT and its 39.3-metre-diameter segmented mirror.

All galaxies have globular clusters, including our own. Globular clusters are important when studying UDGs because they're brighter than the UDGs that hosts them, and are easier to observe. They also extend outward further than the UDGs, so they can help measure the dark matter halo better. That should let astronomers measure a more accurate mass constraint. Not only that, but the number of GCs can help determine the mass of the halo because their number scales with mass.

Omega Centauri, seen in this image captured with the WFI camera from ESO's La Silla Observatory, is probably the most well-known of the Milky Way's globular clusters. It has as many as 10 million stars. GCs are bright, so when they're satellites of ultra-diffuse galaxies, they serve as good probes of the galaxies. Image Credit: ESO

Another important thing GC spectra tells scientists is their formation history. The spectra show the ages and the metallicities of GCs, which reveals how quickly the stars in the GC formed. That can show astronomers what kind of UDG they're looking at.

Measuring a handful of GCs around a small number of UDGs won't sufficiently characterize the population of either. NEXUS@SHARP will bring more of them within observational rearch, and it won't require much telescope time because NEXUS examines many GCs at once. "We show that NEXUS can efficiently help to characterize the GC population in UDGs across diverse environments â out to 60 to 70 Mpc â with a few hours of ELT time," the authors write. 70 megaparsecs is about 230 light years.

The ELT is a beast of a telescope, and its heart is the 39.3 meter segmented primary mirror. Its instruments, including NEXUS@SHARP if its built, sit on a separate stationary platform. Image Credit: ESO

What this boils down to is two different scenarios for how UDGs form, and what it tells astronomers about dark matter and Lambda-CDM.

"These capabilities will allow for precise measurements of radial velocities and stellar populations, enabling robust dynamical mass estimates and distinguishing between competing UDG formation scenarios," the authors write.

One scenario says that UDGs are failed massive galaxies that were quenched early on. These ones preserved their dwarf-like stellar masses, but lie within more massive halos typical of a regular galaxy. "These galaxies would thus appear red, old, metal-poor, DM dominated, and host unusually rich globular cluster (GC) populations," the authors write.

The second says that UDGs are puffed-up dwarfs. They are genuinely low-mass galaxies that have expanded in space for some reason. "Relative to the previous scenario, such objects are expected to be bluer, exhibit extended star formation histories, and have DM content and GC populations resembling those of typical dwarfs," the authors explain.

NEXUS@SHARP can shed light on these scenarios and help explain how UDGs formed by examining globular clusters around Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies. That's why the authors wrote that ""Globular Clusters (GCs) serve as excellent tracers for probing the halos of these faint systems."

"To shed light on the nature of UDGs, deep spectroscopic observations of a large sample of UDGs are needed, as combining the analysis of GC richness with the kinematic and evolutionary properties of field stars provides a powerful diagnostic for distinguishing between different formation channels," the authors write.

Basically, the dataset gathered by NEXUS@SHARP will have two independent mass measurements for each UDG, both arising from measuring many GCs at once. One mass measurement comes from the velocity dispersion of the GCs, which shows how much mass is required to keep them in orbit. That's dynamical mass.

The second is also from GCs, but measures their total number. That relies on the knowledge that the number of GCs roughly rises linearly with the mass of a UDG's dark matter halo. That's GC-count mass.

This figure from previous research shows how the number of globular clusters (y-axis) correlates with galaxy mass (x-axis). Each orange or blue circle represents the number of GCs in an individual galaxy. Image Credit: Minh Ngoc Le and Andrew P. Cooper 2025 ApJ 978 33

If both of these ways of measuring mass agree, and both are high, that means that the UDG has an overmassive halo compared to its number of stars. That supports the first UDG formation scenario: it's a failed regular galaxy that quenched early.

If the different measurements both agree, but they're low, that supports the second UDG formation scenario: it's a puffed up dwarf inside a normal sized dwarf dark matter halo.

There's another wrinkle. If the dynamical mass is low, but the GC-count mass is high, then the formation of GC was unusually inefficient for some other, environmental reason.

NEXUS@SHARP is just a concept at this point, and the paper just makes the case for building it. If it's built, the results could go a long way to a better understanding of dark matter and Lambda-CDM. Understanding Lambda-CDM is an ongoing effort, and it's being tackled from different directions with different facilities. While Lambda-CDM is still the best match for lots of data about dark matter and dark energy, it doesn't fit everything.

If built, NEXUS@SHARP can chip away at some of the uncertainty around Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies and their dark matter halos, and can help either confirm or contradict Lambda-CDM.

"By enabling the systematic spectroscopic survey of GCs in low-surface-brightness systems, NEXUS@SHARP will act as a definitive tool for constraining the formation channels of the most elusive galaxy populations in the local universe," the authors conclude.

Hera spacecraft photographs asteroid from 37 miles away

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 6, 2026 3:19 PM EST

In September 2022, NASA’s Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) spacecraft smacked into a cosmic rock named Dimorphos at a speed of around 14,000 miles per hour. But mission engineers weren’t crestfallen by the news—they were thrilled. That’s because DART was specifically designed for this head-on asteroid collision. Since then, researchers around the world have poured over data from the multimillion dollar, car-sized craft’s smashing success to find ways of improving planetary defenses in the event that Earth ever finds itself in the crosshairs of a rock like Dimorphos.

In the meantime, the European Space Agency (ESA) sent its own observer, Hera, to examine the collision scene. On November 5, the ESA released the first image taken by the spacecraft. It was about 37 miles from Dimorphos’ much larger binary asteroid, Didymos. Although DART’s crash site is hidden in the shadow of the roughly 2,559-foot-wide rock, the perspective is allowing astronomers to begin examining how debris from a direct strike affects not only the primary asteroid, but any nearby objects in its vicinity.

“The first thing we said was, ‘Wow!’” Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel said in a statement. “This is the other side of Didymos from the one that DART’s camera showed us shortly before impact, so this feels like looking at a whole new world.”

Although Didymos exhibits the same central equatorial ridge visible from DART’s earlier views, the surface initially appears to be extremely different from its moonlet asteroid, Dimorphos. Because barely half a mile separates the two objects, astronomers are curious to see how much of Didymos’ features come from debris impacts.

“We have exciting work ahead of us to sort it out,” added mission scientist Michael Kueppers. “We look forward to seeing much more of both bodies and systemically mapping all the changes we identify.”

The ESA plans to release even more detailed photos of both asteroids on November 25.

How Do You Land on a Moon With Almost No Gravity?

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Japan’s MMX mission aims to sample the Martian moon Phobos

Japan's MMX spacecraft will fire its engines to slow down and enter orbit around Mars.

Last August, looking up from the edge of an ancient river delta on Mars, the Perseverance rover’s electronic eye spied an oblong shadow crossing the distant, glowing Sun. Phobos, the Martian moon named for the Greek god of fear and panic, was transiting the solar disk. Too small to darken the pale, dust-tinged sky, but orbiting close enough to eclipse a significant fraction of the Sun, Phobos passed in less than a minute.

NASA’s Perseverance rover captured Phobos passing in front of the Sun from the surface of Mars.ASU/MSSS/SSI/JPL-Caltech/NASA

The tiny moon, with its murky origins, is an enigma, and one whose makeup and history could tell us much about Mars and the wider solar system. And now the international Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) mission, which successfully launched on 20 October, seeks to explore that mystery with an unprecedented series of landings on the unknown surface of Phobos.

This mission brings unusual challenges—for example, designing an orbit to study a microgravity moon orbiting a much larger planet. Engineers also had to figure out how to assess and land on this low-gravity pile of loosely assembled dirt and rock, how a rover might rove across it, and how the spacecraft might take samples and return them to Earth.

“I often say that landing on Phobos is like trying to land a balloon,” says Yasuhiro Kawakatsu, project manager of MMX at the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA). “It’s very difficult to drop a balloon to the ground and prevent it from tilting or bouncing.”

Phobos is one of two small Martian moons; the other is Deimos, named after the Greek god of dread and terror. Their cosmic origins are unknown, though there are two prevailing theories. One holds that the moons were once asteroids from the belt between Mars and Jupiter, captured by the smaller planet’s gravity. In the other theory, Phobos and Deimos formed when a large celestial body slammed into Mars, sending debris into orbit. Some models suggest the pair are the last survivors from a cluster of satellites.

The enormous Stickney crater, roughly 9 kilometers across, dominates this view of Phobos taken by NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter.University of Arizona/JPL-Caltech/NASA

Scientifically speaking, Phobos is considered a rubble pile, a loosely consolidated aggregate of rock and dust. Far too small to become spherical under its own mass, it’s shaped like a lumpy potato, roughly 27 kilometers in length, with only 1/1,800th of Earth’s gravity. On Phobos, a typical adult would weigh about as much as a tennis ball does on Earth.

More than 10 years in the making, MMX launched on Japan’s flagship H3 rocket from Tanegashima spaceport on 20 October local time. It will arrive at Mars in 2027 carrying scientific payloads from several space agencies, including NASA, and a new rover called Idefix developed by the French and German space agencies (CNES and DLR) in collaboration with JAXA.

Engineers assembled MMX’s modules at Japan’s Tanegashima Space Center before the spacecraft’s October 2026 launch.JAXA

MMX consists of three modules: a propulsion module to power its journey to Mars and apply the brakes to enter orbit around the red planet; an exploration module carrying the rover, sampling gear, and other scientific instruments; and a return module, attached to the exploration module, that harbors the capsule destined for a high-speed reentry into Earth’s atmosphere. After collecting samples on Phobos, the exploration and return modules will lift off together. The exploration module will then be jettisoned while the return module sets a course back home.

If all goes according to plan, MMX will deliver a 10-gram sample to Earth in the second half of 2031. But the mission will face a series of daunting challenges to reach that end point, including navigating complex orbits around Phobos and landing on its surface while Mars periodically blocks communications with Earth.

The MMX’s modular design includes the propulsion module [left] and the exploration and return modules [right].Kyodo News/Getty Images

“It’s the most complex mission I have ever seen,” says Patrick Michel, director of research at CNRS (the French National Center for Scientific Research) and co-principal investigator on Idefix.

An H3 rocket carried MMX into space on 20 October 2026, beginning the mission’s five-year journey to Phobos and back.The Asahi Shimbun/Getty Images

Once MMX reached low Earth orbit, its propulsion module’s engine fired, kicking off a nearly year-long deep-space cruise to Mars. On arrival, the spacecraft will need to fire its engines in an extended, carefully timed burn to slow down enough to be captured by Mars gravity. The propulsion module will then be jettisoned, leaving the exploration and return modules to orbit Mars.

“If something doesn’t go according to plan, MMX will fly by Mars and get catapulted into the solar system,” says Nicola Baresi, an astrodynamicist who coauthored a paper on specially designed orbits for MMX.

From that initial orbit, MMX will transition into a specially designed quasi-satellite orbit that allows it to keep pace with the small moon but also move around it. Since Phobos is too small to orbit in the conventional sense, the spacecraft will orbit Mars alongside the moon, following a clever egg-shaped path that slings MMX alternately ahead of and behind the moon. “When you’re closer to Mars, you’re accelerating with respect to Phobos, and when you’re farther from Mars, you’re decelerating. So you essentially go back and forth,” explains Baresi.

Martian Maneuvers To reach Phobos, MMX will execute three major maneuvers: MOI-1 will put the spacecraft into orbit around Mars, MOI-2 will transfer it toward Phobos, and MOI-3 will place it into its final orbit alongside the moon.Chris Philpot

To reach Phobos, MMX will execute three major maneuvers: MOI-1 will put the spacecraft into orbit around Mars, MOI-2 will transfer it toward Phobos, and MOI-3 will place it into its final orbit alongside the moon.

The best existing images of Phobos, from spacecraft such as NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter and ESA’s Mars Express, have resolutions of only a few meters per pixel. That’s larger than Idefix, the mission’s rover, itself about the size of a microwave oven. To map the surface in greater detail, MMX will loop in front of and behind Phobos at distances ranging from 200 kilometers to less than 20 km from the surface.

During its two-year remote sensing campaign, the exploration module will use several cameras and a laser altimeter to map the surface in high resolution and look for candidate landing sites. When Mars passes behind the Sun as seen from the Earth, causing a roughly two-week communications blackout, MMX will retreat into a larger, stable orbit. After communications resume, MMX will prepare to send its first visitor down to the surface.

Before the exploration and connected return modules attempt to land, Kawakatsu says, MMX will send a scout that in Japanese is called “the first penguin,” leaping into unknown waters. For this mission, the first to leap will be the Idefix rover, named for the canine companion to Obelix in the French comics series Asterix. The exploration module will release the rover during a low-altitude pass of between 40 and 100 meters above Phobos’s surface in 2028 or early 2029.

MMX will use its TENGOO camera [left] and LIDAR laser altimeter [right] to map Phobos’s terrain.JAXA

It will be a tense moment that calls back to 2005, during Japan’s Hayabusa mission to explore the asteroid Itokawa. In a similarly designed drop, the MINERVA rover was released from an orbiter, but erroneously at 200 meters instead of the intended 70 meters. The rover missed the asteroid and, without propulsion, drifted off into deep space.

Idefix will get a slight nudge out of the exploration module toward its negligible-gravity target, freefalling and likely tumbling as it goes. It’s expected to touch down with a velocity of less than 1 meter per second. Unlike rovers that have landed on other surfaces with airbags or dedicated landing legs, Idefix will land without protection, apart from some extra padding on the solar panels due to their critical nature. The barely-there gravity of Phobos should allow the 23-kilogram rover to land softly, though exactly how this landing will play out is hard to predict.

The Idefix rover will travel to Phobos with its wheels tucked in and solar panels folded.DLR

“It will bounce probably several times,” says Stephan Ulamec, Idefix’s co-principal investigator and project manager at DLR. “It will come to rest somehow in some position.” Once it’s still, four retracted legs will deploy to right the rover autonomously, regardless of its orientation. It will then unfold its solar panels to charge its batteries for exploration tasks ahead.

A simulation shows how Idefix is expected to tumble onto Phobos, right itself using its articulated legs, and begin exploring the moon’s surface. DLR

Once operational, Idefix will roll at a snail’s pace, topping out at one millimeter per second. Its metal wheels have protruding blades like a paddle wheel, designed to gain traction in Phobos’s microgravity. “If we were to drive our rover faster, then it would actually be more like we were swimming through instead of driving across the surface,” says Cecily Sunday, a doctoral student involved in testing Idefix’s WheelCams at the Institut Superieur de l’Aeronautique et de l’Espace (ISAE-Supaéro) in Toulouse, France.

The WheelCams, a pair of cameras placed on the underside of the rover, will observe the wheels and their tracks during Idefix’s planned 100-Earth-day primary mission. The images will help researchers study the size and distribution of grains in Phobos’s regolith and how cohesive they are. How much or how little the grains stick together will greatly impact how Idefix moves. Sunday likens this to the difference between driving on dry and wet sand. “Think of trying to build a sandcastle with dry sand,” she says. “You can’t. Add water, and suddenly you can.”

The Idefix rover was mounted between the MMX spacecraft’s landing legs for the journey to Phobos.DLR

The team tested the rover’s nine-bladed wheels on a variety of materials, including Phobos regolith simulants provided by JAXA and DLR, lunar simulants, glass beads, quartz sand, and clay pebbles. “We characterize the mechanical properties of each simulant precisely, then test across the full range, with varying grain size, angularity, and even color, so we can map what we see in the WheelCam images back to actual surface properties,” Sunday says.

Despite the microgravity and Phobos’s rubble-pile nature, Sunday says Earth-based tests can approximate the rover’s motion on the surface of the small moon fairly well. While Phobos’s reduced gravity lowers the rover’s effective weight, there is also reduced pressure between the grains beneath its wheels. “As long as you’re moving very, very slowly and using the same materials and the same weight of the system, they should be representative,” Sunday says.

With four paddle-like wheels and deployable solar panels, Idefix is designed to crawl across Phobos.Ardan Fuessmann/Imago/Alamy

Idefix’s interactions with its new home will offer “sub-millimeter resolution and information on porosity, on grain size, on surface strength, on how hard is the material,” Ulamec says. “It fills a gap between remote observations from orbit and detailed observations of the return samples back on Earth.”

What Idefix finds on this rocky, rugged world will help determine how and where JAXA attempts to land the main spacecraft. Michel says the touchdown will provide ground truth for understanding of Phobos. When NASA’s OSIRIS-REx spacecraft touched down on the asteroid Bennu to collect a sample, its sampling arm surprisingly sank nearly half a meter into the loose material. But Ulamec doesn’t think Idefix will sink deep into Phobos, noting that Bennu is very different and much smaller, with a diameter of 500 meters.

Once Idefix has investigated the surface, MMX will face an even greater challenge: landing its much larger exploration module on Phobos. The spacecraft will make a controlled descent to roughly 10 meters above the surface, then drop the remaining distance in free fall. With so little gravity to pull it downward, even a small sideways motion could cause the lander to tip or bounce away. A successful, upright landing would mark the start of up to two hours on Phobos for the crucial sampling phase.

The MMX mission draws on the experience and technologies of Japan’s two Hayabusa sample-return missions, which landed on the asteroids Itokawa and Ryugu. But this mission has many more moving parts. MMX’s exploration module will use two sampling devices—a coring sampler developed by JAXA and a pneumatic sampler provided by NASA and fabricated by Honeybee Robotics, a subsidiary of Blue Origin. Data from these tools, and those carried by the Idefix rover, could answer questions about the origin of Phobos and how the Mars system has evolved.

The coring sampler uses a robot arm and a cylindrical corer designed to reach at least 2 centimeters below the surface. The arm can maneuver to find the best site for digging. “That by itself is a challenge because we’ve never done it,” Michel says. “You never know what can happen at the very sampling site itself, where you might have a boulder and the arm cannot move.”

Probing beneath the top layer will be valuable because solar radiation, galactic cosmic rays, and micrometeorites have battered and weathered the surface. The material below should offer a better record of the moon’s origins, and JAXA aims to collect at least 10 grams of it.

MMX will carry both the Idefix rover [bottom] and a robotic coring sampler [top] designed to dig beneath Phobos’s weathered surface.JAXA

The pneumatic sampler takes a different approach. Mounted on one of the lander’s legs, it will inject pure, non-reactive nitrogen gas into the surface and capture the grains stirred up. This sample could illuminate the changes Mars and its moons have undergone over time. The dual system also adds redundancy in the face of Phobos’s unknowns. “If the surface of Phobos proves to be very hard, [the coring sampler] may struggle to penetrate the ground. [The pneumatic sampler] would not experience this issue and still be able to gather material,” Kawakatsu says.

One of the most intriguing aspects of MMX is the additional possibility of collecting material from the planet below. It’s likely that Phobos has been peppered by Martian ejecta thrown into space by meteor impacts, so MMX also provides an opportunity to return the first Martian samples to Earth. Michel estimates that the samples could contain “a few hundred grains coming from Mars.” Scientists can distinguish those grains from native Phobos material by analyzing their oxygen isotopes.

That possibility raises a planetary-protection question. A direct Mars sample-return mission would have to follow strict rules to ensure that potentially living biological material from Mars doesn’t contaminate Earth. But those protocols don’t apply to MMX, even if its sample does collect grains of Martian soil. A panel of experts concluded that any microbes blasted off Mars wouldn’t survive the violent impact and ejection or the intense radiation exposure on the way to Phobos. Meteorites that may have delivered carbon to early Earth carry an oxygen signature older than their asteroids

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Organic specks in primitive meteorites carry an oxygen signature set before their asteroids formed, likely in the cold outer solar system.

Some of the carbon on the early Earth may have arrived inside meteorites, carried as dark specks of organic matter far too small to see.

Where those specks were first made is still an open question. A new study suggests their oxygen dates from before the asteroids that carried them had formed.

In seven primitive meteorites that were never strongly heated, the organic matter carries nearly the same oxygen fingerprint.

Water on the asteroids barely changed it. That makes the oxygen a record of where the material formed, not of what happened to it later.

Where the organics formed is the team’s interpretation, drawn from a small set of meteorites and two lab tests.

Daniel Crocker and David Johnston of Harvard’s Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences led the work. Their co-authors were Conel Alexander of the Carnegie Institution for Science and Mark Thiemens of the University of California, San Diego.

Oxygen atoms come in three stable forms: oxygen-16, oxygen-17 and oxygen-18. They differ only in weight. Scientists call these forms isotopes, and the mix of the three in a sample works like a fingerprint.

On Earth, almost any process that sorts oxygen by weight changes the share of oxygen-17 about half as much as the share of oxygen-18.

The oldest minerals in some carbon-rich meteorites break that rule. In them, the two heavier forms change by equal amounts.

That odd pattern is usually explained as a blend of two ancient pools of oxygen, one rich in oxygen-16 and one poor in it. Researchers drew a reference line for it in 1977 from minerals in the Allende meteorite, but what produced it is still debated.

The meteorites in the study belong to a group called carbonaceous chondrites. The most primitive ones hold up to 4 percent carbon by weight in their organic matter. Between 30 and 80 percent of that organic matter won’t dissolve in acids or solvents.

Alexander’s lab separated that material from the rock. Crocker then measured its oxygen with a method the Harvard group had recently developed.

Water had altered the seven primitive meteorites on their home asteroids, always below about 302°F (150°C). Their organic matter came out almost identical.

The oxygen-18 readings varied by only about 2 parts per thousand, and every sample was within the margin of error of the reference line.

“What surprised me was how similar the oxygen isotope compositions were across meteorites that experienced very different alteration histories after they formed,” Crocker told Earth.com.

These asteroids once held liquid water, and water can swap oxygen atoms with the material around it.

The team calculated what would happen if the organic matter had traded much of its oxygen with that water. Its oxygen-18 reading would be 27 to 58 parts per thousand above a standard.

The actual readings were 10 to 12. So the organic matter most likely swapped very little oxygen with the water. If it had, samples from several separate asteroids would have needed nearly identical conditions to end up with the same values.

The water still changed the material’s chemistry, and some oxygen-bearing parts of the molecules were lost. But it left the oxygen’s mix of weights mostly alone.

“That preservation gives us an opportunity to look past what happened to the organic matter on the asteroids and investigate the environment in which it originally formed,” Crocker said.

Organic matter from six meteorites heated on their asteroids, or by impacts, looked different. It held less of both heavy oxygen forms, and the readings formed a trend away from the unheated group.

To test whether heat alone could do that, the team warmed organic matter from the Mighei meteorite in a vacuum for an hour. One run reached 932°F (500°C) and the other 1,112°F (600°C).

The samples lost about 59 and 67 percent of their oxygen respectively, and what remained shifted along the same trend as the heated meteorites. Undo the heating, and they would move back toward the primitive group, which fits one shared starting fingerprint.

Water didn’t set the fingerprint, and heat changes it in a different direction. So the authors concluded the oxygen most likely went in when the organic matter itself formed, before it was gathered into any asteroid.

The organic matter holds less oxygen-16 than almost every mineral in these meteorites. In the early solar system, water was the main ingredient low in oxygen-16. So the authors think the organics took their oxygen from ancient water as they formed.

Extra heavy hydrogen and nitrogen in the organics already hinted at a cold place with strong radiation. The team favors a birthplace in the outer solar system, with the same well-mixed supply ending up in several asteroids.

“Rather than starting with the chemistry that occurred on the early Earth, we can ask what organic material Earth and other young planets inherited from the Solar System environment in which they formed,” Crocker and Johnston told Earth.com.

The researchers said they “cannot yet identify a single location or oxygen-bearing molecule as its source.” They also checked for lab effects, such as stray mineral grains and the chemicals used to free the organics. They concluded neither changed the readings in any meaningful way.

The unusual pattern in the ancient minerals, first spotted more than 50 years ago, still lacks a full explanation. “That doesn’t solve the mystery, but it provides a new constraint,” the two researchers added.

Next, the team sees the oxygen as a way to narrow down where, and under what conditions, the material formed. That could show how widely the raw ingredients for life’s chemistry were spread among the young planets.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

Eliminating invasive rats could help islands survive rising seas

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Removing invasive rats could help protect low-lying islands from rising seas by restoring seabirds, boosting coral reef growth, and increasing sand production.

Low-lying atoll islands are usually written off as the first places a rising sea will cover. Around some of them, though, the coral reef is growing fast enough to keep pace with the sea through the end of this century.

One important factor is whether invasive rats have driven away the island’s seabirds.

Where seabirds nest, their droppings feed the reef.

Lagoon reefs there build upward almost twice as fast as reefs beside rat-overrun islands and produce more than twice as much sand.

Native forests produce more than four times as much fine root growth as areas dominated by coconut palms. This can hold the island’s soil in place.

Nick Graham, a marine ecologist at Lancaster University‘s Lancaster Environment Centre, led a team that measured all three factors on 28 islands in the Indian and Pacific Oceans.

The team compared islands as they are today, with and without rats.

“Atoll islands are not static, they are naturally active systems that can change and adapt to their environmental conditions if they are allowed to do so,” Graham said.

Boobies, frigatebirds, terns and noddies feed at sea, up to 620 miles (1,000 kilometers) from the island where they nest. They come home to roost, and the guano they deposit on the island contains nitrogen.

On islands with the largest seabird colonies, their droppings deposited more than 320 pounds of nitrogen per acre (366 kilograms per hectare) each year.

Rats that arrived on ships eat seabird eggs and chicks, and on most atolls the bird colonies are gone. Many islands have also lost their native forest to coconut plantations.

These palms now make up about 60 percent of the forest on Pacific atolls.

The team counted nests on islands in the Seychelles, the Chagos Archipelago and Tetiaroa Atoll in French Polynesia.

Islands with no rats and mostly native forest got the heaviest rain of bird nitrogen. The rest got very little.

Corals near seabird islands photosynthesized more and laid down more limestone skeleton. From that, the team estimated how fast each reef could build upward.

Lagoon reefs beside rat-free islands rose by an estimated 0.23 inches (5.9 millimeters) a year.

This is 1.8 times the rate of reef rise beside rat-infested islands. Ocean-facing reefs showed a smaller and less certain edge.

For the central Indian Ocean, the sea is projected to rise about 0.24 inches (6 millimeters) a year by 2100 if the world warms by 2°C (3.6°F).

The bird-fed lagoon reefs could nearly match that. The reefs beside rat islands, however, are predicted to fall short of even today’s rise of about 0.16 inches (4 millimeters) a year.

A reef that keeps pace with rising seas helps weaken waves before they reach the shore.

“The study shows beautifully how atolls are an intimately interconnected island-reef system, a geo-ecological system,” said Yadvinder Malhi, an ecologist at the University of Oxford and a co-author.

Parrotfish scrape coral and excrete it as sand. On some Indian Ocean reefs they produce up to 85 percent of the new sand.

Around islands with seabirds, parrotfish had more energy available, and the surrounding reefs produced 2.3 to 2.8 times as much sand.

In an interview with Earth.com, Graham was asked what surprised him most.

“The increase in sediment production by seabirds was something that had not been investigated before, and the increase is quite dramatic,” he said.

He put a figure on it for a hypothetical island of about 35 acres (14 hectares), roughly three quarters of a mile long.

With seabirds present, the surrounding reefs would produce about 220 cubic yards (170 cubic meters) more sediment each year than reefs around an island without seabirds.

“That is a really meaningful quantity,” Graham said.

Not all of that sand reaches the beach. The grains on the reef and the grains on the shore match closely, though, which points to a steady supply.

On Tetiaroa, the team measured fine roots in forest plots for two years.

Where native trees such as Pisonia grandis covered more than 75 percent of the ground, each square yard of soil held an estimated 8.2 miles (13.2 kilometers) of fine root.

Where native cover was below 75 percent, the figure was 1.8 miles (2.9 kilometers).

Coconut palms have short, rounded root systems. Native broadleaf trees grow long, dense roots that hold sand in place when storm waves wash over an island.

Seabirds strengthened that effect. With plenty of bird nitrogen in the soil, root growth rose by about 9 percent for every extra 1% of native forest cover. With few birds, more native forest didn’t change root growth at all.

The reef growth and sand figures come from six islands in the Chagos Archipelago, and the root figures from 19 forest plots on five islands of Tetiaroa.

The team inferred the gains from islands that already differ, rather than following one atoll from rat eradication through to a faster-growing reef.

How much reef sand actually reaches an island, and how reef growth changes the scouring of a shoreline, still have to be measured. Island size and local currents also shape what happens to any one atoll.

Jennifer Appoo, a co-author at the University of Seychelles, said the results matter most where seawalls and other engineering are unaffordable or out of place.

Many of the Seychelles’ atolls are uninhabited or sparsely inhabited, she said.

Graham told Earth.com that eradicating invasive predators such as rats should be the top priority for island managers.

It helps the island’s wildlife and the reef around it, he said, and this study shows it can boost the islands’ climate resilience too.

“I think what is key here from a policy perspective is that restoring atoll islands can have major gains in two core areas: biodiversity conservation, and climate adaptation,” he said.

Rats have already been removed from about 1,000 islands worldwide.

Asked by Earth.com which islands he is watching next, Graham said he has just started an Island-Ocean Research Hub at Lancaster.

It will study islands from the tropics to the Arctic, in the Seychelles, the Red Sea, the Mediterranean, the U.K. and Arctic Norway.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

EPA throws in the towel on policing air pollution from factory farms

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Critics say EPA is effectively abandoning Clean Air Act enforcement at factory farms.

The Environmental Protection Agency late last week announced it would end a 20-plus-year effort to develop methods to monitor air pollution from factory farms, effectively shuttering the agency’s stop-and-start attempts to regulate the potentially pollutant-spewing facilities under the Clean Air Act.

The announcement came just days before the midterm elections and was characterized by the agency as a long-awaited reprieve for farmers, a historically important constituency for Trump and the Republican party.

When tens of thousands of animals are packed into one place, pollution from their waste is blown from ventilation fans or emanates from giant lagoons of liquid effluent. Livestock facilities emit fine particulate matter, ammonia, nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide and volatile organic compounds, all contributing to a toxic mix. Research has shown that emissions from these operations are a major cause of air pollution, causing nearly 12,700 deaths in the United States a year.

Strictly speaking, concentrated animal feeding operations, or CAFOs, are required to get permits under the Clean Air Act and report emissions of these pollutants under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation and Liability and Act and the Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act. But through a deal crafted in tandem with the EPA and the livestock industry, they’ve been given amnesty from doing so for more than 20 years.

The arrangement goes back to the early 2000s when the agency attempted to prosecute CAFOs for exceeding limits on some of these pollutants. The industry pushed back, arguing there was no adequate way of measuring them. So the agency worked with industry groups to develop what was dubbed the Air Consent Agreement, through which the EPA granted thousands of CAFOs safe harbor from legal action in exchange for their participation in a study that was funded by the industry.

The goal of the study, the agency said, was to develop tools and models that CAFOs could use to “determine whether they emit pollutants at levels that require them to apply for permits under the CAA or submit notifications under CERCLA or EPCRA” and “produce a scientifically sound basis for measuring and estimating air emissions” from these facilities.

But since the adoption of the agreement in 2005, the agency has failed to produce any air “emissions estimating methodologies,” even prompting a 2017 report from the agency’s Office of Inspector General that faulted it for slow-walking the process.

Meanwhile, the Trump administration and Congress have also gutted reporting requirements for factory farms to report air pollutants under CERCLA, also known as the Superfund Act, and EPCRA.

“As EPA has been saying it’s proceeding with this plan, it’s been dismantling all of its own tools to regulate emissions,” said Tarah Heinzen, the legal director for the advocacy group Food & Water Watch, which has tracked the proceedings from the beginning and filed dozens of lawsuits attempting to push regulators to control pollution from CAFOs. “Now it has announced it has no intention, basically, of ever holding factory farms accountable under the Clean Air Act, either.”

In its Oct. 30 announcement, the agency noted that in the two decades since the study, known as the National Air Emissions Monitoring Study, was conducted, many farms have modernized their barns and manure systems.

“Good science requires good data, and these data are too old and too limited to support methods that farms could rely on,” said Abigale Tardif, the acting assistant administrator for the agency’s Office of Air and Radiation, in a statement. “We would rather tell farmers that plainly than issue numbers we cannot stand behind.”

Rob Brenneman, president of the National Pork Producers Council, which has long pushed for the decision, said in a statement: “This move confirms what farmers have long known: Every farm is different, and there is no one-size-fits-all model to apply to them.”

Heinzen noted that the agency’s decision simply means that this administration has no intention of regulating air emissions from CAFOs, but for now, the Clean Air Act still requires permits for facilities that exceed emissions thresholds. The amnesty granted to these facilities will no longer be in effect now that the agreement has concluded.

“There’s no EPA-issued methodology to estimate emissions,” she said. “That doesn’t mean that there aren’t good studies out there of emissions from different types of animal feeding operations that can be used.”

Heinzen pointed to California, which has models for livestock emissions that it uses to enforce its own air regulations. “We just don’t have one set of national emissions factors from EPA,” Heinzen said. “That does not absolve these factory farms from their obligation to comply with federal law.”

Still, under this administration, it’s clear air emissions from factory farms won’t be measured and monitored any time soon. “No one’s going to estimate anything,” Heinzen noted. “As EPA said in its press release, we’re certainly not about to try to enforce the law.”

The Air Compliance Agreement did not extend to methane. The U.S. livestock industry is the single-biggest source of methane in the country, but Congress effectively blocks livestock facilities from reporting methane emissions in annual spending bills.

Why You Shouldn’t Take Anything Personally

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Posted November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Don’t take anything personally. Easier said than done. This represents the second agreement in the bestselling book The Four Agreements written by Don Miguel Ruiz (1997). In it, he astutely states, "Even when a situation seems so personal, even if others insult you directly, it has nothing to do with you. What they say, what they do, and the opinions they give are according to the agreements they have in their own minds."

Despite this, we find ourselves allowing someone's criticism, disapproval, or lack of responsiveness to determine how we feel about ourselves. Our mood and confidence can instantly shift because of a perceived disapproving tone or sensing that we’re the cause of someone’s irritation or frustration. We may react irrationally, impulsively, and intensely based on someone’s reactions, and our perceptions and interpretations.

Taking things personally is often an automatic psychological response shaped by our insecurities, attachment experiences, learned beliefs, and fundamental desire and need to belong. Research suggests that perceived rejection can activate our sympathetic nervous system, helping explain why interpersonal slights can feel disproportionately painful (Eisenberger et al., 2003).

1. You do not like everyone; therefore, you cannot expect that everyone will like you.

Consider how many people you have encountered whom you did not particularly connect or resonate with. Sometimes we have negative feelings that get evoked solely by someone’s presence or mannerism, even if there isn’t any rational justification for it.

Our personal preferences are influenced by many factors such as another’s personality, values, expectations, etc. When we’re not readily accepted, or outwardly rejected, we assume and interpret it as evidence of our inadequacy or lack of worthiness.

Our brain’s tendency is to seek external approval and acceptance and gauge it as a valid measure of our worth. We’re taught this early on in our development and societal standards reinforce it. We frame it as: “If they think I have value, then I must. If they think I do not, then I must not.”

We do not learn that someone else’s preference or opinion is just that, and that our standards and opinion about ourselves matter most. You can reframe and consider that someone's preference for you doesn't determine your value, and you can be an incredible person, despite not being someone’s preferred person.

2. You cannot read minds. Your triggers, perceptions, judgments, and assumptions are all yours.

We frequently confuse our thoughts, feelings, and interpretations as absolute facts. If someone mistakenly doesn’t acknowledge us, we conclude that they don’t care about us and we’re not important enough to them. We also sometimes fail to own and take accountability for our own triggers and often get defensive, deflect, and blame to avoid facing our own fears and vulnerabilities.

In relationships, mind reading, personalization, catastrophizing, and other cognitive distortions can skew reality and be counterproductive and harmful (Beck, 2020). The inaccuracy is understandable given that our mind constructs explanations based on our past experiences, expectations, and emotional states.

To balance out your thinking, you can ask yourself: "What do I actually know, and what am I assuming? How else can I see this?" This is approaching the circumstance and person with overarching openness, curiosity, and expansiveness.

3. Criticism, aggression, and other harsh responses may reflect someone else's internal struggles.

Individuals can externalize their shame, frustration, insecurity, or unresolved emotional pain through hostility, defensiveness, or criticism. Psychodynamic theory describes projection as a defense mechanism through which individuals attribute aspects of their internal experience to others (Bailey & Pico 2023).

For example, a highly insecure person may be especially critical toward others. To distinguish whether a projection is being enacted, you can consider their delivery, intensity, or emotional reaction.

4. Negative energy is to be observed, not absorbed.

Imagine someone is speaking to you impatiently and with irritability. Within seconds, your chest tightens, your heart rate accelerates, your thoughts spiral, and you feel compelled to relent to protect yourself or actively defend yourself. Their emotional state has comingled and become yours.

Research on emotional contagion suggests that people can influence one another's emotional experiences, sometimes without conscious awareness (Wang, 2024). Self-awareness, self-regulation, and self-compassion don't require emotional absorption.

You can connect with and offer empathy for someone else’s reaction and experiences without absorbing and taking on their negative energy. It requires thoughtfully and mindfully deciphering what is theirs and what is yours, and how you choose to react to it.

5. Someone's negative mood is not automatically about you

You may encounter distance, irritability enacted by a harsh tone or body language. Your immediate thought might be, "What did I do?" We often personalize others’ behavior, as connected to something we said or did, without evidence to support it.

Their mood can be reflective of their disappointment, fatigue, hormones, stress, or concerns that can be entirely unrelated to you. Before jumping to conclusions, approach with curiosity and consider asking, "I wonder if something’s going on for you. Is everything okay?" You can choose to approach with compassion and be attuned to someone's emotions without automatically assuming you're responsible for them.

Rejection can be particularly painful because belonging is a fundamental psychological need. Experiencing rejection can contribute to negative self-confidence, social anxiety, and the ability to sustain healthy trusting friendships and intimate relationships.

When someone rejects us, we often transform a specific experience into a more global conclusion. We make inferences such as “They didn’t choose me" becomes "I'm not good enough." Even though intellectually we know that rejection can reflect differing needs, incompatibility, timing, or circumstances beyond what we can control, we tend to personalize and generalize it.

You can remind yourself that your worth isn't determined by whether someone recognizes, attunes to, appreciates, or chooses you. You can’t avoid rejection, but you can strengthen your self-respect and self-compassion based on how you respond to it.

7. People's reactions provide you with vital information

You can learn a lot from observing others’ reactions. It can give you a glimpse of their expectations, perspective, judgments, and emotional state. Emotional maturity involves vulnerability in noticing and acknowledging each person’s contribution, taking accountability, and repairing and healing when appropriate.

In order to accomplish this, you can identify if there is a difference of opinion, a divergent way of seeing circumstances, or an altered way of behaving based on differing core values. The goal is to evaluate, validate and reach mutual understanding so that future interactions can be healthier and more satisfying.

8. Proactively protect your emotional energy

We can spend hours self-doubting, replaying conversations, anticipating rejection, questioning ourselves, or trying to change someone else's opinion of us. This rumination or perseveration spirals into thoughts about the thoughts, feelings about the feelings, thoughts about the feelings, and feelings about the thoughts. The emotional cost is exhaustion, exasperation, and typically extends beyond the original interaction.

Learning to notice, acknowledge, and interrupt your thoughts allows you to redirect your attention, even though your mind wants desperately to problem-solve. You could more readily focus on what you can directly influence, such as your interpretations, boundaries, responses, and choices.

The greatest emotional freedom comes when you no longer require other people to behave differently to feel secure within yourself. You recognize that you have choices regarding the way you respond.

You can recognize that you can be impacted by someone’s behavior without it defining you or automatically driving your behavior.

It can be challenging to notice parts of yourself that you would rather avoid or rid yourself of. Your activation, which can manifest itself as aggression, distancing, or in other ways, is about you. No one triggers you; you get triggered. Recognizing that can change not only how you experience others, but how you experience yourself.
