Oldest evidence of cannabis use discovered in 2,700-year-old royal tomb

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 5, 2026 2:00 PM EST

The use of cannabis is documented across cultures stretching back thousands of years, but it frequently relates to harvesting the plant for hemp and other non-psychoactive products. That said, one Bronze Age society of horseriders living in present-day Russia called the Scythians may offer the oldest example of intentionally ingesting cannabis for its intoxicating properties. In the Eurasian steppes of Siberia, archaeologists have discovered a princely Scythian tomb complex featuring an estimated 1,000 cannabis seeds intentionally placed among the dead.

“The seeds show that cannabis played an important role in the rituals surrounding the burial,” Gino Caspari, an archaeologist at the University of Bern in Switzerland, explained in a statement.

These weren’t simply symbolic objects, either. As Caspari and his colleagues detail in a study published today in the journal Science, human hair samples from the central burial room indicate Scythian royals also partook in cannabis use.

The Scythians were a group of ancient eastern Iranian nomadic equestrians renowned for their horseback skills. The community migrated from Central Asia into present-day Ukraine and southern Russia beginning in the eight and ninth centuries BCE, and stayed there for roughly the next 500 years. Their regional influence eventually waned before the Scythians ultimately assimilating into neighboring populations after being displaced by the Goths during the third century CE.

The burial complex Caspari’s team examined is located in Tuva, a south Siberian republic of Russia located along Mongolia’s northern border. Although ancient plant material like leaves, flowers, and resin generally decompose long before archaeologists can ever locate them, seeds sometimes endure when shielded from the elements. In this case, the royal tomb ensured near optimal conditions to protect the cannabis remnants. Laboratory radiocarbon dating of six recovered seeds indicate Bronze Age attendants likely placed them inside the burial chamber during the early eighth century BCE.

The exact details of Scythian cannabis cultivation and use remains unclear. Researchers theorize the plants were wild variants that naturally grew in the region. At the same time, hair analysis through methods like high-resolution mass spectrometry revealed the clear presence of cannabinol (CBN), which is produced by the oxidation of cannabis’ primary intoxicant, tetrahydrocannabinol (THC). The team also noted weak signs consistent with THC itself.

The detection of these molecular signals doesn’t reveal how much cannabis the Scythian royals ingested, or the methods they used. At the same time, other Central Asian archaeological sites contained cannabinoid chemical remnants on heated stones and inside vessels, which strongly suggest inhalation.

“The molecular traces in the hair and the cannabis seeds come from the same grave, and suggest that cannabis was used for its intoxicating properties,” concluded Caspari.

Archaeologists are now sequencing the DNA from some of the tomb’s seed samples, which will help them trace the plant’s spread and later human domestication. Cannabis wasn’t the only addition to the royal Scythian tomb, however. Prior work at the site has yielded the skeletal remnants of 18 horses arranged on the burial mound, along with an assortment of human bones.

“In this princely tomb, the burials were accompanied by extensive horse sacrifices and, most likely, human sacrifices as well,” added Caspari.

How Yoga Brings You Closer to Yourself and the Life You Want

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

If you buy through our links, we may earn an affiliate commission. This supports our mission to get more people active and outside.Learn about Outside Online's affiliate link policy

In Yoga Journal’s Archives series, we share a curated collection of articles originally published in past issues beginning in 1975. This article about yoga as a science and an art first appeared in the January-February 1977 issue of Yoga Journal. Find more of our Archives here.

Yoga is a science, an art, and a technique which spans the multilevel functioning of the individual. It is a science because of its empirical approach, presented most completely by the classic Yoga Sutras of Patanjali at least 1,000 years ago. Science is defined as a methodology which requires empirical proof of its theories; it requires that which proves a theory be easily repeatable by another experimenter if he/she follows the rules of the experiment.

Yoga, by this definition, is indeed a science. Its rules are given in sutra form and the presentation of Patanjali is a “how-to” book of the highest order. The practitioner need only follow the lead of Patanjali in order to have his own personal empirical results. (It is true that the directions are in parts esoteric and apparently illusive; this points out the necessity of having a teacher upon whom the practioner can rely for advice during certain intervals.) In addition to the work by Patanjali, the Hatha Yoga Pradipika and the Gheranda and Shiva Samhita are references which can explain the science of yoga.

But yoga is an art, too. Individuals have dedicated their lives to the perfection of this art. This does not necessarily imply the production of tangible output such as texts, although these have been produced in the history of traditional Indian thought. Rather, the use of the word “art” in this context implies the movement toward perfection within, the embodiment of beauty as it can be expressed through the human being. If one dedicates his/her life to the art of yoga, this implies a dedication which does not involve merely daily practice numbering a few hours. Instead, it implies a minute by minute practice which spans years, and some believe, lifetimes of existence.

The art of yoga is the practice of yoga on all levels of existence; this means every thought, action, dream, relationship, food intake, sexual activity, occupation, etc., are brought into the wider perspective of observation in light of the individual’s desire to understand the Self. This understanding does not imply intellectual understanding alone. It implies the “living” or the “being” in the Self rather than just mentally knowing about the Self. One can compare this to the experience of being in a hurricane with merely reading about the experience of a hurricane; the effect would be quite different.

One who lives in the Self has come into a wholeness of being, possibly for us all by virtue of our human existence, but available presently to only a handful due a complex interplay of events which, for whatever reasons, has brought them to a newer threshold of consciousness. Thus the art of yoga lies in bringing perfection into action through becoming one with oneself.

Part of the ability to do this lies with the powerful psychological technique of “acting as if.” If one wishes to meditate, one often begins by acting as if one were meditating. As the form of meditation is assumed, the content of meditation begins to be experienced. If one acts as if one loves another, and acts with sincerity and with honesty, often feelings of love toward that person do begin to flow. So with yoga. If one watches and practices as if yoga were an art and the Self were the artist, then this lends validity to the possibility of this being true.

This brings one to the question of technique. Yoga is also a concrete way of bringing about profound physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual changes within the individual. The student is instructed in yama and niyama (basic ethical precepts), in asanas (meditative postures), in pranayama (conscious voluntary control of the energy associated with breath), in pratyhara and dharana (watching and observing the mind), and finally, in dhyana, or meditation. The final experiencing of samadhi (union) cannot be taught; it is both the result of the foregoing disciplines and independent of them. The understanding of this apparent paradox is part of the process of learning about yoga.

Thus yoga can be seen from the angles of science, art or technique. It can be approached as a small practice of one’s life, or as the way that life is lived, a quality which is brought to live which liberates one from the strong bonds of ego.

Eliminating invasive rats could help islands survive rising seas

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Removing invasive rats could help protect low-lying islands from rising seas by restoring seabirds, boosting coral reef growth, and increasing sand production.

Low-lying atoll islands are usually written off as the first places a rising sea will cover. Around some of them, though, the coral reef is growing fast enough to keep pace with the sea through the end of this century.

One important factor is whether invasive rats have driven away the island’s seabirds.

Where seabirds nest, their droppings feed the reef.

Lagoon reefs there build upward almost twice as fast as reefs beside rat-overrun islands and produce more than twice as much sand.

Native forests produce more than four times as much fine root growth as areas dominated by coconut palms. This can hold the island’s soil in place.

Nick Graham, a marine ecologist at Lancaster University‘s Lancaster Environment Centre, led a team that measured all three factors on 28 islands in the Indian and Pacific Oceans.

The team compared islands as they are today, with and without rats.

“Atoll islands are not static, they are naturally active systems that can change and adapt to their environmental conditions if they are allowed to do so,” Graham said.

Boobies, frigatebirds, terns and noddies feed at sea, up to 620 miles (1,000 kilometers) from the island where they nest. They come home to roost, and the guano they deposit on the island contains nitrogen.

On islands with the largest seabird colonies, their droppings deposited more than 320 pounds of nitrogen per acre (366 kilograms per hectare) each year.

Rats that arrived on ships eat seabird eggs and chicks, and on most atolls the bird colonies are gone. Many islands have also lost their native forest to coconut plantations.

These palms now make up about 60 percent of the forest on Pacific atolls.

The team counted nests on islands in the Seychelles, the Chagos Archipelago and Tetiaroa Atoll in French Polynesia.

Islands with no rats and mostly native forest got the heaviest rain of bird nitrogen. The rest got very little.

Corals near seabird islands photosynthesized more and laid down more limestone skeleton. From that, the team estimated how fast each reef could build upward.

Lagoon reefs beside rat-free islands rose by an estimated 0.23 inches (5.9 millimeters) a year.

This is 1.8 times the rate of reef rise beside rat-infested islands. Ocean-facing reefs showed a smaller and less certain edge.

For the central Indian Ocean, the sea is projected to rise about 0.24 inches (6 millimeters) a year by 2100 if the world warms by 2°C (3.6°F).

The bird-fed lagoon reefs could nearly match that. The reefs beside rat islands, however, are predicted to fall short of even today’s rise of about 0.16 inches (4 millimeters) a year.

A reef that keeps pace with rising seas helps weaken waves before they reach the shore.

“The study shows beautifully how atolls are an intimately interconnected island-reef system, a geo-ecological system,” said Yadvinder Malhi, an ecologist at the University of Oxford and a co-author.

Parrotfish scrape coral and excrete it as sand. On some Indian Ocean reefs they produce up to 85 percent of the new sand.

Around islands with seabirds, parrotfish had more energy available, and the surrounding reefs produced 2.3 to 2.8 times as much sand.

In an interview with Earth.com, Graham was asked what surprised him most.

“The increase in sediment production by seabirds was something that had not been investigated before, and the increase is quite dramatic,” he said.

He put a figure on it for a hypothetical island of about 35 acres (14 hectares), roughly three quarters of a mile long.

With seabirds present, the surrounding reefs would produce about 220 cubic yards (170 cubic meters) more sediment each year than reefs around an island without seabirds.

“That is a really meaningful quantity,” Graham said.

Not all of that sand reaches the beach. The grains on the reef and the grains on the shore match closely, though, which points to a steady supply.

On Tetiaroa, the team measured fine roots in forest plots for two years.

Where native trees such as Pisonia grandis covered more than 75 percent of the ground, each square yard of soil held an estimated 8.2 miles (13.2 kilometers) of fine root.

Where native cover was below 75 percent, the figure was 1.8 miles (2.9 kilometers).

Coconut palms have short, rounded root systems. Native broadleaf trees grow long, dense roots that hold sand in place when storm waves wash over an island.

Seabirds strengthened that effect. With plenty of bird nitrogen in the soil, root growth rose by about 9 percent for every extra 1% of native forest cover. With few birds, more native forest didn’t change root growth at all.

The reef growth and sand figures come from six islands in the Chagos Archipelago, and the root figures from 19 forest plots on five islands of Tetiaroa.

The team inferred the gains from islands that already differ, rather than following one atoll from rat eradication through to a faster-growing reef.

How much reef sand actually reaches an island, and how reef growth changes the scouring of a shoreline, still have to be measured. Island size and local currents also shape what happens to any one atoll.

Jennifer Appoo, a co-author at the University of Seychelles, said the results matter most where seawalls and other engineering are unaffordable or out of place.

Many of the Seychelles’ atolls are uninhabited or sparsely inhabited, she said.

Graham told Earth.com that eradicating invasive predators such as rats should be the top priority for island managers.

It helps the island’s wildlife and the reef around it, he said, and this study shows it can boost the islands’ climate resilience too.

“I think what is key here from a policy perspective is that restoring atoll islands can have major gains in two core areas: biodiversity conservation, and climate adaptation,” he said.

Rats have already been removed from about 1,000 islands worldwide.

Asked by Earth.com which islands he is watching next, Graham said he has just started an Island-Ocean Research Hub at Lancaster.

It will study islands from the tropics to the Arctic, in the Seychelles, the Red Sea, the Mediterranean, the U.K. and Arctic Norway.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

This Cult-Hit Sci-Fi Horror Series With Stephen King Vibes Has Become A Major Netflix Hit

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

There are so many problems with streaming media, but a big one is that each of these companies is hellbent on creating new movies and shows to fill out their library of licensable titles. There's a lot that's worth watching, but there's nowhere near enough time to watch these worthy works. As a result, great films and series fall between the cracks or get unceremoniously cancelled before they can complete their run.

So it's something of a miracle that "From," a science fiction horror series created by John Griffin that flew under the radar for several years, survived for five seasons. The final season was greenlit by MGM+ last April, thanks in large part to the show's enthusiastic cult following, but the company might consider lobbying Griffin for another season. Because this cult favorite has become a bona fide hit on Netflix.

Netflix began streaming the first three seasons of "From" last month, and it's currently the fourth most popular series on the service. It's not hard to understand why. Griffin's show is basically "Lost" with major Stephen King vibes, and it even features one of the show's stars, Harold Perrineau.

"From" opens with a family of four being forced to re-route their road trip when their chosen path is blocked by a fallen tree. They wind up in a small town where they receive directions out of the area from Sheriff Boyd Stevens (Perrineau), but they soon find themselves back in the town again. Gradually, they discover that there is no escaping this unnamed town.

"From" quickly makes "Lost" look like a lovely tropical vacation when it's revealed that the town turns decidedly lethal at night, due to the presence of vicious, human-looking monsters. And the show is unsparing in depicting these creatures' savagery. It's a gorehound's delight.

A major reason why "From" never caught on with mainstream viewers is that the first season streamed on Epix, which would eventually be acquired by MGM+. So Netflix is essentially doing a huge favor for MGM+, because "From" is every bit as addictive as "Lost."

Given the series' uptick in visibility, perhaps the critically acclaimed show (all four seasons rate over 90% on Rotten Tomatoes) will finally earn some Primetime Emmy nominations. It won the Saturn Award for Best Horror Television Series last year, and Perrineau is well overdue for Emmy consideration. The only impediment here is that voters will have to binge four seasons of a show that was off their radar.

If voters do take the plunge, they'll find a smart, terrifying series that flirts with the bleakness of Stephen King's legendary horror bummer "The Mist." A lot of people may not have the stomach for that, but Griffin and his writers adroitly plumb the darkness of our era, where days often feel like a purgatorial drag. But "From" keeps us watching because we need to believe there's a way out of this.

EPA throws in the towel on policing air pollution from factory farms

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Critics say EPA is effectively abandoning Clean Air Act enforcement at factory farms.

The Environmental Protection Agency late last week announced it would end a 20-plus-year effort to develop methods to monitor air pollution from factory farms, effectively shuttering the agency’s stop-and-start attempts to regulate the potentially pollutant-spewing facilities under the Clean Air Act.

The announcement came just days before the midterm elections and was characterized by the agency as a long-awaited reprieve for farmers, a historically important constituency for Trump and the Republican party.

When tens of thousands of animals are packed into one place, pollution from their waste is blown from ventilation fans or emanates from giant lagoons of liquid effluent. Livestock facilities emit fine particulate matter, ammonia, nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide and volatile organic compounds, all contributing to a toxic mix. Research has shown that emissions from these operations are a major cause of air pollution, causing nearly 12,700 deaths in the United States a year.

Strictly speaking, concentrated animal feeding operations, or CAFOs, are required to get permits under the Clean Air Act and report emissions of these pollutants under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation and Liability and Act and the Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act. But through a deal crafted in tandem with the EPA and the livestock industry, they’ve been given amnesty from doing so for more than 20 years.

The arrangement goes back to the early 2000s when the agency attempted to prosecute CAFOs for exceeding limits on some of these pollutants. The industry pushed back, arguing there was no adequate way of measuring them. So the agency worked with industry groups to develop what was dubbed the Air Consent Agreement, through which the EPA granted thousands of CAFOs safe harbor from legal action in exchange for their participation in a study that was funded by the industry.

The goal of the study, the agency said, was to develop tools and models that CAFOs could use to “determine whether they emit pollutants at levels that require them to apply for permits under the CAA or submit notifications under CERCLA or EPCRA” and “produce a scientifically sound basis for measuring and estimating air emissions” from these facilities.

But since the adoption of the agreement in 2005, the agency has failed to produce any air “emissions estimating methodologies,” even prompting a 2017 report from the agency’s Office of Inspector General that faulted it for slow-walking the process.

Meanwhile, the Trump administration and Congress have also gutted reporting requirements for factory farms to report air pollutants under CERCLA, also known as the Superfund Act, and EPCRA.

“As EPA has been saying it’s proceeding with this plan, it’s been dismantling all of its own tools to regulate emissions,” said Tarah Heinzen, the legal director for the advocacy group Food & Water Watch, which has tracked the proceedings from the beginning and filed dozens of lawsuits attempting to push regulators to control pollution from CAFOs. “Now it has announced it has no intention, basically, of ever holding factory farms accountable under the Clean Air Act, either.”

In its Oct. 30 announcement, the agency noted that in the two decades since the study, known as the National Air Emissions Monitoring Study, was conducted, many farms have modernized their barns and manure systems.

“Good science requires good data, and these data are too old and too limited to support methods that farms could rely on,” said Abigale Tardif, the acting assistant administrator for the agency’s Office of Air and Radiation, in a statement. “We would rather tell farmers that plainly than issue numbers we cannot stand behind.”

Rob Brenneman, president of the National Pork Producers Council, which has long pushed for the decision, said in a statement: “This move confirms what farmers have long known: Every farm is different, and there is no one-size-fits-all model to apply to them.”

Heinzen noted that the agency’s decision simply means that this administration has no intention of regulating air emissions from CAFOs, but for now, the Clean Air Act still requires permits for facilities that exceed emissions thresholds. The amnesty granted to these facilities will no longer be in effect now that the agreement has concluded.

“There’s no EPA-issued methodology to estimate emissions,” she said. “That doesn’t mean that there aren’t good studies out there of emissions from different types of animal feeding operations that can be used.”

Heinzen pointed to California, which has models for livestock emissions that it uses to enforce its own air regulations. “We just don’t have one set of national emissions factors from EPA,” Heinzen said. “That does not absolve these factory farms from their obligation to comply with federal law.”

Still, under this administration, it’s clear air emissions from factory farms won’t be measured and monitored any time soon. “No one’s going to estimate anything,” Heinzen noted. “As EPA said in its press release, we’re certainly not about to try to enforce the law.”

The Air Compliance Agreement did not extend to methane. The U.S. livestock industry is the single-biggest source of methane in the country, but Congress effectively blocks livestock facilities from reporting methane emissions in annual spending bills.

The Swift Observatory is expected to re-enter Earth’s atmosphere on Wednesday, and is ‘unlikely’ to cause harm.

Almost 22 years after it was launched, NASA’s Swift Observatory is now about to fall back to Earth on Wednesday after attempts to push it back into a higher orbit failed. NASA announced that the aging space telescope is anticipated to re-enter Earth’s atmosphere at approximately 3:30AM ET (give or take 11 hours), and that it expects most of the spacecraft to burn up, but some components will likely survive re-entry.

The observatory weighs around 3,200 pounds and measures in at 5.6 meters (18.4 feet) high and 5.4 meters (17.7 feet) wide, though much of the latter includes its deployable solar arrays. The risk of surviving components causing any harm is low, according to NASA:

“Re-entry locations of any surviving components can vary from hemisphere to hemisphere and depend on when it re-enters. Due to Swift’s low inclination, re-entry locations of surviving fragments are limited between 20.6 degrees south latitude and 20.6 degrees north latitude. The risk of harm coming to anyone on Earth is unlikely — approximately 1 in 2,100. NASA and Space Force will continue to monitor the re-entry and update predictions.”

The agency gave up on trying to save the falling telescope earlier this year, after the Link spacecraft that was attempting the rescue experienced technical issues with attitude control. Still, Swift far outlived its original two-year prime mission to study the origin of gamma-ray bursts, and went on to serve as a general-purpose multi-wavelength observatory until solar storms pushed it into a lower orbit. You can follow Swift’s descent by monitoring NASA’s blog for continued updates.

Why You Shouldn’t Take Anything Personally

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Don’t take anything personally. Easier said than done. This represents the second agreement in the bestselling book The Four Agreements written by Don Miguel Ruiz (1997). In it, he astutely states, "Even when a situation seems so personal, even if others insult you directly, it has nothing to do with you. What they say, what they do, and the opinions they give are according to the agreements they have in their own minds."

Despite this, we find ourselves allowing someone's criticism, disapproval, or lack of responsiveness to determine how we feel about ourselves. Our mood and confidence can instantly shift because of a perceived disapproving tone or sensing that we’re the cause of someone’s irritation or frustration. We may react irrationally, impulsively, and intensely based on someone’s reactions, and our perceptions and interpretations.

Taking things personally is often an automatic psychological response shaped by our insecurities, attachment experiences, learned beliefs, and fundamental desire and need to belong. Research suggests that perceived rejection can activate our sympathetic nervous system, helping explain why interpersonal slights can feel disproportionately painful (Eisenberger et al., 2003).

1. You do not like everyone; therefore, you cannot expect that everyone will like you.

Consider how many people you have encountered whom you did not particularly connect or resonate with. Sometimes we have negative feelings that get evoked solely by someone’s presence or mannerism, even if there isn’t any rational justification for it.

Our personal preferences are influenced by many factors such as another’s personality, values, expectations, etc. When we’re not readily accepted, or outwardly rejected, we assume and interpret it as evidence of our inadequacy or lack of worthiness.

Our brain’s tendency is to seek external approval and acceptance and gauge it as a valid measure of our worth. We’re taught this early on in our development and societal standards reinforce it. We frame it as: “If they think I have value, then I must. If they think I do not, then I must not.”

We do not learn that someone else’s preference or opinion is just that, and that our standards and opinion about ourselves matter most. You can reframe and consider that someone's preference for you doesn't determine your value, and you can be an incredible person, despite not being someone’s preferred person.

2. You cannot read minds. Your triggers, perceptions, judgments, and assumptions are all yours.

We frequently confuse our thoughts, feelings, and interpretations as absolute facts. If someone mistakenly doesn’t acknowledge us, we conclude that they don’t care about us and we’re not important enough to them. We also sometimes fail to own and take accountability for our own triggers and often get defensive, deflect, and blame to avoid facing our own fears and vulnerabilities.

In relationships, mind reading, personalization, catastrophizing, and other cognitive distortions can skew reality and be counterproductive and harmful (Beck, 2020). The inaccuracy is understandable given that our mind constructs explanations based on our past experiences, expectations, and emotional states.

To balance out your thinking, you can ask yourself: "What do I actually know, and what am I assuming? How else can I see this?" This is approaching the circumstance and person with overarching openness, curiosity, and expansiveness.

3. Criticism, aggression, and other harsh responses may reflect someone else's internal struggles.

Individuals can externalize their shame, frustration, insecurity, or unresolved emotional pain through hostility, defensiveness, or criticism. Psychodynamic theory describes projection as a defense mechanism through which individuals attribute aspects of their internal experience to others (Bailey & Pico 2023).

For example, a highly insecure person may be especially critical toward others. To distinguish whether a projection is being enacted, you can consider their delivery, intensity, or emotional reaction.

4. Negative energy is to be observed, not absorbed.

Imagine someone is speaking to you impatiently and with irritability. Within seconds, your chest tightens, your heart rate accelerates, your thoughts spiral, and you feel compelled to relent to protect yourself or actively defend yourself. Their emotional state has comingled and become yours.

Research on emotional contagion suggests that people can influence one another's emotional experiences, sometimes without conscious awareness (Wang, 2024). Self-awareness, self-regulation, and self-compassion don't require emotional absorption.

You can connect with and offer empathy for someone else’s reaction and experiences without absorbing and taking on their negative energy. It requires thoughtfully and mindfully deciphering what is theirs and what is yours, and how you choose to react to it.

5. Someone's negative mood is not automatically about you

You may encounter distance, irritability enacted by a harsh tone or body language. Your immediate thought might be, "What did I do?" We often personalize others’ behavior, as connected to something we said or did, without evidence to support it.

Their mood can be reflective of their disappointment, fatigue, hormones, stress, or concerns that can be entirely unrelated to you. Before jumping to conclusions, approach with curiosity and consider asking, "I wonder if something’s going on for you. Is everything okay?" You can choose to approach with compassion and be attuned to someone's emotions without automatically assuming you're responsible for them.

Rejection can be particularly painful because belonging is a fundamental psychological need. Experiencing rejection can contribute to negative self-confidence, social anxiety, and the ability to sustain healthy trusting friendships and intimate relationships.

When someone rejects us, we often transform a specific experience into a more global conclusion. We make inferences such as “They didn’t choose me" becomes "I'm not good enough." Even though intellectually we know that rejection can reflect differing needs, incompatibility, timing, or circumstances beyond what we can control, we tend to personalize and generalize it.

You can remind yourself that your worth isn't determined by whether someone recognizes, attunes to, appreciates, or chooses you. You can’t avoid rejection, but you can strengthen your self-respect and self-compassion based on how you respond to it.

7. People's reactions provide you with vital information

You can learn a lot from observing others’ reactions. It can give you a glimpse of their expectations, perspective, judgments, and emotional state. Emotional maturity involves vulnerability in noticing and acknowledging each person’s contribution, taking accountability, and repairing and healing when appropriate.

In order to accomplish this, you can identify if there is a difference of opinion, a divergent way of seeing circumstances, or an altered way of behaving based on differing core values. The goal is to evaluate, validate and reach mutual understanding so that future interactions can be healthier and more satisfying.

8. Proactively protect your emotional energy

We can spend hours self-doubting, replaying conversations, anticipating rejection, questioning ourselves, or trying to change someone else's opinion of us. This rumination or perseveration spirals into thoughts about the thoughts, feelings about the feelings, thoughts about the feelings, and feelings about the thoughts. The emotional cost is exhaustion, exasperation, and typically extends beyond the original interaction.

Learning to notice, acknowledge, and interrupt your thoughts allows you to redirect your attention, even though your mind wants desperately to problem-solve. You could more readily focus on what you can directly influence, such as your interpretations, boundaries, responses, and choices.

The greatest emotional freedom comes when you no longer require other people to behave differently to feel secure within yourself. You recognize that you have choices regarding the way you respond.

You can recognize that you can be impacted by someone’s behavior without it defining you or automatically driving your behavior.

It can be challenging to notice parts of yourself that you would rather avoid or rid yourself of. Your activation, which can manifest itself as aggression, distancing, or in other ways, is about you. No one triggers you; you get triggered. Recognizing that can change not only how you experience others, but how you experience yourself.
