Posted November 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

By Jennifer K. Robbennolt, Alice Curtis Campbell Professor of Law and Professor of Psychology, University of Illinois College of Law

Thousands of lawsuits against social media companies brought by individuals, school districts, and other government entities are making their way through the courts. The cases generally allege that the companies caused harm to youth by designing their social media platforms to foster compulsive use and failing to adequately warn users about the potential harms of their platforms.

The first of these cases brought by an individual plaintiff was decided by a jury in March 2026—a bellwether case that will inform decision-making in future cases (K. G. M. v. Meta Platforms, Inc.). The plaintiff, identified by her initials, K.G.M., claimed that Meta and Google had used the “engineering of addiction” to design their apps in ways that encouraged users, particularly young users, to remain on the platforms for extended periods. K.G.M. claimed that she began using social media compulsively as a child and that this problematic social media use contributed to depression, anxiety, self-harm, and damage to her relationships. A jury agreed and awarded her $6 million in compensatory and punitive damages. Social media companies ByteDance (TikTok) and Snap (Snapchat) are also defendants in the broader set of lawsuits, but each settled with K.G.M. before her case went to trial.

Historically, efforts to hold social media companies liable for harms associated with their platforms have faced a significant legal obstacle: Section 230 of the 1996 Communications Decency Act shields tech companies from liability for harms caused by user-generated content. In this case, however, the legal claims were based not on the content posted on the platforms but rather on the platform design. The lawsuit focused on design features such as infinite scrolling, video autoplay, variable reinforcement, constant push notifications, and beauty filters. K.G.M.’s lawyer likened these features to a “digital casino” designed to keep users on the social media platforms, arguing that adolescents are particularly vulnerable to their effects.

One challenging aspect of this litigation is establishing a causal relationship between the alleged negligence—the platforms’ design choices—and the mental health harms claimed by plaintiffs. To be successful, a plaintiff must show both that a platform’s design feature can contribute to adverse mental health outcomes and that it did so in the plaintiff’s individual case. The social media companies argue that adolescent mental health is complex and question whether any particular mental health outcome can be causally linked to the design of a social media platform.

Reports from APA (Health Advisory on Social Media Use in Adolescence, 2023) and the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (Galea, S., et al. [Eds.], Social Media and Adolescent Health, 2024) note that this causation question is complex. Psychological studies have found that social media is associated with a variety of negative effects, including anxiety, depression, and attention problems. Yet social media is ubiquitous, and links between mental health and social media use are likely bidirectional, complicating causal conclusions. In addition, individual differences make some users more susceptible than others to the relevant design features and to developing patterns of problematic use. Research also shows that social media can have positive effects on mental health, including healthy socialization and self-expression.

Research that more precisely identifies the effects of specific design features on mental health outcomes and examines how individual differences might moderate those effects will be critical in informing the ongoing litigation and any other regulatory or voluntary reforms. Longitudinal, experimental, and quasi-experimental studies will be particularly important in determining which features give rise to problematic use, under what conditions, and for which young people.

Why Chris Pratt Was Recast In Taylor Sheridan's Sicario Meets The Terminal List Action Thriller

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Despite being the original lead of Taylor Sheridan's gritty Sicario meets The Terminal List action thriller, here's why Chris Pratt dropped out. While he initially broke through with comic roles on the likes of Parks and Recreation, Chris Pratt's movie and TV show career has seen him taking a hard right into action in recent years.

From The Tomorrow War to Mercy, it's increasingly common to see Pratt playing soldiers or cops. Pratt's Amazon franchise The Terminal List just scored a season three renewal too, alongside a prequel show. In short, the star would be perfect for a Taylor Sheridan military actioner, and that almost came to pass in 2018 when he signed on for Sheridan's thriller F.A.S.T.

This follows a former soldier recruited by the DEA to take down drug dealers in his local town, and the film was set to be Sheridan's next directorial outing following Wind River. That didn't come to pass, though, and while F.A.S.T. will finally arrive nearly a decade later in 2027, it won't be Pratt scowling on the poster whilst holding an assault rifle.

Sheridan was an actor before switching to screenwriting, after realizing his career had peaked with a supporting role on Sons of Anarchy. In a burst of creativity, Sheridan penned his Neo-Western American Frontier trilogy, consisting of Sicario, Hell or High Water and Wind River. F.A.S.T. was another script Sheridan wrote during this era, and one that Warner Bros. greenlit with a budget in the $75 million range (via Dark Horizons).

Chris Pratt soon signed on, but it soon hit a roadblock due to the surprise success of Sheridan's Yellowstone. Sheridan left the director's chair on F.A.S.T. to focus on the latter's second season, while Gavin O'Connor (The Accountant) stepped in as helmer. Ultimately, that version of the film was shelved, as Warners were concerned about making an R-rated film at that budget level.

With the movie dead (at least for the time being), Pratt moved on to other projects and Sheridan became one of the biggest names on television. After a long period away from the big screen, Sheridan resurrected F.A.S.T. as a producer, with his regular collaborator Ben Richardson (who helmed many episodes of 1883 and 1923) as the new director. The ever-busy Pratt did not return, however, and is likely a little too old to portray the role as written.

For a time, it looked like Sheridan would become the next big director of dark, R-rated thrillers and Neo-Westerns, like Walter Hill. Directing F.A.S.T. in 2019 would have been a level up for Sheridan, being a big-budget actioner with a major star. Had he actually made the film, and it proved a success, it's possible his career would have gone in a different direction.

Instead, Sheridan's Yellowstone universe proved a success, and the ever-prolific writer/producer was soon knocking out more hit shows like Lioness and Tulsa King. While he eventually directed a mid-budget movie called Those Who Wish Me Dead in 2021, his TV schedule has kept him away from the types of cinematic thrillers he made his name with.

Of course, Sheridan had no way of knowing that his decision to step away from F.A.S.T. would have such a profound impact on his career. At the time, it was a way to keep control of a TV series that meant a lot to him, but as a consequence, he became an in-demand showrunner. Who knows, had he chosen F.A.S.T. over Yellowstone, maybe Sheridan would be the one directing the upcoming Call of Duty adaptation instead of simply co-writing it; instead, Peter Berg is taking on that responsibility.

For somebody who had never written a script until his 40s, Sheridan learned fast. Sheridan quickly became known for his quotable dialogue and memorable characters, and F.A.S.T. itself won acclaim when the script did the rounds during the late 2010s. There is a glowing review of the thriller on the screenwriting blog ScriptShadow, which praises how Sheridan takes a familiar premise but approaches it from fresh angles.

The review specifically cites a scene where the main character is talking with a therapist about his wartime trauma. While F.A.S.T.'s protagonist keeps claiming he's fine, throughout his session he is flashing back to horrific combat experiences. It also sounds like the story has a lot in common with Lioness, and deals with the murkier side of warfare as the main character leads a misfit band of soldiers into a private war.

One of the downsides of Sheridan's move to television is that the lean writing style that made his films so great has fallen away. Outside of 1883, some of his shows - especially later seasons of Yellowstone - felt padded simply to fill an episode count. Part of what makes F.A.S.T. so exciting is that it will be a one-and-done action thriller, penned during the same creative run that led to Sicario and Wind River.

It's unknown if Pratt was approached about returning to F.A.S.T., but it's for the best that he moved on. The movie isn't a million miles from The Terminal List, which is also about a former soldier going beyond the law. As written, F.A.S.T.'s lead is a younger character, and the role has now gone to Brandon Sklenar.

Sklenar earned his big breakthrough playing manly hunter Spencer Dutton in 1923, and he has since appeared in hits like The Housemaid with Sydney Sweeney. F.A.S.T. will be his first major test as a leading man, and Sheridan clearly has faith in his 1923 star to pull it off. It helps that the 2027 thriller surrounds Sklenar with a supporting cast that includes screen vets like Jason Clarke, LaKeith Stanfield and Trevante Rhodes (Moonlight).

With F.A.S.T. and Call of Duty, it seems Sheridan is working his way back towards theaters. He hasn't directed a film since 2021, but if F.A.S.T. proves a hit, he might finally step back behind the viewfinder. The movie doesn't have much in the way of competition in May 2027 - though time will tell how audiences greet Mel Gibson's The Resurrection of the Christ: Part One when it arrives earlier that month.

Review: Secrets Playa Blanca Costa Mujeres (Hyatt Inclusive)

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Of note, I was in Cancun, Mexico for business meetings at another resort but wanted to evaluate both this experience for our readers as well as the overall position of Hyatt in its ever-growing Inclusives collection. My stay was almost exactly 24 hours and solo which is uncharacteristic at this destination resort. My experience will likely differ from other guests due to those factors.

Secrets Playa Blanca Costa Mujeres is an adults-only resort in the Hyatt Inclusive Collection, and I toured it on a site visit in November 2026. It sits on a stretch of white-sand beach in Costa Mujeres, 12 miles north of Cancun. Cancun International Airport is the arrival airport. I could not confirm a single official transfer time from the property page. Tour listings commonly put the drive in a 30 to 45 minute range.

Address: Carretera Punta Sam – Isla Blanca, Mz 2, Lt 10, Costa Mujeres, Zona Continental Isla Mujeres, Quintana Roo 77400, Mexico

Google Maps: Secrets Playa Blanca Costa Mujeres

Hyatt is the best in the business at high end resort arrivals. I had given my name at the security gate and by the time I reached the lobby, the door to the vehicle was opened for me, I was greeted by name and a “welcome home”, a constant refrain throughout my stay. It’s a small thing and maybe cliché but it was effective. I was given a Globalist upgrade even though rules at all-inclusives differ from the rest of the chain. As my upgrade was in a Preferred room, I was brought into a private room at the front of the lobby where check-in was done for me.

The lobby is an open-air room under an arched woven ceiling, with a direct view out to the ocean.

The resort is spread across 10 buildings with 505 keys. Of those, 221 are Preferred Club keys with an elevated experience. My building (and others like it) had just five rooms on each floor and just five floors which made it feel boutique rather than massive. Every oceanfront room is Preferred Club, and so are the first two buildings on either side.

The room views follow a simple rule by floor. Every first floor room is a swim-out, floors two through four are tropical view, and the fifth floor is ocean view or ocean front. That makes booking easy to reason through: if you want to step off your patio into the water, go low, and if you want the sea, go high.

The walkways give a sense of grandeur and space. All of the flora is local, and in a touch I loved, the service rooms are named after the plants.

There are four pools, and each one has a unique characteristic. The Jungle pool is drinks only and hidden in the middle of the resort by trees. The Social pool runs activities throughout the day at this adults-only resort for those who want to participate. There is also an infinity pool on the edge of the beach, and then the Preferred Club pool for those staying in that category. These are separate from the first floor swim-outs.

The spa (hydrotherapy is included for Preferred guests) featured an outdoor circuit that included both hot and cold plunge pools, amongst other therapeutic services.

The Preferred Club pool was the most frequented, though some images below look more vacant due to rain that rolled through during my opportunity to collect media on my short stay.

The gym is open to every guest from 6 AM to 8 PM, and it is outfitted with Technogym equipment.

The spa is open 8 AM to 8 PM with 17 treatment cabins and a beauty salon, and hydrotherapy is included. That last part is the headline. The hydrotherapy space is ridiculous, easily the standout of the entire property for me, and it is the kind of amenity that would make me pick this resort over a competitor down the beach. There are indoor and outdoor showers as well.

The men’s changing room featured private showers with an option for indoor or outdoor experiences.

The theater seats 264 people and hosts cabaret and Mayan shows. There are also pickleball courts, which I am starting to think should be required equipment at any resort that wants a younger crowd as well as padel for the European set (and growing American interest.)

Secrets is an adults-only brand, and every room here comes with what the company calls Unlimited-Luxury. For a standard guest that means unlimited dining and top-shelf drinks, Wi-Fi, daytime activities and nightly entertainment, with tips and taxes included. Add 24-hour room service, a mini-bar refreshed daily (you can ask for just about anything you want in there), and pool and beach wait service for all guests.

The Preferred Club is an upgrade, and at this resort it is a big one because it owns the oceanfront. Secrets lists personalized check-in and check-out, afternoon hot and cold hors d’oeuvres, desserts and premium liquors, access to the Preferred Club lounge and beach bar, an upgraded mini-bar, upgraded bath amenities, butler service, Bali beds at the pool and beach reserved for club guests, and exclusive dining at the Preferred Club beach restaurant. Preferred also gives club guests exclusive access to an infinity pool and puts an oversized soaking tub on the terrace of the upgraded suites (at least with the oceanfronts).

I book a lot of Hyatt’s all-inclusives through Hyatt Privé rates, and this resort is one of 388 in the collection. Privé is Hyatt’s invitation-only program for travel advisors, so you only get the benefits when an approved advisor makes the booking, and the stay still earns World of Hyatt points and elite nights.

The all-inclusive version of Privé looks a lot different from a Park Hyatt booking. As I wrote when Hyatt’s all-inclusive resorts joined Privé, there is no room upgrade and no breakfast benefit, since every meal is already included. At this property, Privé guests receive a $100 resort credit per stay, a welcome amenity and letter, hydrotherapy access, and early check-in and late check-out based on availability.

Elite status matters here more than at most all-inclusives. Under World of Hyatt’s terms, Globalists get the best room available at check-in, including rooms with club access, at the resort’s discretion. Explorists are eligible for upgrades too, but not to standard suites or club rooms. Travelers who have stayed at Secrets resorts as Globalists report being designated Preferred Club guests even when they did not land in a club building, which unlocks the lounge, pool and beach areas. This was my experience on all accounts at the resort. Late checkout is 2 PM for Explorists and 4 PM for Globalists, subject to availability at resorts. It was able to honor my request for a late check-out, I did not arrive early enough to request the early check-in. Hyatt waives resort fees on eligible paid rates (at least for now) and award nights for Globalists, and on award nights only for every other tier, and Globalists also get free parking on free nights though in 2028 Globalists will have to select this as a preference. One catch is that Suite Upgrade Awards cannot be used at Secrets resorts, so you cannot lock in a suite with one here.

Weddings are big business here. The courtyard outside the theater is the setting, with the sunset behind it, and the resort hosts lots of them. The resort disclosed its occupancy to me but it wasn’t clear if it was for public consumption. It was shockingly high especially for a Thursday in mid-November. Over the holidays or spring break all the hotels are full but this is a period where I would have expected a lull. Still, despite being extremely full, it never felt crowded.

For meetings and groups, the convention space measures 9,800 square feet and splits into five sections. The boardroom was occupied at the time so I couldn’t include images of that.

Restaurants can be booked in advance through the Hyatt Inclusive Collection app, which I wish more all-inclusives offered. Nobody wants to find out at 7 PM that the restaurant they wanted is full and through the app this can be done well in advance of a guest’s arrival. The dress code in the restaurants is resort casual, though the patio does not require slacks and closed toed shoes for men, shorts are acceptable outside.

The New York Jets activated veteran wide receiver Sterling Shepard from the practice squad ahead of their game against the Los Angeles Chargers. Rookie receiver Omar Cooper Jr. is out for today's game with an ankle injury, leaving the Jets short at wide receiver.

Sterling Shepard is a 33 year old, 5' 10", 195 pound wide receiver. He was originally drafted out of Oklahoma by the New York Giants with the 40th overall pick in the 2nd round of the 2016 NFL draft. Shepard was an effective receiver for the Giants early in his career. He spent the first eight years of his career with the Giants before moving on after the 2023 season. Shepard played with the Tampa Bay Buccaneers in 2024 and 2025 before being signed as an unrestricted free agent by the Jets in 2026. Sterling Shepard has been elevated to the active roster twice this season before today. He has been targeted twice by the Jets, with one reception good for three yards.

The Jets needed another body at wide receiver with Cooper out. The team has Garrett Wilson and Adonai Mitchell active, but behind those two are Tim Patrick and Malik McClain, who are unlikely to provide much value. Unfortunately, Shepard likely has little left to offer the Jets. He will turn 34 years old in February. Shepard has not exceeded 400 receiving yards in a season, a 50% success rate, or 7 yards per target since all the way back in 2020. The Jets might be better off giving practice squad receiver Jamaal Pritchett a shot. Regardless of whether the Jets elevated Shepard or Pritchett, they will be thin at wide receiver until Cooper makes it back. In the meantime, who knows, maybe Shepard has a turn back the clock performance left in his tank.
