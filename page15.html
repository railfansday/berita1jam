Lazio and Roma make Serie A history as unbeaten Rome duo chase Inter

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Lazio and Roma have made history as the first two teams from the same city to be unbeaten in Serie A after 11 games, and they are now challenging Inter at the top.

According to Corriere dello Sport, not even Milan and Inter have ever managed it. Gennaro Gattuso’s Lazio lead on 29 points, Cristian Chivu’s champions are second on 28, and Gian Piero Gasperini’s Roma are third on 27.

Lazio’s points total is the best in the club’s history at this stage, beating the 28 they had under Simone Inzaghi in 2017-18. The only other time they were unbeaten after 11 games was in 1972-73 under Tommaso Maestrelli, the season before their first Scudetto. They also have the best defence in the league, with only nine goals conceded.

Roma have the division’s best attack with 31 goals, and they are only the fourth team in 65 years to pass 30 at this stage. They have already had 14 different scorers and have kept a league-high six clean sheets.

Opta’s supercomputer remains sceptical, giving Roma an 18% chance of the title and Lazio 10%. Gasperini, however, says he “wouldn’t sign for second place”, while Gattuso is still talking about reaching 40 points first.

The two sides meet in the Derby della Capitale on 13 December.

No one knows how to dress for work anymore—even executives

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Companies spent years dragging employees back to the office, but forgot to tell them what to wear. The result is confusion stretching from junior employees to the executive suite.

One of Grant Alexander’s longtime clients is the COO of a big private company. He recently lamented that a few of his vice presidents had asked him what they should wear to an event.

“He looked at me, and he’s like, ‘When was the last time a VP had to ask the COO what they should be wearing for something?’” Alexander, the founder of Grant Alexander Studio, an executive image and styling consultancy, tells Fast Company. “A lot of the executives I work with have never talked to their employees about this.” 

When the COVID-19 pandemic began, people still wore their office attire for the first few weeks. But as the lockdowns went on, smart trousers were replaced with sweatpants, and shirts were put away to make room for T-shirts and hoodies.

“Now there’s a gap between what people believe they should be wearing at the office, and what their wardrobe actually has become,” says Alexander. “So they’re just like, ‘I don’t know what I should be wearing right now’.”

The uncertainty is far reaching. A 2025 survey of 1,000 hybrid workers in the U.S. by International Workplace Group (IWG) found that workers reported feeling stressed or anxious about what to wear to the office around seven times a month. Almost half (46%) said this happened at least weekly, while 14% experienced it daily. 

Zoom dressing is no longer an option

Samantha Harman, a former newspaper editor turned personal stylist, tells Fast Company that knowing what to wear to the office has always been complicated. But return to office (RTO) has made it even harder, with so much ambiguity around what’s expected. 

For people whose work wardrobes have been neglected for the past few years, remembering and updating their personal style can be a daunting prospect.

“I am working with quite a few people who’ve got return to office mandates for the New Year, and they’re now looking at their wardrobe thinking, ‘I couldn’t go into the office like this’,” Harman says. “It’s all very well ‘top dressing,’ or Zoom dressing—where you are presenting yourself in a certain way on screen, but on the bottom you’ve got your pajamas on. But you can’t do that face-to-face.”

In one recent conversation, a manager told Harman that people either came to the office looking like they’d just rolled out of bed, or were so put together they could have been going to the opera. When she asked if he’d spoken to them about it, he replied: “Are you kidding? I don’t even know what the rules or the guidelines are.”

The problem is compounded by the fact that workplace dress codes haven’t always kept pace with changing expectations, Harman says. Companies continue to rely on generic terms such as “business casual” without explaining what they mean in practice. 

The confusion also can add to workers’ mental load. Harman recently overheard two men debating whether or not an invitation specifying “smart casual” meant they needed to wear a tie. “You’re giving your employees more work to do,” she says.

Clarity is particularly important for juniors

Francesca O’Connor, cofounder of the workplace culture, leadership, and communications consultancy HappyHQ, tells Fast Company that leaders need to explain what dressing appropriately actually means for their specific organization. They also must distinguish between expectations for an ordinary office day and something that requires stepping it up, such as a client pitch.

“That doesn’t mean everyone needs to wear a suit,” she says. “It means having the judgment to understand your audience and what the situation calls for.”

This is particularly important for younger workers entering the office for the first time, who may have had little opportunity to observe traditional workplace style.

“If there’s an expectation that people dress differently for certain occasions, explain it,” O’Connor says. “Don’t leave them guessing, particularly when they’re starting out in their careers.”

In the IWG survey, 94% of Gen Z respondents said they wanted guidance on what to wear to work, compared with 84% of millennials, 70% of Gen X and 61% of baby boomers. 

“Gen Z entering the workforce have no idea what to wear and nobody’s talking about it,” Alexander says. “So they’re just like, ‘OK, I have joggers. I’m just going to wear them.’ And it usually takes them getting yelled at and something happening and somebody correcting them.”

Guidelines can help build confidence

Beyond avoiding awkward conversations, clearer expectations could help employees feel more confident at work, Harman says. 

“If you are wearing something that makes you feel really good, you will behave in a different way than if you’re wearing something that makes you feel a bit like a potato,” she says.

The best way for leaders to have this conversation with their employees is to get their buy-in, Harman adds, by asking what they actually love to wear and what makes them feel confident.

“Even if you just host some workshops or forums where you have people talking about this stuff, [it] can be really helpful,” Harman says. “People often tell me, ‘this is not ever something that we ever speak about at work, and yet it affects me every single day—because every single day, the one thing that I have to do is get dressed’.”

Alexander believes that focusing heavily on getting people back to the office without considering how they present themselves is shortsighted. It’s also easily fixed, he says, with a simple email or Slack update.

“You can say, ‘Hey, a few people have asked us lately about what we should wear.’ Nobody’s going to question it,” he says. “Executives can make it easier on the entire organization by just talking about it.”

That’s if the executives themselves know what protocol should be, of course.

This Googlebook is driving me crazy

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Right now, Googlebooks make more sense for Google than they do for users.

After trying for years to bring Android features to Chromebooks, Google decided to start over with Android as the foundation for a new laptop operating system. The first wave of Googlebooks are high-end laptops (priced $900 and up), with Google pitching them as the ultimate computers for Android enthusiasts.

But in their current form, Googlebooks are a disjointed mess, with websites and apps that don’t work quite right and AI features that aren’t fully fleshed out. While having a single operating system and app platform across laptops and mobile devices might make life easier for Google, it doesn’t yet translate to a better experience than the Chromebooks that Google aims to replace.

Android’s not ready for laptops

For the past month I’ve been using Acer’s Googlebook 14, which is the cheapest Googlebook at $900, and the only one whose touchscreen flips around into tablet mode. I have no major complaints about the hardware, which has a featherweight 2.51-pound chassis, a rich 120 Hz 3K OLED screen, and a smooth trackpad (albeit a “diving board” type whose click mechanism stiffens at the top edge).

My frustrations are more on the software side, where Android isn’t ready to carry a real laptop workload.

While Google is pushing developers to optimize their Android apps for large screens, keyboards, and trackpads, Google itself hasn’t always bothered to do so. The preloaded version of Gmail, for instance, is not an optimized Android app, but rather a web-based desktop app powered by the Chrome browser. Same goes for Google Docs, Drive, Meet, and YouTube. Tapping the menu button in these apps reveals a “Running in Chrome” disclaimer, and any Chrome extensions you install will apply to these apps as well.

This wouldn’t be a major concern if the web apps worked the way they’re supposed to. In Gmail, though, cursor hover controls don’t work, so you can’t quickly trash or archive messages line by line in the inbox. In Google Drive, documents open in a Chrome browser tab, while the same documents open in their own windows via Google Docs or Sheets. And if you try to select text in Google Docs using the touchscreen, the comment button pops up directly over the right text selector, making it unreachable.

Third-party Android apps can also have issues. Signing into Slack bounces me out to a Chrome tab, which then fails to authenticate back inside the Android app, so the only way to gain access is by manually entering a URL and password for each workplace. Even then, Slack’s Android app is poorly optimized, without the desktop version’s persistent sidebar.

Video apps still feel unfinished on Googlebooks as well. Those that Google deems “optimized for desktop,” such as Netflix and Disney+, will automatically open in full screen, which is jarring but at least fits the use case of watching video. Others, such as Fox One and NFL, open in a floating desktop window, and clicking their full screen icons accomplishes nothing. You’re actually supposed to ignore that button, highlight the maximize button in the window controls, then click the little “Full Screen” icon. Optimized or not, none of these apps support always-visible picture-in-picture mode like their mobile counterparts.

One last nitpick, admittedly a niche one: Bookmarklets don’t work on Googlebooks. I keep more than dozen of these in my browser’s address bar, which allows me to launch site-specific searches, create page excerpts, find archived versions of web pages, and remove “sticky” page elements. Not being able to use them on the Googlebook is a reminder that you’re not quite getting a full Chrome desktop experience.

The app situation will likely improve over time. When I started using the Googlebook, it wouldn’t let me install the Bluesky app and directed me to the web version. Now the app is available. Google Docs has also gained the ability to mark individual documents for offline use, which wasn’t possible at the outset. The Google Play Store has a tiered labeling system—“Optimized for Desktop,” “Compatible with Desktop,” “Better on the Website”—so you know what to expect, but for now the ecosystem is a work in progress.

Android synergies in progress

Googlebooks do have some neat features for Android users, the best of which is being able to access your phone’s files directly through the laptop’s Files app. There’s also “cross-device continuity,” which doesn’t work with many apps, but lets you quickly continue editing a message in Gmail or a document in Google Docs after opening it up on your phone.

App casting is rougher. If you have a phone running Android 17, this lets you view your phone’s apps through a Googlebook without installing them, and while this might be useful in some scenarios, it’s a subpar experience with jerky scrolling and occasional formatting issues. It can also lead to annoyances, like when a phone notification pops up on the Googlebook, and tapping it takes you into the janky app-casting version instead of the one that’s installed on your computer.

Android as a desktop operating system will also inherently pose some limitations. AI apps like ChatGPT and Claude lack the device control and folder-based file permissions that their desktop apps offer, and there are no command bar apps akin to Raycast for Windows and MacOS. The relative simplicity of an Android-based laptop may be a positive for some, but for me it’s a hindrance.

Too many Geminis

Beyond just catering to Android enthusiasts, Google is pitching Googlebooks as a premier surface for its Gemini AI assistant. In practice this means there are four main ways to invoke Gemini from the computer:

  • Wiggling your mouse cursor
  • Saying “Hey Google”
  • Clicking the Gemini icon in the dock
  • Clicking the Gemini icon in the Chrome taskbar

While having many entry points to Gemini makes sense, the execution feels disjointed. If you open the full Gemini app while a pop-up chat window is already open, for instance, the chat window closes and the full app cuts off your conversation, switching to a new one. The Chrome sidebar and pop-up window are also separate, so conversations in one don’t carry over to the other.

Those little inconsistencies tripped me up constantly, as I’d have to keep careful track of which conversation was happening where based on how I’d originally invoked it. Instead of just cramming AI touch points into different parts of the operating system, Google needs to do a better job of tying them all together.

There’s also a missed opportunity for Gemini to act more as an AI agent. While desktop AI apps on other platforms can work directly with files and folders on your computer, that’s currently not possible with Gemini on Googlebooks.

What’s next for Googlebooks?

Even if Googlebooks don’t feel ready now, they carry an air of inevitability. Google has already indicated that it will phase out Chrome OS by 2034, and it has outlined some vague plans for existing Chromebooks to turn into Googlebooks. Presumably that means Googlebooks will move down-market over time to include the kind of lower-end machines that defined the Chromebook business.

In the meantime, the question is whether Google will sufficiently invest in Googlebooks to address their weaknesses, instead of letting the project languish as it so often does (see: Stadia, Reader, its many attempts at social networking). One reason GoogleBooks feel so unpolished right now is because of Google’s hot-and-cold relationship with Android tablets, leading to a lack of app developer buy-in on devices with larger screens. With Google, there’s always a risk that it gets distracted by the next shiny object and leaves important work undone.

You can see how Googlebooks might eventually make sense: Combine a desktop-class Chrome experience with true Android app support, with easily available AI to help with research and automation. Maybe Google will get there someday, but first it needs to get more of the basics right.

Employers, watch out for this trap when you lay people off

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

“We’re at-will. Just let them go.”

Leaders in the U.S. say these words, or something like them, with regularity. Because employment here is generally “at-will,” many assume they can terminate someone for any reason. But that misunderstands what at-will employment means—and what else is at play.

An employment decision doesn’t end when someone walks out the door. Even in at-will employment, every termination is judged in three courts: the court of law, the court of social media, and the court of public opinion. Each one can impact your business more than you realize.

The court of law

Despite what many people believe, at-will is not a magical shield against any legal liability. It doesn’t override other applicable laws, including those prohibiting discrimination, retaliation, or violations of whistleblower protections.

Most employees who leave an organization, even if they were fired, don’t sue their employer. But when they do, and if it gets to a trial, the question often isn’t whether the employer had the right to terminate them. It’s why they did.

Just ask the Society for Human Resource Management, the world’s largest HR association. In December 2025, a federal jury found SHRM liable for race discrimination and retaliation against a former employee. SHRM maintained that it followed appropriate procedures, even after the employee raised concerns of racial discrimination during her employment. But testimony and evidence at trial showed that the same individual who was responsible for investigating the employee’s complaints was also advising leaders on communications with her behind the scenes, and the employee’s termination paperwork had been drafted the same day she reported retaliation.

The jury awarded her $11.5 million. And the costs didn’t stop there.

In addition to the jury’s award and its own legal expenses, just this month, SHRM was ordered to pay more than $534,000 in attorneys’ fees for the plaintiff. Much like at-will employment, many leaders assume insurance covers everything. But SHRM’s insurer has sued to avoid paying $10 million of the verdict.

SHRM is appealing the decision. But regardless of the final outcome, it’s a reminder that employment decisions can carry significant costs in money, time, and reputation.

If it can happen to the organization that describes itself as “the global authority on work, workers, and the workplace,” it can happen to any employer.

The court of social media

Unlike a courtroom, social media doesn’t have rules of evidence or filing deadlines. There are headlines, screenshots, recordings, and sections that can shape public opinion in hours.

For many employees, a lawsuit feels intimidating and costly. Posting online does not. It feels—and is—accessible. A recorded layoff call, a screenshot of a termination email, or a “Day in the Life of Getting Fired from My Corporate Job” TikTok can reach more people in an hour than your organization reaches on LinkedIn all year.

By the time leaders start asking whether a recording is legal, it’s too late. People have made their conclusions, and continue to do so when you respond. While your lawyer may advise, “We don’t comment on personnel matters,” the public isn’t judging your legal defense. They’re evaluating whether your response reflects the values your organization claims to stand for.

After SHRM’s verdict, its CEO called it “just a blip in the history of SHRM.” That one word, “blip,” made headlines on its own.

Because in the court of social media, perception often shapes reality.

The court of public opinion

This court may have the smallest reach, but the longest memory.

One thing I learned as an employment lawyer and HR leader is that every employee is a walking billboard for your organization. People talk—to former colleagues, to friends, and to candidates, directly and on Glassdoor. And those people pay attention to what’s said, especially when a situation feels unfair.

The story spreads through their personal and professional communities. And there’s no formal appeal process in this court. Just your reputation is decided, one conversation at a time.

Use the 3-court test

None of this means that you can’t let someone go. You can, and sometimes that’s the best path, for your business and even that person. But it means that an employment decision—especially a termination—should be one that you’re comfortable defending in all three courts. Before you move forward, pause and ask:

  • Would it hold up in court? Look at the record: Did this person recently complain or take protected leave? Look at consistency: Are there others who’ve performed the same, or worse, and what happened to them? If a performance review says “Exceeds Expectations” but the termination notice says “Poor Performance,” that inconsistency won’t just concern the employee. It can raise real questions for a jury as well.
  • Would it survive a headline? Explain the decision in one sentence, as if someone who’s a complete stranger to your organization would read it. The decisions that are hardest to explain can be the ones most likely to go viral.
  • What will the team think? Not what an official statement says—what others will actually believe. Especially if the person leaving was a respected or vocal team member.

When you have doubts in any of these three areas, slow down and take a closer look.

When HR or Legal raises concerns, they’re usually not trying to create delays. They’re helping ensure the organization follows a process that is fair, defensible, and consistent.

The difference can be significant: between a fair process and Exhibit A.

A few extra days spent asking hard questions may prevent years of litigation, reputational damage, or rebuilding trust. Because at-will tells you what you’re allowed to do. It doesn’t tell you what you should do.

So, the next time someone in the room says, “We’re at-will,” ask the follow-up: “But would we win in all three courts?”

Which states plan to opt into the federal school choice program?

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

States have until Jan. 1, 2027, to tell the IRS they will join the Education Freedom Tax Credit program.

More than half the states have indicated to the IRS that they will participate in the new federal Education Freedom Tax Credit program that begins Jan. 1 and is projected to direct billions of dollars in taxpayer donations to private and public school services through student scholarships.

Even though rules for the program are not yet finalized by the U.S. Department of the Treasury and the IRS, states and the scholarship-granting organizations that will manage the scholarships can rely on proposed and temporary guidance as they prepare for the program's launch.

Only states that opt into the Republican-led program can participate, and in addition to the 30 states that have formally told the IRS they are interested, at least two other states — New York and New Mexico — have publicly announced they will also take part in the first national school choice program.

The midterm elections saw five state governorships — Georgia, Iowa, Nevada, Ohio and Texas — flip from Republican to Democrat and some state legislative chambers also gain Democratic majorities. This blue wave could affect which states ultimately participate in the federal school choice program.

States must inform the IRS by Jan. 1 of their election into the program and finalize their decision to participate by Feb. 15 with their submission of approved scholarship-granting organizations. Those SGOs will manage and distribute student scholarships.

Anthropic enters student AI privacy agreement with teachers unions

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

AFT President Randi Weingarten said she hopes the safeguards for the tech will one day be “embedded in law.”

In September, AFT and UFT announced Microsoft had signed onto the AI safety and privacy agreement.

The AI Safety and Privacy Standard for Schools bars tech companies from using student and educator data to train AI models and from selling or using that data for advertising purposes. It also requires those companies to implement strong security protections and to design their tools to avoid harmful or manipulative experiences for students.

The unions’ agreement with Microsoft went into effect Nov. 1, and any school district nationwide that wants to add the AI safety and privacy standards to their new or current contracts with Microsoft can do so without being required to renew or renegotiate.

The Tuesday agreement with Anthropic formalizes that company’s own commitment to an agreement drafted by the unions in September.

AI can only “protect and enhance” teachers’ work “if teachers, students and school systems trust us,” Elizabeth Kelly, head of beneficial deployments at Anthropic, said in a Nov. 10 statement. “These agreements provide a foundation for that trust.”

Randi Weingarten, president of AFT, said in a statement that the agreement is "one step closer to an industry standard" now that Anthropic, a major player in the AI space, has joined.

“We said repeatedly that the guardrails for AI are just as important as any training we were doing for AI,” said Weingarten in the statement, adding that eventually the union hopes a standard will be "embedded in law."

“In the almost total absence of federal and state AI regulation, we want to make certain no teacher or parent ever has to choose between AI tools and the safety and privacy of their children," said Weingarten.

Michael Mulgrew, president of the United Federation of Teachers in New York City, said in a statement, "Ideally, the federal government would step in, but since it hasn’t, teachers unions are working to hold AI companies accountable."

The move comes as school systems nationwide have pushed back on the fast-growing technology.

In September, New York City — home to the nation's largest school system — announced that it was implementing the nation's “most expansive” AI moratorium for students.

The one-year pause prohibits student-facing AI tools for New York City Public Schools students in 2-K, a preschool program for 2-year-olds, through 8th grade during the 2026-27 school year. The temporary AI ban is expected to impact 600,000 students — or two-thirds of NYC’s public school enrollment.

The ban did not apply to teachers' use of AI.

That same month, Los Angeles Unified School District also announced it would restrict all students from using AI tools for the 2026-27 school year.

The Democratic Party gained power across the country in the midterms. Now what?

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Newly elected Democratic U.S. Senators walk toward an orientation meeting inside the U.S. Capitol on Nov. 09, 2026 in Washington, DC. From left to right are Sen.-elect Juliana Stratton (D-IL), Sen.-elect Annie Andrews (D-SC), Sen.-elect Peggy Flanagan (D-MN), Sen.-elect James Talarico (D-TX), Sen.-elect Josh Turek (D-IA), and Sen.-elect Troy Jackson (D-ME). Win McNamee/Getty Images hide caption

Two years ago, voters sent Democrats a message when they ousted them from power in Washington. At first glance, last week's midterm results sent a new one.

Democrats will enter the next Congress with one of the largest majorities in a generation, flipping dozens of House districts and at least seven Senate seats in mainly Trump-won communities. The party now controls a majority of governorships, and gained control of several additional state legislative chambers.

"I think one of the big surprises for me on Tuesday night was just how deep and wide our election results were," Democratic National Committee Chairman Ken Martin said in an interview with NPR. "This was the biggest election cycle for Democrats in the history of our party."

Martin, who has faced criticism from some inside the party throughout his term as chairman, said that the stunning Democratic victories across the country were the result of his party's focus on issues like affordability and ending the war in Iran.

He said the midterms were bigger than politics and bigger than party, and acknowledged the results were just as much about serving as a "massive repudiation of Donald Trump and the MAGA Trumpers and the Republican Party." "I think it's an admonition for Democrats, for sure: Now that we're in power, we have to deliver," he said. "But it was a complete message to Republicans. You had the ability to help us and you did nothing."

With an expanded Congressional majority and a lame duck president, one of the frontlines for figuring out if the Democratic party's power will hold will be in the South, Martin said.

"Six of the 10 fastest growing parts of the country right now are below the Mason-Dixon line, and 40% of the U.S. population is going to live in the South after the next Census," he said. "As we go into 2032 and there's a realignment with the reapportionment, states like Minnesota, Michigan, Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, New York, Rhode Island, Illinois and California are all going to lose electoral votes and congressional votes to the South."

Last week's midterms brought the region's power – especially with Black voters – more squarely into focus. Turbocharged Black voter turnout helped elect the country's first Black female governor in Georgia and may elect the first Black U.S. Senator from Mississippi since Reconstruction.

A slew of Republican-led states seeking to redraw House maps for partisan advantage backfired, as Democrats gained several seats across the region.

Georgia Democratic Sen. Raphael Warnock said in an interview with NPR that Democratic victories in 2026 were a "proof of concept" that the South is central to the future of American politics.

House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries (D-NY) speaks during a press conference on Nov. 4, 2026 in Washington, DC. Jeffries is expected to be the next Speaker of the House, the first Black speaker in history. Kevin Dietsch/Getty Images hide caption

"You saw a multiracial coalition of voters stand up on Tuesday night beyond anything I had even hoped for, and they offered a silent rebuke of where we are now," he told NPR. "It's now up to us to provide the leadership, even as we listen to people, and point the way forward into how we build an economy that works for everyday working families."

Warnock said Democrats' direction in the next two years should aim to serve as a cure for the last decade of Trump-dominated politics that has been defined more as top versus bottom rather than left versus right.

"Ordinary people have had their hopes dashed time and time again, cycle after cycle, across Republican and Democratic administrations," he said. "They've seen the widening chasm between the haves and the have nots."

He called the midterms a "moral moment" that will help determine what kind of nation America wants to be.

In the early days after the 2026 elections, a look ahead to the 2028 presidential race finds both parties with the potential to see crowded fields of candidates in several new lanes reacting to signals sent by the electorate.

That includes Warnock, who will be up for reelection in 2028 and is among names floated as a presidential contender, along with former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg, California Rep. Ro Khanna, Illinois Gov. JB Pritzker, Kentucky Gov. Andy Beshear, former Vice President Kamala Harris and others who have spent significant time in the South campaigning for other Democrats.

As new members of Congress descend on Washington for their orientations, competing visions for Democratic governance are on display with postmortem event panels called "What Worked" and "What Just Happened?" and event names like "What's Next" as party strategists argue online about which wing of the party was the most successful.

2026 victories also scramble the traditional map of swing states, Martin said, suggesting that Democrats would start off competitive in places like Florida and Texas and as favorites to win the White House.

"Texas is no longer a red state. It is a blue state. They didn't even go from red to purple to blue, which is a normal sort of progression," he said. "If Texas is now a blue state, and you look at Georgia and you look at other parts of the South, how do the Republicans get to 270 votes?"

Tough-tackling Schlager proves his worth for Forest

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Xaver Schlager has made more tackles than any other player in the Premier League and it helped Nottingham Forest beat Manchester City. With insight from his coaches past and present, Adam Bate examines why the unsung Austrian is quietly impressive...

Football Features Writer @ghostgoal

Xaver Schlager has been quietly impressive for Nottingham Forest this season but what was missing was that big moment. It came in the 3-1 win over Manchester City on Saturday when he dispossessed Elliot Anderson for Liam Delap's first goal.

The free transfer from Leipzig nipped in ahead of the £116m man that he has replaced in Forest's midfield and set Delap on the way to doubling the lead. Schlager is no Anderson but he has his own qualities and they made the difference at the weekend.

"It was a top team performance and it was a top performance by Xaver Schlager," said Forest boss Oliver Glasner in the press conference afterwards. He pointed out that "it must have been a fantastic performance" because City's midfielders are world-class.

Please use Chrome browser for a more accessible video player

Schlager was better. "He is so smart, anticipating and getting balls, one of the most for interceptions in the league. And he was physically so strong in his 50-50 balls. He always stayed on his feet and won these balls, because he can then play forward."

In fact, despite only coming on as a late substitute in Forest's opening defeat at home to Leeds, Schlager has made more tackles than any other player in the Premier League this season. It is a good illustration of the combative style that is his trademark.

Not every supporter has been enamoured by Schlager and perhaps that is inevitable given the team's underwhelming start to the season. When searching for explanations, new arrivals come under scrutiny and the 29-year-old Austrian is not one for the future.

But he has had a fine career. Before Leipzig, he worked with Glasner at Wolfsburg, having emerged at Red Bull Salzburg before that. Oscar Garcia spotted him in their feeder team Liefering, another of Glasner's former clubs, and gave him his senior debut.

"He had very good potential," Oscar tells Sky Sports. "Some players in midfield left and we wanted to give him the chance to play. In the structure of Red Bull there were other players ahead of him but we saw that he was a player who could do almost everything."

Not that the self-deprecating Schlager would admit that. Speaking in the summer, he acknowledged his limitations, revealing that "you will not see me too much" as he keeps the game simple. "I do the dirty things you do not recognise but someone has to do it."

Aside from his standout moment that was impossible to miss, that was exactly what he did in the win over City. In what was a particularly intense game, Schlager was the only outfield player on either side to win possession of the ball more times than he lost it.

As a result, the player who in that amusing introduction to the fans admitted that he was "not the quickest" and "not as skilful as some" is actually crucial. Maybe this was the key line? "The most important thing is I want to make my teammates around me better."

James McAtee, Schlager's partner in midfield for much of the season so far, is enjoying the best form of his Premier League career. Ryan Yates took the plaudits on his return to the starting line-up on Saturday. Schlager is a facilitator, a true team player.

Speaking to his former coach Oscar about him, he remains a huge admirer. "In midfield, he can have the ball, he can make the last pass, he can play with the left, he can play with the right, and he can recover the ball very well when the team is gegenpressing."

The passing aspect is not obvious but the statistics show that while his pass completion rate is 84.5 per cent, his expected completion rate is just 77.6 per cent given the difficulty of those passes under pressure. He is in the top 20 for overperformance on this metric.

In other words, Xaver Schlager is tidy on the ball and working incredibly hard off it. On Saturday, he ran 11.92 kilometres, more than anyone else on the pitch. He is not Elliot Anderson. But he is contributing. That big moment against City highlighted it perfectly.

‘The Odyssey’ Has Delivered Record Profits for IMAX

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

‘The Odyssey’ Has Delivered Record Profits for IMAX

IMAX reported its strongest quarterly financial performance in the company’s history last week, with record box office, revenue, and profits for the third quarter of 2026.

Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey drove the results, becoming the highest-grossing IMAX release of all time.

THR reports that the company generated $712 million in global box office during the quarter, a 90% increase year over year and a significant rise from the $372 million recorded during the same period last year.

The Odyssey accounted for more than $550 million of that total in worldwide IMAX box office, contributing more than 30% of the film’s overall theatrical earnings. Its success was supported by premium screenings, including 70mm presentations that attracted audiences to IMAX locations.

Revenue increased 64% to $175 million, compared with $106.7 million in the third quarter of 2025. Net income climbed to $117 million, up from $22.6 million a year earlier.

“These results demonstrate the significant operating leverage and opportunity as we further expand the network and grow Imax box office,” says IMAX CFO Natasha Fernandes.

Content solutions revenue increased 110% to $94 million, reflecting the rise in global box office, while technology products and services revenue grew 28% to $77 million.

IMAX CEO Rich Gelfond says the results represented a broader shift in audience demand for theatrical experiences.

“What IMAX achieved goes beyond simply a ‘great quarter’ and transcends the many records we shattered in the period. What we believe we are witnessing is an awakening to the power and potential of our platform to impact global culture, unlock new commercial opportunities, and inspire a new level of demand and awe in audiences around the world,” he says, per THR.

“Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey is a historic cinematic achievement crafted entirely with IMAX technology and its record-breaking success marks a new era of opportunity for our company. The record margins we generated in the quarter demonstrate the unique strength of our model, and the growing value of scarce events and experiences in an increasingly AI-driven world underscore a powerful opportunity for our business, as we look ahead to a remarkably strong slate over the next several years.”

The company expects to significantly exceed its previous full-year 2026 box office guidance of $1.4 billion, which would itself represent a record. Its expansion programme is also continuing, with more than 100 agreements for IMAX systems signed worldwide so far this year. Approximately 65% of those agreements involve new locations.

THR notes that IMAX is also exploring new auditorium formats, including smaller premium venues designed to offer audiences an immersive viewing experience through larger aspect ratios, greater depth of view and improved sound. Additionally, the company is considering adding between 10 and 15 new 70mm film locations in response to demand and the upcoming film slate.
