Why You Should Never Underestimate Mule Deer

If you head north and east out of Salt Lake City toward the Wyoming state line you end up in some of the most stunning country in the United States. At first glance, it looks like a semi-arid range of mountains with the exposed shelves of red rock, patches of sage, and grassy benches typical of the Intermountain West at this latitude.

But once you get past the view from the winding highways that snake through this land and gain some elevation on the dirt roads that take you back to more remote areas, you’ll notice something else. These mountains give birth to a surprising number of springs, providing water that ends up supporting a vast amount of life.

The wet country reveals itself in the stands of aspen that grow thick in places you wouldn’t expect, with waist-high grass that contrasts with the blanket of drought-resistant scrub oak that covers much of the terrain.

This almost contradictory mix of moisture, cover, food, and remoteness provides a recipe for great elk and mule deer, and is the reason I’ll return to this country whenever I can, as happened a few weeks ago this fall. I was there on an invitation from Winchester Ammunition to try its newest offering, Supreme Long Range, on my favorite North American game.

There are many reasons mule deer have such a strong claim on my psyche. I love the wild places they call home. The hours spent glassing them is about the most rewarding way to pass time. And I find it infinitely fascinating to watch and study their behavior.

You see, mule deer are a study in contradiction, too. Are their numbers in decline? In many places, without a doubt. They’ve certainly struggled to maintain their hold in much of their traditional range. Housing and energy development disrupt their winter grounds and migration routes. Diseases like CWD have taken their toll, especially with older bucks. We know that blossoming herds of elk and whitetails can outcompete mulies for food and water, putting more pressure on their numbers.

But they are also resilient and, in some areas, thrive. Good numbers of mule deer live in suburban areas throughout the mountain west. And you can find them in some of the most inhospitable desert regions of North America, and tucked away in other unlikely places.

Though they can seem more docile than their high-strung white-tailed cousins, I have witnessed mule deer put on incredible displays of strength, aggression, and John-Wick-caliber willpower — another contradiction.

Years ago in Colorado, I watched a hunter fail to follow up his shot on a mule deer. The high-shoulder shot dropped the buck where it stood, knocking it senseless. The hunter started celebrating, thinking he had killed it, and when the deer regained its feet the hunter wasn’t ready — and the buck disappeared into the alders. I helped track the deer for several hours through the snow, following a substantial blood trail. We kept going as long as we could after dark but eventually had to call it quits.

The hunter left for the airport and I kept looking for the deer with my good friend Cody while trying to fill my own tag. Two days later, Cody and I spotted the buck tending his does, despite the grievous wound in his upper chest and one ruined leg. He was 500 yards away, skylined on the ridge, and I couldn’t get a shot off before he fled over the mountain.

There was no doubt in my mind the buck was going to die from that injury, but procreation was his sole preoccupation. Despite the obvious pain, and the imminent certainty of death, he would not be deterred.

That buck spurned death, but I’ve also seen mule deer inflict it. I was hunting in southeast Montana a few years back in mid-November when the rut was in full swing. Bucks were bird-dogging does, scent-checking the ladies, cruising in search of mates, and squaring up against potential rivals.

Usually when two bucks face off they will posture a bit and, after sizing each other up, one will back off before things get too heated. Sometimes they will fight — those antlers aren’t just for decoration — and the vanquished male will make his excuses to be elsewhere.

In this case, the smaller of the deer lost his footing during the scuffle and fell back, rolling on his side. The larger buck lowered his head and drove his antlers into the other’s exposed belly.

The deer on his side flailed his legs in a desperate attempt to get up and escape, but through my binocular I could see the spreading circle of red in the snow as the dominant buck continued the savage attack, never letting up.

After several minutes — it felt like an eternity from my vantage — the buck’s fury was spent and he lifted his head and walked off as the other deer lay still, surrounded by his own gore.

I relate this because it is one thing to enjoy hunting, which I do, but it is another to, on occasion, experience the wild at its most raw and profound.

So, when I got this latest opportunity to test my legs and lungs in the high-country in Utah, I had hopes of encountering a mature buck, and perhaps more.

I was joined on the trip by Kyle Masinelli, the vice president of manufacturing operations at Winchester Ammunition, and a friend and work colleague of mine, Ed Friedman, the editor-in-chief of American Rifleman. Kyle was one of the architects of Winchester Supreme Long Range and a great source of information about it.

As the name implies, Supreme Long Range (SLR for short) is geared toward big-game hunting where longer shots might be in the offing, which is often the case when mule deer hunting. This new ammo uses what Winchester calls their “BC Max” bullet, which is a heavy-for-caliber cup-and-core design with an oversized polymer tip to initiate rapid expansion.

Over the summer, I shot several boxes of this new ammunition, chambered in 6.5 Creedmoor, with excellent results in terms of its precision and consistency.

I gathered 20 shots of data with three different rifles, shooting four 5-shot groups with each. Those twelve 5-shot groups ranged in size from .53 inches to 1.10 inches. The overall average group size was .818 inches.

In a curious coincidence, the mean radius value for each of the three rifles was .32 inches, which is very good for hunting ammunition.

The three rifles I used to gather the data were Proof’s MTR Elevation FDX, which won an Editor’s Choice for best hunting rifle of the year; Seekins Precision’s latest PH3 NRL Hunter, which won an Editor’s Choice for best crossover rifle; and a Seekins Precision SIC Short Action, which won’t be officially launched until early 2027.

For the hunt itself, I went a different route, taking a Winchester XPR chambered in 6.8 Western. The XPR has been a perennial choice of ours for an entry-level hunting rifle. It is a no-frills bolt-action that delivers good performance. In its most basic black synthetic stock trim it can be had for less than $600 new.

While I’ve shot plenty of XPRs over the years, I hadn’t had as much time with the 6.8 Western. I shot this souped-up .270 Winchester a bit when it was first rolled out in 2021, but had never done a deep dive on the cartridge or taken it hunting.

I didn’t have as much trigger time with the XPR and the ammunition as I would have liked before rolling into camp, but I did establish a solid zero prior to making the drive from Montana to Utah and knew that the 175-grain 6.8mm SLR bullets were exiting my 22-inch barrel right at 2,700 fps.

We were hunting with the R&K Hunting Company, which has a base camp in Coalville, Utah, and I confirmed my dope on targets they had there. With three shots, I checked my point of impact at 200, 500, and 700 yards, the bullets ringing the center of the steel each time.

Our hunting camp was another hour’s drive into the mountains, most of it off-road, and much of it pitted with craters like small bomb impacts.

But the camp itself was an alpine Shangri-La. The main lodge was a cozy wood structure where the hunters stayed, with a series of small cabins for the guides, and other out buildings, all overlooking a post-card scene of a small lake surrounded by golden-leafed quakies and distant mountains.

Kyle and I spent the first day with our guides, two young brothers named Keston and Kandon Mosher, glassing the heights. In the afternoon, we spotted two excellent bucks engaged in pre-rut posturing.

From nearly a mile away we watched the bucks through our optics. One of the bucks appeared to have a larger, darker body and a heavier rack. The other buck was mature as well, seemingly younger, but with larger antlers. Both were stunning.

We watched them circle each other a bit in a small clearing between stands of alders. We needed to move quickly, because the cover around them was thick, it was getting late, and we were losing light.

We grabbed our packs and guns and hoofed up the mountain as fast as we could. It took about half an hour of hard climbing to get to the knob we wanted to set up on. That put us within 800 yards of where we’d last seen the deer.

Assuming we spotted them again, we’d make a final plan. But that didn’t happen. We glassed the ridge until dark. We could see many of the other deer we’d been watching, but the bucks had vanished. As has happened to me so many times while going after big mule deer, they’d given us the slip.

We returned to the base of that mountain ahead of sunrise, peering into the cover as the light came up. With each passing minute, we took in more ground.

The mountain was actually a long, fairly flat ridge running north to south that topped out at 9,200 feet, and encompassed five separate large drainages. From end-to-end it spanned over three miles. The middle section seemed to hold the most animals, but we frequently spotted elk and deer at the northern and southern edges too.

Early on, Keston caught a flash of antler in an alder thicket not far from the top of the ridge. We all trained our optics on the spot and after a bit the lighter-colored buck stepped into the open. We easily identified him based on his coloration and the picture-perfect four-point frame he carried above his head.

He was feeding on his own about 350 yards from where we’d seen him the previous afternoon. About 30 yards below him was a doe and two fawns, also munching on grass. The other big buck was nowhere to be seen.

There was no way to get to him from below. Even if we could approach from that direction without busting him he’d be out of sight until we stepped into the same clearing. That clearly wouldn’t work. Plus it would take well over an hour and a half to hike up there at which point he’d certainly be gone even if we were as stealthy as ninjas.

Instead, we hiked back down to our side-by-side and took a long looping route to the top of the ridge. From this new vantage, the terrain presented more of a challenge than I would have guessed. Looking down, I could see benches and pockets of cover that were invisible from lower down. This included small natural ponds and abundant feed. An animal could move through that all day and never reveal itself to a hunter glassing from below.

We parked well away from where we saw the buck last and snuck along the spine until we were above his feeding spot. We crept toward the edge and peered down the slope.

At this point the wind shifted so it was coming from directly behind us. We saw the doe and her fawns scoot out of the pocket, having clearly caught our scent.

But the buck should have been between us and them, and if he followed a similar escape route, I would get a shot. We held tight for a quarter hour but saw no sign of him. He’d obviously slipped away in a safer, more hidden direction.

I wasn’t surprised. Mule deer get a bad rap for acting dumb during the rut. And it’s true that some bucks seem to throw caution to the wind when love is in the air. But in my experience I’ve seen more evidence of their clever and well-honed survival instincts preserving them from eager hunters, especially when it comes to truly mature bucks.

That afternoon we returned to the base of the ridge. We set up beneath the knob from the previous night because we didn’t want to overcommit and potentially bust the bucks in case they had moved lower down the mountain.

It wasn’t long before we spotted a new buck working its way down a drainage to our right. He was a handsome 4×4 with eyeguards, and Kyle wanted to take him.

He set his .300 Win. Mag. in a tripod and shot the buck at 350 yards from a kneeling position. The bullet was perfectly placed and the buck ran a few steps and tumbled over. Kyle, Keston, and Kandon (which sounds not unlike a law firm) took off to recover the buck while I stayed behind to keep glassing.

By the time Keston returned we had about an hour left of shooting light. As luck would have it, we caught sight of the light-colored deer trying to sneak off to the south.

With the pressure we’d applied to the north half of the ridge that morning and afternoon (Kyle’s deer was in the same drainage as the bench where we spotted the buck after first light) I wasn’t surprised to see the older deer trying to put some distance between us and him.

Once again it was a race against the falling light. Keston and I took off up the mountain to get within range before dark.

We hiked for 20 minutes to an exposed point that gave us a good view. Keston found the deer again, walking to our left and down toward a thick stand of aspen in a steep draw, where he was about to disappear.

I ranged the deer at 616 yards and got prone, deploying my bipod and setting the butt of the XPR on my shooting bag. The wind, which had been howling earlier in the day, was calming down, but still needed to be accounted for.

I dialed elevation into my scope, held left .4 mil for the wind, and broke the shot. The deer dropped as if poleaxed, kicked a bit, and then lay still. I watched him through my scope with a fresh round chambered until I was sure he was dead.

By the time we got to the vicinity of the deer it was pitch black. Finding an animal under these conditions can be difficult. And while technology is often a two-edged affairm in this case it was a godsend.

For the last year, I’ve been using Leupold’s BX-6 rangefinding binocular in shooting competitions and while hunting. I won’t go into all its virtues here, but one feature that’s a game changer is its ability to range a spot and have it drop a pin into a map, like onX Hunt.

That pin led us right to the buck, saving us who knows how much time stumbling around the dark.

Every hunting bullet has the same end goal, which is to inflict massive tissue damage so that the animal quickly bleeds out, shutting the central nervous system down. (Alternatively, a bullet can strike the CNS directly, with a brain or spinal shot, killing the animal that way.)

How a bullet accomplishes this depends heavily on its construction. Bonded bullets, for instance, are meant to mushroom to a degree, while retaining weight and penetrating deeply.

The BC Max bullet takes a different approach. The large polymer tip on the projectile initiated quick and violent bullet upset. The entrance hole, in the upper portion of the chest, was about two inches across and busted two ribs on the way in.

I could tell the bullet fragmented significantly based on three things: that large entrance wound, the massive tissue damage observed on the deer’s lungs, and the fact that the bullet didn’t penetrate the off side of the ribcage.

We quartered the deer and took backstraps, loins, and neck meat without gutting it, so I didn’t try to recover what was left of the bullet, though I doubt it would have been much.

Of all the big mule deer I’ve shot over my career, this one comes closest to the Platonic ideal. He’s a large, clean four-point, with deep, nearly identical, forks front and back, has a tall boxy frame, carries good mass throughout his rack, and has nice eye guards to boot. If one were to draw an idealized symmetrical mule deer, it would look a lot like this one.

I never take an animal like this for granted. He was a wily deer and had given us the slip a couple times already, and was about to make it a third. And, of course, there was his older cousin, that darker, heavier brute, who only showed himself once.

We were the last deer hunters on that mountain this season. And my fingers are crossed that the other big buck, and his kin, make it through the winter.

I don’t say that because I expect to hunt that spot again. There’s very little chance that will happen. But I do hope some other hunter, who loves these animals the way I do, at least gets to encounter that buck and possibly take him home.

Children’s brain responses to food may help predict later fat gain

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Children who ate more ultra-processed foods showed stronger brain responses to food pictures, which were linked to greater body fat gain later.

Breakfast cereal, packaged bread and instant soup get blamed for childhood weight gain because of what’s in them.

A new study links children’s ultra-processed food intake to how their brains respond to pictures of food.

A year later, the children with stronger reactions had gained more body fat. Every child was a healthy weight when the study began, and this kind of study can show a link but not prove a cause.

Dr. Kathleen Page, a physician and professor of medicine and pediatrics at the Keck School of Medicine of USC, is the study’s senior author, and Jasmin Alves is the first author.

“Children’s brains are still developing, habits are still forming and food preferences are still being made. It’s an important period where the brain is very sensitive to food and the food environment,” Dr. Page said.

The 46 children were 7 to 10 years old and part of the BrainChild study.

That long-running project follows children whose mothers did or did not have diabetes during pregnancy, and it recruits them through Kaiser Permanente Southern California.

Each child had a BMI below the 85th percentile for their age and sex.

On two separate days, each child and a parent walked trained staff through everything the child had eaten in the previous 24 hours.

The researchers then sorted every food by how processed it was. On average, ultra-processed foods supplied about 68% of the children’s daily calories, close to the 67% a 2021 analysis found for American kids as a whole.

About a month later, each child lay in an MRI scanner after a 12-hour fast and then a glucose drink.

The scanner recorded how active two deep brain areas were during the food photos.

Both areas are part of a structure called the striatum, which handles motivation, learning and reward.

The ventral striatum is involved in anticipating and experiencing rewards. The dorsal striatum helps link cues with learned actions and habits.

Children who got more of their calories from ultra-processed foods had stronger dorsal striatum responses to the food photos.

The ventral striatum showed no clear pattern. The association with dorsal striatum responses remained after accounting for each child’s age, sex, body size and whether their mother had diabetes during pregnancy.

In an interview with Earth.com, Dr. Page emphasized that the association held even after the researchers accounted for total calorie intake, suggesting that how much children ate did not fully explain the pattern.

The children also consumed a standardized drink before their scans, “so the stronger response to food cues wasn’t simply due to hunger,” she said.

Rodent studies had already shown that a steady diet of sugar-rich food changes how the dorsal striatum works.

No earlier study had looked directly at how much ultra-processed food people habitually eat and how their striatum responds, the authors wrote.

The children came back for measurements roughly a year later, with an average gap of about 15 months.

Their BMI had risen by one point on average, their body fat by close to one percentage point, and their waists by about 1.5 inches (3.9 centimeters).

Children whose dorsal striatum had reacted more strongly to the food photos gained more in BMI and body fat. Eating more ultra-processed food was linked to a bigger waist a year later.

Ultra-processed food intake’s links to BMI and body fat were weak enough that chance could explain them.

The team then asked how much of the link between diet and fat gain involved the brain.

Once the dorsal striatum response was in the model, the association between ultra-processed food and body fat gain shrank by 44%, and the one with BMI by 31%.

Dr. Page said the result does more than repeat that these foods are tied to weight gain, because it points to a pathway in the brain.

“It actually gives us insight into the neurobiological underpinnings by which these foods may be linked to body composition over time.”

What stood out most to Dr. Page was that every child was a healthy weight when the study began.

“This suggests that the link between ultra-processed foods and the brain’s response to food cues may be detectable before obesity develops, rather than simply resulting from excess weight,” she told Earth.com.

Added sugar is in most ultra-processed foods, so the team checked whether sugar was driving the result.

Children who ate more added sugar tended to have stronger striatum responses too, but the pattern wasn’t clear enough to rule out chance.

When the team accounted for added sugar, the link between ultra-processed food and the dorsal striatum weakened and was no longer statistically clear.

Still, the authors wrote that sugar is unlikely to be the whole story.

Forty-six children is a small group, and the authors wrote that this limited their power to detect modest effects. Brain scans of this kind are also hard to reproduce in small groups.

Each child was scanned only once, so no one knows yet whether the stronger responses persist or fade as children get older.

An observational study like this one can’t say which came first. Children whose reward circuits already react more strongly to food may be the ones who reach for ultra-processed foods, rather than the other way around.

And because every child started at a healthy weight, the pattern may look different in children who already carry extra weight.

The research team said the burden shouldn’t fall only on families and children, because these foods are often the most accessible, affordable and convenient ones available.

“Changing the food environment to help people access healthier, more minimally processed foods is an important part of the solution,” Dr. Page noted.

For families in the meantime, Dr. Page said the practical goal is to shift the overall diet toward more minimally processed foods. “Parents should not feel that they have to eliminate every packaged food.”

Her examples were water instead of soda and fruit instead of fruit juice or fruit snacks. The aim is more vegetables, beans, nuts, eggs and other foods closer to their original form.

One way to test whether the diet is driving the brain response is to change the diet.

“A future intervention study could test whether replacing ultra-processed foods with mostly minimally processed foods changes how children’s brains respond to food cues and helps prevent excess body-fat gain,” Dr. Page said.

Meanwhile, the BrainChild team will keep following these children through puberty and into young adulthood.

Dr. Page said the next step is to see how their diets relate to changes in the developing brain. The team will also look at their risk for obesity over time.

The full study was published in the journal JAMA Network Open.

Scientists Just Challenged a 50-Year-Old Theory About How Evolution Works

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

In bacteria and yeast, a gene’s importance did not predict its mutation rate, challenging the idea that evolution gives vital DNA preferential treatment.

Not all genes are equally important. Some are essential for survival, controlling basic functions such as copying DNA and keeping cells alive. Others can be lost with little or no effect. But does that mean evolution has found a way to shield its most vital genes from potentially harmful mutations?

For decades, scientists have debated whether essential genes are naturally less likely to mutate. Now, researchers at the University of Bath have put that idea to the test, with results that challenge a long-standing evolutionary hypothesis.

Dr. Sofia Radrizzani and Professor Laurence D. Hurst, from the university’s Centre for Evolution, examined spontaneous mutations in the bacterium Escherichia coli (E. coli) and baker’s yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae). Their study, published November 12 in PLOS Biology, found no evidence that essential genes receive extra protection and offers a new explanation for why earlier research may have suggested otherwise.

“We’d expect natural selection to rapidly remove mutations when they occur in more important genes, because these mutations are more likely to be harmful to the organism. This can create the illusion that important genes have fewer mutations when, in reality, some mutations have simply disappeared before we have a chance to analyze them – what you can think of as ‘ghost mutations’,” Hurst said.

To reduce that problem, the team used data from “mutation accumulation” experiments designed to minimize the effects of natural selection. Three separate tests found no evidence that selection had substantially skewed the results.

“This looks like the largest and cleanest sample of mutations assembled to date,” Radrizzani said.

The researchers compared mutation rates with experimental evidence showing what happens when individual genes are removed. Deleting an essential gene kills the cell, while losing other genes has little or no effect. This gave the team a way to test whether genes a cell depends on most were less likely to mutate.

“Analyze the data any which way and we found nothing. In neither species did these key genes have a lower mutation rate,” Hurst said.

The team also assessed importance through the amount of a gene’s protein the cell produces and the strength of natural selection acting on the gene over evolutionary time.

“We found that genes producing more abundant proteins don’t have lower mutation rates. Nor does the way genes evolve over thousands of years predict how frequently they mutate,” Radrizzani said.

These findings address a debate that began in 1972 and resurfaced as recently as 2023. At stake is the relationship between the two steps in the conventional account of evolution. Mutations first generate genetic variation. Natural selection and other forces then act on that variation, favoring some traits over others.

Darwin’s finches, which Charles Darwin encountered on the Galápagos Islands in 1835, offer a familiar example. Random mutations contribute to differences in beak size, allowing birds to use different food sources. When conditions favor particular foods, birds with beaks better suited to eating them are more likely to survive and reproduce. Their traits consequently become more common over generations.

The disputed proposal suggests that a gene’s importance also influences how often mutations arise in it. Vital genes would receive greater protection, while genes where change could help adaptation would mutate more frequently. Examples of the latter include genes in which mutations give bacteria antibiotic resistance or alter birds’ beak dimensions.

There is a theoretical difficulty with that proposal. Gene mutation rates are already so low that reducing them further for individual genes would offer very little benefit, making it difficult for natural selection to favor such a protective system.

“The claim that mutation is slower for important genes is a triumph of hype over substance,” said Radrizzani, who co-wrote the paper as part of her PhD. “It’s a really old suggestion and is reinvented in various forms routinely.”

Genes copied later during DNA replication did tend to mutate more frequently in the team’s analysis, showing that some features of the genome can help explain differences in mutation rates.

“While we see known causes of variation in mutation rates, we found no evidence for the effects that have been proposed as a challenge to the conventional model. None predicted which genes would have high or low mutation rates, and there are certainly no grounds for a shift in paradigm,” Hurst said.

What goes down must go up, though. After reaching the bottom of the valley, the trail immediately twists up towards the airport. I could see the airport from the top of the valley before descending, and it looks barely closer. The sun has come out and is beating down, even this early. This side of the valley is more exposed, with no trees and minimal foliage. The descent actually had a few trees, but it’s clear this ascent is closer to what the rest of the day will look like.

Airport in the Sky taunting me as I climb toward it.

Breakfast “sandie” – eggs, bacon, cheddar, avocado on sourdough.

My only regret is that I never got to meet the airport’s resident black cat.

A lovely blooming cactus flower from along the side of the trail.

The side-blotched lizard, known for the male’s throat colors indicating their mating style.

The Catalina Fox, staring longingly at our food. It looks a little like a stray dog.

Tomorrow is the day I am most nervous about. Somehow, though, I don’t feel too daunted. I feel excited, rejuvenated by my lovely evening. I knew the trail was short, but tomorrow is my last full day on trail. I can’t believe how fast it has flown by.

I crawl into my tent early, ready to get an early start to beat the heat tomorrow. Perhaps tomorrow is the day for my bison sighting!

Crocodiles face a hidden threat that may weaken a vital survival response

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Caimans with more mercury in their blood showed a weaker stress hormone response, raising concerns about how pollution affects their survival.

Mercury pollution may be weakening the stress response in crocodiles and their relatives.

Researchers studying smooth-fronted caimans in South American rainforests found that animals with more mercury in their blood had a weaker stress hormone response.

Gold mining is a major source of mercury pollution in South America. The findings suggest that mercury may interfere with a natural defense system that caimans and other crocodilians depend on to survive in the wild.

Jérémy Lemaire of the University of Vienna led the project with colleagues there and at the University of Veterinary Medicine Vienna.

The researchers studied 20 adult and nearly adult caimans in French Guiana between November 2023 and June 2024.

“Given the overlap of crocodylian habitats with gold mining activities, this is a conservation concern that deserves immediate attention,” Lemaire said.

Since the late 1800s, atmospheric mercury has risen an estimated 300% to 500% above its natural level, mostly from burning fossil fuels.

Small-scale gold mining is a major contributor to this pollution. It produces 38% of the mercury released into the air worldwide, and more than 80% of mercury emissions in South America and sub-Saharan Africa combined.

Caimans live a long time and take in mercury mainly through their food. Over many years, it builds up in their bodies, which makes them a useful measure of how polluted their streams and forests are.

The authors describe mercury as an endocrine-disrupting chemical, meaning one that interferes with the body’s hormones.

Theirs is the first study to measure this hormone response in wild animals living in a tropical forest with high mercury levels, the team wrote.

The researchers studied caimans in different parts of the rainforest. One of the sites was the Nouragues nature reserve.

They caught the animals between 8 and 11 p.m. to account for the natural changes in stress hormone levels throughout the day.

Researchers caught each caiman with a snare pole. They taped its snout shut and laid a wet towel over its eyes, so every animal went through the same experience.

Then they drew blood four times. The first sample came within 3 to 5 minutes of capture to estimate baseline hormone levels. The rest came 15, 30, and 60 minutes after capture.

Red blood cells went to the mercury test. The liquid part of the blood went to a test for corticosterone, the main stress hormone in reptiles and birds.

Mercury in the caimans’ dried red blood cells ranged from about 0.3 to 2 parts per million. The team split the animals into three groups: four with low levels, nine in the middle, and seven with high levels.

At the first blood draw, resting hormone levels were about the same in all three groups. The groups differed only in the one-hour sample.

In the low and middle groups, hormone levels rose over the hour. In the high-mercury group, the levels didn’t change by a statistically significant amount at any point.

At 60 minutes, the high group’s levels were lower than those of both other groups. The high-mercury caimans started with similar hormone levels but showed a smaller increase after capture.

Across all 20 animals, the more mercury a caiman carried, the smaller the difference between its resting level and its level at one hour. Mercury levels accounted for about 40% of the variation in that difference.

The most contaminated caiman’s hormone increase was more than 100 times smaller than that of the least contaminated one.

“A fast, robust stress response is essential for survival in the wild,” Lemaire said.

“We show that even relatively low mercury burdens affect this response in caimans, potentially limiting their ability to cope with predators, extreme weather, human disturbance, and ultimately survive in the wild.”

Stress hormones work by attaching to a protein called the glucocorticoid receptor, which helps activate the body’s stress response.

Mercury can interfere with this process. Studies in male rats found that mercury made it harder for hormones to attach to the receptor.

Computer simulations also suggest that mercury can partly block the hormone’s binding site by attaching to an amino acid called cysteine.

Lemaire’s team found this same amino acid in the receptors of crocodilians and other vertebrates. This could help explain their findings, although they haven’t tested the effect directly in caiman cells.

The caimans had mercury levels similar to those found in other crocodilians worldwide.

Some slender-snouted crocodiles in Gabon, for example, have had blood mercury levels as high as 11 parts per million.

The researchers say the problem may extend beyond areas near gold mines. Even at the levels found in this study, mercury was linked to a weaker stress response.

More than a third of the 26 living crocodilian species are threatened with extinction, according to the IUCN.

Mercury pollution could put them at greater risk if it affects their ability to survive and reproduce.

The effects on wild populations could take years to become noticeable.

Since the late 1800s, crocodilians have gone through about five generations. In comparison, large land animals with generations lasting 5 to 10 years have gone through around 20.

Crocodilians take a long time to produce new generations, which can make changes in their populations difficult to detect.

Researchers still need to find out whether caimans with weaker stress responses are more likely to die or have fewer offspring.

The full study was published in the journal Communications Biology.

Gut bacteria cannot see the sun so they use your meal times to control how your body absorbs nutrients

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Gut bacteria in mice read mealtimes, not daylight, and tell the intestine when to absorb, helping underfed mice grow and overfed ones get fat.

The body clock is supposed to run on light. Sunrise wakes the brain, and the rest of the body falls in line. But the bacteria in the gut live in the dark.

In mice, a common microbe keeps time another way: it reads when food arrives and tells the cells of the intestine to start absorbing.

The signal is a small molecule the bacterium makes while it digests phytate, a compound in grains, nuts and seeds. The molecule switches on genes that pull amino acids and fats out of a meal.

Give it to undernourished young mice and they grow. Give it to mice on a high-fat diet and they get fatter.

Zheng Kuang, an associate professor of biological sciences at Carnegie Mellon University, led the work from his lab. Colleagues at the University of Pittsburgh, UT Southwestern Medical Center and the Quadram Institute in England took part.

All of it was done in mice and in human gut cells grown in a dish.

In an interview with Earth.com, Kuang said what struck him most was that the bacteria convert the timing of a meal into chemistry.

“Their absolute numbers did not need to change substantially across the day – their metabolic activity changed,” he said.

The bacterium is Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, one of the most common residents of the human gut.

Kuang’s team raised mice with no gut microbes at all, then gave some of them this one species and nothing else. They sampled the lining of the small intestine around the clock.

Mice eat at night. In the hours after dark, the colonized mice carried more of the bacterial signal molecule in their gut lining than they did in the morning.

Germ-free mice showed no such rise, and neither did mice given a mutant strain missing the enzyme that breaks down phytate.

The bacteria themselves were no more numerous at night than in the morning, Kuang’s team found. What rose was the activity of that one enzyme.

“We are not claiming these bacteria have their own internal clock,” Kuang told Earth.com. “Rather, they respond to feeding cues and produce molecules called inositol phosphates, which activate a protein called HDAC3 in intestinal cells.”

That protein, HDAC3, works inside the cells lining the intestine and helps decide which genes are on.

The bacterial molecule makes it grip a partner protein more tightly, and the pair then turns on a set of transporter genes that carry nutrients across the gut wall.

In colonized mice, 14 amino acids and 14 fatty acids rose in the blood during the feeding hours, while in germ-free mice they stayed low.

Of 248 transporter genes the team tracked, 122 cycled more strongly with the bacterium present, and most of those handle amino acids, sugars or fats.

The body’s master clock genes kept their normal daily rhythm either way. The bacterium didn’t reset the clock; it strengthened the daily rise and fall of the metabolic genes that follow it.

The team then fed mice only during the day, which is their normal sleeping time. The signal molecule fell and the gut’s daily rhythm in gene activity flattened, though the animals ate no less than before.

Mice allowed to eat only at night looked like mice allowed to eat whenever they liked.

The feeding experiment is the result Kuang pointed to when asked what people should take from the work. “The key message is to align your feeding schedule with the natural day-night cycle – eating during your active, daytime hours,” he said.

Artificial light has pushed human eating into the night, he added, and the study suggests that misalignment disrupts the bacterial signals.

Kuang said there was likely a metabolic cost to that disruption. The feeding experiment itself tracked the signal and the gene rhythms, not the mice’s weight.

Young mice were weaned onto a diet with the usual calories but little protein or fat, a model for childhood malnutrition.

Those given the bacterium grew longer bodies and longer thigh bones than those given the mutant strain. They had heavier organs, more amino acids in their blood and better blood sugar control, and they ate the same amount.

The signal molecule on its own did the same, in mice with and without a normal gut community. In mice bred to lack HDAC3 in their gut cells, neither the bacterium nor the molecule helped, and a drug that blocks HDAC3 blunted the effect too.

In a public dataset of children’s stool samples, the bacterium was among 19 species more common in healthy children than in undernourished ones.

Adult mice fed a high-fat, high-sugar diet for 12 weeks gained more weight with the bacterium than with the mutant strain.

They carried more belly fat and liver fat and had higher blood fats and worse blood sugar handling. They absorbed more fat from food and passed less of it in their stool.

Again, the mice lacking HDAC3 in their gut cells showed none of this, and food intake didn’t differ.

Kuang said that double edge is the finding he most wants understood. “This illustrates why it is too simple to label a bacterium as inherently ‘good’ or ‘bad’ – its effects depend on diet and the metabolic state of the host,” he explained.

The team’s reading is evolutionary. A pathway that extracts the most from every meal was an advantage when food was scarce, and the same pathway can work against a body surrounded by high-fat diets.

Whether this bacterium even lives and works in the human small intestine is an open question. The team also could not prove that the signal molecule in the gut cells came from the bacteria rather than from the cells themselves, which make related molecules.

And phytate intake varies from one diet to another, so the signal’s strength probably does too, the authors wrote.

Whether the same signal shifts in people who eat on a jet-lagged or night-shift schedule is a question the study does not reach.

Asked how soon the bacteria could be tested against malnutrition, Kuang said it is too early to give a reliable timeline for human testing.

The next step, he said, is to look for the pathway in human intestinal samples and in children with undernutrition. Until then, the case that mealtimes talk to the gut through its bacteria rests on mice.

The full study was published online in a publication of the Carnegie Mellon University.

Oral Food Challenge Clears Many Infants Thought to Have Peanut Allergy

— Researchers say biomarkers and clinical history of reaction alone aren't enough for diagnosis

More than a third of infants thought to be allergic to peanuts were not actually allergic, an oral food challenge study showed.

With the aim of treating peanut allergy among babies who reacted to peanut before 1 year of age and had a positive skin prick or blood test, 34.8% who were subject to an oral food challenge (OFC) to set the dose for oral immunotherapy came up negative and could be considered non-allergic by this gold-standard test.

That rate was "far higher than expected," noted Kirsten Perrett, MBBS, PhD, of Murdoch Children's Research Institute at the Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne, Australia, and colleagues in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology: In Practice.

Typically, a history of reaction plus sensitization is considered enough for diagnosis of peanut allergy, and children are sent home with instructions to avoid peanut products. While a majority of children outgrow milk or egg allergies, only about a quarter to a third of children outgrow peanut allergies without treatment by age 6 years.

The current results potentially indicate that some infants have a transient immune response to the allergen that resolves quickly (an average of 6 months between initial reaction and OFC in this study) or doesn't result in ongoing reactions upon further exposure, Perrett and team said.

Determining that early allows reintroduction of peanut in infancy, which may change the trajectory of peanut allergy and reduce the burden of food allergy, independent of an oral immunotherapy program, they added.

OFC "should be routinely considered in infant peanut allergy evaluation regardless of the proposed management strategy (immunotherapy or allergen avoidance)," Perrett and colleagues argued. "Biomarkers and clinical history alone are insufficient to diagnose peanut allergy in this age group."

That hasn't been the case "for a variety of reasons including carer or clinician concerns regarding safety, time, and/or resource constraints, or the belief that an OFC is unnecessary when there is a clear clinical history of reaction and a positive test of sensitization," they wrote.

With regard to safety, their study showed 61 cases of anaphylaxis during the 778 threshold OFCs (7.8%), which is a higher rate than in the conventional use of OFCs for cases in which diagnosis is unclear from clinical history or the allergy is suspected to be outgrown. While that means these OFCs should be performed in centers with appropriate medical support, the trade-off is worthwhile, Perrett and colleagues suggested.

"Accurately confirming allergy via OFC in infants can reduce the burden of allergy for patients, families, and society through misdiagnosis, and before the commencement of OIT [oral immunotherapy], it can help avoid unnecessary treatment," they pointed out.

The Allergy Development to an Accelerated Pathway to Tolerance (ADAPT) OIT program was a partnership with 10 major pediatric hospitals in five states across Australia to develop and implement the first standardized nationwide model of care for food allergy treatment.

The program was to enroll all infants presenting with a clinical history of an immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated allergic reaction to peanut before age 12 months and evidence of sensitization from a peanut skin prick test wheal of at least 3 mm or a peanut-specific IgE or Ara h 2-specific IgE of at least 0.35 kUA/L.

The 59 infants whose reaction to peanut had been anaphylaxis (Consortium for Food Allergy Research grade 3) could go straight to oral immunotherapy. Notably, 14 of the 29 patients in this group (48.3%) who had a threshold OFC did not react and were deemed tolerant to peanut.

Babies with an initial grade 1 to 2 reaction to peanut were to undergo OFC to peanut before commencing OIT both to confirm the diagnosis of peanut allergy and to determine peanut reaction threshold to inform the OIT starting dose. The OFC used a commercially available peanut flour (PB2).

Median age at OFC was 13 months, and it took a median 3 months from the time of initial reaction to the first allergy assessment in the allergy clinics.

Skin prick and blood test results were both significantly linked to a positive OFC (P<0.001) but did not predict the reaction threshold or severity.

Pickle Juice Vs Buttermilk: Which Marinade Is Best For Fried Chicken?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

When it comes to down-home Southern cooking, buttermilk and pickles both have a prominent place thanks to their flavors and how they affect the chemistry of cooking. Todd Johnson, Proprietor of Strips Chicken & Brewing, insists that both have a place in fried chicken — although he asserts that buttermilk is the better all-around choice.

"If you're going to marinate your chicken, buttermilk wins," Johnson told Food Republic. "It's gently acidic, so it softens the surface of the chicken without turning it mushy. It's also thick enough to leave a tacky layer that flour clings to. That's the foundation of a craggy, crunchy crust." Just about any marinade can infuse the muscle fibers with moisture and keep it more tender, but it's that combination of subtle acid, sugars, and milk solids that gives it a more well-rounded, heartier flavor and makes it such an essential ingredient in fried chicken. Since it lacks the potency of a pickle brine, it also tends to absorb other seasonings quite well, so you don't have to rely solely on your breading.

While Johnson admits to being "not a fan of pickle juice as a marinade," he noted that it can have its place. "It's already salty and acidic, so it seasons and moistens the meat quickly," he explained. "You'll notice results in a couple of hours instead of overnight." Still, its high acidity means you have a narrower window between tender and mushy chicken, so it's not an ideal choice for beginners. Brine is a pretty potent ingredient, so while it's effective, it also calls for an experienced hand unless you're willing to experiment.

Good fried chicken has three components: meat, breading, and oil. Buttermilk is such a mainstay in recipes because it has a delicious effect on all three of these, promoting flavor, browning, and texture.

One of many major mistakes everyone makes with fried chicken is creating a breading that doesn't stick, and dry ingredients aren't the only contributor to this. "Oil and fat act like a release agent between meat and crust," Todd Johnson explained. "That's why we use fat-free buttermilk." For this aspect, it may seem like pickle brine is the right choice, but Johnson said that its lack of fat comes at the cost of a thinner consistency. Fat-free buttermilk is far more adhesive, giving your dry ingredients a prime surface to cling to, so they won't fall off the meat once they hit the oil.

Johnson conceded that buttermilk has a tendency to "over-brown" if your oil gets too hot, burning the milk sugars and solids, fouling the flavor. This is a pretty hefty disadvantage, but one that can be turned to an advantage with a careful eye. If you maintain Johnson's recommended oil temperature of 350 degrees Fahrenheit, you can toast, rather than burn, those solids, delivering a robust flavor profile focused on a hearty, umami-rich breading. Buttermilk is also a more convenient option, as you can just remove the excess drippings and drop your meat directly into your breading.

When you're looking to make fried chicken on the fly or don't have a surplus of seasoning, there are few options better than pickle brine. Todd Johnson said it's indispensable for achieving that "sandwich shop flavor" that some folks prefer, even if it has its quirks.

While you can always add some salt to your buttermilk, it won't have quite the same effect as using a jar of leftover pickle brine. "It's already salty and acidic, so it seasons and moistens the meat quickly," Todd Johnson clarified. Its extremely high and well-dissolved sodium content loosens up muscle fibers, making them far better at retaining moisture. While it lacks the rich solids and sugars of buttermilk, it brings far more tang, so it'll cut through even the heartiest, most marbled pieces of fried meat quite easily.

The biggest downside to pickle juice in your marinade is inconsistency and lack of other seasoning options. Not all brines are made the same, so unless you work with the exact same brand and product every time, there's always some amount of guess work involved. While pickle juice is one of the most powerful vehicles for flavor, it also leaves very little room for anything more subtle or nuanced. After marinating your meat, you'll also need to carefully pat dry each piece before tossing it in a batter or dry ingredients. Excess surface moisture creates a steam barrier between your chicken and breading, so even a bit left on the surface can ruin your food.

Our classic pecan pie recipe has no chocolate chips, no booze, and no hard-to-find ingredients. Instead of focusing on embellishments, we focused on what matters most: the deep flavor of toasted pecans, a caramely custard, and a buttery, flaky pie crust.

Brown sugar and a spoonful of dark molasses add depth to the filling, while toasting the pecans before incorporating them makes their flavor stand out and their texture more robust. Blind-baking the crust—baking it before adding the filling—helps keep the bottom crisp beneath the gooey custard.

When the pie comes out of the oven, the edges should be set and slightly puffed, but the center should still wobble like Jell-O. It will continue to set as it cools, so resist slicing too soon. Serve it all on its own, or gild it with vanilla ice cream or whipped cream.

Can I make this pecan pie without corn syrup?

Yes. Corn syrup gives this classic pie its perfectly gooey texture, but there are alternatives. Brown rice syrup will add a toasty flavor and can be used in equal parts. Golden syrup, honey, and maple syrup are all too sweet and will change the pie's flavor and texture.
