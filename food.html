Crocodiles face a hidden threat that may weaken a vital survival response

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Caimans with more mercury in their blood showed a weaker stress hormone response, raising concerns about how pollution affects their survival.

Mercury pollution may be weakening the stress response in crocodiles and their relatives.

Researchers studying smooth-fronted caimans in South American rainforests found that animals with more mercury in their blood had a weaker stress hormone response.

Gold mining is a major source of mercury pollution in South America. The findings suggest that mercury may interfere with a natural defense system that caimans and other crocodilians depend on to survive in the wild.

Jérémy Lemaire of the University of Vienna led the project with colleagues there and at the University of Veterinary Medicine Vienna.

The researchers studied 20 adult and nearly adult caimans in French Guiana between November 2023 and June 2024.

“Given the overlap of crocodylian habitats with gold mining activities, this is a conservation concern that deserves immediate attention,” Lemaire said.

Since the late 1800s, atmospheric mercury has risen an estimated 300% to 500% above its natural level, mostly from burning fossil fuels.

Small-scale gold mining is a major contributor to this pollution. It produces 38% of the mercury released into the air worldwide, and more than 80% of mercury emissions in South America and sub-Saharan Africa combined.

Caimans live a long time and take in mercury mainly through their food. Over many years, it builds up in their bodies, which makes them a useful measure of how polluted their streams and forests are.

The authors describe mercury as an endocrine-disrupting chemical, meaning one that interferes with the body’s hormones.

Theirs is the first study to measure this hormone response in wild animals living in a tropical forest with high mercury levels, the team wrote.

The researchers studied caimans in different parts of the rainforest. One of the sites was the Nouragues nature reserve.

They caught the animals between 8 and 11 p.m. to account for the natural changes in stress hormone levels throughout the day.

Researchers caught each caiman with a snare pole. They taped its snout shut and laid a wet towel over its eyes, so every animal went through the same experience.

Then they drew blood four times. The first sample came within 3 to 5 minutes of capture to estimate baseline hormone levels. The rest came 15, 30, and 60 minutes after capture.

Red blood cells went to the mercury test. The liquid part of the blood went to a test for corticosterone, the main stress hormone in reptiles and birds.

Mercury in the caimans’ dried red blood cells ranged from about 0.3 to 2 parts per million. The team split the animals into three groups: four with low levels, nine in the middle, and seven with high levels.

At the first blood draw, resting hormone levels were about the same in all three groups. The groups differed only in the one-hour sample.

In the low and middle groups, hormone levels rose over the hour. In the high-mercury group, the levels didn’t change by a statistically significant amount at any point.

At 60 minutes, the high group’s levels were lower than those of both other groups. The high-mercury caimans started with similar hormone levels but showed a smaller increase after capture.

Across all 20 animals, the more mercury a caiman carried, the smaller the difference between its resting level and its level at one hour. Mercury levels accounted for about 40% of the variation in that difference.

The most contaminated caiman’s hormone increase was more than 100 times smaller than that of the least contaminated one.

“A fast, robust stress response is essential for survival in the wild,” Lemaire said.

“We show that even relatively low mercury burdens affect this response in caimans, potentially limiting their ability to cope with predators, extreme weather, human disturbance, and ultimately survive in the wild.”

Stress hormones work by attaching to a protein called the glucocorticoid receptor, which helps activate the body’s stress response.

Mercury can interfere with this process. Studies in male rats found that mercury made it harder for hormones to attach to the receptor.

Computer simulations also suggest that mercury can partly block the hormone’s binding site by attaching to an amino acid called cysteine.

Lemaire’s team found this same amino acid in the receptors of crocodilians and other vertebrates. This could help explain their findings, although they haven’t tested the effect directly in caiman cells.

The caimans had mercury levels similar to those found in other crocodilians worldwide.

Some slender-snouted crocodiles in Gabon, for example, have had blood mercury levels as high as 11 parts per million.

The researchers say the problem may extend beyond areas near gold mines. Even at the levels found in this study, mercury was linked to a weaker stress response.

More than a third of the 26 living crocodilian species are threatened with extinction, according to the IUCN.

Mercury pollution could put them at greater risk if it affects their ability to survive and reproduce.

The effects on wild populations could take years to become noticeable.

Since the late 1800s, crocodilians have gone through about five generations. In comparison, large land animals with generations lasting 5 to 10 years have gone through around 20.

Crocodilians take a long time to produce new generations, which can make changes in their populations difficult to detect.

Researchers still need to find out whether caimans with weaker stress responses are more likely to die or have fewer offspring.

The full study was published in the journal Communications Biology.

Running from Another Rain Storm on the Arizona Trail

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

This morning we were up and hiking right as the sun came up. I slept really well last night at our tent site. Which is great, because we have a long day of hiking ahead of us today. As of today we have about 100 miles to get to the highway crossing where we can hitch into the town of Pine. It’s Monday morning and a big storm is going to start rolling in late Tuesday night or early Wednesday morning. Our hope would be to be as far south as possible before the storm hits so we can head into town and avoid the imminent flash flooding on trail.

Right off the bat once we got walking this morning we got to enjoy an absolutely incredible sunrise. There was a thick layer of fog rolling in along the surrounding mountains and it settled just below the line of the sunrise. That was beautiful and was a fantastic way to start off our day. The plant life is so lush here right now and the deep greens of the nearby pine trees were complemented well by the fog and glow of the sun coming up. The section of trail that followed had some tall rock walls which were bright orange. We’ve been told that the bright orange rock is actually notably more bright because of the moisture from recent rain. How nice for us!

This sunrise this morning was unbelievable. The trail went right past this cliff and the valley below was filled with fog. The layer of fog rested so perfectly beneath the rising sun.

As we continued along the trail the water sources were super limited. Luckily we filled up last night and each still had a good amount of water this morning. Because the first couple of water sources were cow ponds that looked like chocolate milk! I love chocolate milk, but not chocolate milk-colored water. We bypassed those sources and just hoped for some fresher water up ahead along the trail. For a lot of the morning we were continuing through shaded pine forest. There were some adorable little wildflowers adorning the trail, some of which we hadn’t seen yet on our hike!

I loved the way this red cliff looked, surrounded by pine trees.

In the late morning the trail changed a bit and felt a lot more like a desert environment. The ground was more of a clay/dirt and there were hardly any trees at all. Around this time we saw a massive tarantula crawling along the trail. It was really cool standing there and watching it make its way by us. Then not long after that we saw another huge tarantula! Apparently they are migrating this time of year for mating season. It’s wild to picture tarantulas migrating any sort of distance considering how tiny they are. Perhaps that explains why we saw more than one tarantula though in such a small vicinity.

A fairly large tarantula on the trail. It might be the biggest tarantula I’ve ever seen.

In the afternoon the trail passed by another one of the cool rusted trail markers that it has. The marker is a map of the trail and is engraved, noting how far the distance is to both Utah and Mexico. As of around noon today we had 558 miles remaining of the trail and have traveled over 243 miles. Not too shabby considering we had to take off 3 days for the storm that hit our first week on the trail.

An adorable fluffy little flower alongside the trail.

At this point in the day we were approaching the Mormon Lake area. There is a campground and some sort of restaurant and store there by the lake. Some of our friends popped in there for food and drinks when they passed through. If we wanted to end our day there we would have had to hike a little more than 40 miles today. But there’s no need to do that. We have plenty of food and are actually going to see Gorf tomorrow morning at a road crossing past Mormon Lake. She let us leave some food at her place and offered to drop it off to us tomorrow morning. That way we didn’t have to carry as much for the bigger stretch of mileage between towns.

I really love these Arizona trail signs because they actually indicate where you are at on the trail!

Over the course of the day I was looking at the map and trying to figure out exactly how far we should go and what our options were. I checked the weather and saw that the heavy rain was expected to start in the early morning on Wednesday. Then I started to look around on Far Out at the map to see about some alternate options to the original plan. We have to hike around 35-40 miles today and tomorrow to make the following day even remotely close to 30 miles. Which means that likely we will get to the highway sometime Thursday evening, even if we push out massive mileage the next few days. When I looked on the map though I saw another trailhead about 30 miles before the one we were intending to stop at. If we got off there to go into town instead, then we would only have to do big miles today and tomorrow and maybe be able to get into town Tuesday evening. Otherwise we’ll be hiking for probably a full day in the heavy rain which does not sound like fun.

Only 558 miles remain to the Mexican border! At least as of this morning.

After thinking about all of that I felt a bit of relief about our plan. Sure, we’ll still have to hike big days today and tomorrow. But originally we were thinking that we might have to hike in a full day of rain in order to get to town. Doing 35 miles in heavy rain can be pretty brutal. Usually the biggest issue for me is either being far too wet and cold, or getting major chafing. Plus, all of our friends are going to be in town! 9 Lives messaged me the other day when they got into Pine. Another hiker she was hiking with got injured and wanted to take a couple of days off. So they made the decision to just stay in town for a handful of days to let him heal and then wait out the storm. They got an Airbnb in the town of Strawberry which is right by Pine. Which means if we can get a hitch late tomorrow night, we have a place to stay! It will be a blast waiting out the storm with friends. And when we get back on the trail we will only be 30 or so miles behind them. Originally we started a week after everyone! But thanks to bad weather and big miles, we have caught up significantly. Then when we do hike out after the storm we will hopefully be able to catch up quickly and finally hike with everyone. We’ve been chasing our friends for this whole trail practically.

A large bone just off the trail. Perhaps from the shoulder or collar bone area of a cow?

In the afternoon we finally reached our first decent water source of the entire day. It was around 2PM and we actually had another handful of miles before the marked source that we were aiming for. But we crossed under a bridge that had a pretty clear pool of water beneath it and we couldn’t resist. It looked so much better than any other water we’d seen all day. Getting water earlier meant not having to worry that the next source was gross or dry. We took a nice break there and filtered water. I knew we had a long way to go for the night so we also pulled out more food to each snack on. Then we continued along, enjoying our last few hours of daylight left.

Grabbing water from a delicious puddle along the trail. Somehow this was a much better source than any of the actually listed water sources earlier on in the day.

The next couple of hours went by quickly as we continued along a dirt and pine-covered trail. There was hardly any climbing at all and the trail wasn’t rocky or tough to navigate. It was about as smooth of sailing as it could possibly be. Then the sun slowly began to go down and we enjoyed golden hour as it coated the trees and surrounding brush in its glow. All of the small wildflowers and the tall wheatgrass have such a lovely glow in the early evening. I’ve really enjoyed watching the plants dance around in the breeze, especially as the sun is beginning to set. There was also a dense cloud covering the mountains off in the distance. It sat perfectly on top of them and made for a great view as the sun went down.

The way that these clouds looked resting on top of the mountains in the distance was only made even better thanks to the setting sun.

Before it got dark, the trail crossed over a dirt road and we walked right through a small campground. It appeared mostly closed for the season and the water had been turned off. The privy’s were also locked, which is always a disappointment. But there was actually a really lovely creek flowing through the area though which we were able to enjoy. It was nearly 6PM at this point so we just decided to fill up for the night at this source. That way we could hike as far as we wanted to this evening and not have to worry about getting water later. It was also a good excuse to take another break. The final break of the night before it would get dark.

Lovely views as the last bit of sunlight lit up the trail.

Lucky for us, our early start this morning meant that it didn’t have to be a wildly late night tonight. Our goal was to go around 36-37 miles, and the terrain graciously accommodated us. After passing through that campground we continued on into pine forest for most of the remainder of the evening. Before 8PM we had already made it around 36 miles for the day. Around then we started keeping our eyes peeled for campsites and wound up walking a bit longer before we found a nice one. In the end we did 37.2 miles for the day and I actually felt really good.

Once we got to our site I got situated, made hot chocolate, and snacked a bit. Then I did my stretching and a little bit of editing before calling it a night. We didn’t make it all the way to the Mormon Lake area, but we’re only about 6 miles short of it. We texted Gorf to coordinate a meeting with her tomorrow morning. She’s going to drive out around 8AM to meet us at the trailhead past Mormon Lake. Which is pretty funny now that we’re planning to go into town tomorrow evening from an earlier road crossing! We get to load up heavily on food and then carry it for another nearly 35 mile day. I actually did a great job this time around though and have the perfect amount of food leftover. So I won’t be loaded up too terribly once we see Gorf tomorrow.

Gut bacteria cannot see the sun so they use your meal times to control how your body absorbs nutrients

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Gut bacteria in mice read mealtimes, not daylight, and tell the intestine when to absorb, helping underfed mice grow and overfed ones get fat.

The body clock is supposed to run on light. Sunrise wakes the brain, and the rest of the body falls in line. But the bacteria in the gut live in the dark.

In mice, a common microbe keeps time another way: it reads when food arrives and tells the cells of the intestine to start absorbing.

The signal is a small molecule the bacterium makes while it digests phytate, a compound in grains, nuts and seeds. The molecule switches on genes that pull amino acids and fats out of a meal.

Give it to undernourished young mice and they grow. Give it to mice on a high-fat diet and they get fatter.

Zheng Kuang, an associate professor of biological sciences at Carnegie Mellon University, led the work from his lab. Colleagues at the University of Pittsburgh, UT Southwestern Medical Center and the Quadram Institute in England took part.

All of it was done in mice and in human gut cells grown in a dish.

In an interview with Earth.com, Kuang said what struck him most was that the bacteria convert the timing of a meal into chemistry.

“Their absolute numbers did not need to change substantially across the day – their metabolic activity changed,” he said.

The bacterium is Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, one of the most common residents of the human gut.

Kuang’s team raised mice with no gut microbes at all, then gave some of them this one species and nothing else. They sampled the lining of the small intestine around the clock.

Mice eat at night. In the hours after dark, the colonized mice carried more of the bacterial signal molecule in their gut lining than they did in the morning.

Germ-free mice showed no such rise, and neither did mice given a mutant strain missing the enzyme that breaks down phytate.

The bacteria themselves were no more numerous at night than in the morning, Kuang’s team found. What rose was the activity of that one enzyme.

“We are not claiming these bacteria have their own internal clock,” Kuang told Earth.com. “Rather, they respond to feeding cues and produce molecules called inositol phosphates, which activate a protein called HDAC3 in intestinal cells.”

That protein, HDAC3, works inside the cells lining the intestine and helps decide which genes are on.

The bacterial molecule makes it grip a partner protein more tightly, and the pair then turns on a set of transporter genes that carry nutrients across the gut wall.

In colonized mice, 14 amino acids and 14 fatty acids rose in the blood during the feeding hours, while in germ-free mice they stayed low.

Of 248 transporter genes the team tracked, 122 cycled more strongly with the bacterium present, and most of those handle amino acids, sugars or fats.

The body’s master clock genes kept their normal daily rhythm either way. The bacterium didn’t reset the clock; it strengthened the daily rise and fall of the metabolic genes that follow it.

The team then fed mice only during the day, which is their normal sleeping time. The signal molecule fell and the gut’s daily rhythm in gene activity flattened, though the animals ate no less than before.

Mice allowed to eat only at night looked like mice allowed to eat whenever they liked.

The feeding experiment is the result Kuang pointed to when asked what people should take from the work. “The key message is to align your feeding schedule with the natural day-night cycle – eating during your active, daytime hours,” he said.

Artificial light has pushed human eating into the night, he added, and the study suggests that misalignment disrupts the bacterial signals.

Kuang said there was likely a metabolic cost to that disruption. The feeding experiment itself tracked the signal and the gene rhythms, not the mice’s weight.

Young mice were weaned onto a diet with the usual calories but little protein or fat, a model for childhood malnutrition.

Those given the bacterium grew longer bodies and longer thigh bones than those given the mutant strain. They had heavier organs, more amino acids in their blood and better blood sugar control, and they ate the same amount.

The signal molecule on its own did the same, in mice with and without a normal gut community. In mice bred to lack HDAC3 in their gut cells, neither the bacterium nor the molecule helped, and a drug that blocks HDAC3 blunted the effect too.

In a public dataset of children’s stool samples, the bacterium was among 19 species more common in healthy children than in undernourished ones.

Adult mice fed a high-fat, high-sugar diet for 12 weeks gained more weight with the bacterium than with the mutant strain.

They carried more belly fat and liver fat and had higher blood fats and worse blood sugar handling. They absorbed more fat from food and passed less of it in their stool.

Again, the mice lacking HDAC3 in their gut cells showed none of this, and food intake didn’t differ.

Kuang said that double edge is the finding he most wants understood. “This illustrates why it is too simple to label a bacterium as inherently ‘good’ or ‘bad’ – its effects depend on diet and the metabolic state of the host,” he explained.

The team’s reading is evolutionary. A pathway that extracts the most from every meal was an advantage when food was scarce, and the same pathway can work against a body surrounded by high-fat diets.

Whether this bacterium even lives and works in the human small intestine is an open question. The team also could not prove that the signal molecule in the gut cells came from the bacteria rather than from the cells themselves, which make related molecules.

And phytate intake varies from one diet to another, so the signal’s strength probably does too, the authors wrote.

Whether the same signal shifts in people who eat on a jet-lagged or night-shift schedule is a question the study does not reach.

Asked how soon the bacteria could be tested against malnutrition, Kuang said it is too early to give a reliable timeline for human testing.

The next step, he said, is to look for the pathway in human intestinal samples and in children with undernutrition. Until then, the case that mealtimes talk to the gut through its bacteria rests on mice.

The full study was published online in a publication of the Carnegie Mellon University.

18 Easy Bean Recipes For Dinner That Are Cheap & Nutritious

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

For a couple bucks a bag? You can’t beat ‘em.

Like most American families, we’ve had to get pretty strategic about our grocery budget over the past year as grocery prices have risen (and risen, and risen...). Since when did a pound of 80/20 become a special little treat? We’ve been opting for ground chicken and ground turkey instead for a few years now. As grocery prices continue to go up, what are you turning to to keep your own food bills down? Maybe it’s rice or lentils, but don’t forget about the humble bean. Canned beans and especially dried bagged beans are affordable sources of protein and fiber, and with the right bean recipes for dinner, you can make them so incredibly delicious. Here’s how to load them up with flavor.

Stuffed peppers are such a flavorful dish that even if you’re a meat-eater, you’ll never miss it in this recipe from All The Healthy Things. Don’t be intimidated by the long ingredient list — most of them are just seasonings you most likely have in your pantry already. These only take about 15 minutes of prep, then just pop them in the oven and go help with homework or baths ‘til they’re done.

Burrito bowls are easy, and they never disappoint. Princess Pinky Girls’ recipe clearly includes chicken, but honestly, if you sub in some really tasty refried beans or something, you won’t even miss it. We are big on burrito bowls in my house because they’re also great work lunches to prep ahead of time.

Chickpeas, aka garbanzo beans, are the filling base to this super flavorful Italian chickpea salad from Foodie Crush. With all the additions — red onion, bell pepper, garlic, pepperoncini, and more — this is a delicious dense bean salad that’ll just get better as the flavors meld together in the fridge all week long.

Averie Cooks’ Mediterranean white bean salad looks exactly like what I mean when I say I want a little snack, but can’t find anything I actually want in the fridge (it’s because I’m looking for a restaurant-quality appetizer at all times). This recipe is a bit more involved, but the marinated white beans, crispy asparagus, and shallots served over whipped ricotta sound good enough to be worth the trouble.

If you’re trying to up your fiber intake, Damn Delicious’ spinach and white bean soup is the recipe for you. This delicious dish is ready in 30 minutes and would be so, so good with some Parmesan grated on top and a side of crusty bread.

Chili is a major crowd-pleaser, and it’s a cheap way to make a lot of food at once, especially if you’re subbing in beans for beef. All The Healthy Things’ recipe includes meat, but you can just leave it out. That said, don’t skip the little dash of cocoa powder — it’s a game-changer.

Being able to make some simple but delicious bean sides yourself is a great way to save money, and fortunately, Simply LaKita’s Southern baked beans fit the bill. It takes about an hour, but you’ll have a big batch to pair with other proteins throughout the week.

I’m not sure falafel can be improved upon, but it’s fun to find out. What Jew Wanna Eat’s black bean falafel can be ready in about 20 minutes and doesn’t require many ingredients to pull off. Topped with tomatoes, cucumbers, and tzatziki, this is an easy and filling meal you’ll return to again and again.

If you’re having a bummer of a week, a cheesy, saucy enchilada night is guaranteed to make you feel at least 1% better. This version from Averie Cooks is a fantastic way to get more veggies into you and your kids, because the sweet potatoes and corn inside will indeed be coated in cheesy deliciousness.

Need a light, yummy salad to eat with chips for lunch? Or something easy to throw into burrito bowls or wraps? All The Healthy Things’ black bean and corn salad is easy to customize with your favorite veggies and herbs, and you can eat it in so many ways.

Filling, cheap taco ideas, you say? Damn Delicious has you covered with these black bean and quinoa tacos. They’re simple, satisfying, and inexpensive — the kind of recipe I could see going platinum in my house, personally.

Foodie Crush’s cannellini beans are another flavorful side you can pair with salads, proteins, or put on toast if you’re into that sort of thing. Or if you’re a fan of brothy beans, you can just grab the pot and a big spoon and dive in.

Princess Pinky Girl is the best at quick, easy recipes that are a breeze to make and super satisfying to eat. This cowboy casserole is also a one-pan meal (love) — it’s sort of like a chili base topped with cornbread. While her recipe calls for ground beef, you could easily just sub in extra beans and veggies.

Haute & Healthy Living’s Italian soup is rich and creamy thanks to a little coconut milk, and incredibly rich in flavor (shout-out to sun-dried tomatoes). The whole thing takes about 15 minutes of prep, and then the slow cooker will handle the rest for you.

These black bean burgers from Barefoot in the Pines take 35 minutes start to finish (hello, weeknight dinner MVP) and only take two cans of black beans, an onion, and some seasonings to pull off. You can top them with classic burger fixings or go with salsa and guac, as pictured here.

Fried plantains make a regular bowl of beans and rice feel special all of a sudden. This bowl by Cadry’s Kitchen is an easy, affordable meal that gets you all your food groups in one sitting. Like, if you had a nutritionist, they’d be so proud of you.

I love a side of restaurant-style refried beans, but I never think to make them at home. Haute & Healthy Living’s version will help you keep the sodium in check without sacrificing flavor. Eat these as a side, a dip, or load up some bean and cheese burritos.

Beans and rice is a complete meal, and a delicious one at that. This recipe from Biscuits & Burlap is a copycat of Popeye’s red beans and rice side, which, if you’re from the South, you know they’ve got some of the best around.

Oral Food Challenge Clears Many Infants Thought to Have Peanut Allergy

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

— Researchers say biomarkers and clinical history of reaction alone aren't enough for diagnosis

More than a third of infants thought to be allergic to peanuts were not actually allergic, an oral food challenge study showed.

With the aim of treating peanut allergy among babies who reacted to peanut before 1 year of age and had a positive skin prick or blood test, 34.8% who were subject to an oral food challenge (OFC) to set the dose for oral immunotherapy came up negative and could be considered non-allergic by this gold-standard test.

That rate was "far higher than expected," noted Kirsten Perrett, MBBS, PhD, of Murdoch Children's Research Institute at the Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne, Australia, and colleagues in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology: In Practice.

Typically, a history of reaction plus sensitization is considered enough for diagnosis of peanut allergy, and children are sent home with instructions to avoid peanut products. While a majority of children outgrow milk or egg allergies, only about a quarter to a third of children outgrow peanut allergies without treatment by age 6 years.

The current results potentially indicate that some infants have a transient immune response to the allergen that resolves quickly (an average of 6 months between initial reaction and OFC in this study) or doesn't result in ongoing reactions upon further exposure, Perrett and team said.

Determining that early allows reintroduction of peanut in infancy, which may change the trajectory of peanut allergy and reduce the burden of food allergy, independent of an oral immunotherapy program, they added.

OFC "should be routinely considered in infant peanut allergy evaluation regardless of the proposed management strategy (immunotherapy or allergen avoidance)," Perrett and colleagues argued. "Biomarkers and clinical history alone are insufficient to diagnose peanut allergy in this age group."

That hasn't been the case "for a variety of reasons including carer or clinician concerns regarding safety, time, and/or resource constraints, or the belief that an OFC is unnecessary when there is a clear clinical history of reaction and a positive test of sensitization," they wrote.

With regard to safety, their study showed 61 cases of anaphylaxis during the 778 threshold OFCs (7.8%), which is a higher rate than in the conventional use of OFCs for cases in which diagnosis is unclear from clinical history or the allergy is suspected to be outgrown. While that means these OFCs should be performed in centers with appropriate medical support, the trade-off is worthwhile, Perrett and colleagues suggested.

"Accurately confirming allergy via OFC in infants can reduce the burden of allergy for patients, families, and society through misdiagnosis, and before the commencement of OIT [oral immunotherapy], it can help avoid unnecessary treatment," they pointed out.

The Allergy Development to an Accelerated Pathway to Tolerance (ADAPT) OIT program was a partnership with 10 major pediatric hospitals in five states across Australia to develop and implement the first standardized nationwide model of care for food allergy treatment.

The program was to enroll all infants presenting with a clinical history of an immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated allergic reaction to peanut before age 12 months and evidence of sensitization from a peanut skin prick test wheal of at least 3 mm or a peanut-specific IgE or Ara h 2-specific IgE of at least 0.35 kUA/L.

The 59 infants whose reaction to peanut had been anaphylaxis (Consortium for Food Allergy Research grade 3) could go straight to oral immunotherapy. Notably, 14 of the 29 patients in this group (48.3%) who had a threshold OFC did not react and were deemed tolerant to peanut.

Babies with an initial grade 1 to 2 reaction to peanut were to undergo OFC to peanut before commencing OIT both to confirm the diagnosis of peanut allergy and to determine peanut reaction threshold to inform the OIT starting dose. The OFC used a commercially available peanut flour (PB2).

Median age at OFC was 13 months, and it took a median 3 months from the time of initial reaction to the first allergy assessment in the allergy clinics.

Skin prick and blood test results were both significantly linked to a positive OFC (P<0.001) but did not predict the reaction threshold or severity.

But then the rain really set in and the descent began. Steep, deep mud for 8km. Eventually I lost the couples, preferring to not rush and navigate the mud alone. I got stuck up to my mid-shins and had to dig myself out with my poles. But overall I was feeling great, my usually-a-problem knee was holding up fantastically and I had only fallen once.

Then, about 5km from the end I slipped and my left leg folded under me and bashed into some sharp roots. It was one of those falls where your whole brain sets off an alarm and you have to count to ten before you can start assessing if you’re really hurt. The sharp pain from the sicks had me wondering if my sock had ripped (luckily no) and I stood and tested my ankle – it seemed ok so I kept walking. I didn’t have much choice.

By the time I made it out of the forest, the rain was getting lighter and I meandered through a misty field trying to find the trail markers.

I was so close to the gravel road to camp when I had my third fall of the day, walking down what I thought was regular dirt but was actually extremely slippery mud, toward a gate that would let me onto the road. I fell in slow motion, swearing at the universe. This fall didn’t hurt my body so much as my ego. I threw my pack over the gate and climbed over the stile before hobbling my muddy butt up the road to the campsite.

I was so incredibly happy to see my friends and the sun peaking through the clouds. After nine and a bit hours I had made it! There was a stream for washing and collecting water and I did both, using my muddy shorts to wash both themselves and my legs. I got my tent up and airing out, and someone gave me pre-boiled water for a hot chocolate. Everything was looking up!

But what goes up must come down I guess, and my left ankle started to swell. Turns out the fall and bashing it on those sticks did hurt. I massaged it and did my best to keep off it and by morning it felt walkable, so off we set.

This day was one of emotional ups and downs, it was supposed to be the chillest, easiest day of the section. Mostly gravel roads for 23km and a dairy (convenience store) 6km in. Unfortunately, my ankle was GRUMPY.

There was a mobile voting place at the dairy so I could vote right on trail and have an ice cream at the same time! The ladies at the voting place were so kind and I told them it was the first time I was able to vote in NZ so this was a big deal for me. I cast my vote then went to the dairy and bought myself an icecream and a canned iced coffee. Glorious.

The next 10km or so up a forestry road flew by. Then my ankle decided it was once again in a bad mood. I hobbled down the forestry road to reach a stream about 1.5km out from camp and stood in its very cold waters for ages to get the swelling down. It was gorgeous and a relief.

Camp this night was much more established, with a water tank, toilet and a shelter. I decided that if my ankle was still sore in the morning I’d stay, I had plenty of food, battery charge, and I knew more people would turn up the next day.

I felt a bit sad at this prospect but I knew I’d feel stupid if I pushed it and had to get off trail. The magic of thru hiking struck this night, and we had a lovely time chatting in the shelter and eating the whole block of Whittaker’s chocolate my friend brought. I got to see a ruru (a native NZ owl) for the first time ever. We all had a good laugh when someone’s tent got raided by a possum. It’s all ups and downs.

My ankle was, indeed, still sore this morning. One of my day-one friends decided to stay with me for the day as she also had tons of food and wanted to take some time to sort out her gear and the blisters on her toes. I managed to get some messages out to Fraser and the trail angel who was going to pick me up to let them know that I was okay but delayed.

We spent the morning slowly drinking our tea and coffee, and eating breakfast. I washed some of my clothes, read with my feet up in the air to help with swelling and munched on our over-abundance of snacks. To be honest, it was exactly what I needed.

Another group of hikers turned up in the afternoon and one was a physio (the trail provides!) she assessed my ankle and did a lymphatic drainage massage on it. She was a life saver. In the morning I felt way better but decided it wasn’t smart to continue on the rougher track ahead and backtracked to the road where I was picked up by a trail angel. This is NOT how I wanted this section to go, I really was looking forward to the river sections, but I needed to be smart and recognize that even with some improvement on my ankle, it was likely an overuse injury and needed real rest and to be checked by a physio in town.

To say I was disappointed is an understatement, I have never had an issue with my ankle like that so it really blindsided me. But taking a break is not quitting. And boy will it make for a good comeback story when I’m recovered and back on trail. For now, it’s several rest days and hoping I don’t get too stir crazy.

The whole album Grace by Jeff Buckley. I had never listened to it and Fraser told me it was essential listening. I listened to it while sitting in the sun in the Krishna Sanctuary garden, I loved it.

Honourable mention: that “just keep swimming” song Dory sings in finding nemo which got me through the last 3km of the Raetea forest.

Pickle Juice Vs Buttermilk: Which Marinade Is Best For Fried Chicken?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

When it comes to down-home Southern cooking, buttermilk and pickles both have a prominent place thanks to their flavors and how they affect the chemistry of cooking. Todd Johnson, Proprietor of Strips Chicken & Brewing, insists that both have a place in fried chicken — although he asserts that buttermilk is the better all-around choice.

"If you're going to marinate your chicken, buttermilk wins," Johnson told Food Republic. "It's gently acidic, so it softens the surface of the chicken without turning it mushy. It's also thick enough to leave a tacky layer that flour clings to. That's the foundation of a craggy, crunchy crust." Just about any marinade can infuse the muscle fibers with moisture and keep it more tender, but it's that combination of subtle acid, sugars, and milk solids that gives it a more well-rounded, heartier flavor and makes it such an essential ingredient in fried chicken. Since it lacks the potency of a pickle brine, it also tends to absorb other seasonings quite well, so you don't have to rely solely on your breading.

While Johnson admits to being "not a fan of pickle juice as a marinade," he noted that it can have its place. "It's already salty and acidic, so it seasons and moistens the meat quickly," he explained. "You'll notice results in a couple of hours instead of overnight." Still, its high acidity means you have a narrower window between tender and mushy chicken, so it's not an ideal choice for beginners. Brine is a pretty potent ingredient, so while it's effective, it also calls for an experienced hand unless you're willing to experiment.

Good fried chicken has three components: meat, breading, and oil. Buttermilk is such a mainstay in recipes because it has a delicious effect on all three of these, promoting flavor, browning, and texture.

One of many major mistakes everyone makes with fried chicken is creating a breading that doesn't stick, and dry ingredients aren't the only contributor to this. "Oil and fat act like a release agent between meat and crust," Todd Johnson explained. "That's why we use fat-free buttermilk." For this aspect, it may seem like pickle brine is the right choice, but Johnson said that its lack of fat comes at the cost of a thinner consistency. Fat-free buttermilk is far more adhesive, giving your dry ingredients a prime surface to cling to, so they won't fall off the meat once they hit the oil.

Johnson conceded that buttermilk has a tendency to "over-brown" if your oil gets too hot, burning the milk sugars and solids, fouling the flavor. This is a pretty hefty disadvantage, but one that can be turned to an advantage with a careful eye. If you maintain Johnson's recommended oil temperature of 350 degrees Fahrenheit, you can toast, rather than burn, those solids, delivering a robust flavor profile focused on a hearty, umami-rich breading. Buttermilk is also a more convenient option, as you can just remove the excess drippings and drop your meat directly into your breading.

When you're looking to make fried chicken on the fly or don't have a surplus of seasoning, there are few options better than pickle brine. Todd Johnson said it's indispensable for achieving that "sandwich shop flavor" that some folks prefer, even if it has its quirks.

While you can always add some salt to your buttermilk, it won't have quite the same effect as using a jar of leftover pickle brine. "It's already salty and acidic, so it seasons and moistens the meat quickly," Todd Johnson clarified. Its extremely high and well-dissolved sodium content loosens up muscle fibers, making them far better at retaining moisture. While it lacks the rich solids and sugars of buttermilk, it brings far more tang, so it'll cut through even the heartiest, most marbled pieces of fried meat quite easily.

The biggest downside to pickle juice in your marinade is inconsistency and lack of other seasoning options. Not all brines are made the same, so unless you work with the exact same brand and product every time, there's always some amount of guess work involved. While pickle juice is one of the most powerful vehicles for flavor, it also leaves very little room for anything more subtle or nuanced. After marinating your meat, you'll also need to carefully pat dry each piece before tossing it in a batter or dry ingredients. Excess surface moisture creates a steam barrier between your chicken and breading, so even a bit left on the surface can ruin your food.
