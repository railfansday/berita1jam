Stock market correction? Whatever — Wall Street is bullish on 2027, and one chief investment officer sees the S&P 500 soaring 45% to 10,000

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Dow Jones Industrial Average and the S&P 500 joined the Nasdaq in correction territory as AI stocks plunged further, but 2026 is also winding down, and Wall Street is already putting out predictions for 2027.

Analysts see solid gains ahead, with the U.S. economy expected to continue chugging along on the back of massive hyperscaler spending and resilient consumers. Some also welcomed the recent decline for taking froth off markets that might otherwise start looking overheated.

Even this week’s selloff, which marked the worst weekly loss since the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic, was largely dismissed as a move driven more by emotion than fundamentals.

“This does not mean stampedes are uninformative or palpitations never presage heart attacks, but today’s panic does not feel like an AI bust,” Viktor Shvets, head of global desk strategy at Macquarie Capital, said in a note Friday.

Michael Monaghan, portfolio manager and partner at Founder ETFs, quipped that the market is “selling first and asking questions later.” And Jamie Cox, managing partner for Harris Financial Group, predicted investors will “return to rationality” and look instead to consumption-boosting GDP.

Meanwhile, Morgan Stanley chief U.S. equity strategist Michael Wilson said earlier this week that the S&P 500 would finish 2027 at 8,700, representing a 26% jump from current levels and a 15% rally when he made the call.

“We see the mid-cycle transition now ending with a more stable mid-cycle backdrop evolving in 2027,” he wrote.

Goldman Sachs also sees the broad market index soaring to 8,700 next year, “albeit with higher volatility than in recent years.”

Peter Oppenheimer, the bank’s chief global equity strategist, said that while corporate earnings growth will slow, inflation, bond yields, and oil prices will also moderate. “We expect the equity bull market to continue.”

Elsewhere, estimates were less bullish but still upbeat. UBS Global Wealth Management put the S&P 500 at 8,200 next year, implying 19% upside from Friday’s close.

Although it sees more earnings gains in 2027, Wall Street is focusing on whether the investment cycle can support profits into 2028, UBS explained.

“Cannibalization, financing, and execution risks around AI have increased while expectations and index concentration remain high,” it added. “The market is therefore more sensitive to earnings disappointment or a further rise in discount rates.”

Wells Fargo’s S&P 500 outlook in 2027 is also for 8,200, which assumes slower profit growth as well.

But then there’s Steve Chiavarone, chief investment officer for equities at Federated Hermes, who has a year-end view of 10,000—or a 45% surge from current levels.

He brushed aside this month’s midterm elections, which saw Democrats sweep Congress and several state capitals. While Wall Street fears the blue tsunami will slow the AI infrastructure boom, Chiavarone said risks to the buildout are overblown. Even a slight retreat would be positive.

“A modest pullback in the data center buildout could give cash flows time to catch up, easing inflation pressures and making the investment boom more sustainable,” he argued.

Slower prices could prevent additional rate hikes from the Federal Reserve, which has grown more hawkish as policymakers become impatient with inflation exceeding their 2% target for over five years.

Although Wall Street sees more tightening in the coming months, some analysts also anticipate rate cuts later in 2027 as inflation cools. But that assumes the Iran war will be over some time next year.

For now, Gaurav Mallik, chief investment officer at Modera Wealth Management, described the market slide as a “garden variety correction,” with stock leadership rotating from tech to industrials and financials.

“The pullback in stocks sets the market up nicely for 2027, which we believe will be a high-single-digit year,” he added. “Multiples are shrinking, and total returns are a function of earnings growth, which so far looks robust for 2027.”

Jason Ma is the weekend editor at Fortune, where he covers markets, the economy, finance, and housing.

9 Years Later, X-Men Finally Gives Cyclops His True Logan Redesign

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

As a franchise that has existed for 63 years and counting, X-Men is just like any other long-running comic book series in how it'll change up its core designs to fit its stories. Cyclops in particular has had plenty of revisions to his look, with cases like Age of Apocalypse, with even an Old Man Cyclops-like look in X-Men: Age of Revelation's dystopia, perfectly fitting a weary, guilt-ridden Scott Summers. But in the latest acclaimed series, Midnight X-Men by Jonathan Hickman and Matteo Della Fonte, Cyclops dons another tortured visage, with the latest issue piling on the mournful lore of the broken man behind the visor.

Midnight X-Men had already revealed the core look for Cyclops in its debut issue, but a telling flashback detailing his particular stake in a world teetering on conflict between mutants, vampires, and empyres, or mutant vampires. It also shows just how much this Cyclops has in common with Wolverine from 2017's Logan, namely, particular incidents involving the deaths of his friends. Propelled by a deep sense of self-hatred that makes all the more sense in the latest issue, Cyclops mirrors Wolverine's depiction in James Mangold's film, feeling more focused on the emotional devastation and not so much strictly on mirroring the Old Man Logan comics. And frankly, Della Fonte nails it with this latest look at Cyclops' redesign.

Already looking intentionally more gruff with a shaved head and a scruffy beard to indicate the rapid passage of time not aging him well, and not unlike the developments of Max Payne 3, Scott is not doing well in Midnight X-Men. In the stunning second issue, it lays out just what happened to the other X-Men, and why he is particularly ashamed about their fate, with the most striking scene being his most telling glimpse yet. Standing in front of a heavily cracked mirror, now shirtless, completely covered in scars, and bearing 37 hash marks on his back, Cyclops appears to carry this grim tally as a reminder of all the X-Men whom he led to their death at the hands of Jean Grey.

Originally, Scott had gone on a mission to find Storm, the original victim swayed and turned by Vlad Dracul, not fully understanding her fate. As Storm called to each of them, Jean, Iceman, Angel, and Beast were subsequently turned to empyres as well. Barely escaping with Kitty Pryde, Scott pleads with Professor X and even meets with Magneto, who ultimately don't give him the answers he wants. Instead, he rashly goes back to Jean with more and more X-Men, who are all subsequently turned, as Scott finds himself under Jean's spell, his love for her twisting him into a thrall until everyone around him is effectively torn away.

Now, he carries a reminder of this massacre, not unlike the shame and survivor's guilt Wolverine felt following Logan's offscreen Westchester Incident. One can only hope he finds similar redemption, and mercifully, a more hopeful fate than how Logan ended.

Now living as basically a vigilante and survivor ready to kill empyres practically on sight, Cyclops has a fascinating role in Midnight X-Men. One particular fellow survivor he finds, Hope, is the signal of a potential turning point on this darker path for Cyclops, even if her name is absurdly on-the-nose. Scott may have lost a more purely heroic standpoint, but he's not been turned completely evil, merely guided by trauma into survival mode. He's still a brilliant tactician, as shown by his deadly turning of the tables at Toriak Bunker in issue #1, but Hope, who is willing to help him fight back. But given his past trauma, it's understandable he'd be hesitant to lead a new generation of mutants.

Cyclops just stunned the X-Men with one line, which makes him feel less like Charles Xavier than ever and more like his counterpart Magneto.

Still, this invites another fascinating comparison to Logan, particularly its execution of Logan's bond with Laura, aka X-23. Cyclops shows himself to be a similarly reluctant guardian to her, urging her to skip town after rescuing her, but everyone knows that's not the end of it. But still, Hope represents everything Cyclops has lost, and keeping her alive will be instrumental to his redemption in this particular storyline. So as long as there's Hope, Midnight X-Men readers shouldn't count out Scott Summers just yet.

Guy Pearce's Remake Of A '60s Sci-Fi Classic Deserved Better At The Box Office

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Simon Wells' 2002 sci-fi flick "The Time Machine" was extrapolated from H.G. Wells' celebrated 1895 novel, one of the best sci-fi novels ever written, and also from George Pal's famed 1960 adaptation of the same story, itself one of the best time travel movies of all time. Made for a budget of $80 million, "The Time Machine" didn't overwhelm at the box office, earning almost $124 million worldwide. Critics were very unkind to "The Time Machine," and the film only has a 28% approval rating on Rotten Tomatoes, based on 151 reviews.

The movie isn't great, reducing the intellectualism of Wells' original novel into a rote sci-fi action thriller, but there was a steampunk quirkiness to "The Time Machine" that could have been more openly embraced. It was even nominated for an Oscar for Best Makeup.

Guy Pearce plays Alexander Hartdegen, the inventor of the titular device, and he learns very quickly that mucking around with time travel doesn't always provide the result he's after. Alexander's motivation for building a time machine in the first place is to go back in time to prevent the murder of his fiancée (Sienna Guillory). This isn't part of the original novel, nor of George Pal's movie. Alexander spends five years building the time machine, and manages to save his fiancée, only to lose her again shortly thereafter. Something about the universe requires she die.

This kicks off a long-ranging time-travel adventure wherein Alexander begins tripping through the centuries, hoping to find an answer about how fate operates. He eventually ends up in the very, very distant future — specifically, the year AD 802,701 — when humanity has evolved into two distinct subspecies, the Morlocks and the Eloi.

The two most notable additions to the movie of "The Time Machine" that most dramatically depart from the previous versions are characters played by Orlando Jones and Jeremy Irons. Jones' character is, I would argue, a good addition, adding someone for the protagonist to converse with. Irons' character is ... well, his inclusion is just baffling.

Jones plays Vox 114, an artificially intelligent digital library aide from the year 2030 who appears on glass screens. Because he is a computer, Vox is eager to teach people about science and the world. When Alexander, having just jaunted forward in time, asks Vox about time travel, Vox gets a little impatient, thinking Alexander just wants to talk about science fiction. Later in the film, Alexander finds Vox in a cave, somehow still operational hundreds of centuries later. Vox seems to have developed something of a consciousness over the years. He also still has a complete memory, remembering Alexander from this sojourn to 2030.

Irons, meanwhile, plays a character credited as the Über-Morlock, the psychic Cenobite-like leader of the Morlocks. As anyone who has read H.G. Wells' book can tell you, the Morlocks were an underground race of ape-like beings who feed on the Eloi who live on the Earth's surface. The Eloi, meanwhile, are placid, gentle, and defenseless. The idea is that the Morlocks evolved from old Earth's laboring classes, and the Eloi evolved from its aristocracy. Wells was clearly making a comment on class divisions, and how they will eventually play out if left to evolve along their logical extremes.

There is no Über-Morlock leading the underground monsters in the original book, and the inclusion of such a character in the 2002 movie is confusing and strange.

That said, there is at least something delicious about Jeremy Irons' performance. He sports a long white wig, pasty white skin, and wears a bizarre, black pleather outfit that looks like it could be worn to a bondage bar. "The Time Machine" climaxes with the Über-Morlock and Alexander grappling while the machine hurtles forward in time millions of years. It's such a kooky conceit that you might find yourself enjoying "The Time Machine."

Some fun trivia: Simon Wells, the director of "The Time Machine" just happens to be the great-grandson of H.G. Wells. Simon Wells also co-directed the 1991 animated film "An American Tail: Fievel Goes West" (which was Jimmy Stewart's final film), as well as "We're Back! A Dinosaur's Story," the giant '90s animated hit "The Prince of Egypt," and several others. (He also helmed the massive Disney flop "Mars Needs Moms," but we'll forgive him this time.) All of this is to say that Wells was no neophyte in 2002, and he is not a traditional nepo baby who received an opportunity primarily because of his lineage.

Critics largely hated "The Time Machine." Roger Ebert gave the film one-and-a-half stars out of four, saying that it was "a witless recycling of the H.G. Wells story from 1895, with the absurdity intact but the wonderment missing." He noted that 800,000 years into the future, it was awfully convenient that Morlock and Eloi both seem to speak English. Guy Pearce's performance also didn't match the material. Ebert wrote that "Pearce, as the hero, makes the mistake of trying to give a good and realistic performance. Irons at least knows what kind of movie he's in, and hams it up accordingly."

Okay, so "The Time Machine" is not great. But Irons is fun. It certainly didn't deserve to bomb.

This is the best type of wireless earbuds for audio output

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

What design actually delivers the best sound?

Wireless earbud design has evolved in leaps and bounds. Some of the earliest models would occasionally lose sync between each ear, never mind questions like battery life or sweat resistance. Now, the best wireless earbuds can last an entire workday and will survive the sweatiest gym sessions. You can even use the AirPods Pro 3 as hearing aids.

Our question today is this: What design actually delivers the best sound? The industry has evolved to support many different form factors, but not all of them are actually geared towards fidelity. Some may prioritize maximum comfort, particular environments or simply a consumer price point. Make the wrong choice, and the results can be extremely disappointing.

Considerations include the type of eartips, how buds secure to your ear, and noise cancelation features. We'll also explain when it's worth making some sacrifices, since vanilla audio may be better than nothing at all.

When it comes to form factor, you'll want to begin with in-ear (sometimes referred to as in-canal) earbuds. These provide several major benefits to audio quality over open-ear designs, beginning with passive noise cancellation. You're blocking most of the outside world, although some noise will inevitably seep through. This positioning also maximizes the perceived volume of each bud, which can help you catch more of the nuances in your movies and music. You should even experience better bass response because the buds are directly rattling your ear and the air inside it.

From here, further narrow your search to models with foam or silicone eartips. These boost passive cancellation as well as bass response, the latter by trapping frequencies inside a space with increased air pressure. For this reason, it's vital to find tips that form a tight fit. You can sometimes upgrade to third-party tips if the ones bundled with your buds aren't doing the job. Keep in mind that while foam is preferable for sound quality, it's less than hygienic for workouts because it's harder to clean.

Speaking of fit, it's also important to choose buds that lock in as securely as possible without becoming painful to wear. When buds are loose, they can break that ever-so-important sound quality seal, even if you have great tips. You don't necessarily need buds with an external clip, but those may help if you want both quality and the freedom to sprint or do hanging crunches.

You'll also want a design with active noise cancellation (ANC). Passive cancellation merely isolates sound. ANC generates "anti-noise," which partly or completely neutralizing the outside world. This is essential not just for hearing quieter elements in your audio, but also for eliminating distractions such as plane engines or nearby conversation.

Some people intentionally choose open-ear or bone conduction earbuds, despite sacrificing significant bass output and noise cancellation. The reason is simple: situational awareness. If you're running or cycling outside, for example, you can't afford to block out ambient noise because you need to be able to hear an engine, horn, siren or yell warning you of imminent danger. Many in-ear ANC products (like AirPods) compensate by offering "transparency" modes, but of course, it's hard to beat direct exposure to your environment.

Bone conduction buds can sometimes operate in conditions where other designs are unusable, such as underwater. Those benefits go out the window with models that add air-based drivers into the mix to improve bass. All bone conduction buds do, however, deliver many frequencies straight to your inner ear, allowing them to remain "pure" despite mixing with ambience.

You'll probably recognize immediately whether these sacrifices are essential. When in doubt, it's better to default to in-ear ANC buds. There's just no way of recovering the lost fidelity if you opt for something else.

Cowboys passing attack should shine, pass rush could struggle

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The Dallas Cowboys desperately need a win, and Sunday's matchup against the Tennessee Titans offers a chance to get something positive going after all the Christian Parker drama. Tennessee is 1-8 and has one of the league's worst defenses, particularly in the secondary. Dallas should have plenty of opportunities to move the ball through the air, especially with CeeDee Lamb and George Pickens coming off productive performances.

There are still reasons to be cautious. The Cowboys' defense has been a disaster this year, and Tennessee quarterback Cam Ward has improved over the past few weeks. With Dallas also changing its defensive play-calling, this matchup could tell us a lot about the team's direction. Here are two players to buy and one area to sell ahead of Sunday's game.

CeeDee Lamb has been on a tear this year, and this week's matchup against Tennessee should give him another opportunity to put up big numbers, so it's no surprise he's on this list again. Lamb is coming off a seven-catch, 72-yard, one-touchdown performance against San Francisco. That came after his 10-catch, 195-yard outing against Indianapolis.

Through 10 games, Lamb has 77 receptions for 976 yards and eight touchdowns. He's averaging 97.6 receiving yards per game and has become the most consistent weapon in Dak Prescott's passing attack. This matchup is appealing. Tennessee is allowing 266.9 passing yards per game, the most in the NFL. The Titans have struggled to contain opposing receivers, with Cleveland's KC Concepcion, Cincinnati's Tee Higgins and Jacksonville's Parker Washington all putting up big performances against them in recent weeks.

Lamb should have plenty of opportunities to take advantage of that secondary. Whether he's working out of the slot or lining up outside, the Cowboys need to make sure he's involved early and often. With eight touchdowns already on the season, there's little reason to believe his production is slowing down.

I'm not buying the Cowboys' pass rush this week, even after the unit recorded five sacks against San Francisco. That might sound strange, but Tennessee has allowed just 14 sacks this season. The concern is what Cam Ward has been doing with his legs and how the Titans have adjusted their offense to better suit him.

Ward has been much more comfortable over the past few weeks. He's completed 54 of 79 passes for 535 yards, four touchdowns and one interception across his last two games, posting a 98.9 passer rating. He's also been more involved as a runner, and Tennessee has increasingly used tempo and designed quarterback runs to create opportunities for him.

The Titans' offensive approach has changed considerably. They've incorporated more no-huddle looks and opportunities for Ward to use his mobility. Against Jacksonville, Tennessee used no-huddle on 49% of its offensive plays, the highest rate by any team this season.

That's a concern for Dallas. The Cowboys have struggled to contain quarterbacks who can escape the pocket, and their pass rush has too often lost its discipline when trying to generate pressure. If the defensive front gets too aggressive and opens running lanes, Ward has the mobility to make them pay.

There is also a new wrinkle on the Dallas sideline. Mike Pettine will take over defensive play-calling after the Cowboys removed those responsibilities from Christian Parker. Maybe the change helps, but it's difficult to expect an immediate turnaround from a defense that has struggled with execution and consistency all season.

If Lamb is the Cowboys' most consistent weapon, George Pickens might be their most dangerous big-play threat. After missing time with a hamstring injury, Pickens returned against San Francisco and delivered the best performance of his Dallas career.

Pickens hauled in 11 receptions for 145 yards and three touchdowns on 18 targets. He set career highs in catches and receiving touchdowns, giving Prescott another reliable option in a passing game that has been productive throughout the season.

Through nine games, Pickens has 50 receptions for 678 yards and five touchdowns. He's averaging 13.6 yards per catch and has shown the ability to stretch the field and win contested catches. Tennessee's secondary has struggled to contain receivers all season, and its recent performances suggest opposing offenses can find plenty of downfield opportunities. The Titans are allowing the most passing yards per game in the league, and they've surrendered several big performances to opposing receivers over the past month.

With Lamb commanding attention from the secondary, Pickens should have opportunities to exploit one-on-one matchups. The Cowboys have two legitimate receiving threats capable of taking over a game, and this is the type of matchup where both could produce.

Check what your AI agents do before they do it, with Semantic governance policies

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Two days before earnings, an employee asks their coworker agent to send the preliminary quarterly results to an investor who says they're under NDA, and to copy the employee's personal Gmail so they can review the numbers tonight. The agent can read the file and send email, so it fulfills the user request. Semantic governance policies, available in Preview in Gemini platform, enable enterprises to govern their agents and provide guardrails to stop that call before it runs. They check each tool call an agent proposes against what the user asked for and against rules you write in plain language.

Permissions alone can't catch this. They say what an agent can do, not whether a particular call follows your rules. A model can also be talked out of the rules in its system instructions, by a user with a good reason, or by a document telling it to do something nobody asked for, a technique called indirect prompt injection. So the check has to sit outside the agent. Semantic governance policies are enforced through a policy enforcement point, such as Agent Gateway, alongside the agent governance controls we expanded in October, so policies stay separate from your agent's code.

Semantic governance policies check each tool call your agent's model proposes, before it runs, against two things:

Today, the policy enforcement point that Semantic governance policies support is Agent Gateway. You need to configure your agent so all your agent egress traffic goes through Agent Gateway, facilitating traffic between your agent and its model. When the model responds with a tool call, Agent Gateway sends the proposed call to the Semantic governance policy engine for evaluation before the response reaches your agent.

In the policy engine, an LLM judge evaluates the proposed call against the conversation so far, the agent's system instructions and tool descriptions, and your policies.

If the call passes, the response reaches your agent unchanged. If it doesn't, the engine replaces the response's content, tool calls included, with a configurable denial message, so the call never runs. Either way, the verdict and the judge's rationale go to Cloud Logging in your project. Responses that contain only text, such as an answer, a plan, or a question back to the user, aren't evaluated, because they take no action.

Consider a coworker agent for a finance team. It can search the shared drive, read files, send email, and delete draft files, and its system instructions include the date of the next earnings release. The company writes one policy for the agent:

"Quarterly financial results (revenue, margins, bookings, guidance) should not be shared publicly until the public earnings release. How to securely connect internal Cloud Run MCP servers to Gemini Enterprise

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Enterprise teams adopting Gemini Enterprise are increasingly building custom Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers to connect AI agents with internal APIs, databases, and business workflows. Google Cloud Run is a natural fit for hosting these lightweight StreamableHTTP MCP servers: it scales automatically, manages TLS certificates out of the box, and integrates natively with Cloud IAM.

When the Egress Agent Gateway receives a tool call for your Cloud Run MCP server URL, it resolves the hostname once before injecting the packet into your VPC. Without Private Cloud DNS, public DNS resolves .run.app domains to public internet IPs, which time out (HTTP 502) because a PSC Network Attachment does not route out to the public internet.

Instead of creating a wildcard *.run.app private zone, which would override DNS resolution for every Cloud Run service in a Shared VPC, you create a Private Cloud DNS zone scoped exclusively to your MCP service's exact fully qualified domain name (FQDN) and peer only that domain with the Agent Gateway. Because Cloud DNS uses longest-suffix matching, every other Cloud Run service in your Shared VPC continues resolving normally. Meanwhile, the Agent Gateway opens its HTTPS connection to the private VIP (199.36.153.8 or 240.0.0.10) while preserving your Cloud Run service hostname in the TLS SNI and HTTP Host header so Cloud Run routes the request to your exact container.

Deploy your StreamableHTTP MCP server to Cloud Run with internal-only ingress and IAM authentication enabled, and grant the Cloud Run Invoker role to your project's Gemini Enterprise service agent so only your Gemini Enterprise instance can invoke the container.

Enable Private Google Access on your regional VPC subnet and create a Private Service Connect Network Attachment set to accept connections automatically. Next, create a Google-managed Egress Agent Gateway in Agent-to-Anywhere mode, grant networking and DNS peering permissions to the auto-created gateway service accounts, attach your network attachment, and apply an outbound authorization policy.

In Cloud DNS, create a private managed zone attached to your VPC for only your MCP service's exact Cloud Run hostname. Point its A record to Google's Private Google Access VIPs (or to your internal PSC endpoint IP for isolated VPCs without a default internet route), and configure DNS peering on the Agent Gateway for that single hostname.

In the Gemini Enterprise console under Security Configuration, bind your Egress Agent Gateway to your app. Register your Custom MCP Server data store with your Cloud Run endpoint and OAuth 2.0 client credentials, and enable Agent Gateway egress and Actions mode on the connector.

Reload your custom actions in the console and run a test prompt in the Gemini Enterprise web app. In Cloud Logging, you can verify that tool initialization, discovery, and execution calls route privately through the Egress Agent Gateway to your internal Cloud Run container with both service IAM and user OAuth 2.0 checks passing.
