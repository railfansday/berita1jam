WoW: Forever Exploit Lands Streamers In Hot Water After Farming Thousands Of Gold

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A lucrative World of Warcraft: Forever dungeon-farming exploit that netted some players hundreds of gold per hour has been hotfixed, and Blizzard is taking action against accounts that (temporarily) walked away with thousands in loot.

For the dedicated few who have already reached max level in the new version of Blizzard's MMORPG, a new endgame dungeon, Blackmaw Hold, proved to be quite lucrative. In the dungeon, a room with 30 non-elite enemies proved to be the perfect spot for area-of-effect gold farming, since it's easy to access.

As detailed by Wowhead, the farm was as simple as running to the room in question, killing all the mobs, looting them, resetting the dungeon, and repeating. Not only did the enemies drop anywhere from 25 to 50 silver each, but they also dropped items that could be sold either to a vendor or on the auction house for even more profit.

And some players, including prominent streamers, did just that during Forever's second weekend. One streamer, Ziqo, who was previously in hot water for accepting illegal gold during the game's beta, revealed he made 4,000 gold from the farm. On stream, Ziqo named other streamers who did the same and said his only regret was choosing to sleep for four hours instead of farming longer.

On places like the WoW: Forever subreddit, players worried about the effect such a huge amount of questionably obtained gold would have on the MMO's economy, especially since the game has been out for less than two weeks. Many called on Blizzard to take action against accounts, including streamers, who benefited from the farm.

Blizzard issued a hotfix on November 16, making the enemies from Blackmaw Hold no longer drop loot. In a forum post the following day, Blizzard explained what went wrong and why it was taking action. While Blizzard said finding good farming spots is part of old-school WoW, the enemies players were farming in Blackmaw Hold were incorrectly dropping loot that would normally be reserved for elites, an obvious mistake.

Players who ran the dungeon a few times and "benefited organically from the extra loot did nothing wrong" and don't need to worry, Blizzard said. However, a few "hundred players worldwide" spent "significant time exclusively farming that group of enemies."

Those players are now in more serious trouble. Blizzard locked their ability to engage in Forever's wider economy following the exploit, and said it is removing gold they earned through "exploitative gains." Additionally, "significant abusers" of the exploit will be handed account suspensions.

"As always, we advise all players to refrain from abusing game mechanics that are clearly unintended," Blizzard said. "We will continue to take action against intentional abuse."

The Blackmaw Hold farm wasn't the first gold controversy in Forever, and it certainly won't be the last. During the game's beta, streamers were caught accepting suspicious amounts of gold and encouraging their communities to illegally buy gold on their behalf. As a result, Blizzard issued suspensions, made in-game changes, and made it clear it would crack down not only on accounts selling illegal gold, but also on those buying or accepting it.

Farming exploits aside, Forever's launch has been relatively smooth, though Blizzard can't seem to decide how much experience players should get in dungeons. Despite Forever's launch breaking WoW concurrent-player records, it still wasn't enough to get the MMO nominated for Best Ongoing Game at The Game Awards 2026.

This smell startup says its AI nose is as good as a human’s

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Osmo’s smell model is already being used in the fragrance industry. But are medical diagnostics and threat detection a sniff too far?

In a lab near Newark Airport in New Jersey, a team of around 30 experienced human sniffers are slowly working their way through every odor on Earth. Their careful notes—“nutty,” “peachy,” “marine”—are cross-referenced with a chemical analysis to create a rich set of training data for what AI startup Osmo calls the world’s first foundation model for smell. The company’s goal, says founder Alex Wiltschko, is “to give computers a sense of smell.”

“Scent molecules are really the only language most species on this planet can speak,” he says. “Most species can’t communicate with sound or visually, so it truly is Earth’s original language. And it’s incredibly powerful for us, from a memory and an emotional standpoint. You can smell something once and remember it for the rest of your life. You can smell something on the street and be pulled back to high school.”

In the last few months, Wiltschko claims, Osmo’s model has achieved “human-level olfactory intelligence.” The company’s definition of this is that the model is about as good as the average person at detecting and discriminating between smells, and about as good as a junior perfume designer at creating them. It says its technology is already being used in the fragrance industry to help brands design and manufacture new scents. Last month, the company says, Osmo’s AI model outperformed in-house perfumers at one of the world’s largest consumer goods companies. Wiltschko believes it could be deployed eventually for threat detection at airports, for example, or diagnosing diseases.

Wiltschko spun the startup out of his work at Google Brain, the search giant’s former AI research lab, in 2023, and he is running the same playbook as AI startups in other domains: Throw a lot of data at difficult problems, and see what happens. The challenge with scent, Wiltschko says, is that there was a dearth of good data. Before Osmo, there were fewer than 200,000 human descriptions of smells in existing databases, by his count. Plus, the chemical sensor devices needed to collect data are expensive, and the fragrance industry is secretive about its work on olfactory science.

A lot of the company’s work has been in creating its own dataset of more than 10 million human labels and 800 million “scent tokens,” which describe the exact molecules and concentrations that go into creating specific odors. Osmo uses spectrometers and other chemical sensors, running around the clock, to break down the molecular makeup of everything from fruit and flowers to pantry  staples and organs. A team of around 70 trained data labelers in the US and overseas spend their days sniffing things and writing down what they smell like, using a standardized fragrance vocabulary.

Mapping out scents in this way is throwing out some unlikely parallels. In the same way that changing the order of words can drastically influence the meaning of a sentence, rearranging scent molecules in different proportions can drastically alter the smells humans perceive. “The scent tokens that define the smell of a newborn baby have massive overlap with the scent tokens in a fresh plate of cooked spaghetti,” Wiltschko says. (Specifically Barilla brand spaghetti, he says, which perhaps raises some questions for the people at the pasta factory.)

The company also creates its own scents, using a smell-printing robot the size of a school bus that mixes ingredients according to precise formulas to make samples for Osmo’s own research and fragrances for paying customers. It can design and print scents to order based on a ChatGPT-style prompt. Wiltschko flipped open his laptop to show me how the AI model worked. He opened a text box on screen, and I asked him to generate a fragrance that smelled like a Portuguese custard tart (we were in Lisbon for Web Summit), aimed at 18- to 23-year-old men. The system spent a few minutes cogitating, then generated three options for potential scent profiles that matched the brief (creamy, coconutty, fruity) and the molecules needed to create them. If I’d continued with the ordering process, my signature L’Eau de Custard would have been ready about a week later.

Osmo’s technology has already been used to create retail fragrances, including for giants like Procter & Gamble. The Gates Foundation, which invested $5 million when Osmo launched, later gave the company a $3.5 million grant to assess molecules that repel disease-carrying insects, and Osmo identified eight substances that were more repellent than DEET. Osmo has also worked with StockX, the online clothing marketplace, on using smell to spot counterfeit sneakers by detecting differences in the chemical composition of the materials used in the shoes.

But not everyone is convinced by the technology or the company’s big claims. Avery Gilbert, a smell scientist and author of What the Nose Knows, has been sharply critical of Osmo on his Substack, arguing that some of its marketing claims are undocumented, and that its patent filings still rely on human sensory panels and perfumers, rather than AI. “This heavy reliance on human sensory judgments seems odd for a company premised on super-cool AI,” he wrote in March. A representative for Osmo did not respond in time for publication.

Michael Schmuker, a former professor of neural computation at the University of Hertfordshire who has previously consulted for Osmo, says the company’s technology is an achievement. With colleagues, he created an open-source, reverse-engineered version of Osmo’s 2023 model, but it lacks the reams of data the company has collected since.

But even with all this smell data, Osmo may be missing some of the factors that make human olfaction unique, Schmuker says. “Smell is super complex. Context is very important for smell. The same molecule may evoke a different association depending on context,” he says. “[The smell of] a French cheese like Camembert is partly the same molecule as in used nappies. You don’t confuse the two because of the context.”

In a controlled setting like a fragrance lab, this lack of context may not matter so much. But it could have an impact if Osmo’s technology becomes, as Wiltschko suggests, the “olfactive channel” for AI world models. Imagine a machine that could sniff out spoilage in food on a production line, or detect tumors through smell the way dogs have been trained to do in pilot studies.

For that to happen, sensor technology will need to get a lot better, Schmuker says. The models will need to keep improving. Medical diagnostics is the biggest prize.

“There’s a lot of history of companies that have gone directly at that mountain peak and, unfortunately, not been successful, because it is very expensive and it is very difficult,” Wiltschko says. "So what we’re doing is building this foundation at the foothills of all these mountains. We’re sending expeditions up to the mountains, doing explorations, and basically smelling the whole world.”

Apple HomeView Review (2026): Should’ve Been Better

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I never thought Apple would launch a smart display.

Apple has been historically late to the game in the smart-home world, if it bothers showing up at all. It’s had the most minimal smart speaker (and general smart home) offerings of any of the major brands, with just the HomePod and HomePod mini compared with the variety of speakers and smart home products Amazon and Google have launched in the past decade. A limited selection isn't necessarily a bad thing, but the Apple HomePods have also never excelled in our testing, either, and Apple's overall ecosystem has had the most limited compatibility compared with other devices (though Matter is helping expand that).

With the HomeView's arrival, Apple is making its first foray into the smart display category, nearly 10 years after Amazon’s first Echo Show model. While the HomeView manages to do some things well, it's once again a product made for only the biggest of Apple enthusiasts, and will shine best for users who have an iCloud subscription, love Apple Music, and will invest in Apple’s new, larger smart-home ecosystem, which has been designed in collaboration with LG and Schneider Electric. The HomeView does feel handy to use for making calls and sending texts—more so than any Amazon Echo Show ever was—and I love that I can use it to find my iPhone when I lose it around the house.

Still, I'd love to have been more impressed at this price point. After nearly two weeks of using the HomeView in my house, I find I use it most as a smart-home controller and to answer quick questions, and occasionally to FaceTime someone. Siri struggled to complete some of my music requests and took a long time to think about simple questions. Plus, some features on the device overall were roadblocked by my not having a full commitment to iCloud and Apple Photos. It's a device primarily for Apple devotees—especially the ones who will buy all of Apple's new smart-home products.

The most surprising thing to me is how small this device is. Maybe I've been overly influenced by the announcement of Amazon’s Echo Show 16, which has a generous 16-inch display (hence the name), but the HomeView screen is just 7 inches and square, giving it a different look from most other smart displays, which go for a rectangular design. My colleague Julian Chokkattu described it as a “giant Apple Watch,” and I agree. The swiping side to side to switch between 10 different preloaded faces and the menu bar also feel incredibly similar to the Apple Watch.

Pressing and holding on the face of the HomeView allows you to customize your various “faces” (or remove them—it's a lot of options to have preloaded, in my opinion, and I found myself swiping through too frequently to find the smart home page). You can also click on the plus button to add other faces or informational pages like weather and views of your home cameras. But that's all you can add, at least for now. There's no app store, but the device can reflect certain apps from your phone, along with general apps you'd find on an Apple device like Safari, FaceTime, Messages, Find My, Alarms, and more. I was surprised to see Spotify, Libby, and Yoto specifically ported over from my phone, but no other non-Apple apps have made it over so far, at least from my own collection of downloaded apps.

There isn't a Siri AI app on the HomeView like on my iPhone, but the HomeView does use Siri AI to power its intelligence and answer your questions. You'll get a more conversational response than regular Siri, but I didn't find its answers to be that much more impressive. Siri ran into issues playing my music—it asks me whether the music should come from Books, TikTok, or Spotify if I don't specify, and then most of the time it still can't play the song on Spotify, even with my account set up. It also couldn't use Find My to find my iPhone when I asked. Both of these applications worked perfectly fine on the HomeView screen itself, but the on-device Siri isn't blowing me away thus far.

In fact, most things worked better by touch than voice. Spotify voice requests didn't seem to work, but the Spotify app on the device worked fine, as did using the handoff feature to send Spotify music from my phone to the HomeView. The Find My feature also didn't work over voice command—when I called my phone the same name I had seen it referred to on the HomeView while playing music, it claimed I had no device under that name. However, tapping the Find My app on the HomeView let me find my phone in a list of devices and tap it to make it start going off. While I'm bummed Siri won't let me just voice-request it, I am thrilled to have a faster way to find my iPhone lost in my house than pulling up iCloud.com.

I tested the HomeView with both an iPhone Air and an older iPhone 16 Pro, but you'll want a newer phone like the Air (or 17 Pro, 18 Pro, or iPhone Duo) to get the most out of HomeView and Siri, since that's what gives you access to the expressive, customizable Siri voices. Switching to my original phone left me stuck with a boring Siri voice that I can't customize like I could when using the iPhone Air. No matter which phone you use, you'll also need to update your phone's operating system to OS 27.2.1 in order to set up and connect to the HomeView (which also brings Siri AI onto your phone, much to my husband's chagrin).

The main appeal of the HomeView, at least in my opinion, is the fact that it can control your smart-home devices with touch or voice. This is especially relevant since Apple is rolling out a larger smart-home ecosystem, with several devices coming later this year as part of its Apple Home Collection, including indoor and outdoor cameras, a smart thermostat, a video doorbell, a smart lock, and a couple of sensors. You can already control any Apple Home–compatible device on the HomeView, though, without needing Apple's lineup to release.

The HomeView provides a similar experience to other smart displays, with a page you can swipe to for accessing all of the devices set up through your Apple Home app. It's easy to see the devices and quickly control them. Apple Home's biggest issue, though, continues to be compatibility. Many smart devices are only Amazon- and Google-compatible, though Matter is fixing some of that issue; I was able to connect the Ikea Varmblixt ($100) smart lamp to my Apple Home app thanks to the Matter compatibility. It was fast and seamless to control once set up, both over voice and touch.

You can also touch to access your texts and FaceTime your contacts with the HomeView, which has been a pretty smooth experience for me. What's most interesting, though, is how the HomeView will verify that it's only you looking at your text messages. If it sees that someone else in your family is trying to look at or access your texts, it'll prompt that family member to switch over to their own account. It only works if they're alone in range of the device's camera, though; if they're peeking over your shoulder, access won't be denied.

Apple isn't using FaceID for this, surprisingly enough. It uses biometric data to figure out who's asking via a protected part of the processor Apple calls the Secure Exclave. It's a new technology that's also on the Apple Watch; we'll have to see how it pans out in real time.

Of course, we can't talk about a smart display and speaker without talking about one of the key features: the speaker.

Sound is a big part of the enjoyment from this device, and while I enjoyed the audio quality, I found it frustrating to voice-request a song. When I asked for music without prompting which app it should come from, Siri asked me if I'd like it to come from Books, TikTok, or Spotify. And when I would request a song on Spotify, Siri would say I needed to “unlock my device,” but didn't specify if that needed to happen on my iPhone or my HomeView. Or it would simply tell me there was a problem and tell me to try again.

This is a huge bummer for my day-to-day use: I have a 4-year-old who loves making his own music requests and has gotten excellent at asking Google to play his latest favorite song (we're finally coming off of him requesting “Ghostbusters” and "This Is Halloween" on a daily basis).

Ultimately, the easiest way to play music as a Spotify user was using the handoff feature. The HomeView has a proximity-sharing feature similar to when you tap two iPhones to AirDrop a photo or other information, and it was much, much easier to pull up a song on my iPhone and simply place it near the HomeView to send what I was listening to to the speaker. I did really like that experience, but I think Spotify users will prefer other smart speakers if music playback is a primary feature they’re looking for.

The sound itself was pretty good; I was happy jamming from a range of music, whether an autumn lo-fi mix or “The Call of the Mountains,” by Eluveitie. My long-time favorite song, “Dark Blue,” by Jack's Mannequin, sounded as clear with its piano keys and background drums as it did back in 2007 when I first started listening to it. The HomeView’s audio comes from the cylinder-like base that's only about 3 inches wide, giving it a slimmer profile than most smart display bases and even just stand-alone small smart speakers (the Google Home Speaker is about 5 inches at its widest point, for comparison). Inside that base sit two full-range drivers, a down-firing woofer that promises Spatial Audio, and two force-canceling passive radiators to build out the sound.

So, would I tell you to buy this? Only if you're a total Apple devotee who would use all of your preferred, Apple-run apps in tandem with the Apple HomeView. But it's really best if you're looking to invest in an Apple-run smart home (or already have one) and want a touchscreen to control it with. Or love FaceTiming so much you want an extra device for it. Otherwise, you'd be just as happy with cheaper smart displays or a simple smart speaker.

A Scottish hacker who was part of the Scattered Spider hacking collective has been sentenced to six years in U.S. federal prison for his role in a scheme that stole at least $11.6 million in crypto.

Tyler Robert Buchanan, 24, of Dundee, was sentenced to 72 months by a federal judge in Santa Ana, California, on Tuesday, the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Central District of California said. The judge set a restitution hearing for December 11. Buchanan began hacking as a teenager and was part of Scattered Spider, the group of young, English-speaking hackers blamed for a string of high-profile cyberattacks, the BBC reported.

Buchanan pleaded guilty in April to conspiracy to commit wire fraud and aggravated identity theft. At the time, prosecutors said the scheme had stolen at least $8 million in crypto. That figure has since risen to at least $11.6 million.

Between September 2021 and April 2023, Buchanan and his co-conspirators sent hundreds of phishing text messages to employees of at least a dozen companies, including telecoms, technology firms and crypto companies, according to the indictment and Buchanan's plea agreement. The messages linked to fake websites that captured employees' login details, which were sent to a Telegram channel run by Buchanan and another co-conspirator.

The group used information stolen from those companies to identify individual crypto holders, then carried out SIM swaps, tricking mobile carriers into moving victims' phone numbers to devices they controlled so they could intercept two-factor authentication codes and empty victims' wallets.

In the largest single theft, one victim lost $1.67 million in December 2022, according to the BBC. When authorities searched Buchanan's home in Scotland in April 2023, they found the names and addresses of numerous victims, and a text file containing crypto seed phrases and login details for one victim's account, prosecutors said.

"Those thefts had severe, life-altering consequences for many victims," prosecutors argued in a sentencing memo.

Buchanan is the fifth and final defendant to be sentenced in the case, which the Justice Department charged in November 2024. A Justice Department spokesperson confirmed at the time that the defendants were suspected members of Scattered Spider, the group behind high-profile attacks including the 2023 breach of MGM Resorts. Buchanan was arrested at Palma de Mallorca Airport in Spain in May 2024 while trying to board a private flight to Naples, the BBC reported, and has been in U.S. custody since April 2025.

His co-defendant Noah Urban, of Palm Coast, Florida, is serving 10 years in prison, while Ahmed Hossam Eldin Elbadawy and Evans Onyeaka Osiebo were each sentenced to 45 months, and Joel Martin Evans to two years.

U.S. authorities have continued to pursue alleged Scattered Spider members. In July, a 19-year-old suspect was extradited from Finland over an $8 million crypto ransom demand against a luxury jeweler.

The Best Photos From NASA’s Voyager 1 Spacecraft, Which Is Now 1 Light-Day From Earth

The Best Photos From NASA’s Voyager 1 Spacecraft, Which Is Now 1 Light-Day From Earth

NASA’s Voyager 1 spacecraft, which launched on September 5, 1977, is officially one light-day away from Earth.

Now about 16 billion miles (around 26 billion kilometers) from Earth, Voyager 1 is the first human-made object to reach this distance. While space enthusiasts are very familiar with the concept of a light-year, a “light-day” is far less commonly used. However, that doesn’t make it any less impressive.

To put Voyager 1’s accomplishment into perspective, if someone flashed a light on Earth right now, it would take an entire day to reach the spacecraft. By comparison, light needs just over 130 milliseconds to circle the globe and about 500 seconds to travel from the Sun—which sits nearly 93 million miles (about 150 million kilometers) away.

Voyager 1 is at a truly mind-boggling distance from Earth. As NASA puts it, “Nothing in the universe travels faster than light. Moving through space about 186,000 miles every second, it crosses enormous distances in a given time period, whether it’s a second, a day, or a year.”

NASA also shared other interesting light-distances. The Moon is 1.3 light-seconds away, even though it took Artemis II astronauts about three days to get there. Neptune, the most distant planet in the Solar System (sorry, Pluto), is about four light-hours and 10 light-minutes away.

Voyager 1 will continue traveling toward AC +79 3888, a star also known as Gliese 445. This obscure star lies more than 17 light-years away, and Voyager 1 is projected to pass within 1.7 light-years of it in a staggering 40,000 years—despite the spacecraft’s cruising speed of nearly 38,000 miles per hour (61,000 kilometers per hour).

As for Voyager 1’s sibling spacecraft, Voyager 2, it will reach one light-day from Earth in November 2035. Despite its name and current position behind Voyager 1, Voyager 2 actually launched a couple of weeks earlier. However, Voyager 1 took a faster path through the Solar System. Voyager 2 will never catch up.

It’s unclear just how far Voyager 1 will ultimately travel. Theoretically, it will never stop as long as the universe exists. While interstellar dust could slow the spacecraft or potentially even grind it down to nothing, Voyager 1 would have to hit a lot of dust just to cut its speed in half. Something human beings made, touched with their own hands, and launched into space will travel out into the cosmos forever. Humanity will never truly know what happens to Voyager 1, though, as communications are expected to be lost in about 10 years.

Man United transfer news: Major new rival emerges in race for Alejandro Balde

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By Axel Clody | 18 Nov 2026 10:15

Lacking game time at Barcelona, Alejandro Balde is looking for a way out in January, with Paris Saint-Germain only just appearing in the picture.

Alejandro Balde is set to be one of the talking points of the winter transfer window. Struggling at Barcelona, where his game time has fallen considerably, the 23-year-old left-back is considering leaving to find a bigger role elsewhere.

His record this season is particularly meagre, with five appearances and only 124 minutes played. It is a situation that contrasts sharply with the status the Spaniard enjoyed in Catalonia until very recently.

According to the latest information from Spain and Italy, several clubs are following his situation. Juventus are considering a loan, while Monaco are eyeing a bigger move. Now Paris Saint-Germain and Manchester United have also entered the fray.

Manchester United are reportedly going all out to sign Balde in January.

According to TEAMtalk, the club’s recruitment department believes the left-back has a high ceiling and Barcelona are open to a loan, which would allow Manchester United to assess him before committing to a permanent deal.

The same report says Balde is willing to move to the Premier League and that his relationship with Barcelona manager Hansi Flick is ‘completely broken and glacial’.

Fabrizio Romano has described the chance of a January exit as ‘serious and concrete’, adding that Balde is ‘genuinely ready to consider leaving’ and exploring options with his agent, Jorge Mendes. Manchester United were interested in the summer but did not proceed, with Balde considered too expensive for a left-back, in talks said to have involved a fee of €50m-€55m (£42.5m-£46.5m) and a net salary of more than €5m (£4.3m).

Valued at around €40m (£34m), the Barcelona player represents a market opportunity for Paris Saint-Germain. At 23, Balde already has significant experience at the highest level and still has room for improvement.

Quick and explosive, he fits the profile of a modern full-back and offers better guarantees than Lucas Digne, the disappointing summer signing. His ability to get in behind and create overloads would give Luis Enrique’s team another attacking dimension, and the move could also be a fresh start for a player who has lost confidence and continuity at Barcelona, not least because Luis Enrique rotates his starting line-up heavily.

The main obstacle is Nuno Mendes, who owns the left-back position at Paris Saint-Germain and is one of the best players in the world in that role, if not the best.

At 24, the Portuguese is a major part of the club’s project, and the left-footed Balde cannot play on the right side of defence, unless the manager comes up with something.

Luis Enrique also generally prefers a right-footed winger on the left, so Paris Saint-Germain could sign an excellent player without offering him a lasting prospect of starting, with a real risk of frustration.
