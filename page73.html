A coding agent reads a memory file that says the checkout migration is blocked on database approval.

The approval landed last week. The migration shipped. The task closed. Nobody removed the note.

The agent now plans around a blocker that no longer exists.

This failure is easy to misdiagnose as weak retrieval. The agent retrieved the exact memory the team gave it. The problem is lifecycle: temporary state survived without an owner, status, or expiry, so old context still looked current.

Do not solve stale memory by adding more memory.

When every line looks identical, the agent cannot tell which one is current or authoritative.

A deployment, owner, ticket, or customer configuration moved on. A copied note did not.

A blocker or next step survived after the branch merged.

Search retrieves the idea without the verdict.

The old record remains useful history but no longer governs current work.

A rule written for one provider or service gets applied everywhere.

Root, nested, personal, and team rules disagree. The agent may pick one arbitrarily.

The maintenance strategy depends on which form you have.

A tool may store several categories, but the records still need distinct fields and lifecycle rules.

Not every note needs every field. Significant team decisions do. Temporary handoffs need task and status. Live facts need a verifiable source and observation time.

A timestamp alone is weak. The newest note may still be an unapproved proposal. Status and ownership establish meaning.

When a decision changes, keep the old record but mark it superseded and link the replacement.

The new record should name what it replaces. This lets an agent follow an old search result to the current answer and preserves the reason the system used to behave differently.

Deleting history removes useful context. Leaving old history unmarked creates false current context.

This reduces duplicates and keeps freshness tied to the system that owns the state.

A simple CI or review check can flag a current handoff that points to a closed task or has not changed for a chosen period.

Paramount’s 4-Part Action Fantasy Epic Has One of the Best Seasons in TV History

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

With one of the best villains of all time

Few things feel more grown-up than dealing with the consequences of your actions. Whether you stay up too late and still have to get up the next day or make a mistake at work and have to answer for it, adulthood means no one’s coming to fix things. For the most part, it’s all up to you.

The Legend of Korra has been the subject of much criticism over the years, but it’s always been a show about its young hero Korra (Janet Varney) growing from a feisty teen into a relatively less feisty adult. It’s not until season 3 that she experiences her biggest change, dealing with the consequences of one of her biggest decisions: leaving the spirit world portals open.

It’s one of the best decisions Korra makes, but it also creates her most thought-provoking villain.

Season 3 is appropriately titled “Book 3: Change,” and it picks up after the Harmonic Convergence, the planetary alignment that flooded the world with spiritual energy. After a great battle (literally, Korra becomes a kaiju via astral projection), Korra leaves the portals open, believing that people and the spirits should live in harmony together. It’s a decision with vast ramifications, from spirits adorably popping up throughout Republic City to the awakening of air-bending skills in random people, reviving a culture that had been nearly lost.

One of these new benders is Zaheer (Henry Rollins), a member of the Red Lotus, which is a secret anarchist organization whose mission is to disrupt world order and eliminate the Avatar cycle. Zaheer uses his airbending to escape his high-security prison and rescue his comrades, who are some of the coolest villains in animation. These include the lavabender Ghazan (Peter Giles), waterbender Ming Hua (Grey DeLisle), and combustion bender P’li (Kristy Wu).

Meanwhile, Korra and her team travel the world, slowly collecting new airbenders in the hopes of rebuilding the Air Nation.

“Change” is a blockbuster of a third season, taking full advantage of Studio Mir’s animation capabilities. It’s the one season the studio animated from start to finish, and it shows. While The Legend of Korra always had incredible fights, in this season, the bending takes center stage. Ming Hua offers a new flavor of waterbending; she doesn’t have arms, so she fashions several out of water, looking like a terrifying spider. P’Li’s combustion is accompanied by a high-pitched whine, with the energy launching like a rocket through her third eye. Her hair is braided, and she’s huge. On a design level, these are the types of characters you only need to see once to remember forever.

Increasing the sense of peril is the fact that the Red Lotus feels so adult and experienced when compared to Korra and her friends. Zaheer and P’Li are devoted partners. Together, these four are close comrades, with their experiences and crimes weathered on their skin. It results in some of the franchise’s best showdowns, often involving characters that were previously sidelined, like Tenzin (J.K. Simmons), who has a standout battle in the Air Temple, and Korra’s father Tonraq (James Remar), who comes to her aid and prevents her from being kidnapped by the Red Lotus.

Then there’s Zaheer himself. He is a blank slate, the quietest and least charming member of a gang of incredibly cool villains. He’s so detached from the world and so at home with his ideologies that he feels inaccessible — no one’s convincing this guy of anything. His one tether to the world is P’Li, a relationship that the season cashes in on in spectacular fashion.

In arguably one of the coldest moments in kids' animation, Zaheer kills the Earth Queen by taking the air out of her lungs. Her death throws Ba Sing Se into chaos. And while the series never backs away from his extremism, like every great villain, Zaheer makes a lot of sense. His beliefs cause Korra to wonder if maybe the world would be better off without corrupt leaders like the Earth Queen, who lives surrounded by obscene wealth and imprisons her subjects for her own gain while the people in her kingdom suffer.

When it comes to Korra, there’s so much breathing room when the show finally drops her love triangle with Mako (David Faustino) and Asami (Seychelle Gabriel). It’s a development that allows Korra to focus on other things, like how bad she is at talking to bureaucrats or her having to hold back when speaking with people that are incredibly annoying.

Revenge is a dish best served 12 years later

Korra gets to explore her dynamics with other characters, including Suyin (Anne Heche) and Lin Beifong (Mindy Sterling), two standouts in the franchise. She’s also frequently paired with Asami, with the two finally moving past the fact that they dated the same guy (even if they’ll end up falling in love with each other the following season).

“I think Korra was more likable in Book 3 because — but that was actually part of her arc that we’d set out to do — is that she was becoming more selfless, and it wasn’t just about her own stuff,” co-creator Bryan Konietzko told The Mary Sue in 2014.

By the end of the “Change,” Korra has saved the world once more, but is more depleted than ever. After being poisoned by the Red Lotus, she’s in a wheelchair, watching her past self’s civilization restored as the Air Nomads rise once more. Another adult lesson Korra learns? Sometimes life’s greatest moments are bittersweet.

TL;DR: A November 6 update from blockchain intelligence firm Elliptic says roughly $124 million of assets stolen in the October Binance breach had been converted into Bitcoin, including funds originally held as XRP and SOL. Onchain tracing can reveal movement, but it cannot by itself identify every person controlling those wallets or prove that all the converted funds have been cashed out.

The laundering trail from the huge October cryptocurrency theft attributed by investigators to North Korean-linked actors continues to evolve. In a November 6 update to its technical analysis, Elliptic said the value of stolen assets converted into Bitcoin had reached approximately $124 million.

The headline figure is striking, but the more useful story concerns how the funds moved, what remains traceable and why conversion into Bitcoin is not the same as spending money through the conventional financial system.

Elliptic’s investigation describes activity involving tens of thousands of wallets, multiple networks and an assortment of services. By its November 6 checkpoint, analysts had identified about 1,650 BTC held in new addresses following conversion from other stolen assets. The firm said roughly $50 million worth of XRP had begun moving largely towards Bitcoin, while stolen Solana tokens had also been swapped, bridged or mixed.

The firm presented this as an ongoing, rapidly changing investigation. An estimated figure tied to a timestamp should not be reported as a permanent total, and wallet clusters can change as further transactions occur.

Cross-chain movement complicates tracing because the deposit and payout sides of a transfer may occur on different networks. It can also make the trail harder to follow when funds pass through pools, aggregators and intermediaries. However, the public nature of blockchain transactions and the ability to connect wallet activity through known services can still help investigators reconstruct substantial portions of the flow.

Elliptic reported that approximately $2.85 billion of the original stolen assets had been frozen and about $3.11 billion remained in addresses linked to the theft at its latest checkpoint. The firm cautioned that only a negligible portion appeared to have been cashed out. Such estimates may change with later tracing, and asset values vary with market prices.

This matters because a criminal actor may hold large amounts of Bitcoin or Ether for some time before attempting to move value through brokers or exchanges. Public addresses can show coins being shuffled, but they generally cannot prove the real-world identity of each subsequent controller or the final ownership arrangement.

The distinction is especially relevant to compliance services. Screening against attributed addresses can alert businesses to potential sanctions or illicit-funds exposure, even when the funds have crossed networks. Yet address blacklists and automated risk scoring are not perfect substitutes for investigation and human judgement.

The evolving theft occurred alongside a different security incident affecting Chainalysis, another blockchain-analysis provider. The two incidents should not be conflated. Chainalysis described its own systems and credentials issue as unrelated to the Binance theft, while Elliptic’s work here concerns tracing funds from that separate exchange incident.

For operators, the lesson connects to the custody, counterparty and incident-response themes covered in NewsBTC’s reporting on digital-asset infrastructure restrictions. Protecting users involves controls over withdrawals, counterparties and incident communications — not simply watching one chain’s transaction explorer.

The next meaningful updates will concern verified freezes, attributed movements to identifiable services and evidence of actual cash-out. Elliptic’s data show the stolen funds are still moving through complex paths. They do not yet tell a complete story about who ultimately receives them or how much can be recovered.

"That's a question I can only answer partially. I can answer it fully, but I'll decline to answer part of it just because it wouldn't be the right thing to do. But I'll tell you this, we are doing the right things defensively in terms of scheme. And I know that because I've coached defense for decades and been fortunate to be around some of the best defenses in football, quite frankly. You go back and look at the Saints and 49ers and some of those teams and we were great.

"You have no option (but to keep showing up). You can tap out and you can hang up your cleats and go away, but these guys aren't built like that. It's not easy at all."

"There's just a real inconsistency to the way we're performing right now defensively. You see some really good plays and then we're not exacting enough in our gap control in the run, we're not challenging enough in certain situations in pass. You see that third down dig route they caught and you'll go 'well it's the DBs.' Well, it wasn't. Underneath we weren't in our proper space. We were chasing ghosts, chasing decoy routes. There's a function of playing with discipline because these are disciplined players."

The Hawkeyes (7-2, 4-2 Big Ten) have allowed only two touchdowns in the past three games. They recorded six sacks and didn't allow the Wildcats inside their 26-yard line until the fourth quarter.

Brown was 16 of 21 passing for 126 yards with an interception after going 20 for 24 in last week's win over Wisconsin.

Northwestern has dropped two straight after a 6-1 start. The Wildcats (6-3, 3-3), who won twice at their temporary lakefront stadium before winning the first three games at their new $875 million home, were trying to move to 6-0 at home for the first time in 122 years.

Iowa, which scored on 98-yard drives in each of its previous two games, had a 97-yard drive Saturday.

Marchiol connected with Joseph Hilmon II on a 2-yard touchdown pass with 1:51 to play.

Iowa: Linebacker Cam Buffington had 12 tackles to lead the Hawkeyes for the fifth time this season, his fourth game with double-digit tackles.

Northwestern: The Wildcats had been converting third downs at a 46% clip and were 5 for 7 on fourth down with the dual threat Chiles at quarterback this season, but Iowa held Northwestern to 1 for 11 on third down and 1 for 4 on fourth down.

Simple Plan Are Meeting a Whole New Generation of Fans, 25 Years In

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Drummer Chuck Comeau talks TikTok, Good Things Festival, and why a farewell tour is "not in the cards"

Simple Plan have been playing the same songs for 25 years. Lately, they’re playing them to a lot of people hearing them live for the first time.

“Every night we ask people, ‘Hey, how many people are here for the first time?’” drummer Chuck Comeau tells Billboard. “Sometimes it’s like 85% have never seen the band.”

The Montreal pop-punk band is drawing fans who weren’t born when “I’m Just a Kid” arrived in 2002, alongside the original listeners who are now bringing their own kids to shows. Some of the younger crowd have even started calling Simple Plan “classic rock.” Related Deep Purple's Ian Gillan Shares Encouraging News After Undergoing Surgery Richard Lloyd, Television Guitarist, Dies at 75 Benson Boone Brings Young Fan Battling Cancer Onstage at Brisbane Concert Explore Simple Plan See latest videos, charts and news

“And you’re like, ‘What are you talking about?’” Comeau says.

“There’s nothing cooler than what’s happening to our band right now,” he adds.

Much of that can be traced to “I’m Just a Kid” going viral on TikTok, where it soundtracked a challenge encouraging users to recreate their childhood photos. Comeau calls the trend a “gigantic gift,” but says meeting fans online has always come naturally to the band, which was filming backstage antics and sharing them through its website and DVDs long before TikTok existed.

“It’s the same thing,” he says. “It’s just a new platform.”

The renewed attention builds on a strong early run. The band’s 2004 album Still Not Getting Any… debuted at No. 3 on the Billboard 200, while “Perfect” and “Welcome to My Life” reached Nos. 24 and 40, respectively, on the Billboard Hot 100.

Next up is a return to Australia for Good Things Festival, where Simple Plan join a bill led by Limp Bizkit and featuring Spiritbox, the All-American Rejects, Bad Religion and MxPx. The festival hits Melbourne’s Flemington Racecourse on Dec. 4, Sydney Showground on Dec. 5 and Brisbane Showgrounds on Dec. 6, marking the band’s third trip Down Under in three years.

“We love Australia. What can I say?” Comeau says. “Any excuse, we come back.”

The lineup mixes pop-punk with nu-metal, hardcore and heavier rock, the kind of bill Comeau admits might once have made him nervous. “Being on this lineup would have been a bit more intimidating 25 years ago,” he says.

He credits streaming with breaking down the scene boundaries that once divided fans, making it easier for younger listeners to move between genres. The band has seen it firsthand at European festivals, sharing stages with acts like Slipknot.

“At first, you’re a little terrified,” he says. “You’re like, ‘How’s that gonna go?’ And then you actually realize that people are stoked.”

He has his own Good Things wish list. Bad Religion, whom he calls his favorite band, are near the top, along with Swedish punks Millencolin, who are playing Pennybridge Pioneers in full. He’s also keen to finally see Limp Bizkit live. “I’ve seen a lot of crazy videos right now that are popping up everywhere,” he says.

Meanwhile, Simple Plan have started writing their seventh studio album, with work set to continue into 2027. There’s no release date, but Comeau says the band has a batch of songs it’s excited about, and no intention of rushing.

“We’ve been around for 25 years and we only have six albums,” he says. “So we take our time.”

The record will follow 2022’s Harder Than It Looks, and Comeau says the aim is to refine the band’s sound rather than overhaul it. “We know who we are,” he says. “We’re not going to turn around and make a black metal record.”

The harder challenge is competing with the band’s own past. Songs that soundtracked fans’ teenage years carry a weight that new material has to earn.

“When you get to this stage of your career, you’re almost playing against yourself,” Comeau says. “You’re playing against your history.”

“You’re not the new shiny young band anymore,” he adds. “So you’ve got to work harder to have people pay attention.”

That’s why he won’t put a deadline on it. “There’s never a good time for a bad record, and there’s never a bad time for a good record,” he says.

Next year also marks 25 years since the band’s debut album, No Pads, No Helmets…Just Balls. Comeau says a special-edition vinyl and a few anniversary performances are on the table, but not a full album tour.

“I think we’re bigger than just one album,” he says. “But that album was really consequential for us. It’s the one that started it all.”

Another milestone arrives March 9, 2027, when the band releases its first children’s picture book, I’m Just a Kid: The Story of Simple Plan, illustrated by Jomike Tejido. Comeau says it grew out of watching the band’s original fans grow up and start families of their own.

“We were with them throughout their difficult moments,” he says, recalling how songs about breakups, school and friendships became part of listeners’ teenage years. “Fast-forward 20 years, 25 years, now they’re starting families, they’re having kids. We want to be there with them in this new chapter of their lives.”

The book explores friendship and perseverance and includes nods for longtime fans. “It’s as much for the parents as it is for the kids,” Comeau says.

For all the anniversaries and nostalgia, Simple Plan have never broken up, gone on hiatus or staged a reunion, which is rare among their peers. Comeau believes the years since the pandemic have been some of the band’s best, with bigger crowds and a sharper focus on the live show.

“When we were young, you were going out and you were partying,” he says. “Now it’s like, no, we don’t do that anymore. We’re all doing yoga and stretching.”

“We’ve always just kept going,” he says. “I feel like there’s a lot of work to be done.”

“There’s no talk about a farewell tour,” Comeau says. “Not in the cards for now.”

9 Near-Perfect Dragon Age Replacements For Fans of Fantasy RPGs

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Messy, morally gray RPGs wherever you look

BioWare’s Dragon Age is a fantasy RPG series about warring factions, world-ending threats, and, yes, the occasional dragon – but that’s the first-glance take. Get into one Dragon Age game (start with Origins!) and you’ll see that the true heart of this series is the people you drag into hell with you.

The choices you can make in Dragon Age are messy. Your companions get legitimately attached to you — or not; your dating life can complicate your mission; and innocuous decisions you make in hour one can affect the sweeping political options available to you in the series’ biggest moments. Whether you seek more of that exact mix of party banter, branching loyalty quests, and a world that remembers you, or you’re intrigued by the same bones in a different skin — sci-fi crew instead of fantasy party, perhaps — here are 9 games to add to your queue.

Why you’ll like this if you like Dragon Age: The cast of well-written characters and intricate combat system (along with Larian Studios’ whimsical charm)

Where to play: PS4, PS5, Windows PC, Xbox Series X

Although Divinity: Original Sin and its sequel are set 1,000 years apart, in both, you’re protecting your fantasy world, Rivellon, from a magical energy (Source) and monsters emanating from the series’ overarching Void. But, luckily, you’re not tackling that alone. Each game gives you the opportunity to gather a party of intrepid explorers to save the world, but, more importantly, bicker constantly as you do.

This dynamic within Divinity: Original Sin and especially Divinity: Original Sin 2 makes the characters the true reason for replay after replay. Sure, you’re solving a murder mystery in Divinity: Original Sin and escaping imprisonment, then surviving the wilds in Divinity: Original Sin 2 — but those are details. Wait until you meet Sebille. You’ll either be best friends or unable to stand each other immediately.

Why you’ll like this if you like Dragon Age: It’s a 2002 game that helped form today’s RPG genre of real-feeling companions and branching questlines

Where to play: Windows PC, PS4, PS5, Switch, Windows PC, Xbox Series X

Before Dragon Age, there was Neverwinter Nights — a fellow BioWare title that paved the way straight to Dragon Age. At the start of the game, a fatal plague called the Wailing Death (chill; normal) is striking the city of Neverwinter. You’re in quarantine, but you still need to graduate from the Neverwinter Academy and figure out how to help Lady Aribeth create a cure for the plague. People are panicking. There are riots to navigate. Everything’s absolute chaos behind the city’s closed walls.

Since Neverwinter Nights is nearly a 25-year-old-game, some parts of it are harder to love than others, but it’s worth a play for Dragon Age players seeking a nostalgic title.

Why you’ll like this if you like Dragon Age: It’s got similar-feeling companion depth and combat primed for a party tactician

Where to play: Windows PC, PS5, Xbox Series X, Switch

In Warhammer 40,000: Rogue Trader by Owlcat Games, you’re exploring uncharted space…specifically, the Koronus Expanse. But while the setting differs from mostly land-based Dragon Age, the problems your characters face are similar. Here, you’re navigating a succession crisis and working to claim your place within the Von Valancius family. Much like Dragon Age’s Blight and Darkspawn, Rogue Trader has its share of existential corruption for you to weather (here, it’s Chaos and Warp mutations) while you also have to deal with the possibility of much more immediate betrayal.

Dragon Age players will recognize Rogue Trader’s starting class and character background system – will you be a Warrior who was a military officer, or a priest-turned-Operative? – but the turn-based combat will be a change of pace.

Why you’ll like this if you like Dragon Age: Everywhere you turn, there are fiendishly difficult narrative choices. Buckle up

Where to play: PS4, PS5, Switch, Windows PC, Xbox Series X

Welcome to Baldur’s Gate 2 — surprise, you’re trapped in an underground laboratory. You’re Gorion’s Ward, a Bhaalspawn (e.g., kid of the murder god Bhaal). There’s a devilish wizard, Jon Irenicus, who’s torturing you – ahem, conducting tests to study your essence. You need to escape, rescue your also-trapped childhood friend Imoen, and then rest and recover…as long as nothing happens to interfere with that plan, of course.

In Baldur’s Gate 2, as in the Dragon Age series, you’re learning about the world around you and figuring out who you can trust or recruit to help you accomplish a goal that’s both very personal and potentially world-ending. But here, there are vampires.

Baldur’s Gate 2 is another BioWare title from before the company moved away from D&D, so the two games also share developer DNA. (As a bonus, if you’re a Dragon Age person and you’ve not played Baldur’s Gate 3…please do.)

Why you’ll like this if you like Dragon Age: It’s got the same BioWare choice-and-companion architecture. Just swap your sword for an assault rifle

Where to play: PS4, PS5, Windows PC, Xbox Series X

Mass Effect and Dragon Age are both BioWare RPG franchises with relationship building, storytelling, and party- or squad-based tactics. In each, your choices shape the narrative. In each, you can use save data from one game to influence the next. In each, you have a base — a camp or a spaceship — where you can regroup with your companions.

But that’s where the similarities end. In Mass Effect, you start as newcomer Commander Shepard aboard an advanced starship. You’re working to investigate a beacon on a human colony world…until things may or may not very suddenly take a turn for the worse (brace for attack!). But at least you’re fighting alongside friends. Getting to hang with the sullen, sarcastic Garrus Vakarian might be one of the greatest joys in the series.

Why you’ll like this if you like Dragon Age: It’s got party-based tactics and large-scale kingdom management.

Where to play: PS4, PS5, Windows PC, Xbox Series X

If the political games you could play within Dragon Age: Origins were the draw for you, Pathfinder: Kingmaker has that in spades. With even more management systems layered on top. Here, you start the game as a group of adventurers gathered by one Lady Jamandi Alordi. You’re going to the Stolen Lands to bring back the head of the Stag Lord. If you do, you get to found your own barony. If not…well, let’s not contemplate that alternative, shall we?

While this game isn’t just routine kingdom management – there are mysterious fey threats and an ancient curse getting in the way of your day-to-day – steering Pathfinder: Kingmaker’s plot via being a decent lord to your barony is a big focus in this game. But, if you’re looking for explicitly similar structure to feel anchored here, Dragon Age: Origins players rejoice: the combat is real-time-with-pause, which should feel just like home.

Your character is a Legate tasked with traveling to an island untouched by the plague to discover the cure, but you’re not the only one with that idea. As multiple colonial factions – including the religious nation Thélème and the fond-of-science Bridge Alliance – fight over the island, your job is simple: Protect the island, search for the plague’s cure, and fend off political disaster. You do find the titular Crown of the Magister along the way, an artifact that might just be able to help you save your world.

Dragon Age players who loved the series’ focus on deep character writing may find Solasta a bit lacking. Solasta’s focus is not on story. Instead, you’re managing turn-based combat, organizing inventories, and figuring out how to use your class abilities to their greatest effect.

To the “less of a story” point, though, Solasta’s sequel is in development. It’s theorized that the team’s leading harder into story this time around, so stay tuned.
