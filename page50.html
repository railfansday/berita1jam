China Unveils Their Plan for a Crewed Moon Landing

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Yesterday, on November 3rd, the deputy chief designer of China's crewed space program and chief designer of the lunar exploration program (Wang Xiang) unveiled an official statement detailing its plans for a crewed mission to the Moon. Under the proposed architecture, China will conduct two launches using the Long March 10 rocket, sending the Lanyue lunar lander and the Mengzhou crewed spaceship to the Moon, where they will rendezvous in orbit. Of the three taikonaut crew, two will then transfer from the spaceship to the lander and descend to the surface to conduct science operations.

The plans were outlined in a keynote speech Wang delivered at the 7th Manned Space Symposium, held from Oct. 31st to Nov.1st at Beihang University in Beijing. This year's theme was "Gathering Wisdom for the Galaxy: Digital and Intelligent Empowerment for High-Quality Development of Manned Spaceflight," and saw more than 800 experts and scholars from fields related to crewed spaceflight convene to discuss strategies, technologies, and the hurdles China's space program will face.

These plans echo the preliminary plan statements made at the 9th China (International) Commercial Aerospace Forum in July 2023. As Deputy chief engineer Zhang Hailian of the China Manned Space Engineering (CMSE) office said at the time, two Long March-10 rockets would launch the elements to the Moon separately. Zhang's slideshow also included a rendering of a lander depicted on a mural inside China's Manned Lunar Deep Exploration Project Office.

The CMSE unveiled the lander design to the world in September 2023 and officially named it Lanyue ("embracing the Moon") in early 2024. Since then, development of the Long March 10, Mengzhou spacecraft, and Lanyue lander has proceeded apace and reached several key milestones. This included a tethered test of the lander last year, which successfully simulated descent, guidance, control, and engine shutdown.

The Mengzhou spacecraft also successfully completed a series of zero-height abort and maximum dynamic pressure escape tests in June 2025 while the Long March-10 successfully completed a low-altitude demonstration and validation test earlier this year. These developmental milestones place China on track to complete a crewed lunar mission by 2030.

In an interview with China's state-controlled media company, CCTV, Wang said that their planning approach follows two principles and will consist of using different vehicle variants for different stages. To prepare for rendezvous in lunar orbit, China will use the Tiangong space station in Low Earth Orbit (LEO) to validate the rockets, lander and space capsule. An uncrewed flight mission of the Lanyue lander will also happen before a crew landing is attempted, which will gather critical environmental data on the luanr surface.

In addition, Wang highlighted how in preparation for crewed missions, the mission components will all be tested in an uncrewed state first:

We will use the near-Earth version of the Long March 10 to launch the near-Earth version of the Mengzhou spacecraft to perform space station missions in Low-Earth Orbit, validating the rocket's basic technologies and the critical re-entry and return phases of the spacecraft. Then we will use the lunar version of the Long March 10 to launch the lunar version of the Mengzhou spacecraft to execute the lunar landing mission. This is the validation process for the rocket: near-Earth first, then the Moon.

Artist's recreation of the two Chinese spacecraft that will put 'taikonauts' on the Moon. Credit: CSM

For the near-Earth phase of the plan, the Long March 10A (CZ 10A) will be used, a single stick variant with only two stages and no boosters. For the lunar mission, the standard, three-stage variant with additional boosters will be used, which has a payload capacity of 27 metric tons (~30 U.S. tons). This process is virtually identical to what NASA has been pursuing with its Artemis Program. Initially, NASA hoped to use the Block 2 variant of the Space Launch System (SLS) to deliver crew and payloads to the lunar surface (which has since been cancelled).

China's incremental "near-Earth first, then the Moon" approach is also very similar to the NASA's current launch plans. Before a crewed Orion spacecraft flew four astronauts around the Moon with Artemis II, NASA launched the uncrewed Artemis I capsule on a circumlunar flight to validate all the key systems. China's stated plans for an orbital rendezvous is also similar to NASA's revised plans for the Artemis III mission scheduled for next year, involving the seperate launch and an Orion spacecraft with a yet-to-be-determined Human Landing System (HLS).

"In an uncrewed state, we will validate many key technologies of the entire crewed process as possible, including launch, orbiting the Moon, docking with the Mengzhou spacecraft, landing and staying on the lunar surface, taking off again, and re-docking with the Mengzhou spacecraft," Wang added. "This is to ensure that the crewed flight is safe and reliable." Wang further indicated that the program has established system plans, technical standards and quality requirements under a single development framework.

China's long-term vision includes the creation of an International Lunar Research Station, the construction of which is currently planned to commence in 2031 using the super heavy-lift Long March 9 rockets. Roscosmo's involvement in the project is unclear at this time due to Russia's ongoing war in Ukraine and the way this has led to record lows in terms of annual launches and delays in the super-heavy Yenisei rocket. The details of this base are sitll somewhat vague, with the stated description claiming it will be located on the surface or in orbit.

A Roman-era belt buckle discovered in a Ukrainian forest hints at the ancient conflict between the Romans and the Dacians.

Archaeologists in Ukraine have found a colorful belt buckle that may have been worn by a nomadic equestrian warrior 1,800 years ago on the fringes of the Roman Empire.

The belt buckle was unearthed by local residents in the Tsetsyn Forest in southeastern Ukraine and given to Antonii Moisei, a professor at Bukovinian State Medical University, according to a translated statement. The roughly square metal buckle, which is about 0.8 inches (2.1 centimeters) on each side, has teal, blue and yellow enamel decorations.

The style of the decoration and the metal construction place the creation of the buckle in the second to third centuries A.D., when this part of Ukraine was in the ancient province of Roman Dacia, according to Moisei.

The column that the Roman emperor Trajan erected in Rome following the empire's triumph in the Dacian Wars.

Under Emperor Trajan, Rome fought the Dacian Wars (A.D. 101-102 and 105-106) against the denizens of the Dacian kingdom and its leader, Decebalus. Roman legions destroyed Dacia and annexed it as a new province. Trajan celebrated his victory in Rome, complete with a famous column that depicts details of the Dacian Wars.

In addition to the buckle, Moisei collected a decorative belt fitting and three small, round metal objects that probably belonged to the same belt. Moisei created a reconstruction of what the belt might have looked like before it was lost in the forest nearly two millennia ago.

But it's unclear who lost the belt. One of Moisei's theories is that the belt belonged to a horse-riding Sarmatian warrior originally from the Black Sea region. The Sarmatians were a group of tribes that lived outside the borders of the Roman Empire, in what are now central Ukraine, southern Russia and the Balkans. While some Sarmatian warriors fought against the incursion of the Roman Empire, others fought with Rome as mercenaries.

The metal belt fragments are part of a Roman provincial craft tradition, according to the statement, and they may have been obtained through trade or as a trophy of war. But Moisei is still studying the artifacts to confirm whether they were Sarmatian. Either way, the military belt provides new evidence of Roman military contact on the outskirts of the empire, and future work could help experts understand how the Romans interacted with the local Dacians during wartime.

Harriet Phillips Says Raising Her Daughter Solo Was “Painful.” But Now She Can Ask Kate Middleton for Advice

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Harriet Phillips, the wife of Peter Phillips, son of Princess Anne, sat down with Hello! for an exclusive interview five months after her royal wedding, which took place at All Saints’ Church in Kemble, Gloucestershire, the village where she grew up. The ceremony was attended by King Charles III and Queen Camilla, as well as many other members of the royal family.

For Harriet, who until then led a life far removed from high society, her official entry into one of the world’s most famous royal families inevitably brought about some big changes.

Harriet and Peter Phillips at Royal Ascot

She and her daughter, Georgina, now live at Gatcombe, Princess Anne’s estate. Peter’s sister, Zara Tindall, also lives there with her husband, Mike, and their three children. After 24 years with the British National Health Service (NHS), where she specialized in child mental health and caring for the most vulnerable children, Harriet left the NHS to embark on a new professional venture at a private foundation, continuing to focus on early-childhood development.

She has also taken on her first official role as patron of Home-Start Cotswolds, an organization that supports families with young children. “Becoming a parent can be one of the most wonderful experiences in life, but it can also feel overwhelming and isolating,” Phillips told Hello! “We often assume that parenting comes naturally, but the reality is that everyone needs support at different points. Home-Start offers vital, practical, and nonjudgmental support. Just having someone to listen can make an enormous difference.”

For Harriet, the decision to leave the health service does not represent a break with the past: “It’s been an enormous privilege to spend 24 years working in the NHS, and I’ve loved my career. It has taught me so much, professionally and personally. Over time, I’ve become increasingly passionate about the importance of promoting healthy brain development. If we can support parents during this critical early period, we have an opportunity to make a lasting difference for children.”

The Phillips couple at the 2026 DMMI Royal Charity Polo Cup

Harriet then looked back on her love story with Peter, which began in an entirely unexpected way. The two first met properly in early 2022, when their three daughters were on the same hockey team. At first there was just some casual small talk between them, then walks with their dogs, followed by dinners at pubs away from prying eyes and, finally, evenings spent cooking together. A low-key courtship, away from the spotlight, blossomed into a love story.

When she met Peter, there wasn’t a “thunderbolt moment”; Harriet said she simply felt comfortable with him, enjoying an unspoken sense of calm. “I just felt incredibly at ease with him and experienced a lovely, peaceful, safe feeling when talking to him—like being with a really good friend,” she told Hello!

He was the one who made the first move: “Peter was the brave one in recognizing that there was something between us and acting on it. This time, I thought, If I’m lucky enough to meet someone again, I want things to move slowly and ensure that everything is entirely right.” Then she added, unable to hide her happiness: “He is very romantic, loving, kind, loyal, amazing, and my best friend.” Their early dates were simple and low-key. “He’s a great cook and I love cooking. He’s great at barbecues. It was wholesome and suited to who we both are as people,” she said.

On July 25, 2025, during a weekend in the south of England, Peter proposed on a beach. “We were on a weekend getaway in the south of England on a hot day during the heat wave. He asked me to marry him on the beach; I was completely overcome,” she said. “He had the ring made at Pragnell beforehand—it’s absolutely beautiful.” Neither of their families had been told about the surprise: “It was the best-kept secret ever.”

During the interview, Harriet also reflected on her childhood in Gloucestershire and fondly remembered her parents and their long marriage. “I was brought up in a village in the middle of fields. My parents celebrated an incredible 51 years of marriage, and sadly, my father died just before Peter and I got together.” It was an experience that left a deep mark on her life, and today, as she said in reflecting on her own love story, Harriet also sees it in the context of meeting the Princess Royal’s son: “I often think maybe that’s why Peter came along.”

The loss of her father, and the difficulties she faced in the years that followed, helped shape her outlook on life. Harriet explained: “There is a beauty that comes out of difficult situations. A beautiful jewel is formed under pressure. Difficulties in life enable you to relate to people and build empathy.”

Harriet and Peter with Princess Royal Anne

Harriet then talked about her first meeting with Princess Anne, her eventual mother-in-law, which was much more informal than she had imagined it would be. They met in the garden at Gatcombe, sitting on benches and drinking cups of tea. “There was always going to be initial nerves about meeting anyone’s family members, but I am quite a calm person,” she said. “It was really relaxed…It couldn’t have been nicer.”

Harriet has come to appreciate, above all, the princess’s dedication to her work and local charities: “She sets an amazing example with her work ethic. What I’ve seen is her tireless attention to local charities and her genuine interests. It’s important to do things that match who you are, where you live, and what your passions are.”

And their relationship already seems very strong. “She’s brilliant, really good fun, and straight-talking,” Harriet said. “She’s incredibly generous—we had our wedding reception at Gatcombe, and it was amazing and special for Peter and I to have our wedding where he grew up.”

Charles and Camilla also welcomed her warmly right from the start, as seen in the various photos taken at public events. Her first meeting with the king and the queen consort took place at Royal Ascot: “It was so lovely and relaxed and there was lots of laughter. You feel as though you’re meeting family, but at the same time, it feels otherworldly. Both the king and the queen have been so welcoming to me and Georgina. I can’t thank them both enough.”

The couple with the king and queen at Royal Ascot

After their wedding, Harriet and Peter attended several public events, including Wimbledon. In September, they were together at Somerset House in London for the opening of the exhibition “Reviving Craft: Reflection of the Mind,” dedicated to traditional Chinese craftsmanship and featuring over 70 artists.

Harriet also spoke with Hello! about Kate Middleton, including the admiration she feels for her and the bond they’ve formed. “I can look to Catherine for advice and guidance,” she said. She recalled a special moment during a vacation on the Isles of Scilly: The children (Georgina, George, Charlotte, and Louis) found themselves playing in the water together, in a spontaneous and informal atmosphere. “I’ve had an incredible experience being welcomed in. We happened to be on holiday in the same place. It was great because the children were all jumping into the water at the same time,” she shared. “It was very casual, but there is a clear level of respect there.” And speaking of the Princess of Wales, she added: “She’s very openhearted, and I would always feel comfortable asking her if I needed advice about something.”

Her first stay at Balmoral with her daughter was also a special experience. Scotland is one of Harriet’s favorite destinations, and vacations with the royal family have remained surprisingly simple: “It’s the loveliest family holiday with an extraordinary layer…. We go walking and running, play football with the children, cycle in the hills, and go wild swimming, which is fantastic.”

Then, inevitably, her memories turned to the wedding, an event marked by emotional moments. Harriet got ready with her mother and the three girls in the family: Georgina, Savannah, and Isla. Then her brother, Nicholas, arrived to drive her to the church. “It was quite wet and it had rained that morning, but the girls kept my train completely white and immaculate,” said said. “They did a really good job.”

In the church, decorated with floral arrangements that seemed to bring nature indoors, Harriet carried a bouquet with myrtle and lily of the valley: “All my family members have had myrtle in their wedding flowers, so I continued that tradition.”

Before going in, the couple prayed together with the vicar. “Then [we] walked into the fragrance of the flowers in the church. It was incredible,” she said. “It’s such a beautiful church, and I grew up going to it. It’s tiny—we were all squished in!” The presence of the king, the queen, and other members of the royal family didn’t distract her: “I was so focused on being in the moment. Of course, you’re aware that people are around you, but I really wanted to enjoy it and be present. I was so happy. That’s very natural for me. I was peaceful. I prayed. Maybe the peace of God came over me.”

The reception took place in the garden at Gatcombe. The sun came out at one point; there was live music, Champagne, and a casual menu featuring lamb raised on the estate. Instead of the traditional wedding cake, there was a huge tiramisu. “There was lots of laughter and it was an amazing family day. It finished at midnight, but everyone wanted it to carry on,” she said.

Before meeting Peter, Harriet was a single mother raising her preteen daughter, Georgina. It wasn’t always an easy journey, which made her particularly sensitive to the challenges that parents face and the importance of having a support network.

“Although at times it was a painful experience, I did have the support of people who loved us. Any time in life when you’re under pressure or facing adversity, something good always comes from it,” she said. “Lean in; don’t be scared.” Her Christian faith played an important role—“It’s definitely the foundation of how I live; it’s like a golden thread that has woven through my life. I was brought up in a Christian family, but this is a little bit different because it’s like making a decision for yourself: It is a personal relationship and a conscious choice, rather than just being religious.”

Harriet is very proud of her daughter and the relationship they’ve built. “Becoming a mother gives you an inner strength to protect your loved one and face whatever comes,” she said. “Everyone has challenges in life that are dressed up differently. Maybe this has been my challenge—the experience of bringing up a child on my own. I’ve been able to see the good things that have come out of it.”

Harriet and Peter with their three daughters on their wedding day

Today her family has grown, and Harriet looks to the future with enthusiasm, but without rushing: “Peter and I are so fortunate to have such wonderful girls between us. I’m excited about what’s to come with our family and always mindful that going gently is so important.”

Her new life with Peter doesn’t mean setting aside her own identity. “I will continue as I am. I’m a professional and will continue working in the area I’m passionate about,” she said. “I’ve got a wealth of knowledge that I don’t want to waste, so I will continue to use that.” When asked what she’d like to accomplish in the next 12 months, she gave an answer that once again came back to children and families: raising greater awareness of the work of Home-Start Cotswolds. As she explained, “We don’t just support babies by caring for them directly—we support babies by supporting the people who care for them.”

It’s a philosophy that also seems to capture her new sense of balance in her life. She has joined the royal family without ceasing to be Harriet, the woman who for 24 years worked alongside children and their parents, and who today has found in Peter a life partner and, above all, a best friend.

Her mission at Home-Start brings her even closer to the Princess of Wales, who has also been committed for years to supporting the well-being of children in their early years, a crucial period that shapes their personalities and determines the adults they will become. Who knows if the two women, now close friends, might one day form a professional partnership as well.

Nabrawind’s Crane-Free Wind Towers Face a Supplier Test

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Spain’s Nabrawind has developed a way to install exceptionally tall wind turbine towers without bringing an enormous crawler crane to the site. Its Nabralift system uses a tubular upper tower supported by a three-legged steel structure. Hydraulic equipment raises the assembled turbine while modular sections are inserted below it near ground level. The lifting frame can then be removed and used on another installation. This addresses genuine logistical and construction problems, particularly at remote sites where specialist cranes are expensive to transport and where wind can interrupt conventional lifting operations.

The company has progressed beyond demonstration-scale hardware. In September 2026, UL Solutions issued type certification for Envision’s 7.8 MW turbine on Nabrawind’s 188-meter tower. Fortescue is using 17 of those tower configurations at the 133 MW Nullagine wind project in Western Australia’s Pilbara region, where construction is underway. Nabrawind previously erected a 160-meter prototype in Spain in under two weeks and has supplied smaller commercial towers in Morocco and France.

The engineering benefits are credible. Nabrawind reports using roughly 20% less tower steel than comparable conventional tubular designs and reducing foundation concrete by as much as 80%, depending on the design and site. Those are supplier claims rather than independently established savings across all wind farms. The potential to avoid large cranes, simplify transport and raise turbines in windier conditions could also matter substantially to total installed cost and construction schedules. High hub heights only earn their keep when those costs are justified by the electricity generated over the project’s life.

The current deployment has an unusual commercial structure. Fortescue acquired Nabrawind in September 2025 and is building Nullagine to serve its own mining operations. Fortescue has the capital, internal electricity demand and industrial incentive to support a technology it owns. Successful installation in that context would be important evidence of construction performance, but it would not establish that an unrelated wind developer can finance and maintain the same system under ordinary third-party commercial arrangements.

Nabralift’s proprietary construction approach creates a procurement issue. Conventional tubular towers can be sourced from several qualified manufacturers, and experienced contractors can compete for much of their construction work. A developer selecting Nabralift currently depends on one supplier for the core tower system, specialist lifting knowledge and associated support. Wind turbine original-equipment manufacturers also create supplier dependencies, but concentrating another critical part of the project in a proprietary installation system requires its own contract and risk assessment.

A type certificate provides valuable technical assurance about the certified turbine-and-tower configuration. A lender or insurer still needs enforceable delivery schedules, long-term warranties, repair and maintenance provisions, clarity on replacement parts, and a workable remedy if the supplier’s capacity or commercial priorities change. These questions become more important when a wind farm must operate for 25 years or longer and there is no qualified alternative provider of the same system.

Nabrawind’s next important proof point is an independently developed and financed large wind farm that accepts those risks on commercial terms. Qualified installation partners, credible long-term service arrangements and greater competitive capacity could make its engineering advantages easier for developers to buy. The 188-meter tower has earned technical attention; its broader market depends on financing and procurement confidence catching up with the machinery.

OpenAI’s chief global affairs officer Chris Lehane thinks the 2028 presidential election could be a defining moment for AI. And he wants politicians to start developing policies that address Americans’ concerns about the technology, rather than simply paying lip service to anti-AI sentiment and the backlash against data centers.

In a new essay, Lehane compares AI’s potential to reshape the economy to President Franklin D. Roosevelt’s New Deal, the 1930s set of federal programs and reforms enacted after industrialization and the Great Depression shattered the old economic order. He observes that Americans today feel similarly anxious about losing control over their economic futures and urges politicians to find a “compelling third path” that allows people to share in AI’s benefits. He lays out five policy priorities, spanning worker protections and education to AI safety and data center development.

The essay comes on the heels of a stunning Democratic victory in the midterm elections, in which Democrats flipped control of Congress amid widespread voter frustration over the cost of living. Kate Brennan, who leads AI and corporate power policy at the AI Now Institute, told TechCrunch that Lehane’s rhetoric marks a notable shift from OpenAI’s policy agenda last year, which focused on accelerating AI development, reducing regulatory barriers, and expanding infrastructure.

Unsurprisingly, many of Lehane’s proposals would encourage wider AI adoption and support the infrastructure expansion companies like OpenAI need to grow. The original New Deal established protections for workers and placed constraints on corporate power. It’s less clear how Lehane’s vision would hold AI companies accountable for the economic disruption their technology could create.

“Invoking a New Deal metaphor is to invoke state capacity to actually benefit the public,” Brennan said. “This is written by a private company that is trying to advocate for policies for its own benefit. It is ahistorical at best and harmful at worst.”

While AI wasn’t a dominant issue in the midterms, opposition to data centers emerged as a major bipartisan concern in several races in the midterms. And Lehane may be right that AI will become a bigger part of the conversation over the next two years.

Affordability is already shaping up as a key issue for the 2028 election as Americans grapple with inflation, tariffs, and the rising cost of living. AI could become an increasingly large part of that conversation as voters stress about data centers hiking up electricity bills, AI replacing their jobs, and technology’s economic gains flowing only to a handful of wealthy companies.

Lehane acknowledges many of these concerns, but he offers few concrete proposals to prevent AI’s gains from becoming concentrated. Instead, he largely pitches AI as a path to greater prosperity for Americans willing to embrace it.

Take his proposals for the tax code. He suggests incentives for workers to learn AI skills, easier capital access for entrepreneurs, and new approaches to employee ownership and profit-sharing. Those are not the same as binding requirements that would ensure workers share in AI-generated wealth or that companies pay the costs of displacing workers.

“I was struck by how much of this policy is for a future…that is already predicated on mass AI adoption and AI benefiting society, which we have yet to see,” Brennan said. “All of it is predicated on benefiting from AI’s upside.”

Brennan said it would make more sense to see things like a revised corporate tax rate or tax incentives for hiring human beings rather than automating jobs, as well as ways to keep companies from amassing outsize market power.

Lehane’s emphasis on expanding AI’s economic opportunities, rather than limiting potential harms, extends to data centers. He notes concerns about data centers while emphasizing the construction jobs and local tax revenue they could generate. He then warns that politicians who support a data center moratorium could jeopardize America’s lead over China, going so far as to compare that stance to the 2002 vote to authorize the Iraq war.

Lehane also makes child safety and education central to his proposed agenda, stressing the importance of age-appropriate protections and safeguards around AI companions for minors. He also warns against keeping AI out of classrooms, arguing that doing so could lead to inequality between wealthy students and “children in rural communities.” But the argument takes for granted that AI improves learning, a claim that remains unproven and one critics say deserves more examination than more funding or individualized attention from human teachers.

Lehane also calls for enforceable AI safety standards, independent evaluations, public oversight, and the ability to restrict AI systems or use a kill switch to shut them down — though recent studies show that OpenAI and other AI labs haven’t published comprehensive plans for what they would do if a model escaped human control.

Brennan called these stances “disingenuous,” pointing to OpenAI’s history of opposing legislation designed to impose such measures. In 2024, for example, OpenAI opposed California’s AI safety bill, SB 1047, which would have mandated catastrophic risk evaluations and shutdown capabilities. Last year, OpenAI also sought to limit state regulation, arguing that states could undermine American innovation. The company has more recently shifted some of those positions, backing several state safety laws and saying it would be open to independent evaluators embedded in the company.

Lehane also argues that people should maintain control over consequential decisions made using AI, rather than handing off judgment entirely to machines. But that raises the question of what human control really looks like in a society where humans are increasingly being required to use AI in education, work, healthcare, and government.

Throughout his essay, Lehane frames AI as a technology that could give Americans back their sense of agency and economic opportunity. But he ties average Americans’ “middle class” success to participation in an economy increasingly shaped by companies like OpenAI. Whether his policies will meaningfully redistribute power or subtly entrench it is less clear.

CMS finalizes steeper Medicare physician pay cuts for 2027, delays restriction on third-party RPM vendors" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services finalized sweeping Medicare payment reforms it says will shift the program from volume-based "sick care" toward prevention, primary care and accountable care.

The 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule rule includes a roughly 2% reduction in baseline physician payment rates with targeted increases and new payment opportunities for accountable care, primary and chronic care and behavioral health. CMS finalized steeper physician payment cuts than it had proposed in July.

An unpublished PDF version of the 2,468-page final rule can be found here.

In a press release, CMS said it is "prioritizing prevention, strengthening primary care, cracking down on duplicate billing and reinforcing fiscal stewardship across Medicare."

The final rule modernizes physician payment structures to "reward health maintenance and coordinated primary care rather than disjointed, volume-based intervention," CMS said.

"Medicare should reward clinicians for keeping people healthy," HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. said in a statement. "Under President Trump’s leadership, we are putting prevention and primary care at the center of Medicare, closing payment loopholes, and cracking down on waste. This rule directs resources toward coordinated care that puts patients first. We are changing what Medicare pays for to change what our health system delivers."

Under the final rule, CMS will officially sunset the traditional Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS) reporting option beginning with the CY 2031 performance period. Clinicians will have through CY 2030 to transition to the MIPS Value Pathways (MVPs), which already cover approximately 98% of clinicians, or Advanced Alternative Payment Models. This reflects two additional years of transition time compared to the proposed rule. The rule also finalizes three new MVPs focused on diabetes, hypertension, and hospitalists, and requires clinicians to report at least one specialty-specific "Core Measure" starting in CY 2027.

For physicians, while the proposed rule includes statutory payment increases, Medicare payment rates face an overall baseline decrease for 2027 due to the expiration of a temporary 2.5% legislative increase from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) in 2026.

Under the Medicare Access and CHIP Reauthorization Act (MACRA), the CY2027 conversion factors reflect statutory updates of +0.75% for qualifying Alternative Payment Model (APM) participants and +0.25% for non-qualifying participants. The changes to the PFS conversion factors for CY 2027 include these updates as required by statute and an estimated -0.12% adjustment necessary to account for proposed changes in work RVUs for some services, according to CMS in a fact sheet.

For clinicians who don't qualify through an advanced alternative payment model, the conversion factor drops from $33.40 to $32.63, a 2.31% reduction. For qualifying APM participants, the conversion factor drops 1.83% from $33.57 to $32.95. In the draft rule issued in July, CMS projected cuts of 1.68% and 1.19%, respectively.

CMS is also addressing what it sees as overpayment and duplicate reimbursement when multiple services are delivered during the same patient encounter. Under the final rule, when an office visit and minor procedure occur on the same day, CMS will reduce payment by 20% for the lower-cost service, reducing duplication in payment by patients and the Medicare program for clinical work already covered in the procedure’s valuation.

CMS had initially proposed paying the higher-cost service in full and reducing payment for the other service by 50%.

Physician groups were quick to criticize CMS' payment changes in the final rule.

"Notwithstanding modest policy improvements to enhance value-based care through MSSP, MGMA is disappointed by CMS’s decision to finalize several destabilizing policies in the 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule," Anders Gilberg, senior vice president of government affairs at the Medical Group Management Association, said in a statement.

"Conversion factor cuts of 1.83% for qualifying APM participants and 2.31% for all other clinicians further degrade an already fragile payment system and amplify severe financial challenges caused by years of inadequate Medicare reimbursement," Gilberg said.

Compounding this ongoing reimbursement decline, CMS will cut payments by 20% when office visits billed with modifier 25 are performed on the same day as certain procedures, "interfering with physician practices’ ability to offer same-day care," he noted.

MGMA is urging Congress to intervene by immediately fixing cuts to the conversion factor, along with changes to modifier 25. "To prevent annual disruption, Congress should prioritize comprehensive MACRA reform by passing the Patients First Act," Gilberg said.

The agency is overhauling the office/outpatient evaluation and management visit complexity add-on code, HCPCS code G2211. CMS is transitioning HCPCS code G2211 from a flat add-on payment to a modifier that would increase the payment of the associated E/M code by 16%. That would maintain an equal percentage increase across all levels of E/M codes, CMS said.

The agency also finalized a new modifier that would offset the costs incurred by practitioners in accountable care organizations (ACOs) when providing longitudinal care and care coordination. The modifier would only be available for practitioners participating in a Shared Savings Program ACO or participant providers in a Long-term Enhanced ACO Design (LEAD) Model ACO and would increase payment of the associated E/M visit by 32%, CMS said.

The final 2027 PFS rule also establishes new 340B reporting requirements. Covered entities will have to report certain Medicare Part D claims involving drugs purchased through the 340B Drug Pricing Program to the Medicare Part D Claims Data 340B Repository beginning with claims with a date of service on or after Jan.1, 2027. The deadline for the first quarterly submission is delayed until Sept. 30, 2027.

In the final rule, CMS tightened remote patient monitoring and remote therapeutic monitoring requirements but backed off an immediate restriction on contractors.

Beginning in 2027, as CMS outlined in the proposed rule, RTM services can only be furnished to established patients, and providers must provide a separately reportable initiating visit before billing for RPM or RTM services. CMS initially proposed only allowing payment for RPM or RTM services when performed by clinical staff employed by the practice and not when those services are delivered by third-party vendors. The agency provided a two-year reprieve on that requirement as it goes into effect Jan. 1, 2029, "giving practices that rely on contractors two additional years to adapt," CMS said.

Health tech companies, providers and lawmakers pushed back on the proposal to restrict third-party vendors, urging CMS to reconsider the proposed changes to preserve patients' access to these tech-enabled services. Many rural, small, and independent practices, as well as both urban and rural health systems, rely on specialized clinical and technology partners to make remote monitoring services available to their patients.

CMS finalized changes to the Medicare Shared Savings Program, the nation’s largest value-based payment program, to "accelerate accountable care service delivery, further CMS’ goal of aligning spending and value in Original Medicare, and support achieving other related strategic objectives," the agency said in a fact sheet.

The overhaul to the MSSP program aims to make ACO participation more financially attractive, encourage organizations to take on risk, expand beneficiary participation and ease some quality-reporting requirements.

The agency says the changes are projected to grow the program by adding up to 1 million assigned beneficiaries in an average year and produce $5.5 billion in net federal savings over 10 years. CMS also projects the changes will generate an overall increase in shared savings net of losses to ACOs by $1.1 billion over a decade.

The final rule changes the Shared Savings Program’s benchmarking and financial methodology to balance incentives between Level E of the BASIC track and the ENHANCED track. The shared savings rate for Level E of the BASIC track increases 50% to 60% and reduce the maximum weight used in calculating the positive regional adjustment for lower-spending ACOs participating under the ENHANCED track from 50% to 35%.

The agency is increasing the prior savings adjustment's scaling factor from 50% to 75%, allowing more of an ACO's previous savings to be reflected in future benchmarks.

Under the final rule, CMS will risk-adjust the 5% cap on benchmark increases, potentially allowing ACOs that care for sicker, higher-cost patients to receive larger upward adjustments.

Data shows that patients aligned with an ACO are less likely to experience uncontrolled diabetes or hypertension and are more likely to receive recommended preventive care, according to CMS. In performance year 2024, 75% of the 476 participating ACOs earned shared savings totaling $4.1 billion, while generating net savings for the Medicare Trust Funds of approximately $2.5 billion compared to projected spending benchmarks.

Under the final 2027 PFS rule, approved ACOs may reduce or eliminate Part B cost-sharing for many items and services for assigned beneficiaries, directly lowering out-of-pocket costs for essential care. That change goes into effect April 1, 2027.

The rule also increases shared savings rates for eligible ACOs, provides new incentives for first-time participants and sets more predictable spending targets. And, the rule simplifies electronic health record reporting requirements, streamlines patient notice mandates and removes outdated administrative hurdles.

The blue wave across the country has rippled through the stock market too, driving healthcare and clean energy names higher and data center plays and defense companies tumbling.

The broader market soared after Democrats won control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate, and key gubernatorial races in Texas and Georgia. In Washington, the Democratic Congress will now serve as a counterweight to President Donald Trump's agenda, which has included tax cuts and policies that sent inflation, like tariffs and the Iran war.

Local and state elections will also have an impact on data center stocks, with construction limits passed in some states and a Democratic governor-elect in Texas who promised a moratorium.

"Democratic control could slow the push for deregulation in financials, energy and digital assets," Wells Fargo Investment Institute said in a note Wednesday. "We still believe both parties have an incentive to compromise on data center location and AI generally."

Here are some of the biggest movers as a result of the election outcome.

Nextpower jumped more than 11.2 % and Canadian Solar rallied 6.7%, while Plug Power gained 3.6%, First Solar climbed 4.6% and Sunrun ticked 1.3% higher. Nextera Energy added 4.9%. Investors are hoping that Democrats will push back against the Trump administration's rejection of clean energy policies.

Healthcare companies rallied on investor optimism over a slowdown or reversal in healthcare spending cuts, and the potential that enhanced subsidies will extended in Affordable Care Act plans.

Molina Healthcare and Centene gained 8.9% and 12.6%, respectively. HCA Healthcare soared 10.3% and Universal Health Services jumped 10.9%, while Tenet Healthcare rallied 9.6% and Elevance Health added 4.9%.

Musk, CEO of both companies, spent at least $474 million backing Republican candidates in the midterms, including $224 million in the Lone Star State, where both Tesla and SpaceX are headquartered.

Defense companies could face headwinds if Democrats challenge military spending. Shares of RTX slipped 2.4%, Lockheed Martin tumbled 6.9%, Northrop Grumman sank 4.5% and Howmet Aerospace fell 4.3%. Palantir Technologies shed 2.1%.

The Trump administration has requested $1.5 trillion for national defense in fiscal 2027. It is also separately asking for an $87.6 billion supplemental appropriation from Congress for fiscal 2026, largely due to the Iran conflict.

Drone stocks also fell. Kratos Defense & Security Solutions dropped 12.3%, while AeroVironment lost 8.3% and Red Cat fell 10.4%. Ondas shed 7% and Unusual Machines tumbled 9%.

Names connected with data centers moved lower amid concern about the potential for restrictions or moratoriums being placed on AI infrastructure buildouts.

Neocloud provider CoreWeave, the poster child for fears over massive AI-related debt, slumped 9.9%. Peers Nebius and Iren dropped 6.7% and 11.2%, respectively.

AI infrastructure stocks also tumbled, with Comfort Systems losing 15.1% and Vistra sliding 13.8%. Micron Technology fell 7%.

That said, five of the Magnificent Seven rallied. Apple rose 5.3% and Microsoft jumped 6.7%, with Apple touching an all-time high. Alphabet gained 3.2% and Meta added 1.4%.

A consumer relief trade also took hold, sending housing, travel and consumer staples stocks higher. Affordability was a big theme in the election, with Americans feeling the pressure of high prices.

Housing stocks like Pulte, D.R. Horton and Lennar gained roughly 7.2%, 8.1% and 7.1%, respectively. Airlines also moved higher, with United Airlines rising 8% and American Airlines climbing 6.3%. Southwest Airlines jumped 6.8% and Delta Air Lines climbed 7.2%. Cruise operator Carnival increased 7.3%, while Royal Caribbean and Norwegian Cruise Line added 6.4% and 6.8%, respectively. Booking Holdings rallied 9.8%.

Consumer staple names also caught a bid. Dollar Tree soared 9.6%, Walmart gained 3.5% and Constellation Brands climbed 5.3%. Dollar General advanced 8.5%, while Conagra Brands increased 7%.
