Binance hack is ‘cyber war,’ TRM Labs executive says

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

TRM Labs executive Ari Redbord called for governments to take offensive action against North Korean hackers following the record $6.3 billion Binance theft.

The $6.3 billion Binance hack sent shockwaves through the crypto industry. Following a series of increasingly severe cybersecurity incidents, the attack on the world’s biggest crypto exchange demonstrated how far state-backed hackers can, and will, go.

TRM Labs, a blockchain intelligence company, was on the front lines of efforts to stop the theft, releasing a report in its aftermath. The firm was early to attribute the attack to the North Korean hacking group, TraitorTrader, citing “indisputable” onchain and offchain evidence. The $1.5 billion hack of Bybit in February 2025 was attributed to the same group.

Binance said in its postmortem on Nov. 3 that attackers posed as recruiters to plant malware on an engineer’s work computer, then used a compromised internal AI tool to reach its wallet-signing service.

Ari Redbord, TRM Labs’ global head of policy and government affairs, told Cointelegraph that although the industry’s coordinated response to the attack was unprecedented, “we are only going to see much more of this.”

The speed and scale of industry’s response to the hack were among the factors that stood out to Redbord.

Tether froze 971.1 million USDt (USDT) tokens just 37 minutes after the main sweep. Combined, stablecoin issuers froze tokens worth about $2.67 billion, according to TRM. “Nothing like that has ever been done before,” Redbord said. “They were able to claw back 40% right out the gate.”

The race to catch stolen funds before hackers can move them offchain is a critical part of defending against such attacks. TRM’s Redbord explained that the Beacon Network, an intelligence-sharing system TRM launched in 2025, allowed companies to coordinate quickly to immobilize the funds before working to seize them. “Normally I see a few Beacon alerts maybe every day. I’ve seen, I don’t know, hundreds or maybe even a thousand Beacon alerts over the last few days,” he said.

The push for coordination comes as Bloomberg reported on Nov. 4 that the T3 Financial Crime Unit, which TRM formed with Tron and Tether in September 2024, disbanded in late August, with sources citing slow responses and poor coordination. Tether said it would continue working with TRM through Beacon.

Although Redbord called for greater coordination, he warned that such efforts may do little to deter future attacks. He noted that North Korea’s Oct. 29 theft was equivalent to roughly a third of the country’s GDP and accounted for half of the $12.5 billion stolen by state-backed hackers since 2017. Even if attackers recover only $1 billion, the potential payoff remains enormous, he argued.

“North Korea will keep doing this as long as it’s effective,” he said.

Redbord argued that stopping such attacks requires offensive action from governments in response to North Korea’s use of hackers operating as an arm of the state.

TRM’s capacity gap report found that the gap between attackers and cybersecurity defenders continues to widen, driven by hackers’ early adoption of frontier technologies such as AI. He said law enforcement and compliance teams need AI to find and patch vulnerabilities as quickly as hackers can exploit them.

“Every time North Korea hacks, we should be hacking them back,” he continued, calling for offensive cyber operations and action against laundering networks and banks that hackers use to cash out. “This is cyber war,” he said.

Greece Passes Crypto Tax Law With 10% Gains Levy, 15% on Staking and DeFi

Greece Passes Crypto Tax Law With 10% Gains Levy, 15% on Staking and DeFi

Greece's parliament passed a bill Thursday imposing a 10% capital-gains tax on cryptocurrency profits, marking a shift from a proposal discussed in October to legislation approved by lawmakers.

The measure also puts a 15% tax on income from staking, crypto lending and decentralized finance, or DeFi.

The legislation’s crypto provisions sit inside a wider Finance Ministry package dealing with debt servicing, financial oversight, and government accounting, with the new crypto income-tax rules scheduled for January 1, 2027.

Individual investors with annual net crypto gains of €500 ($557) or less will be exempt. Cross that threshold, though, and the 10% tax applies to the entire gain, rather than just the amount over €500 ($557).

For example, an investor with €600 ($669) in net gains would face a €60 ($67) tax under the new rate, not €10 ($11). The rules generally calculate gains by subtracting the acquisition cost from the value received when the crypto is disposed of.

Selling Bitcoin for euros is one taxable event, but spending crypto on goods or services can also count as a disposal. Investors will need transaction records to establish what they paid for their assets and what those assets were worth when sold or spent.

Yield from staking, lending, and providing liquidity to DeFi protocols is treated differently. Greece will classify those returns as interest income and tax them at 15%, according to the Finance Ministry.

Qualifying crypto losses exceeding €500 ($557) can be carried forward for five years and offset against future crypto gains. The bill also gives investors a 12-month window to voluntarily declare previously unreported crypto income without late-filing penalties or interest, provided they meet its conditions and pay within 60 days of filing.

The passage follows an October public consultation on the proposal.The draft proposed a 10% rate that was already lower than the 15% levy Greek officials had discussed in June.

Athens says exchanging one cryptocurrency for another should not itself trigger a capital-gains tax. But the wording lawmakers approved may not make that exclusion as clear as the government intended.

A review by parliament's Scientific Service flagged the bill's broad language covering disposals made "for consideration." That phrase could include token-to-token trades, the review said, even though the accompanying explanation says such swaps should not create taxable gains.

The reviewers recommended an explicit exemption and suggested clearer rules for exchanges involving stablecoins and electronic-money tokens. Those details matter for traders who rotate between assets without cashing out to fiat currency.

Greece is also preparing the administrative rules needed to put the tax into practice. A senior Finance Ministry official told Reuters that guidance on transaction transparency and tax audits would be issued in the coming weeks.

The government has not estimated how much the tax will raise. Officials say the market is difficult to measure because many Greek crypto investors use trading platforms based overseas.

Supreme Court agrees to weigh in on Goldman Sachs arbitration dispute

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Supreme Court on Friday afternoon added a new case to its oral argument docket for the 2026-27 term, granting a petition for review filed by Goldman Sachs Bank in a case over whether and when a bankruptcy court can override the Federal Arbitration Act and refuse to honor an agreement to arbitrate disputes.

The dispute in which the justices agreed to weigh in, Goldman Sachs Bank USA v. Brown, began three years ago, when Rhea Brown and Gregory Maze filed for bankruptcy. Both listed credit card debts with Goldman Sachs Bank as one of their debts.

The Bankruptcy Code imposes an automatic stay, which (among other things) bars creditors from attempting to collect debts from someone who has filed for bankruptcy. Because Goldman Sachs, according to Maze and Brown, repeatedly continued to try to seek payment of their credit card debts, they filed an adversary proceeding (that is, a separate lawsuit within the broader bankruptcy case) against the bank, in which they contended that the bank had willfully violated the automatic stay provision.

Pointing to an arbitration clause in their credit card agreements, Goldman Sachs asked the bankruptcy court to put the adversary proceeding on hold and require Brown and Maze to arbitrate their dispute. The bankruptcy court denied that request, and both the federal district court and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 4th Circuit upheld that ruling. A divided three-judge panel of the 4th Circuit concluded that requiring Brown and Maze to arbitrate their claim “would interfere and conflict with the strong and established policies and purposes of the Bankruptcy Code.”

Goldman Sachs came to the Supreme Court in June, asking the justices to take up its case. It told the court that the 4th Circuit’s ruling squarely conflicted with a decision by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 2nd Circuit. Moreover, it added, “[t]he practical stakes of these issues are enormous” because of the ubiquity of arbitration clauses in consumer credit agreements. The 4th Circuit’s decision, it said, “imperils the enforceability of arbitration agreements in many of them.”

After considering Goldman Sachs’ petition for review at four consecutive conferences, the justices granted it on Friday afternoon. The case likely will be argued sometime next year, with a decision to follow by late June or early July.

More orders from the justices’ private conference on Friday are expected at 9:30 a.m. EST on Monday.

10 Zelda: Ocarina Of Time Tips For The First-Time Player

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Nintendo Switch 2The Legend of ZeldaThe Legend of Zelda: Ocarina Of Time

A happy Zelda weekend to you all! While a lot of us are going into this latest Zelda release confident and comfortable in our ancient (or, in my case, recent) knowledge of how to play Ocarina of Time, this game is 28 whole years old and there are many, many people who have never played it, don’t remember it, or weren’t even alive when it came out. And whew, this is a very different Zelda game than some of the more recent ones!

So I put together some tips for you all. Hopefully at least a few of them help you have a smoother adventure in Hyrule this weekend. Enjoy the ride, folks, this one’s a hell of a classic:

In Kokiri Forest, when you first get control of Link, you can immediately head out of your house and down the path to the left to reach the Know-It-Alls. The characters inside the house will give you some useful tips, while the fellow outside will show you some basic combat moves including rolling and dodging. He’ll then point you in the direction of a sword, after which point you can return and learn how to do the mighty jump attack (more on that momentarily). These are (almost) all of the combat moves you’ll need to know for the whole game, so it’s good to master them early.

The jump attack is powerful, doing double the damage of a regular swing. It’s also a little tricky to execute, requiring two button inputs instead of just one…seemingly. You can actually also do this move by tilting forward on the control stick (while targeting) and just pressing A. I recommend doing the attack this way, and I recommend using this attack aÂ lot.

Everyone in Hyrule wants you to follow the main story path. They are, in fact, very worried you’ll miss it. So you’ll constantly hear NPCs directing you: go to the Great Deku Tree! Go to Hyrule Castle! Well…maybe don’t. At least, not right away. Ocarina of Time has tons of secrets, items, and just generally stuff to do that isn’t on the main path, so don’t feel obligated to stick to it. Even in Kokiri Forest, there are extra items you can obtain early if you just wander around for a while. You also can (and should!) backtrack to areas you’ve previously visited at just about any time!

Once you reach Castle Town, you’ll start to run into minigames. Ocarina of Time has a lot of them, and many are located in shops off the square. In some Zelda games, you don’t really need to do the minigames on offer, and technically that’s true in this one. But I recommend you at least give them a whirl. Some of them have great rewards, like bigger storage bags for certain items, or empty bottles. Don’t be afraid to goof off a little on your adventure!

Which, speaking of empty bottles. these are by far your most precious resource. Get as many as you can as soon as you can. If someone’s got a bottle for you, drop everything and do what they say. Empty bottles are used to hold all sorts of things: items you need for quests and to unlock secret paths, potions to refill your health, and fairies that can revive you automatically if you die. There are a total of five empty bottles in the game: try to get them all!

It can be tempting to just hack and slash your way to victory in Ocarina of Time, but some enemies aren’t so easily taken down. If you’re struggling, it’s good to test out some of your other items on enemies and see which, if any, have an effect. You might be surprised what a well-placed hookshot or boomerang can do, or what you can accomplish with some Deku Nuts. A lot of enemies can be stunned, knocked away, or even killed from a distance, no close combat required.

I mean, really, you should just do this anyway. The writing in Ocarina of Time is often silly and worth reading on its own, but characters you speak to will also frequently give you hints about what to do next, or even about secrets you may not have found otherwise. Characters will also adjust their dialogue over time depending on what is going on in the game, so circle back with folks even if you think you’ve heard all they have to say.

And yes, Navi can help, too. She can be a bit grating, but her tips on fighting monsters are a huge help.

Again, this one seems pretty basic, but sometimes you’ll be stuck in a dungeon and have no idea how to solve a puzzle to proceed. I cannot emphasize enough how often the answer is just to look,Â really look around you. Look for weird symbols on the walls, or bits of floor that look funny. Look for fire, or places to light things on fire. Look for switches, or platforms directly below you that you didn’t see before. Look at how things connect to one another. Look underwater. Most of the time if you’re stuck, it’s because you’re not seeing some key element you need to move forward…and often, those things are hidden for a reason.

New to the remake of Ocarina of Time are fairy symbols, odd glowing runes you’ll find inscribed on walls or pillars or other out-of-the-way surfaces as you explore. You can use Navi to examine them. They’ll often give you hints about secrets, or other little bonuses, for interacting with them properly. Again, looking around pays off!

Finally, gold skulltulas. You won’t necessarily know these are important when you first start the game, but they absolutely are. There are 100 of these in the game total, and you get rewards for reaching certain milestones of collecting them. Many of these rewards are very, very good. You’ll want to listen for a scritchy-scratching noise as you explore, that indicates there’s one nearby. You can also find them by rolling into trees that seem to be shedding their leaves. In towns, you’ll only ever find them at night (in dungeons, they’re around all the time). Grab as many as you can!

The Cuccos are the chickens. Don’t attack them. You can pick them up, carry them around, and throw them. But don’t HIT them.

Men Are Outspending Women on Botox. Here’s What They’re Asking For

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Guys are going to increasingly wild lengths to achieve their version of the idealized man. Looksmaxxing, as we’ve come to know it, has included surgical procedures to reshape their eyes, taking a hammer to their face to create a more chiseled jawline, and breaking their legs to gain a couple inches. But an OG in cosmetic procedures is seeing a resurgence in men, too.

Chances are you’re familiar with Botox: localized injections to smoothen wrinkles in common areas like the forehead, the eyes, or around the mouth. Long viewed as a treatment limited to the ladies, it’s increasingly becoming another form of male upkeep. Now we’re learning that men are outspending women on the popular procedure. According to an October 2026 report by Guidepoint Qsight, a medical aesthetics data and intelligence provider, men spent an average of $529 per neurotoxin visit in Q3 of 2026, compared with $455 for women. (The figures include not just Botox but also competitors like Daxxify and Xeomin.)

The potential explanation is simple: Botox is an easy way to look a little younger, more well-rested, and even less angry or severe. (As a built-in bonus, it’s virtually painless—especially compared to the barbaric looksmaxxing trends circulating as of late.) According to a 2025 survey of 2,900 aesthetic plastic surgeons, men accounted for over 15% of all Botox cases that year. It was also their top non-surgical treatment by a landslide.

The growing cohort of men opting into “Brotox” believe it’s money well spent. Nancy Samolitis, MD, a board-certified dermatologist in Los Angeles and co-founder of facile, has been treating men for years but has recently seen an uptick in male clientele, aged anywhere from 35 to 70. These days, “they’re more interested in skincare and more open to procedures,” she says. Many also book their first treatment nudged by their spouse or significant other who are existing patients. Others come in simply wanting to look better but not knowing what treatment to get. In many cases, Botox fits the bill.

While the uninitiated might think the injections make guys look surprised or less masculine, good work can be undetectable. Still you, just fresher.

Kyle Waldrop, a 37-year-old product designer in Los Angeles, first started getting Botox three years ago. “I’ve always been good about keeping up with a skincare routine and taking care of myself, so Botox felt like a natural extension of what I was already doing,” he says. His goal isn’t to change his face but to slow down wrinkles before they become obvious. He gets Botox for his forehead lines and crow’s feet, and recently started treating his neck lines, too.

But it’s not always about looking younger. Men are also using Botox in less obvious places. David Kim, MD, board-certified dermatologist and the founder of Soho Dermatology in New York City, treats the chin for more projection, as well as the underarms to combat excess sweating. Bell Yoo, NP, a board-certified nurse practitioner and the founder of Mimo Aesthetics in West Hollywood, adds that she injects the masseter (and sometimes temporalis) muscles for guys who struggle with jaw clenching or teeth grinding.

Aesthetically, men usually aren’t looking for the same thing from Botox as women. For starters, many want to soften their lines without erasing them. Preserving some natural movement is key. “My female patients often love that smooth, ‘glass skin’ appearance, whereas some of my male patients think it looks too waxy or oily,” Yoo says. Men also tend to opt out of a Botox brow lift, which many women request. “Men would much rather maintain a slightly heavier, lower-set brow than end up with an overly arched or ‘Spock’ brow,” Yoo says. Per Samolitis, this requires different injection points and doses tailored for guys. Kim, meanwhile, is careful not to overdo the crow’s feet. “It looks more natural when men have lines around the eyes, and it makes their face look better overall,” he says. These techniques quell the fears that guys have about looking less masculine or overdone. For most, the ultimate goal is to look relaxed and rejuvenated—not tight and taut. “When it’s done well and with the right amount, you look like yourself, just a bit more rested,” Waldrop says. “I still have all my natural movement and expressions, which is important to me.”

All that subtlety comes at a price. Men may end up paying more than women to treat the same areas, but the higher price tag is actually expected: Men often have larger, stronger facial muscles, which require more units to achieve their desired results, Yoo says.

While more men are embracing Botox, some remain tight-lipped about it. Yoo says that most of her male Botox patients are pretty discreet. “I think there’s still some stigma around men receiving cosmetic treatments, and many would rather keep it to themselves,” she says. Waldrop, however, is an open book. “I don’t really see it as something worth hiding or feeling that weird about,” he says. “Guys are already spending money on skincare, haircuts, hair transplants, working out, and all sorts of things to look and feel better, so I don’t really see why Botox should be treated any differently.”

For Waldrop, Botox is less a dramatic intervention than another item on his grooming checklist. He doesn’t see it as something that needs to be announced or apologized for. At the end of the day, “it’s really not that deep,” he says.

New Federal Reserve Research Finds Cutting Merchant Swipe Fees Even Hurts Consumers Who Don’t Earn Rewards

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

You can earn no miles, no points and no cash back from your credit card and still lose if the government cuts credit card merchant swipe fees. Research from two Philadelphia Federal Reserve economists explains why: the money banks collect when you spend helps pay for better borrowing terms. That’s a problem for the retailers’ argument that consumers who don’t collect rewards are simply paying for everyone else’s vacations. I wrote this week about why the legislation threatening card rewards isn’t dead, even with Dick Durbin retiring and Roger Marshall losing his Senate seat. The political sales pitch is that reducing merchants’ payment costs saves consumers money. That isn’t true and has never been true anywhere in the world it’s been tried. It’s cover for retailers who want the benefits of accepting credit cards, but think the government should require the service be offered to them for less money. But credit cards aren’t just a convenient way to make purchases. They also provide financing for those purchases, and the merchant fee helps support that. Even A Card With No Rewards Can Give Something Back Satyajit Chatterjee and Burcu Eyigungor’s Credit Effects of Credit Card Interchange Fees uses bank supervisory data to examine an overlooked part of the debate: what interchange does to credit itself. The June 26 paper was highlighted in a new Bank of Japan summary. In the data, rewards cards have higher credit limits. Cards without rewards have lower interest rates despite higher default rates. They argue that rewards make repayment more attractive because default means losing access to future rewards. And when a bank keeps interchange instead of paying rewards, competition encourages it to offer better rates and limits to attract the customer’s business. They find that both rewards and non-rewards cardholders gain, even after accounting for higher retail prices. The ELT Can Probe Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies and Their Dark Matter Mysteries

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

We've been waiting a long time for the Extremely Large Telescope's first light, scheduled for March 2029. Telescopes this large, powerful, and costly always have multiple science goals, and that's true for the ELT. One of its science goals is to study fundamental aspects of the Universe, including dark matter.

The current model of cosmology is called Lambda Cold Dark Matter (Lambda-CDM or ÎCDM.) It's basically the Big Bang model of cosmology. There are, as every space-head knows, unanswered questions about Lambda-CDM, including the fact that we don't even know what dark matter is. (Some don't think it even exists.)

This is where the ELT comes in. One of its proposed instruments, and telescopes like this always have multiple instruments attached, is called NEXUS@SHARP. SHARP stands for Spectrograph for High Angular Resolution Physics, and NEXUS is a Multi-Object Spectrograph designed to work with SHARP.

New research in the journal New Astronomy outlines how NEXUS@SHARP can probe questions about Lambda-CDM. It's titled "SHARP(ening the) view of unresolved globular clusters in ultra diffuse galaxies," and the lead author is Marco Mirabile. Mirabile is a PhD student at INAF-Astronomical Observatory of Abruzzo (INAF-OAAb) and Gran Sasso Science institute (GSSI) in Italy.

"Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies (UDGs) challenge current galaxy formation models within the ÎCDM framework, particularly regarding their dark matter content and formation histories," the authors write. "Globular Clusters (GCs) serve as excellent tracers for probing the halos of these faint systems; however, current spectroscopic facilities struggle to reach the depth required to fully characterize GC populations beyond the local neighborhood."

UDGs are unusual because they're about as large as the Milky Way, but can have thousands of times fewer stars. The stars are spread out and the galaxies are dim. Basically they're too puffy to agree with Lambda-CDM.

This visualization shows how stars are distributed in a simulated UDG. UDGs are as faint as dwarf galaxies, but are spread out like large galaxies like the Milky Way. Astronomers thought that supernovae explosions could've been responsible for spreading out the stars, but that interpretation hasn't endured. Image Credit: Arianna Di Cintio, Chris Brook, NIHAO simulations and HST

A galaxy's size is shaped by its dark matter halo, one of the cosmos' basic structural units. There's an apparent mismatch between the sizes of UDG halos and the sizes of the UDGs themselves. The expected size of the halos shouldn't produce such diffuse galaxies.

A way to understand this is to examine the globular clusters around UDGs. But the problem is, the spectroscopes astronomers have at their disposal now aren't powerful enough to fully characterize the globular clusters around UDGs. NEXUS@SHARP can extend their reach.

"In this work, we present the scientific potential of NEXUS@SHARP, a near-infrared multi-slit spectrograph proposed on the upcoming Extremely Large Telescope (ELT). We demonstrate that NEXUS is uniquely positioned to overcome current observational limitations by combining high spatial resolution with efficient multiplexing," the authors write. Multiplexing means it's a Multi-Object Spectrograph (MOS) that can observe and capture data from many GCs at once. MOSs are common; Keck, the VLT, Gemini, and the JWST all have them, so do many others. But NEXUS@SHARP would be attached to the extraordinarly powerful ELT and its 39.3-metre-diameter segmented mirror.

All galaxies have globular clusters, including our own. Globular clusters are important when studying UDGs because they're brighter than the UDGs that hosts them, and are easier to observe. They also extend outward further than the UDGs, so they can help measure the dark matter halo better. That should let astronomers measure a more accurate mass constraint. Not only that, but the number of GCs can help determine the mass of the halo because their number scales with mass.

Omega Centauri, seen in this image captured with the WFI camera from ESO's La Silla Observatory, is probably the most well-known of the Milky Way's globular clusters. It has as many as 10 million stars. GCs are bright, so when they're satellites of ultra-diffuse galaxies, they serve as good probes of the galaxies. Image Credit: ESO

Another important thing GC spectra tells scientists is their formation history. The spectra show the ages and the metallicities of GCs, which reveals how quickly the stars in the GC formed. That can show astronomers what kind of UDG they're looking at.

Measuring a handful of GCs around a small number of UDGs won't sufficiently characterize the population of either. NEXUS@SHARP will bring more of them within observational rearch, and it won't require much telescope time because NEXUS examines many GCs at once. "We show that NEXUS can efficiently help to characterize the GC population in UDGs across diverse environments â out to 60 to 70 Mpc â with a few hours of ELT time," the authors write. 70 megaparsecs is about 230 light years.

The ELT is a beast of a telescope, and its heart is the 39.3 meter segmented primary mirror. Its instruments, including NEXUS@SHARP if its built, sit on a separate stationary platform. Image Credit: ESO

What this boils down to is two different scenarios for how UDGs form, and what it tells astronomers about dark matter and Lambda-CDM.

"These capabilities will allow for precise measurements of radial velocities and stellar populations, enabling robust dynamical mass estimates and distinguishing between competing UDG formation scenarios," the authors write.

One scenario says that UDGs are failed massive galaxies that were quenched early on. These ones preserved their dwarf-like stellar masses, but lie within more massive halos typical of a regular galaxy. "These galaxies would thus appear red, old, metal-poor, DM dominated, and host unusually rich globular cluster (GC) populations," the authors write.

The second says that UDGs are puffed-up dwarfs. They are genuinely low-mass galaxies that have expanded in space for some reason. "Relative to the previous scenario, such objects are expected to be bluer, exhibit extended star formation histories, and have DM content and GC populations resembling those of typical dwarfs," the authors explain.

NEXUS@SHARP can shed light on these scenarios and help explain how UDGs formed by examining globular clusters around Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies. That's why the authors wrote that ""Globular Clusters (GCs) serve as excellent tracers for probing the halos of these faint systems."

"To shed light on the nature of UDGs, deep spectroscopic observations of a large sample of UDGs are needed, as combining the analysis of GC richness with the kinematic and evolutionary properties of field stars provides a powerful diagnostic for distinguishing between different formation channels," the authors write.

Basically, the dataset gathered by NEXUS@SHARP will have two independent mass measurements for each UDG, both arising from measuring many GCs at once. One mass measurement comes from the velocity dispersion of the GCs, which shows how much mass is required to keep them in orbit. That's dynamical mass.

The second is also from GCs, but measures their total number. That relies on the knowledge that the number of GCs roughly rises linearly with the mass of a UDG's dark matter halo. That's GC-count mass.

This figure from previous research shows how the number of globular clusters (y-axis) correlates with galaxy mass (x-axis). Each orange or blue circle represents the number of GCs in an individual galaxy. Image Credit: Minh Ngoc Le and Andrew P. Cooper 2025 ApJ 978 33

If both of these ways of measuring mass agree, and both are high, that means that the UDG has an overmassive halo compared to its number of stars. That supports the first UDG formation scenario: it's a failed regular galaxy that quenched early.

If the different measurements both agree, but they're low, that supports the second UDG formation scenario: it's a puffed up dwarf inside a normal sized dwarf dark matter halo.

There's another wrinkle. If the dynamical mass is low, but the GC-count mass is high, then the formation of GC was unusually inefficient for some other, environmental reason.

NEXUS@SHARP is just a concept at this point, and the paper just makes the case for building it. If it's built, the results could go a long way to a better understanding of dark matter and Lambda-CDM. Understanding Lambda-CDM is an ongoing effort, and it's being tackled from different directions with different facilities. While Lambda-CDM is still the best match for lots of data about dark matter and dark energy, it doesn't fit everything.

If built, NEXUS@SHARP can chip away at some of the uncertainty around Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies and their dark matter halos, and can help either confirm or contradict Lambda-CDM.

"By enabling the systematic spectroscopic survey of GCs in low-surface-brightness systems, NEXUS@SHARP will act as a definitive tool for constraining the formation channels of the most elusive galaxy populations in the local universe," the authors conclude.

AI is dramatically accelerating AI research

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This post discusses a public METR report on AI R&D acceleration at Anthropic. I did not work on this project or see the unredacted report.1 All opinions are my own and do not represent METR or the report authors.Thanks to Ryan Greenblatt for discussion, feedback, and help with modeling, and Daniel Kokotajlo, Eli Lifland, and Hjalmar Wjik for comments.

Earlier this week, Anthropic stated in their S1 that they “may imminently meet” the threshold of “dramatically accelerated progress due to AI” as defined in their Responsible Scaling Policy. In their latest RSP (effective July 2026), say this threshold will be met when:

They observe or expect overall capabilities progress to be going twice as fast as the fastest sustained rate before AI was contributing much to AI R&D.2

This is plausibly attributable to AI automating AI R&D, rather than acceleration in compute or other inputs.3

For the last few months, some of my colleagues at METR have been working on a deep-access investigation to understand how much Anthropic is being accelerated by its AI systems. From my reading of their report and Anthropic’s RSP, it looks like the “dramatic acceleration” threshold has already been crossed.4

Between October 2025 and October 2026, capabilities progress at Anthropic has been going about twice as fast as it had been over the previous twelve months. The measurement they report is an internal version of the Epoch Capabilities Index called AECI; a wide range of other supporting evidence for acceleration has been redacted. They said that this redacted evidence was "a majority of the evidence [they] found most informative."

While the pace may have initially started picking up for a mix of reasons, AI automation is probably the large majority of the reason why capabilities are still improving at this faster pace today. Their reasons for believing this have been redacted.

This is a much bigger deal than it might sound at first. I think this report is the most important update we have gotten about AI risk in the last couple of years — much bigger than the Hugging Face attack. Naively, it points toward much shorter timelines than most external researchers (including the AI 2027 team) have projected.

Importantly, if AI is roughly doubling capabilities progress overall, it must be speeding up human researchers by much more than that.

Researchers work on improving algorithms and architectures; Anthropic has sometimes referred to these innovations as “compute multipliers.”5 Overall AI capabilities (“effective compute”) is the product of compute multipliers and training compute. Because the compute multipliers are only one input into capabilities, this means that if AI capabilities are advancing twice as fast overall because of software progress, the rate of growth in compute multipliers would need to be more than twice as fast: perhaps ~2.5-3x faster based on reasonable assumptions.6 Since there’s uncertainty about the size of speedup and how much of it comes from AI, I’ll go with ~2-3x.

Similarly, researcher labor is only one input to discovering these compute multipliers: they need to test their ideas, which requires compute for experiments. If we assume a very simple Cobb-Douglas model where experiment compute and labor are similarly important, that means the uplift to labor roughly needs to be the square of the acceleration in compute multipliers.7 This would imply that we would need a (~2-3x)^2 = ~4-9x speed up in labor to make overall AI capabilities go twice as fast.8

However, once capabilities start speeding up significantly, this also means that low-hanging fruit is plucked at a correspondingly faster rate. Each new compute multiplier takes more and more work to discover and implement (i.e., “ideas are getting harder to find”). Before there was much acceleration from AI, labor roughly grew at some roughly fixed exponential rate from hiring more humans. Combined with growth in compute, this compensated for diminishing returns enough to maintain the baseline rate of capabilities progress. But to maintain a new faster rate of capabilities progress without scaling compute faster, we would need a new faster rate of growth in overall labor.

Since the human labor force is stuck growing at more or less the old rate, the uplift from AI would need to get larger and larger over time just to sustain capabilities progress at its new rate. That means the uplift today, after several months of accelerated progress from AI,9 needs to be even higher than ~4-9x, the estimate for what it would take to double progress at the start: maybe the necessary labor uplift is ~10x. This is very uncertain, and substantially more or less is plausible. But it lines up surprisingly well with informal self-reports from Anthropic staff, who say they are now getting a ~12x uplift from the use of internal models.

When you’re picturing a 10x uplift, you should not be imagining that every researcher is now a team of ten researchers — as anyone who has ever done anything knows, a team of ten does not get ten times as much valuable work done per hour as one person. Instead, it’s as if every researcher at Anthropic is in a time warp bubble where they experience ten days for every day that passes in the outside world. As if AI lets them think, read, speak, type, and do everything else ten times faster.10 Anthropic probably wouldn’t have been able to speed up its research this much by hiring any number of people.11

A reasonable default expectation is that as AI capabilities continue to improve, AI researchers will be sped up more and more from automation: if today it takes them a month to get as much work done as they used to in a year, soon it will take a week, and then perhaps a day. If this increasing labor uplift is strong enough to overcome diminishing returns, then in the absence of other bottlenecks it would translate into even larger speed ups in the rate of AI progress: progress will start going 4x faster, and then 8x, and then 16x, and so on.

The report authors were very uncertain about this question, and say that their picture changed several times with new information. But ultimately, they did not find evidence of bottlenecks or steep diminishing returns that would get in the way of compounding acceleration.12 They also report seeing some (confusing) redacted evidence indicating that acceleration could already be above 2x and increasing over time.

If we take it at face value that AI is now nearly doubling the rate of AI capabilities progress and assume acceleration from AI is smooth, this naively implies that we would reach extreme superintelligence within a year. It seems that around a year’s worth of AI progress got us from modest acceleration to the point where AIs now make AI progress roughly twice as fast. If acceleration from AI is smoothly increasing, then at this new rate, we should expect that we will double the rate of progress again within six months. At that point, it would take three months to double it again, then six weeks.13

In theory, these doubling times would add up to infinite capabilities within one year. In reality, at some point we would reach the effective ceiling on software efficiency. But I expect these limits to be very far from where we are now.

If we go through this feedback loop, I expect that the AI systems that come out the other side will be able to very quickly develop novel weapons and very easily overpower human civilization in a conflict. If these systems were seriously misaligned, I think they would likely disempower humanity, and this could lead to human extinction.

The “dramatically accelerated progress due to AI” is such an important and extreme milestone because it is a strong concrete indicator of extreme capabilities in the very near term. Indeed, in an earlier version of Anthropic’s RSP (May 2025), the highest named risk threshold for AI research automation (AI R&D-5) corresponded to a somewhat weaker milestone.14 According to the old RSP, Anthropic would be required to have, at a minimum, security good enough to “protect against model-weight theft by state-level adversaries”15 by the time it reached AI R&D-5.

I agree that very strong security, both for model weights and algorithmic secrets themselves, is a minimum prerequisite for undergoing an intelligence explosion next year without running very large risks of killing billions of people. The AI industry is nowhere near this bar: both OpenAI and Anthropic recently had major security breaches by amateur groups. Beyond strong model weight security, I also think we would need robust logging and monitoring with universal coverage, reliable measurements of monitorability, effective countermeasures against monitor collusion, principled alignment training techniques with very convincing model organisms or interpretability to understand how well they’re working, governance infrastructure for pacing progress if we notice these alignment and control interventions start to break down…and many other measures if we’re going to approach an intelligence explosion in anything like a responsible way.

Unfortunately, most of the important evidence that fed into these conclusions has been redacted from this report. Analytical choices will inevitably be fraught, and the report authors say that the evidence was often confusing.

I trust that my colleagues did a careful and rigorous job navigating making sense of what they saw, and I made a big update based on this report. But I wish much more of the underlying evidence was transparent so readers don’t have to rely on trust. We may be running out of time to get on the same page about timelines and takeoff speeds, and it is much harder to do that when external scientists cannot examine the data and test out their own models.

Because I may have seen other confidential information that might be relevant to timelines and takeoff speeds, I will only discuss implications of public evidence rather than sharing precise bottom line forecasts.

The precise condition is: “We observe or expect double the rate of progress in AI aggregate capabilities compared to both the rate we’d expect and the fastest rate of extended progress we’ve observed in the absence of significant AI contributions to AI R&D.” My understanding is the reason to have both of these conditions (double the rate of progress both compared to the counterfactual and compared to the fastest sustained rate without much AI assistance) is to exclude cases where the pace of capabilities would have slowed down a lot without AI automation, but we manage to keep the old trends going with dramatic uplift from AI.

The precise condition is: “It is plausible that this doubling is significantly attributable to the automation of research and/or engineering (as opposed to other factors, such as increased headcount, compute, or general productivity), such that continuation of the trend in AI progress seems likely to lead to even greater acceleration.”

The definition of the threshold is a bit finicky, partly because two speedup conditions must be met (speedup relative to the counterfactual and speedup relative to the past). But on a plain reading, my best guess is that it has been met. The authors of the METR report estimated roughly a doubling in the rate of progress relative to the past, but they only attribute roughly 80% of the current speed-up to AI, which would mean the central estimate for observed speedup relative to no AI was roughly ~1.8x as of the time of writing their report, in October 2026. But they also found that the contribution of AI has been growing over time, and I would guess that now (as of November 2026) it is appreciably higher. Even if the rate of progress is not fully double the counterfactual right this second, the threshold incorporates reasonable expectations (“We observe or expect double the rate of progress…”).

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei explains the idea of compute multipliers in this essay: “The field is constantly coming up with ideas, large and small, that make things more effective or efficient: it could be an improvement to the architecture of the model (a tweak to the basic Transformer architecture that all of today’s models use) or simply a way of running the model more efficiently on the underlying hardware. New generations of hardware also have the same effect. What this typically does is shift the curve: if the innovation is a 2x “compute multiplier” (CM), then it allows you to get 40% on a coding task for $5M instead of $10M; or 60% for $50M instead of $100M, etc. Every frontier AI company regularly discovers many of these CM’s: frequently small ones (~1.2x), sometimes medium-sized ones (~2x), and every once in a while very large ones (~10x).”

This is a simplified version of the modeling that my colleague Ryan Greenblatt goes through here. His numbers are based on his prior public modeling of the factors like the importance of software vs training compute and the importance of labor vs experimental compute.

Common formulations of Cobb-Douglas often measure labor as the number of humans. In that formulation, there needs to be a parallelization penalty on top of labor and the coefficients don’t add to 1. As I discuss below, I’m choosing to represent labor in terms of serial labor acceleration rather than the number of humans; this formulation was suggested by Ryan Greenblatt in this post.

To make the math simple, I just assumed that labor and experiment compute contributed equally (i.e. compute multipliers = (labor)^0.5 * (compute)^0.5. This means you square the acceleration in compute multipliers to back out the acceleration in labor. The range I gave omits uncertainty on the size of the contributions: for example if labor contributed ⅔, you would instead need to raise it to the power of 3/2.
