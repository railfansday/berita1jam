How many SpaceX Starship rocket tests have failed?

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In late September 2026, SpaceX's Starship entered orbit for the first time, deploying 26 Starlink satellites in what was heralded as a major step for SpaceX's cosmic ambitions. However, the celebration was cut short when a failed engine forced the mission to end shortly after reaching orbit. The malfunction prevented the intended six-revolution trek through Earth's orbit, falling well short of its 10-hour goal.

This result is typical for Starship, as the company's "fail fast, learn fast" mindset recasts the program's failures — anywhere between four and 11 of 14 total launches, depending on how generous you are — as opportunities for growth. This has been true since its first test flight in April 2023, which exploded 65 miles short of its intended climb into Earth's atmosphere. As Musk tweeted after the fiery test, "Congrats @SpaceX team on an exciting test launch of Starship! Learned a lot for next test launch."

Given SpaceX's success to date, it is hard to argue with the company's logic. According to analytics firm BryceTech, SpaceX launched 85 percent of all spacecraft in 2025, including over half the world's orbital launches. To boot, Starlink's 9,800 active satellites currently account for over half of the world's total, according to satellite tracker Orbital Radar.

The largest and most powerful rocket ever assembled, Starship has a cargo capacity five times larger than SpaceX's Falcon 9 rocket. This makes it essential for SpaceX's grander ambitions, including its million-satellite constellation, NASA's lunar missions and Musk's Mars colony dream. With another launch set for November 13, 2026, stakeholders will soon see whether the September launch represented progress or foreshadowed further additions to SpaceX's growing list of frustrations.

Gauging the success of the Starship project is difficult. Although some missions may fit within a binary success-failure matrix, most land somewhere in between. And while it has yet to deliver a fully-reusable rocket capable of missions to "Mars and beyond," progress was always going to be incremental. As such, its only fair to judge each mission against its own standards of progress.

Starship's start was turbulent. After its initial test combusted within minutes, its second flight saw the first-stage booster separate before the booster and vessel met fiery ends. Five months later, Starship's third test flight completed many of its objectives, reaching an altitude of 145 miles before disintegrating on its return. SpaceX then had two back-to-back successful launches, culminating in its launch tower successfully catching Starship's first-stage booster.

Unfortunately, another troubled period followed. Test six saw SpaceX call off its lauded mechanical arm before crashing the Super Heavy booster into the Gulf of Mexico. Two months later, Starship 7 exploded over the Bahamas just eight minutes after takeoff, sparking investigations by both the FAA and the Turks and Caicos government. The FAA launched another investigation after Starship's eighth test exploded in space, grounding planes across Florida. Starship 9 achieved minor improvements before spinning out of control, forcing SpaceX to cancel its planned deployment of 8 mock satellites.

Starship followed the disastrous run with a pair of its most successful tests. In August 2025, Starship released its first cargo of Starlink satellites. Two months later, SpaceX successfully launched and landed its 11th Starship rocket, fulfilling all major objectives.

Starship's twelfth test launched its upgraded Starship V3 rocket. While it accomplished several objectives, engine failures caused it to crash into the Gulf, triggering yet another FAA investigation. In July 2026, Starship 13 successfully deployed 20 of Starlink's new high-powered satellites before its first-stage booster failed to stick the landing. Considering its scattershot history, Starship 14's semi-successful run is par for the course.

Judging each mission against its stated objectives, most Starship launches have failed to meet expectations. SpaceX, however, would likely claim that its willingness to learn from mistakes puts its true failure rate closer to zero. The better question is whether the program is learning quickly enough to keep pace with its own meteoric ambitions.

If one takes Musk's proclamations as a measuring stick, Starship has underdelivered. In 2025, Musk predicted there was a 50 percent chance Starship would land on Mars in 2026. And with the once-every-26-months Mars launch window closing this November, you can guess which side of the coin SpaceX landed on. Of course, Musk is notorious for overpromising, so this is business as usual at Starbase.

Earlier this year, NASA announced that the program had fallen behind schedule by at least two years since the Agency selected SpaceX's rocket to carry its Artemis moon lander in 2021. How Do Borderline and Narcissism Differ?

Posted November 11, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Although they may appear superficially similar, borderline personality disorder and narcissistic personality disorder are very different from each other. Although both develop similarly—a child with a particular inborn temperament tries to adapt to a parenting style that doesn't fit their needs—they diverge. The borderline adaptation focuses on getting love and nurturing and avoiding abandonment, while the narcissistic adaptation focuses on getting admiration and high status and avoiding being seen as flawed.

(In this post, I am using BPD and NPD, adaptations, and the terms borderline and narcissist as shorthand for someone who qualifies for a full diagnosis of borderline personality disorder or narcissistic personality disorder.)

Both lack whole object relations and object constancy.

This means that people with BPD and NPD cannot form an integrated, realistic, and stable sense of themselves and other people. They can only shift back and forth between seeing themselves and other people as either all-good or all-bad.

A three-part unit forms when someone with a personality disorder interacts with others; however, it only shows one side of itself. Masterson called this a “split object relations part unit.” It comprises only good feelings or only bad ones.

Part 1—How the person with the PD feels about themself and acts.

Part 2—What the person with the PD believes the other person feels and thinks.

Part 3—The emotional atmosphere between them.

Carol has borderline and severe abandonment issues from her childhood in foster care. Her husband, Bill, is leaving on a regularly scheduled business trip. Her old abandonment fears return when she sees him packing his suitcase. She knows he is traveling for business and will be back in three days, yet she becomes furious with him. She accuses him of not caring about her feelings and keeps him up half the night arguing.

Part 1-Carol’s View of Self: Unwanted and unlovable.

Part 2-Carol’s View of the Other: An abandoning, rejecting parent.

Part 3-Atmosphere: Poisoned by Carol’s hurt and anger.

Dave is a very controlling narcissist married to Callie. They divided their responsibilities. She gave up work to have their children and run the house, while he focused on earning enough money for their lifestyle. But Dave keeps picking fights over trivial household details. He kept Callie up half the night fighting because he felt disrespected when she rejected his suggestions of how she should load the dishwasher.

Part 1-Dave’s View of Self: Disrespected.

Part 2-Dave’s View of the Other: Acting superior to shame him.

Part 3-Atmosphere: Poisoned by Dave’s hatred and desire for revenge.

People with BPD or NPD often sabotage important intimate relationships when they get emotionally triggered. The trigger is usually something associated with traumatic childhood experiences. Their reactions become more understandable when we look at their rage and splitting through an object relations lens. They are not really fighting with the actual person in front of them. This person has become an unconscious symbol of someone from their past who hurt them.

Once you understand the limitations that a lack of whole object relations imposes on intimate relationships, it is easier to understand why these relationships fail or are filled with ugly and unnecessary fights (Masterson, 1976; 1981). Here are some different versions one might encounter.

BPD Form of All-Good: I am lovable, and you love me, and we are feeling great right now. You are my good mommy or daddy. You will never do anything bad to me.

BPD Form of All-Bad: I am ugly and unlovable, and you are rejecting and abandoning me. You are my bad mommy or daddy.

NPD Form of All-Good: I am perfect, special, and entitled to the best of everything. You are my admiring audience who mirrors my perfection back to me. I feel great.

NPD Form of All-Bad: I am worthless, defective garbage. You are attacking and humiliating me. I feel deep shame and fear public humiliation.

If we have object constancy, we do not get as mean during a fight. We can remember things we like about our partner and our past good history together. If we lack object constancy, we lose touch with everything good when we feel hurt, disappointed, or angry with our partner.

Even if your lover with NPD or BPD declared their undying love for you 10 minutes ago, once he or she gets angry, their love turns to hatred.

Part of Carol’s problem with Bill leaving on a work trip is that she lacks object constancy and cannot feel her emotional connection to Bill (or his to her) when he is not at home with her. And her lack of it made it easier to abuse Bill because everything good about him was out of her awareness when she split on him the night before he left.

When they feel good in your presence, they project all good things onto you. When they feel unhappy, they blame you. Sometimes the all-good role is projected onto one person and all the split-off bad things are projected onto someone else.

BPD Example: Jane sees a psychiatrist for medication and a psychologist for psychotherapy. She splits and sees her therapist as her hero and her psychiatrist as her enemy.

NPD Example: Catherine treats her son as her “golden child,” while making her daughter into the family scapegoat.

People with BPD or NPD can get overly caught up in their current emotional state. They do things they later regret because they act impulsively and do not stop to think through the likely consequences.

BPD Example: Tanya got triggered by her new lover not texting her “good night” and impulsively blocked him on social media because she became convinced that he was cheating on her. He decided he did not want drama and broke up with her.

NPD Example: Married Harry ended up in therapy trying to save his marriage because his wife caught him having an affair with a pretty intern at work. Her attention had made him feel special.

BPD: Get unconditional love and nurturing from a combination lover/parent figure.

NPD: Gain high status, be acknowledged as special, all-knowing, and always right.

BPD: Being abandoned or engulfed by the emotional needs of the other person.

NPD: Experiencing a humiliating public failure that exposes them as flawed.

People with borderline personality disorder and people with narcissistic personality disorder sometimes behave in a similar fashion. But when you look closely, it is obvious that they are very different diagnoses. The disorders create different types of vulnerabilities, and people with BPD or NPD want different things from their partners and get triggered by different types of events. Think of a pear and an apple. They are both fruit, but most people can easily tell them apart if they have experience with both.

The Best Ways For Star Trek Fans To Play Magic's New Set: A Guide For Beginner Players

When I sat down at my friendly local game store to participate in a pre-release drafting event for Magic: The Gathering's new "Star Trek" set, several players saw the Enterprise tattoo on my forearm and asked me the same question. If a longtime Magic player wanted to sample "Star Trek," what was the best place for them to start?

Ironically, I was there to ask the Mirror Universe version of that same question: If a longtime "Star Trek" fan wanted to sample the new Magic set, what's the best place for them to start?

I am new to Magic, having only picked up the game a few months ago to prime myself for the new "Trek" release. And one thing Magic and "Star Trek" have in common is that they're both absolutely daunting at first blush. One is a collectible card game with over 30 years of history and dozens of ways to play. The other is a sci-fi universe with nearly 1,000 episodes of television and 13 movies, with dozens of possible entry points.

I can answer one question immediately. "Trek" newbies should start with "Star Trek: Strange New Worlds," with its glossy production values and modern streaming sensibilities, before backtracking and sampling the various other shows and finding the one that connects with their tastes. (This is a sometimes controversial opinion, but I stick by it.)

But the other question involved some legwork, a lot of time spent at a gaming table, a great deal more time sorting through cards, and even a question proposed to the lead designers of the set itself. So, what is the best way for a "Star Trek" fan to sample the new Magic: The Gathering set? Here's what I learned.

One thing I will not do here is explain the rules of Magic: The Gathering, which are intuitive and easy to learn, but challenging to master. There are countless online resources to learn before you try anything I recommend below. I'm writing this piece under the assumption you intend to seek those out on your own.

The best way to truly dive in headfirst is to visit your local gaming shop and participate in a sealed or drafting event. Naturally, this is dependent on your location and whether your local gaming shop is an inviting and safe space (shout out to Mothership Games in Austin, Texas for playing host to my first sealed event). If such a place exists for you, check the calendar and see what's on offer. The good news is that the "Star Trek" set is brand new and there should be plenty of opportunities.

At events like these, players are organized into one-on-one matches and construct their decks from scratch, tearing open booster packs, examine their cards, and trying to build the most effective strategy possible around a 40-card deck. While experienced players will have an edge, they can't bring finely honed and powerful decks from home to immediately wipe the floor with you. Everyone starts at square one. And "Star Trek" fans should take very easily to this format, since the characters you'd expect to be powerful tend to have cards that are powerful. It also leads to some amusing team-ups, like how my winning deck involved an unlikely alliance between Deanna Troi and Gul Dukat.

In a friendly shop with a friendly crowd, this is Magic at its purest: a direct, competitive game where tactics at the table are as important as the strategy behind your deck construction. And the mechanics in the "Trek" set are intuitive and mesh well, meaning that creating a deck that functions as needed is fairly straightforward, and going with your gut choices (i.e., building around characters you like) often leads to strong results.

I can hear your next question: You don't have access to a local shop, or you don't feel comfortable going to one, so what's the best way to play at home? Once again, there are options.

You and a friend (or more) could always buy booster packs, tearing them open and building personal collections and constructing 60-card decks for standard play. However, I would recommend booster packs being the start of your collection, and once you have bought a few, track down single cards to complete your strategy rather than buying more and blindly hoping for strong pulls.

Another option is for you and a friend to buy a pre-release kit, as there are always plenty leftover after the initial week of special event. Use these to construct imperfect but fun starter decks to learn the game, and then grow and expand those decks as you see fit, doubling down on the characters and strategies you're enjoying.

However, the best at-home experience would be to pick up one of the set's Draft Night boxes, which are pricy but offer bang for your buck: enough cards for four friends to craft decks and go head-to-head for an evening, leaving everyone with enough cards to build upon in the future.

However, all of these options require tinkering, and suggest that you'll enjoy the process of building a deck as much as playing one. These options require additional legwork, additional financial investment, and even a bit of luck (although that can be mitigated by buying single cards from reputable online and brick-and-mortar shops). If you want to truly just dive into the game itself and skip the rest of the process, there is another way.

When I had a chance to speak to the key designers behind Magic: The Gathering's "Star Trek" set during a launch event in Los Angeles, I asked if the new set was friendly for "Star Trek" fans with no gaming experience, and if they had a recommendation on where to start.

First, they noted that the set was designed with new players in mind and puts narrative first. A card will generally behave like its counterpart in the "Star Trek" universe, so a fan knows that a Captain Kirk card will be a bit more headstrong and aggressive while Spock will allow for more wily, thoughtful strategy. This means a "Trek" fan should be able to grasp the new cards fairly quickly, using their existing knowledge as a crutch.

Second, they highlighted their recommendation for the best way to sample the game: the "Star Trek" commander decks.

Commander is the most popular format in Magic, where players utilize 100 card decks in four-player games that are slowly paced, socially driven, and generally take an entire evening to play. Think of them as more of a board game experience — or a nerd poker night — than a standard Magic set. Many Magic sets are accompanied by a few pre-constructed commander decks, which players can purchase, open, and play immediately without any prep. The "Star Trek" set has four of them: Federation Fleet, We Are the Borg, Klingon Fury, and Landing Party.

Specifically, the designers recommended the Federation Fleet commander deck as their choice for a "Star Trek" fan hoping to play the game.

Naturally, the next step was to test the designers' suggestion. I rounded up three friends and assigned them each a different Star Trek commander deck, taking Federation Fleet for myself. It's easy to see why this one was recommended: With Picard on the cover and a deck full of '90s "Trek" staples, it's the most popular era of the franchise in one 100-card deck.

Our four-player game took over three hours, and frankly, it was some of the best Magic I've ever played, with all four decks interacting in surprising and wild ways that left everyone at the table screaming and laughing. The "Trek" fans were impressed by the thematic touches, and how the mechanics relate to character personalities and motives, while the Magic players simply relished in the new design elements. After seeing the commander decks in action, I'm inclined to agree with the designers' recommendation. The Borg deck is scary and powerful, but makes them an immediate threat the other players will team up against to defeat. The Klingon deck is pure aggression, which may work against a new player's inherent shyness. The Landing Part deck, with its focus on Original Series characters, is fairly straightforward, but has a lot of moving pieces to consider. The mechanical simplicity of the Federation Fleet deck, and its arsenal of beloved characters, makes it easy to grasp and understand quickly.

Much like with "Star Trek" itself, there are countless ways to get into Magic: The Gathering, and no incorrect way. Ultimately, I agree with the designers: the pre-constructed commander decks, and specifically the Federation Fleet deck, are the best point of entry. Ideally, you'd buy all four and learn with friends for the best possible experience.

But like with "Star Trek" itself, you should be warned: once Magic has sucked you in, be prepared to be sucked in for the long haul.

Scientists Discover a Hidden Weakness in One of the Deadliest Cancers

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Researchers have identified another potential approach to treating pancreatic cancer, with early experiments suggesting that blocking a protein could slow tumor growth and strengthen immune defenses.

Pancreatic tumors can be surrounded by tissue so dense that it compresses blood vessels, making it difficult for nutrients to reach cancer cells. Even under these conditions, the cells continue growing. How they obtain the materials needed to survive has remained an important question.

Researchers at Fox Chase Cancer Center have identified one way pancreatic cancer cells obtain cholesterol and other fats, known as lipids. Their study, published in Nature Cancer, also identified a protein that helps control this process and could offer a potential target for cancer treatment.

The research focused on pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), the most common type of pancreatic cancer. These tumors contain large numbers of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), cells that produce and organize connective tissue. As CAFs and their collagen fibers expand, they can compress nearby blood vessels, restricting the tumor’s blood supply.

Despite contributing to this nutrient shortage, CAFs also help cancer cells overcome it.

“We found that CAFs can act as a source of these nutrients,” said Edna Cukierman, PhD, a professor and co-leader of the Cancer Signaling and Microenvironment Program at Fox Chase and co-senior author of the study. “Using laboratory and animal models, we discovered that CAF units acquire cholesterol from the bloodstream and transfer it to nearby pancreatic cancer cells through a process called trogocytosis”

Trogocytosis occurs when one cell takes small pieces of another cell’s membrane during direct contact. In this case, pancreatic cancer cells acquire pieces of CAF membranes containing cholesterol and other lipids that support their survival and growth.

The researchers discovered that this transfer depends on a protein called anoctamin 6 (ANO6), which helps prepare the CAF membrane for the exchange.

“These findings help explain how pancreatic cancer cells obtain essential nutrients despite the unusual structure of the pancreatic tumor microenvironment,” said Igor Astsaturov, MD, PhD, a professor at Fox Chase and co-senior author of the study. “Cancer-associated fibroblasts are not passive bystanders. They actively support tumor growth by transferring lipids to cancer cells.”

When researchers reduced or eliminated ANO6 in CAFs, the cells became less effective at supporting pancreatic cancer cell survival. In mouse models, tumors supported by CAFs with functional ANO6 grew substantially faster than those without functional ANO6.

The same nutrient transfer process also appeared to interfere with the immune system.

Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) are immune cells that recognize and kill cancer cells. When these T cells come into contact with CAFs, they can acquire membrane material containing cholesterol. Excess cholesterol in the tumor environment can contribute to T-cell exhaustion, making these immune cells less effective at fighting cancer.

“With Serge Fuchs, professor at the University of Pennsylvania and co-author on the paper, we were surprised to find that the same cellular mechanism can benefit the tumor in two ways – by supplying cancer cells with lipids while also weakening cancer-fighting T cells,” said Astsaturov. “Targeting ANO6 could potentially disrupt both of these tumor-supporting functions at the same time.”

In mouse models, blocking ANO6 reduced cholesterol accumulation in T cells within tumors and improved markers of their activity. Laboratory experiments also showed that T cells exposed to CAFs with reduced ANO6 were better able to kill cancer cells.

The researchers then tested whether drugs that interfere with ANO6-dependent activity could slow tumor growth. In mouse models, treatment with niclosamide or clofazimine significantly reduced tumor weight. Niclosamide also reduced cholesterol delivery to pancreatic cancer cells.

These findings suggest that targeting ANO6 could both restrict a nutrient source for pancreatic tumors and improve the immune response against them. However, the results remain preclinical, and further research is needed to determine whether selectively blocking ANO6 would work as a treatment in patients.

Trogocytosis is also only one of several ways pancreatic cancer cells may obtain lipids. The researchers suggest that the drugs tested could potentially help treat other difficult cancers, although that possibility requires further investigation.

“We believe our findings identify ANO6 as a potential therapeutic target that could simultaneously disrupt the nutrient supply supporting pancreatic cancer cells and reduce the immune-suppressive effects of the tumor microenvironment,” said Astsaturov.

The findings also build on nearly a decade of research into how the tissue surrounding pancreatic tumors supports cancer growth.

“Excitingly, reducing ANO6 expression also disrupted the stromal synapse-mimicry circuit we previously identified, which coordinates tumor nutritional support, CAF-unit metabolism, and immune suppression. These findings position ANO6 as the missing mechanistic link that unifies nearly a decade of discoveries by our team,” said Cukierman.
