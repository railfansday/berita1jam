Hyperliquid API in Python: our $25 cap refused 892 of 892 $25 orders

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 3 • Originally published at botcookery.com

Originally published on BotCookery, a free site of build recipes for AI-built trading and prediction-market bots. Written with AI assistance. Every number below comes from real runs against Hyperliquid's public API on Oct 25, 2026. Signed calls are testnet only, and nothing here is financial advice or a trading signal.

We publish a copy-paste prompt that has an AI coding tool build a small Python starter for the Hyperliquid API: reads on mainnet or testnet, and signed orders on testnet only, capped at $25. On Oct 25 we checked whether that prompt actually holds up. We pasted the Oct 22 version into a clean folder, let it build, and ran it live: reads on both networks, a dry-run order for every listed perp, and signed testnet calls with a throwaway key that held no funds.

Most of it worked. Reads, asset lookups, price rounding, the --mainnet flag and the testnet-only guard all did what they should. Every one of the 158 live testnet perps and 178 live mainnet perps got a valid price at $10.

Then we asked for $25, and the bot refused. Every time, on both networks: 424 of 424 dry-run $25 orders on testnet and 468 of 468 on mainnet. The cap meant to keep orders small was the thing blocking them. That was one of four problems the rebuild turned up; here's each one, what we saw in the logs, and how the prompt changed.

Everything is a JSON POST. Reads go to /info with a type (meta, allMids, l2Book, candleSnapshot, clearinghouseState, openOrders) and need no signature. Orders go to /exchange and are signed, which is where the official hyperliquid-python-sdk earns its keep. Testnet lives at api.hyperliquid-testnet.xyz, mainnet at api.hyperliquid.xyz, and they are different markets with different prices. The first lines of each of our cycles were testnet mids, then mainnet mids:

A few rules the prompt spells out, because an AI tool will otherwise guess:

The starter places a post-only (Alo) limit 10% away from the mid, so the order rests instead of filling. Size is usd / limit, rounded up to szDecimals. Rounding up is deliberate: rounding down, or sizing on the mid, leaves a $10 buy under the $10 exchange minimum.

But rounding up always lands the notional a little over what you asked for. And the cap compared that notional to $25. So in the original build, plan BTC buy 25 printed:

That wasn't a BTC quirk. It refused all 424 dry-run $25 orders on testnet and all 468 on mainnet. The only orders that could pass were ones small enough that rounding didn't push them over, which made the "$10 to $25" range really "$10 to a bit under $25".

The fix is to check two different things. The requested amount must be between $10 and $25. The order's own notional must be at least $10 and no more than the request plus one size step (10^-szDecimals x limit). From the rebuilt version:

0.00035 x 72,876 = 25.5066. Over $25 by one step's worth of rounding, which is exactly what the cap now allows.

Across three cycles of the rebuilt version, testnet listed 212 perps and mainnet 234. Every live one passed at both $10 and $25 on both sides (316/316 testnet, 356/356 mainnet), and every delisted one was refused:

The prompt has the bot call scheduleCancel before every order: a dead-man switch that cancels all open orders at a set time if the bot dies. The original rule was strict. If scheduleCancel didn't return ok, send no order.

The problem is that Hyperliquid only allows scheduleCancel above a trading-volume threshold. A published reply shows the error as Cannot set scheduled cancel time until enough volume traded. Required: $1000000. A brand-new testnet account will never clear that, so the strict rule meant a reader following the recipe could never place an order at all.

To be clear about what we saw: our throwaway key was never approved and the account was never funded, so the exchange stopped us earlier, before the volume check could come up. The orders.jsonl log from the run shows that refusal on every attempt:

So the volume error itself is sourced, not observed, and the code path for it is tested offline only.

The new behavior keeps the safety without the dead end:

Without the exchange's dead-man switch, the bot is its own. That's a weaker guarantee, so the hold is short and the cancel is confirmed.

One more check from the same runs: place and cancel-all don't accept --mainnet at all. Passing it gets cli.py: error: unrecognized arguments: --mainnet before anything is signed.

Hyperliquid lets a main account approve an "agent" (API wallet) key that signs on its behalf. The prompt's safety notes say to keep the main key away from the bot and give the bot only the agent key.

The original approve_agent.py loaded the main key from the same env file it then wrote the agent key to. So after a successful approval, the key you're supposed to keep away from the bot sat right next to it.

Now it reads the main key once with getpass.getpass(), never writes it anywhere, and saves only HL_MODE=testnet, HL_AGENT_PRIVATE_KEY and HL_ACCOUNT_ADDRESS (the main address) to ~/.config/hl-bot/.env with chmod 600. It also saves only the agent key the SDK returns from approve_agent, and only if the result is ok. On our unfunded account it saved nothing:

Trusted SSL Certificates for Localhost

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 3 • Originally published at kevinleary.net

The best way I’ve found to add SSL certificates to a localhost environment is using mkcert, which is technically not a self-signed certificate at all. Instead, it creates a local certificate signing authority, adds it to your machine, and uses that to sign certificates. The end result is a fully valid SSL certificate that is not self-signed, so it works with modern browsers and aligns with security protocols that block self-signed SSL certs.

mkcert is an open source command line tool written by Filippo Valsorda, a cryptographer who spent years on the Go security team at Google. As described on GitHub, it’s:

a simple tool for making locally-trusted development certificates that requires no configuration.

Under the hood it does two things. First, it generates a private certificate authority (CA) that lives only on your machine and installs it into your system and browser trust stores, the same lists of trusted authorities that let your browser trust certificates from Let’s Encrypt or DigiCert. Second, it signs leaf certificates with that CA for whatever hostnames you ask for. Because the signing authority is trusted by your machine, every certificate it issues is trusted too: real HTTPS, green padlock, no warnings, no exceptions to click through. This is fundamentally different from the classic openssl self-signed certificate. A self-signed cert has no authority behind it, so browsers correctly treat it as untrustworthy. mkcert gives you the full chain of trust, just scoped privately to your computer. The private CA key never leaves your machine, which is also why this is safe: nobody else’s browser will ever trust certificates signed by your local CA.

mkcert supports Mac, Windows and Linux machines, so it should work with your setup.

The nss package is only needed if you use Firefox, which keeps its own certificate store separate from the operating system’s.

What a killed Playwright script leaves behind on Linux: processes, profile dirs and a SIGTERM that does not exit

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A Playwright script ends in more ways than await browser.close(): it throws, someone calls process.exit(), a container runtime sends SIGTERM, the OOM killer sends SIGKILL. I wanted to know what each of these leaves behind on a Linux host: running Chromium processes, temp directories, or a Node process that ignores the signal.

I ran all of it on 2026-11-03: Linux 6.8, Node v24.22.2, Playwright 1.63.0 with the bundled chrome-headless-shell (revision 1243). Everything below is that one machine and one browser build. Containers, systemd, macOS and Windows are not covered.

The child script launches a browser, opens a page, prints READY, and then either waits for a GO line on stdin and ends in a chosen way, or hangs so that the harness can send it a signal. The cdp mode does not launch anything: it attaches to a Chromium that the harness started separately, which is what many browser pools do.

The harness reads the process tree from /proc while the child is running, ends the child, waits three seconds, and then checks which of those PIDs are still alive (zombies do not count) and whether the browser's profile directory is still on disk. It kills and deletes only what it started.

In the cdp rows the first count was 14 or 15 and the second 10 or 11 across my runs, so read that as "most of the tree stays". The other rows were identical on every run. In the cdp rows the profile directory belongs to the browser the harness started itself, and it is still there because that browser is still running.

Playwright's own launch cleans up after a crash, even after SIGKILL. throw, exit and SIGKILL all end with zero Chromium processes. In a one-off check (script not included) I printed the command line of the launched browser, and it ends in --remote-debugging-pipe. I did not trace the mechanism, but it fits a browser that exits when the other end of its pipe closes. The cdp rows are the contrast: that browser listens on a port, and nothing ties it to the script. In the same one-off check, chromium.launchServer() also left no processes after SIGKILL, so it is not only launch().

Browsers you did not launch are not cleaned up at all. In the cdp rows, about ten Chromium processes stayed alive after browser.close() and after SIGKILL. This is the case where a leak actually happens. The docs for browser.close() say that for a browser you connected to, it "clears all created contexts belonging to this browser and disconnects from the browser server". It does not stop the browser. Whoever started it owns its lifetime. If you run a pool this way, you need a supervisor that restarts or kills the browser itself, and a periodic check that counts processes.

SIGKILL leaves the profile directory. The launch SIGKILL row says profile dir left: true. Each one was about 224 KB in my runs, which is nothing for a single crash and something for a worker that gets OOM-killed a few times a day on a small /tmp. A normal close(), an exception, process.exit(), SIGTERM and SIGINT all removed it. I did not look into which Playwright process does the deleting. Whatever it is, SIGKILL gives it no chance to run.

launch SIGTERM says node: still running. The child had a setInterval standing in for an HTTP server, and after SIGTERM the browser was gone but Node kept going. The reason is the handleSIGTERM launch option, which defaults to true and is documented as "Close the browser process on SIGTERM". Playwright installs a handler that closes the browser. Node's documentation says that once a SIGTERM or SIGINT listener is installed, the default behaviour (exit with code 128 plus the signal number) is removed. So the server stays up with no browser behind it. SIGINT ended with exit code 130 in my run; I did not look into why it differs from SIGTERM.

I did not test what Docker or Kubernetes do from here. The consequence I can state from this measurement is only that a service which does nothing on SIGTERM keeps running with a dead browser, and anything that waits for it to exit has to fall back to SIGKILL.

This is the service skeleton I used to test two remedies: close the browser and exit on SIGTERM and SIGINT, and sweep profile directories that nothing is using when the service starts.

The sweep treats a directory as stale only if no process on the machine has it in a --user-data-dir argument and it is older than a minute. Chromium rewrites its own argument list with spaces on Linux, which is why the pattern stops at whitespace and not only at the NUL byte. I found that when my first version of the harness reported "profile left: false" for a directory that was plainly still there.

To test it I used a second script that is not included: it starts the service, SIGKILLs it, starts it twice more, sends SIGTERM to both, and counts new profile directories under /tmp each time. The sweep deletes everything under that prefix that no process references, so the test script lowered the age limit to five seconds and waited six. Results:

After SIGKILL one profile directory stayed. The next start swept it (swept stale profiles: 1). Two live services each kept their own directory, so the sweep did not touch a running browser. Both services exited with code 0 on SIGTERM, in 106 to 281 ms across my runs, and no new directories were left.

Sources: Playwright BrowserType launch options for handleSIGTERM and handleSIGINT, the browser.close() text from the Playwright 1.63.0 type definitions, and Node.js process signal events.

Written with AI assistance. Every code sample was run on 2026-11-03 with Playwright 1.63.0 (chrome-headless-shell revision 1243) and Node v24.22.2; the output above is from that run.

Chord Buys Its Rival Zenlytic To Build An Agentic Version Of A CDP

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Two data infrastructure startups with agentic commerce ambitions are combining forces.

On Tuesday, Chord, a six-year-old commerce tech startup, acquired Zenlytic, an AI startup with an anthropomorphized data analyst agent named “Zoë,” for $5 million.

Chord’s co-founder and CEO, Bryan Mahoney, told AdExchanger that the combined business has around 30 full-time employees and some 60 customers, with an annual run rate nearing $10 million.

Chord launched as a “headless commerce” software company, Mahoney said.

At the time, the headless approach was catching on with Shopify merchants and social-native sellers. The idea was to decouple the front-end website from back-end fulfillment, making for a more agile business and faster-loading pages at a time when Google’s SEO prioritized speed. Shopify has since addressed the problem by taking steps to make its own storefronts faster.

Since the rise of LLMs, Chord and Zenlytic have both pivoted to become explicitly AI-based data infrastructure services. For instance, Mahoney said, Chord is now paid on a usage basis rather than traditional subscription pricing. And because pricing is usage-based, Chord closely tracks how often users prompt its AI agent for an answer – a rate that has accelerated to about once every 20 seconds, he said.

Although the more people use agents, the more ways they find to abuse them.

Mahoney pointed to a recent story in which someone figured out they could use McDonald’s online customer support and ordering agent to answer demanding math and computer science questions – a workaround to having to pay for one’s own AI tokens. (McDonald’s has since fixed this, by the way.)

Chord guards against this kind of misuse. It’s trained its AI agent with the necessary context so that off-base prompts return a response that this isn’t the right agent for the task. That includes requests to use its agent “like a therapist,” say, or for something like “make these results look better to a CFO,” Mahoney said.

Those guardrails matter more as Chord’s business shifts.

The business model is now more like an agentic version of a CDP, Mahoney said. For example, Sonos, a Chord client, can use it to track customers and enrich first-party data, such as understanding when customers are moving to a new home – an opportune time to send emails or SMS notifications about new products.

The data analyst agent “largely replaces” Chord’s own analyst agent, Mahoney said.

Guess human data analysts aren’t the only ones who need to be worried about being replaced by AI. Even agents can be put out of work.

But Chord also has other agents that work better alongside Zoë, Mahoney added. Chord has gone deeper into commerce infrastructure, for instance, with use cases geared toward Shopify-type businesses, and customers using the Snowflake data warehouse in particular. Zenlytic, by contrast, has tended to work with large enterprise brands of all kinds, not just in ecommerce, and supports many more cloud data warehouses.

The goal, Mahoney said, is for some of Zenlytic’s large customers, including Black & Decker, Verizon, J.Crew and Stanley, to adopt Chord’s data infrastructure and activation, including the ability to segment profiles or audiences for campaign targeting and measurement.

But winning over large brands requires confidence in the technology, and right now there’s an odd mix of growing trust and distrust of AI agent outputs.

Mahoney noted that the outright “hallucinations” of earlier LLMs have become relatively rare. “Now it might be more like a lucid dream,” he said. The outputs are believable and often can feel correct.

And so marketers need to be aware that a confident-sounding AI agent can still lead them badly astray. It’s a concern Mahoney shares.

“We constantly [need to] evaluate the answers that we’re getting back to make sure that the way we’re using AI is increasing confidence on a daily basis, not getting eroded,” he said. “These are some of the things that keep me up at night.”

EPA throws in the towel on policing air pollution from factory farms

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Critics say EPA is effectively abandoning Clean Air Act enforcement at factory farms.

The Environmental Protection Agency late last week announced it would end a 20-plus-year effort to develop methods to monitor air pollution from factory farms, effectively shuttering the agency’s stop-and-start attempts to regulate the potentially pollutant-spewing facilities under the Clean Air Act.

The announcement came just days before the midterm elections and was characterized by the agency as a long-awaited reprieve for farmers, a historically important constituency for Trump and the Republican party.

When tens of thousands of animals are packed into one place, pollution from their waste is blown from ventilation fans or emanates from giant lagoons of liquid effluent. Livestock facilities emit fine particulate matter, ammonia, nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide and volatile organic compounds, all contributing to a toxic mix. Research has shown that emissions from these operations are a major cause of air pollution, causing nearly 12,700 deaths in the United States a year.

Strictly speaking, concentrated animal feeding operations, or CAFOs, are required to get permits under the Clean Air Act and report emissions of these pollutants under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation and Liability and Act and the Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act. But through a deal crafted in tandem with the EPA and the livestock industry, they’ve been given amnesty from doing so for more than 20 years.

The arrangement goes back to the early 2000s when the agency attempted to prosecute CAFOs for exceeding limits on some of these pollutants. The industry pushed back, arguing there was no adequate way of measuring them. So the agency worked with industry groups to develop what was dubbed the Air Consent Agreement, through which the EPA granted thousands of CAFOs safe harbor from legal action in exchange for their participation in a study that was funded by the industry.

The goal of the study, the agency said, was to develop tools and models that CAFOs could use to “determine whether they emit pollutants at levels that require them to apply for permits under the CAA or submit notifications under CERCLA or EPCRA” and “produce a scientifically sound basis for measuring and estimating air emissions” from these facilities.

But since the adoption of the agreement in 2005, the agency has failed to produce any air “emissions estimating methodologies,” even prompting a 2017 report from the agency’s Office of Inspector General that faulted it for slow-walking the process.

Meanwhile, the Trump administration and Congress have also gutted reporting requirements for factory farms to report air pollutants under CERCLA, also known as the Superfund Act, and EPCRA.

“As EPA has been saying it’s proceeding with this plan, it’s been dismantling all of its own tools to regulate emissions,” said Tarah Heinzen, the legal director for the advocacy group Food & Water Watch, which has tracked the proceedings from the beginning and filed dozens of lawsuits attempting to push regulators to control pollution from CAFOs. “Now it has announced it has no intention, basically, of ever holding factory farms accountable under the Clean Air Act, either.”

In its Oct. 30 announcement, the agency noted that in the two decades since the study, known as the National Air Emissions Monitoring Study, was conducted, many farms have modernized their barns and manure systems.

“Good science requires good data, and these data are too old and too limited to support methods that farms could rely on,” said Abigale Tardif, the acting assistant administrator for the agency’s Office of Air and Radiation, in a statement. “We would rather tell farmers that plainly than issue numbers we cannot stand behind.”

Rob Brenneman, president of the National Pork Producers Council, which has long pushed for the decision, said in a statement: “This move confirms what farmers have long known: Every farm is different, and there is no one-size-fits-all model to apply to them.”

Heinzen noted that the agency’s decision simply means that this administration has no intention of regulating air emissions from CAFOs, but for now, the Clean Air Act still requires permits for facilities that exceed emissions thresholds. The amnesty granted to these facilities will no longer be in effect now that the agreement has concluded.

“There’s no EPA-issued methodology to estimate emissions,” she said. “That doesn’t mean that there aren’t good studies out there of emissions from different types of animal feeding operations that can be used.”

Heinzen pointed to California, which has models for livestock emissions that it uses to enforce its own air regulations. “We just don’t have one set of national emissions factors from EPA,” Heinzen said. “That does not absolve these factory farms from their obligation to comply with federal law.”

Still, under this administration, it’s clear air emissions from factory farms won’t be measured and monitored any time soon. “No one’s going to estimate anything,” Heinzen noted. “As EPA said in its press release, we’re certainly not about to try to enforce the law.”

The Air Compliance Agreement did not extend to methane. The U.S. livestock industry is the single-biggest source of methane in the country, but Congress effectively blocks livestock facilities from reporting methane emissions in annual spending bills.

Organic sales grew 19%, orders and backlog hit records, and management raised its organic growth guidance. But GAAP earnings per share rose only 7%, to $2.77. Acquisition debt and amortization absorbed most of the extra operating profit.

Debt is the main drag. Net interest expense nearly tripled, to $198 million, as borrowings roughly doubled alongside the Boyd Thermal and Ultra PCS purchases.

Amortization is the second drag. Acquired intangible assets are expensed over time, and that charge more than doubled. Adjusted EPS excludes both charges, so it grew far faster than GAAP. Full-year GAAP EPS guidance is roughly flat with 2025, while adjusted guidance implies double-digit growth.

Orders are the real signal. Twelve-month rolling orders hit record dollars in all three segments, and Electrical backlog grew 46%.

Watch whether fourth-quarter organic growth lands in the guided 13% to 15% range, down from 19% this quarter. Also track net interest expense, which sets how much operating profit reaches GAAP earnings.
