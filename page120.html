When it comes to generating AI images, both Gemini and ChatGPT do a passable job. In most cases, the results will look AI-generated, but they likely won’t have glaring spelling issues or missing limbs. Now, OpenAI is trying something new, introducing GIF support for ChatGPT. It's the company's first "moving images" feature since OpenAI shut down Sora, and while you can generate hyper-realistic videos in Gemini, this new ChatGPT feature is trying to fill in the gap, in a small way.

OpenAI is pitching the new feature as an animation tool, not exactly as a way to create looping videos—though that’s how I ended up using it. You give ChatGPT a prompt, as detailed as you can make it, and the chatbot produces a GIF in just a few seconds. You can start with your own drawings or images, too. ChatGPT uses sketches as the base for animations, though it can turn any still image into animated GIFs. Like other AI editing tools, you can edit it if ChatGPT didn't quite get it right: You can ask ChatGPT to make the GIF longer or shorter, remove certain objects, change the style, slow down transitions, among other edits.

AI art can be quite sterile, but I was surprised by how adding motion and animation to the mix makes generating images a lot more fun. Unlike traditional GIFs, which usually pull from existing video clips, you can turn anything you want into a GIF, which is especially cool if you're using your own art. In my testing, I found this to be the most rewarding part. It’s not always possible to one-shot a GIF with a single prompt. But after a few rounds of edits, I ended up creating some GIFs that I actually wanted to share in my WhatsApp groups.

That being said, I think human-made GIFs are still the gold standard here. For example, I asked ChatGPT to generate a video of someone brewing a pour-over, with a gooseneck kettle, showing the coffee blooming process. It did okay, but it’s nothing like actually making a pour-over. The kettle doesn’t move in circles, the water doesn’t rise evenly, there are no bubbles, and the steam is all over the place.

Compare that to the GIF below, which comes from a royalty-free video. Even though it’s a compressed, low-quality, and noisy GIF, it still shows the movement of the pour, the water rising, and even the bubbles. The details captured by a real video just aren’t there in ChatGPT GIFs. If that’s what you’re expecting, you will be disappointed. But I don't think that's the point of this feature anyway.

I found that ChatGPT excels when you’re making something fun, and you don’t look into the details too much. It pulled off a loop of a cat playing with a ball, and it was great at making a festive GIF for Diwali. It even made a GIF based on a photo. I asked it to create a fun promotional GIF for my JBL Bluetooth speakers, and it did the job quite well. At first, the transitions were too jarring, and the movement just made no sense. After some editing, though, I got it to a decent place.

Still, the main issue with ChatGPT GIFs is that complex movement and transitions aren’t nailed down. A hand shifts position weirdly between frames, and colors and objects appear out of nowhere. But if you can get over these issues, you can make some fun, lighthearted GIFs and memes to share with your friends on Instagram or WhatsApp.

‘an open field’ Is a Deeply Personal Story of Loss, Grief and Compassion (Exclusive Netflix Trailer)

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The award-winning short film, directed by Teboho Edkins and produced by his father, dives into the mourning following the death of the filmmaker’s brother in a Boeing airplane crash over Ethiopia.

In March 2019, a Boeing 737 MAX crashed in Ethiopia just six minutes after taking off from the airport in the country’s capital, Addis Ababa. It had 157 people on board, and all of them died.Among them was Max Thabiso Edkins. His older brother Teboho Edkins decided to make a film about the experience of grief and mourning that his family and the local community went through.The result is the very personal 38-minute short film an open field, directed by Edkins and produced by his father, Don Edkins, and Carine Chichkowsky. The film is now going global, hitting Netflix on Dec. 1, about a year after its world premiere at the International Documentary Film Festival Amsterdam (IDFA) 2025, where it won the best short documentary award. Related Stories Business Netflix Taps Mattel's Roberto Stanichi to Lead Consumer Products and Experiences Business Dark Slope to Open New Virtual Production Stage in Vancouver (Exclusive)

The title of the movie becomes clear when you watch it. In an open field, the filmmaker finds out that dozens of nearby villagers immediately converged on the crash site to help. Some even stood vigil for a month to protect and preserve the area. Years later, they continue to mourn alongside family members who visit the site, including Edkins and his father, also making the film a story about collective grief and compassion.

“Boeing’s widely criticized response to the crash stands in sharp contrast to the community’s enduring compassion,” highlights a synopsis. “Both a personal essay on loss and an exploration of the potential healing power of collective mourning, Edkins’s film is a quiet testament to humanity – moments of warmth, resolve, grief, fear, patience, and simple acts of kindness.”Indeed, the film feels like a cinematic attempt to connect with a late brother and son’s spirit. In that sense, an open field is a family film. And it may be set in a far-away place for many viewers, but it transcends borders, given that the plane’s passengers came from more than 30 countries and that anyone who has lost a loved one has faced the burden of that loss.

an open field is a South Africa-France-Germany production by Day Zero Film and Video Productions in South Africa, Day Zero Films UG in Germany and Survivance in France. The cinematographer is Jide Akinleminu, the editor is Anne Fabini. The film, shot in color and black-and-white, features dialogue in both English and Amharic.

“This is a delicate and personal work and about something fundamental to being human,” says director Teboho Edkins. “I hope that the film can find its way to as many people as possible, and that something in it might resonate with them. Having Netflix bring the film to its global audiences is very meaningful to me, and I’m grateful for their support as it makes its way out into the world.”

Motorhead’s Classic ‘Orgasmatron’ Gets a Mighty 40th Anniversary Sonic Upgrade and Deluxe Edition: Album Review

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The best rock bands create their greatest work with their backs against the wall — witness the Rolling Stones — and the legendary British hard-rock powerhouse Motorhead was no exception.

Forty years ago, the band had seen the dissolution of its classic “No Sleep ‘Til Hammersmith” lineup accompanied by a pair of sub-par studio albums, and had been largely given up for dead. But the band’s leader, Lemmy Kilmister, was nothing if not a survivor. He regrouped with a pair of blazing guitarists and thunderous ex-Saxon drummer Pete Gill and, for this album, joined forces with the unexpected choice of producer Bill Laswell, who was well known in experimental-leaning New York rock/jazz circles — and producer Herbie Hancock’s left-field 1983 hit “Rockit” — but seemed an odd fit for Motorhead. Related Stories BBC Deputy Director-General Says ‘A Lot Has Changed’ After Editing Error That Triggered Trump’s $10 Billion Lawsuit: We’re in a ‘Much Stronger Place’ Trump Says Google CEO Rejected His Request to Rename Atlantic Ocean on Google Maps

However, Laswell had been experimenting with hard rock at the time, and he came to Motorhead fresh from overseeing the pioneering 1984 rap-rock fusion, John Lydon and Afrika Bambaata’s “World Destruction,” as well as Public Image Ltd.’s fiery “Album,” which united Lydon with a wide-ranging band including guitar wizard Steve Vai, ex-Cream drummer Ginger Baker and Funkadelic’s Bernie Worrell.

The result of this unlikely union was “Orgasmatron,” certainly Motorhead’s most experimental album and one that drew a polarized reaction from many fans and the band itself — the press materials for this reissue euphemistically state, “The album’s production was the subject of much discussion among the band and their fans” — who said its mix was too cluttered and took the band too far from the basics. But personally speaking, I loved it and still do.

In an era when music fans were much more tribal than today, Motorhead were one of the rare bands who were respected in both punk and heavy metal circles. Like AC/DC, their earlier music was rock and roll stripped down to muscle and bone — scorching guitars, piledriving drums and Lemmy’s vein-bulging roar of a voice — like a Terminator version of Chuck Berry. And on “Orgasmatron,” we got all of that in a more-adventurous sonic setting: The songs are classic Motorhead, but hazy guitar overdubs, percussion and effects swoop in and out of the mix, especially on the closing title track, a cauldron of seething guitars, driving drums and Lemmy’s voice, which is laden with effects that make him sound like Darth Vader and Vecna morphed together. The song is like molten lava in musical form, and includes Lemmy’s best-ever lyrics: “I am the one, Orgasmatron, the outstretched grasping hand/ My image is of agony, my servants rape the land/ Obsequious and arrogant, clandestine and vain/ Two thousand years of misery, of torture in my name.” (The song

Subsequent re-releases have punched up the mix, and this new 40th anniversary edition presents not only a remastered version of Laswell’s original mix, but a 2026 remix from the band’s longtime latter-day producer Cameron Webb that clears away some of the haze and presents the guitars and drums in cleaner sound (although to be honest it doesn’t sound radically different). They’ve also cleaned out the vault, adding some rehearsal tapes (which sound like they were recorded on a boombox), an outtake and a BBC session featuring several songs from the album along with the classic “Killed by Death” — as well as Lemmy reading the lyrics to the song “Orgasmatron,” unaccompanied, like some sort of evil poet.

Despite the polarized reaction to the original mix, “Orgasmatron” proved definitively that Motorhead were back, and the band launched into its second golden era in earnest. And the band got back to basics soon enough: Classic-era drummer Phil Taylor returned the following year for the “Rock N’ Roll” album, which marked a return to their usual sound and production, and the band roared off into the sunset, only disbanding after Lemmy’s death in 2015 at the age of 75.

While the bonus tracks here are nice to have but not necessarily electrifying, the remastered and otherwise soup-ed up sound on the original album makes this one of the top reissues of the year — “Orgasmatron” now roars louder than ever.

Anker Soundcore Space 2 Pro review: Big bang for less bucks

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The company’s latest headphones rival Apple, Bose and Sony at a much lower price.

We may receive a commission on purchases made from links.

If you don't like the idea of paying $350 to $500 for a set of flagship headphones, Anker's Soundcore line offers a lot of performance at much lower prices. Over the years, the company has refined its formula by balancing sound and features in various packages so as to compete with the likes of Apple, Bose, Sony and Sennheiser. With the Space 2 Pro, Anker put its new Thus audio chip in headphones for the first time, making lofty claims about the upgraded voice clarity as a result. At $200, these aren't just an affordable alternative to the big names in headphones, they're a worthy contender in their own right.

The first thing that stood out to me about the Space 2 Pro is the design. These do not look like they cost $200, especially not the grey version that I tested. Anker refined the look of the Space line here for a premium aesthetic and a build that's lightweight, but not overly plastic. The sliders for fit adjustments are metal and both the headband and ear pads are wrapped in soft leather. There are also subtle metallic accents to complete the premium design, like the circles on the outside of the ear cups and subtly placed product name.

Even though the Space 2 Pro is the flagship Soundcore headphones, the price tag pits them up against decidedly midrange competition. Design-wise, the Space 2 Pro is vastly superior in that category, and that advantage continues into audio performance. These headphones have more bass and overall clarity than you'll typically find in the $200 range. The 35mm drivers are made with two different materials in order to maximize both high-end clarity and deep bass.

When I listened to Mastodon's Marrow Deep, Brann Dailor's drums boom throughout and Troy Sanders' bass provides the backbone for the guitar riffs. Thanks to Anker's "crystalline highs" here, guitars and synths cut through the mix clearly, so you never miss a note of Bill Kelliher's handiwork. The overall clarity and detail on the Space 2 Pro is really on display with more acoustic-driven genres, like Zach Bryan's Young Manhood and Other Donatable Organs. The piano, acoustic guitar and string section seem to float behind the interwoven vocals of Bryan and Lana Del Rey on "Lies," for example.

Anker is very proud that the Space 2 Pro currently holds the Guiness World Record for the "clearest over-ear headphones for calls." I was immediately skeptical that this would hold any weight in real-world use, but boy was I wrong.

Plenty of headphones manage to nullify background noise, but they do so at great expense to voice clarity. But thanks to that Thus chip and a six-microphone array, the Space 2 Pro offers extremely clear voice performance while effectively blocking background noise. Not only do they combat would-be distractions exceptionally well, they do so without making you sound even the slightest bit robotic. I thought I sounded pretty close to how I did in a quiet room when the processing kicked in, maybe only the slightest bit muffled, but never like a digitized version of myself.

The company promises battery life of up to 30 hours when you have active noise cancellation (ANC) on, or up to 60 hours with it off. During my testing, which I conducted almost entirely with noise cancellation enabled, I got 31 hours before the Space 2 Pro shut off. I did take a call or two a day where I needed to switch to transparency mode so I didn't feel the need to shout to hear myself speaking, but I'd say 95 percent of the time, I was using ANC.

The most disappointing aspect of the Space 2 Pro to me are a number of subpar audio features. The Thus chip powers what Anker calls Hear ID 5.0, a feature that uses a hearing test to customize the sound of the headphones to your preferences. I didn't think the resulting tailored audio profile offered any meaningful improvement over the stock tuning, neither did spatial audio settings or the collection of curated presets. I tested all of them, of course, but every time I used one, I found myself longing for the out-of-the-box sound after just a few minutes of use.

Anker boasts Adaptive ANC 3.0 on the Space 2 Pro, a noise-canceling setup that relies on the Thus chip and eight microphones to combat background noise. The company specifically lists "blocking office chatter and keyboard clicks for instant silence" among the headphones' strengths. While I do think the Space 2 Pro does an above average job of lowering the volume on human voices, and it definitely blocks keyboard clicks, its general noise canceling abilities are just okay. The headphones struggled with constant noise sources, like white noise machines and particularly raucous fans.

On Anker's flagship earbuds, the Liberty 5 Pro Max, the company built an AI note taking assistant into the charging case (which has a touchscreen). Even though the Space 2 Pro doesn't have a case, it does have plenty of microphones, and "AI Note-Taker" is currently listed as "TBD" on the spec sheet. If that feature does eventually arrive, there are two potential issues with it.

First, it will be a lot more awkward to wear over-ear headphones in a meeting than it would be to slide a small earbuds case to the middle of the table. Second, if you plan to regularly use Anker's transcription service and cloud storage, you'll have to pay for a subscription. You only get two hours of transcriptions and 10,000 minutes of audio storage monthly for free, and only for two years. Plans are then either $8.33 or $20 per month (billed annually) depending on your needs. Since it's unclear if this feature will ever arrive, its absence doesn't impact my overall assessment.

It's easy to dismiss Soundcore as merely a budget brand, but that would be a mistake. With products like the Space 2 Pro, Anker proves it can hang with the best, offering comparable performance for significantly less money. In fact, I'd argue if voice clarity is your primary factor in choosing a new set of headphones, this model should be your first choice. For many, though, that's further down the list of priorities.

While there are caveats when it comes to ANC performance, audio features and a (potentially) unnecessary note taker, the Space 2 Pro has a lot of premium traits. The overall sound quality is good, the design is refined, the fit is comfortable and battery life claims hold true. At a time when the best overall headphones you can buy are $460 at full price, Anker's ability to produce a $200 alternative that won't disappoint you is no small feat.

Sovereign AI: What Changes When The Cloud Is No Longer Enough

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Karan Alang, principal software engineer at Versa Networks with 25 years of experience in AI, cloud and big data.

For most companies, cloud computing is a sensible way to start developing AI systems. On-demand resources and managed services make it easier to build data pipelines, try use cases and scale as adoption increases.

However, public clouds don’t always satisfy the legal, operational and security requirements of a sovereign environment.

Gartner defines sovereignty as an organization’s ability to control its data, technology and digital operations. It’s “about who operates and supports your IT systems.” This includes control over provider operations, personnel and access paths, not just where the data is located.​

This is becoming increasingly important in the AI/ML age, especially for regulated and security-sensitive organizations. Financial institutions face privacy, record-keeping and jurisdictional requirements; healthcare systems must protect sensitive workloads, and government agencies may require FedRAMP-authorized or fully air-gapped environments​.

AI makes this complex because data is no longer confined to traditional databases and applications. It can flow through training pipelines, vector databases and inference systems. With agentic AI, autonomous agents may also access and act on it. Organizations therefore need to understand not only where the data resides but where it’s processed, and what systems and agents access it.​​

Sovereignty can be achieved on the cloud as well as an air-gapped on-premise environment. A sovereign cloud environment provides a middle ground between using a cloud-based environment and an air-gapped sovereign environment. The comparison below shows how sovereign cloud and on-premise environments satisfy sovereignty.​

• Sovereign Cloud: High, depending on the provider and region. Data can be constrained to an approved country or jurisdiction, with regional storage or processing and controls over data movement.

• On-Premise: Very high. Data is stored within the organization’s air-gapped controlled environment.

• Sovereign Cloud: Partial to high. The customer has significant controls, but the cloud provider still operates parts of the underlying infrastructure. Support personnel, privileged access, telemetry and management-plane dependencies need be evaluated.

• On-Premise: Very high. The customer controls administrators, physical infrastructure, networking, patching, deployment, monitoring, keys and access.

• Sovereign Cloud: Partial. The customer generally remains dependent on the provider’s hardware, cloud platform, APIs, managed services and service roadmap. For example, GPU availability on the cloud might be restricted to specific regions.

• On-Premise: Potentially very high, but not absolute. The customer selects and operates its compute, storage, networking, Kubernetes/platform stack, models and inference environment. However, dependencies can remain on GPU vendors, commercial software, firmware and externally developed models.​

The sovereign cloud provides on-demand provisioning while satisfying the sovereignty requirement of many organizations. But for highly sensitive data and AI workloads, some organizations require stronger isolation and operational controls provided by air-gapped environments.

Air-gapped sovereign AI has traditionally been cost-prohibitive and difficult to manage. However, technological changes have made air-gapped AI more practical.

• AI compute is increasingly accessible outside hyperscale data centers. Enterprise servers with modern GPUs now support meaningful inference workloads inside a customer-controlled environment.

• Local LLM inference has matured. Open-weight models can run on customer hardware without organizations having to send sensitive data to an external model API. Additionally, quantization techniques help reduce model size to run efficiently on enterprise server GPUs.

• The open-source infrastructure stack is production-ready and portable. Technologies like Kubernetes, Kafka, Airflow and Spark run across cloud and on-premise environments, bringing cloud-native principles to sovereign environments.

I’ve spent the last few years building cloud-native data platforms for network and security intelligence. We started with the cloud, which was the right starting point. It let us move quickly, iterate and scale. However, for some customers, an air-gapped sovereign AI solution is the correct one.

Moving the platform into an air-gapped environment taught us that sovereign AI isn’t simply cloud architecture deployed on-premises. The operational considerations and requirements change significantly.​

There are several key points to be considered while formulating an enterprise AI strategy and in the design/implementation of an air-gapped sovereign AI solution.

• Capacity is now finite. Every microservice and Kubernetes workload must be right-sized upfront—CPU limits, memory requests, persistent and ephemeral storage, and GPU allocation. The elasticity of the cloud isn’t available in an air-gapped on-premise environment; hence, the size needs to account for the peak loads.

• The upfront infrastructure cost may be significant and needs to be calculated appropriately. The lag between the hardware procurement and delivery must be factored into capacity planning.

• Replacing the managed services and their capabilities needs additional engineering effort and planning

• Observability is a key part of the design. The pre-built metrics visualizations for managed services like Composer, Mongo Atlas and Dataproc needs to be recreated.

• Log aggregation needs to be built using technologies like Grafana and Loki, This is critical for observability and compliance.

• CI/CD must be redesigned for the air-gapped environment. Internal artifact registries need to be sized and set up to securely store and distribute docker images and other software artifacts.

• Support teams need to be equipped to handle infrastructure and product issues. The cost estimate needs to include 24/7 support.

• Model and hardware selection must match the workload. NetOps and SecOps environments combine high-volume structured telemetry, real-time processing and language-based investigation. Model size, quantization, inference throughput and GPU memory all affect the architecture—and in an air-gapped deployment, getting those choices correct is critical.

As AI becomes embedded in organizations, sovereignty needs to be part of the architecture design considerations from the beginning. For many organizations, a sovereign cloud environmemt provides the right balance of control and flexibility. For highly sensitive or mission-critical workloads, an air-gapped infrastructure may be more appropriate.

The technological advancements in AI models and Infrastructure now makes these deployments increasingly practical, but greater control also means greater responsibility. Capacity, observability, security, software distribution and operational support need to be incorporated into the platform design considerations.

That’s the key lesson of sovereign AI: Organizations should design for sovereignty from day one rather than trying to retrofit it later.​

Seahawks All-22 review: The good and bad from an ugly loss to the Bears

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Losing to the Chicago Bears isn't exactly a shocking outcome. Ben Johnson has built a competitive team, and Chicago has shown it can hang with anyone. But the way the Seattle Seahawks lost is what should frustrate Seattle fans.

The offense struggled to sustain drives, the defense failed to make enough plays, and several issues that have followed this team throughout the season resurfaced at the worst possible time. Seattle had opportunities to make this a competitive game, but too many mistakes on both sides of the ball made that difficult.

The loss also carries significant weight in the standings. The Seahawks are now further behind the 49ers and have dropped an important head-to-head matchup against a team that could be competing with them for a Wild Card spot down the stretch. These are the kinds of games Seattle needed to win.

Seattle's third-down problems returned in a big way against Chicago. The Seahawks converted just 1 of 11 attempts, a dismal 9.1% conversion rate.

That's particularly frustrating considering how well the offense had handled some difficult third-down situations against the Chiefs. Seattle had been finding ways to convert third-and-long situations at an impressive rate, but there's a difference between being good at converting those situations and constantly putting yourself in them.

Converting third-and-long is a valuable skill, but it shouldn't be mistaken for a sustainable offensive strategy. When an offense consistently finds itself facing long third downs, it usually means something has already gone wrong on first and second down. Whether it's ineffective runs, poor play sequencing, or a lack of answers against the defensive front, the offense is making life harder for itself.

Eventually, a good defense is going to make you pay for that.

Chicago did exactly that. The Bears consistently put Seattle in difficult situations, and the Seahawks had no answers when it mattered.

There's been plenty of debate among Seahawks fans about who's more responsible for the offensive struggles. Is it Brian Fleury and his play-calling, or is it Sam Darnold and his decision-making?

The answer is pretty simple: both are to blame.

Fleury has struggled to put together a consistent offensive plan. The route concepts don't always complement each other, the play sequencing can feel disconnected, and there are too many situations in which the offense appears to have no answer for what the defense is doing.

One example is how defenses have been sending blitzes on early downs, anticipating an under-center play-action pass. Fleury hasn't found a way to counter those looks, repeatedly leaving Darnold under pressure.

It's not just about individual play calls. Good offensive design should create opportunities from one play to the next, manipulate defensive reactions, and give the quarterback answers against different coverages. Too often, Seattle's offense looks like a collection of unrelated plays rather than a cohesive system. In other words, even some creative play designs struggle to work because they aren't connected to a larger plan.

The red zone offered another example. In this play, Darnold finishes the play without a dropback, and the routes essentially just run toward the defenders. There's no real attempt to create space or take advantage of the double coverage on JSN. It's discouraging to watch.

Having him call two running plays with ten minutes left in the fourth quarter, while trailing by more than one possession, was one of the strangest decisions I've seen in a long time. Even if it looks like Darnold changed the play the second time around, the decision itself was bizarre.

Darnold's performance is concerning, too.

He held onto the ball too long on several occasions, missed throws he should have made, and made questionable decisions throughout the game. There were also instances in which he abandoned progressions that appeared to offer better options.

His pre-snap recognition was particularly concerning on the play that resulted in his rib injury. On the second snap of the game, Kyler Gordon lined up in an obvious blitz position. Yet Darnold didn't appear to make any meaningful adjustment to the protection or prepare for the pressure coming from that side.

Gordon wasn't exactly hiding his intentions. The alignment itself should have been enough to trigger an adjustment, whether that meant changing the protection or preparing to get the ball out quickly. Instead, Darnold appeared caught off guard by a pressure that should have been anticipated.

Darnold hesitates too much and abandons his reads. It's not necessarily that he's missing throws to completely uncovered receivers; he's failing to hit windows that an NFL quarterback should be able to recognize and attack. Here, at the end of his dropback, he has JSN, who has confused the defensive back. With better anticipation and timing, the ball should already be on its way. On several occasions, Darnold failed to anticipate his receivers' routes.

Here, he tries to read JSN's release, but Kyler Gordon does a good job covering him. Darnold moves to his next read and finds Horton. However, he hesitates for a moment and puts the receiver directly in harm's way, throwing toward the safety. It's an exact example of what a hospital ball looks like.

Here's a rare good play call from Fleury. Horton's route creates separation with the cut in JSN's route. Darnold sees it, but once again hesitates, doesn't throw the ball, and decides to run. I'm not saying this is an easy throw, but it's an NFL throw.

Darnold was intercepted for the ninth time, but that's not the play I want to focus on. Instead, there's an interception that was wiped out by a penalty. Darnold recognizes that JSN has drawn the safety's double coverage and, once again, just as he did on his second interception against the Broncos, puts the ball where it shouldn't go. The throw to Kupp should have been placed toward the back of the end zone, away from the defender and out of harm's way. Instead, it goes right into the defender's hands.

The Bears finished with 34 points, and Caleb Williams became the first quarterback to in awhile against this Seattle defense to surpass 200 passing yards. Chicago found success through the air and on the ground, while also creating problems for the Seahawks in the red zone.

The Seahawks defense had been excellent against Kenneth Walker in its previous outing, particularly when it came to tackling. Against Chicago, that discipline disappeared.

Missed tackles repeatedly turned what should have been negative plays into significant gains. There were also opportunities to bring down the quarterback or finish plays behind the line of scrimmage that Seattle couldn't capitalize on.

Seattle continues to be relatively solid against the run between the tackles, but the edges have become a concerning weakness.

The defensive ends haven't consistently generated pressure, and their run fits have also created opportunities for opposing offenses.

Take DeMarcus Lawrence, for example. At times, he attacks an interior gap that is already occupied by Ernest Jones, leaving the outside vulnerable and forcing someone like Julian Love or a cornerback to make the replacement. That alignment issue led to two touchdowns in this game alone.

Derrick Hall has also had situations in which he crashes inside and leaves the edge open for Caleb Williams to escape.

These are the kinds of mistakes that can turn an otherwise well-defended play into a significant gain. The defensive front needs to maintain its assignments, especially when facing a quarterback capable of creating plays outside the pocket.

The pass rush as a whole is another concern. Mike Macdonald's defensive scheme is designed to create pressure through different combinations and often relies heavily on interior disruption. That means the edge defenders won't always be the ones finishing plays with sacks. Sometimes their job is to occupy space, control the pocket, and allow other defenders to get home.

But there's a difference between not recording sacks and failing to generate pressure altogether.

This has become a recurring issue for Seattle, and Chicago found ways to exploit it throughout the game.

The Bears repeatedly attacked the space behind the linebackers, particularly by putting Ernest Jones in difficult coverage situations. Jones has a lot of responsibilities in this defense, and opposing offenses are finding ways to make him choose between multiple threats.

The trick play here is designed to hold Jones and move him out of position, opening a window behind him. Spoon's coverage doesn't come off perfectly, and that creates the space needed for the pass to work.

The Bears executed this concept repeatedly, and Seattle struggled to find an answer. There were at least four instances in which Dig routes found space behind the second level of the defense.

There's an old saying: when your best players have a bad night, it's going to be a long night.

That's exactly what happened to Seattle's defense.

Leonard Williams also had a chance to make a tackle behind the line of scrimmage, potentially turning the play into a sack or a significant loss of yardage. Instead, he couldn't finish the play, allowing the offense to gain ground.

Julian Love was late on a few plays, got lost in coverage, and dropped an interception. It wasn't an easy catch, but it's one he would normally come down with.

These are players Seattle expects to make a difference. They're among the most important pieces of the defense, and the team needs them to deliver when opportunities arise.

Of course, nobody expects perfection. But when your best defenders fail to capitalize on the opportunities that could change the game, it becomes increasingly difficult to overcome the mistakes elsewhere.

Liverpool must sacrifice control in games in order to fix their creativity problems, Andoni Iraola has demanded ahead of their weekend trip to Crystal Palace, live on Sky Sports.

The Reds have created the second-fewest big chances in the Premier League this season and scored only four goals in five home games, their joint-fewest at this stage of a season in their history.

They set an unwanted record against Arsenal last weekend, failing to register a shot on target in a home Premier League game for the first time in more than 440 games since Opta records began in 2003.

Iraola arrived at Anfield last summer with a reputation for front-foot, direct football but has had difficulties imposing his philosophy on his new players in the first three months of the season, and now wants them to take more risks to turn their domination into clear-cut opportunities.

He said: "Obviously, we want to score more and concede fewer. It's a matter of controlling the games but also sometimes, against Fenerbahce we were controlling the game too much.

"In possession we can have good positions, but sometimes we have to throw numbers inside the box, arrive with more players.

"It will take a little bit of control away, but it will give us more threat when we arrive. Especially against Arsenal, the way we attacked the box had to be better.

"We are one of the teams which arrives the most in the final third. In terms of volume of shots and crosses, we are very high - but if you analyse the quality level of chance, it is low.

"When we start losing games, attacking against a lot of positions, it's not going to be the quality of the chances.

"The number of times we get to the box, we have to arrive better. Even if that means losing some control, we have to get into the six-yard box more. It will give us more clear shots."

Liverpool's 1.05xG in Sunday's 1-0 defeat to Arsenal would have been significantly boosted had they been awarded a clear penalty for a shirt pull by Jurrien Timber on Alexander Isak.

Iraola admitted after the game he felt a spot kick would have been awarded had Isak gone to ground, and after being praised by the officials in Wednesday's Champions League defeat at Fenerbahce feels his side can be "too honest".

Nothing will ship its first AI device in December, and it’s built for work

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Chief executive Carl Pei shared only a dark teaser image of the product. He argues that AI will win over offices before homes, and that its biggest gap is context.

Nothing will ship its first AI device in December, and it is built for work. Chief executive Carl Pei announced it on Wednesday in a long essay on X. He gave no name, price or launch date.

“It’s a tool, but one built with the personality and care that have come to define a Nothing product. It has no new screen, and it couldn’t have been an app,” Pei wrote.

His only image shows the dark corner of a rounded device. It carries the words “Nothing (R)” and “Coming soon”.

Most of Pei’s essay argued against the hunt for a single breakthrough AI gadget.

“The whole industry is hunting for its iPhone moment: the pendant, the pin, the glasses, the companion, some magical consumer device that makes AI part of everyone’s life,” he wrote. “I admire the boldness, but I read this moment differently.”

Pei said new technology wins people over at work first. Computers spread as spreadsheet machines and mobile phones as car phones for dealmakers, he wrote. He said coding alone makes up more than half of what companies spend on AI built for specific jobs.

Pei wants to fix what he calls a “context gap”. AI models are capable, he wrote, but they know little about a person’s day. People still copy emails, calendars and Slack threads into a chat box, and they lose ideas from conversations.

“Close it, and everything AI can already do gets dramatically more useful,” Pei wrote.

Glean chief executive Arvind Jain also talked about giving AI more context about work on the TNW podcast in October.

Pei also argued that work tools should look as good as consumer ones, and pointed to Olivetti’s Valentine typewriter. Nothing makes phones and earbuds, and Pei wrote that “tens of millions” of its devices are in use.

Nothing first announced plans for “AI-native” devices about a year ago, The Verge noted. A report last month said it plans two pairs of AI smart glasses. Rivals already sell AI glasses, from Alibaba’s Qwen N1 to Meta’s Ray-Bans.
