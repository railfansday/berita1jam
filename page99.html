5 Qs, 5 As: Washington fans ‘hate’ the name Commanders

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Washington football team (not to be confused with the Washington Football Team) is on their fifth season of being named the Commanders. And it sounds as though fans of the team are no bigger fans of "Commanders" today than they were in 2022. In fact, the longer they go into the name change, the more they might want to change it again.

It's hard for me to imagine or accept that there is a team in the NFL named the "Commanders", which still feels like a made-up name for a fictional pro football league in a Keanu Reeves or Kevin Costner movie.

Even so, this is the name that they're stuck with today, and it is the team that the Los Angeles Rams will face on Sunday in a critical moment for both franchises:

If the Rams lose, all hell could break loose before the trade deadline Tuesday.

If the Commanders lose, head coach Dan Quinn may not be associated with the "football team" by Monday.

Washington is 2-4-1 and seeking their second win in a row since the return of quarterback Jayden Daniels. To get to know them — and their Football Team Fans — better, I sent five questions to Andrew Y at Hogs Haven. He sent me back five answers.

Q: The Commanders don't have a player in the top-30 for rushing yards or the top-60 for receiving yards. I feel like that would be very disappointing for fans, to not have one skill player so far this season who you can at least look forward to watching, win or lose. But those are stats, not necessarily reality. Who is the one healthy skill player on the team who Washington fans can look forward to watching each week?

A: I think the player most Commanders fans would point to is rookie WR and 3rd-round pick Antonio Williams. He's shown tremendous route-running nuance and short-area quickness to get open in the middle field and has been a consistent target getter, with 23 catches for 259 yards so far this season. Those simple stats don't rely do credit to his ability though, as he's generated a 96.7 passer rating when targeted and is PFF's second-highest graded rookie receiver. Dan Quinn made clear that with the loss of Terry McLaurin, he expects Antonio Williams' role to increase the most and indeed Williams led all receivers in snaps and routes last week against the Eagles.

Q: Five years in, how is the name "Commanders" sitting with fans? Do you ever long for the days of Football Team again?

A: Honestly, I think most fans hate the name Commanders. It's the last stinky gift given to us by former owner Dan Snyder that the new leadership hasn't been able to reverse. The names that seemed most popular with fans (and with me) were Red Wolves (a nod to the old name, but with a cool animal attached) or Warthogs (which has alliteration with Washington and could be shortened to 'Hogs to reference the famous Hogs offensive line of the glory days). There is a contingent of fans that actually liked the name Washington Football Team and many would probably still take it over Commanders, but I always suspected it was more driven by nostalgia for the old name and a feeling that if we can't have that name, then no name is better. Washington Football Team feels to me more like having no name and certainly doesn't lend itself to new branding. I'd rather have a new team name and branding, it just needs to be a good name and good branding.

Q: It's the end of 2026 and the beginning of 2027. No changes have been made yet. Dan Quinn is the head coach, Adam Peters is the GM, the roster is intact. If you can make one change to the Commanders team/organization to set them on the right path, what do you do?

A: Unless the rest of the season plays out very differently than it has up until now, I would fire Dan Quinn and let Adam Peters lead a search to choose the next head coach. I think Adam Peters has actually done a pretty good job of rebuilding the roster and giving the team enough depth to withstand an avalanche of injuries this year. However, they often look poorly coached, with the most yards erased by penalty in the NFL, slow playcalls and players taking too much time to get into position, and regular mistakes in situational awareness and game management that lose games.

However, one other change needs to be made. The biggest reason the team was able to go on that 2024 playoff run and has been unable to repeat is that our 2024 season was one of our healthiest ever, with few major injuries to core players in the regular season. That's a major outlier, as Washington has had the most Adjusted Games Lost (AGL) due to injury in the NFL since 2009 (!) and only the Giants have been in the same tier of AGL. This year is no different, with four of the team's highest-paid players already done for the season. To be one of the NFL's most-injured teams over that span of time is significant and unusual in that it covers multiple front office staffs, coaching staffs, and even medical staffs.

The one constant in that time has been the strength and conditioning staff, led by Chad Englehart. Englehart started as an assistant strength and conditioning coach in 2010 before being promoted to head of strength and conditioning in 2017. He's stayed on so long because he's extremely popular with players and rates highly in NFLPA surveys, but I'm convinced his staff are doing something that leads to Washington's players being more injury prone, perhaps by over-training them. An outlier injury rate over 17 years can't be simple bad luck. I would order a full external evaluation of the team's strength & conditioning program and make major changes there.

Q: Who are you happier to see fail this season: The Cowboys or the Eagles?

A: It's a delight to see both of them fail, but more so the Eagles. The Eagles have come to be seen by people around the NFL as the "class of the division" and their fans love to loudly reiterate that belief. It's great seeing them get taken down a peg with a nightmare season most didn't see coming. I've noticed there's a bit of an age gap in how people respond to this question. Most fans over 50ish years of age hate the Cowboys more and still vividly remember "we want Dallas" chants and the heyday of the rivalry from the 80s and 90s, but I think younger fans would generally prefer to watch the Eagles flounder.

Q: What's your happiest memory of the Washington team over the last 30 years? What's been the pinnacle of being a fan of this team since the mid 90s?

A: Honestly, my happiest memory in that time period was when Dan Snyder finally sold the team. His rotten ownership had been the millstone around the team's neck that prevented it from ever rising up and ensured it was always surrounded by negative drama. Most fans by that point knew we'd never be a serious football operation with Dan Snyder in charge, so the sale was the first real light after a long period of darkness that signalled things could finally change.

I wasn't prepared for how quickly that would happen though, because then followed the Commanders 2024 season with a dynamic rookie QB. That season blew out anything that happened under the Dan Snyder era: first time the team won 12 regular-season games since the '91 Super Bowl team, first time the team made the NFC Championship game since the '91 team, etc. In addition, so many of those games ended in exciting, last-minute wins that the team got the nickname "Cardiac Commanders." That season was the pinnacle of being a fan of the team since the mid-90s. The victory over the Lions in the playoffs was the single best game, because nobody expected us to boat race the Lions and advance to the conference championship like that.

SpaceXAI released a free Grok add-on for Google Workspace in July 2026, and getting it running is a matter of just a few clicks. Install it once from the Google Workspace Marketplace, open it via the Extensions menu in whichever app you're working in and off you go.

That one installation covers all three of Google's Workspace editors, with Grok opening in a side panel next to the file rather than in a separate tab. It's the same spot where you'll find Google's Gemini panel. From there, Grok can answer questions about a spreadsheet and highlight the cells it used, turn an outline into a slide deck and knock rough notes into a formatted doc. While it's free and easy to insert Grok into your workspace, there are some factors worth considering before rolling out the welcome mat.

Grok is listed on the Google Workspace Marketplace and is free to install. Open it, click Install and choose the Google account you want to use, then click Continue. If you'd rather not leave the file you're in, you can also install Grok directly from inside Docs, Sheets or Slides by navigating to Extensions > Add-ons > Get add-ons. Workspace admins can also deploy it across an entire org.

Clicking Continue brings up Google's permission screens. Grok will ask for see, edit, create and delete permissions for all Google Docs files and every Slides presentation in your account, even if you're installing it from within an individual file. It has narrower access to Sheets, covering only those that the add-on has been installed in, and its overall Drive access only stretches to files you use with Grok. It'll also ask for permissions to connect to an external service, which allows Grok to send what it reads to SpaceXAI.

Once Grok is installed, open any doc, spreadsheet or presentation and nav to Extensions > Grok > Open Grok to bring up the side panel. You'll then be prompted to Get Started and sign in. Once authenticated, Grok will be accessible and usable across all three Workspace apps.

Grok can write directly onto the page in Google Docs, doing all the things you'd expect an LLM to do, such as turn rough notes into a draft with proper docs headings and lists. However, when I asked it to turn notes on installation instructions into a draft, the results were AI slop. Grok's changes show up as visible revisions in Google Docs version history and, with Grok's connectors switched on, it can apparently pull in context from recent emails or files in Google Drive. I personally wasn't willing to give it that level of access to my emails or files and hooked it up to my secondary spam Gmail and Workspace account instead.

In Slides, Grok can build a presentation deck from an outline and add new slides that match the existing theme, researching the topic from the Web or from X (though that doesn't seem like a great idea, in my opinion.) To its credit, SpaceXAI's use cases page has some fairly interesting examples to borrow as prompts. I asked Grok to build a six-slide deck based on its track record for accuracy and political bias since 2025, with cited sources on every slide and concluding with a rating of how reliable Grok is as a source.

In Sheets, Grok can read whatever ranges you select, answer with citations, point to specific cells, write formulas into the formula bar and insert charts. It can also rerun scenarios when you change an assumption, with each change being implemented as an ordinary edit that can be undone later.

Google's Gemini chatbot is built into Workspace by default and opens from the Ask Gemini icon at the top-right of Gmail, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Drive and Chat. But it's not free: Work accounts need an eligible Workspace plan, while personal accounts need a Google AI Plus, Pro or Ultra subscription.

Grok costs nothing to add, and it's the only one of the two that pulls research from X into whatever you're generating, but whether that's a plus or not is certainly debatable. Skyrim Developer Explains Why Todd Howard’s Ideas ‘Just Work’

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Todd Howard once said “it just works” while showing off Fallout 4’s settlement building, and the internet has never let him forget it. Usually, someone brings it up when a Bethesda NPC does something physically impossible. But according to Skyrim’s former lead designer Bruce Nesmith, there’s a less sarcastic reason Howard’s ideas so often work: he knows when to get out of the player’s way.

From Arena to Skyrim, we rank every Elder Scrolls game by story.

Speaking to Steven Lake on The Examined Game, Nesmith explained what it was like working with Howard and why the two designers didn’t always see eye to eye. He also recalled a Skyrim feature he was convinced would confuse players, only to discover that Howard had made the right call.

Nesmith said Howard has always been unusually good at putting himself in the shoes of an ordinary player. That sounds straightforward, but it can be surprisingly difficult when you’ve spent years building a game and know every system inside out. The temptation is to add another layer of depth, even when it does little to improve the moment-to-moment experience.

Nesmith admitted that he was often the one pushing for more complexity. Howard, meanwhile, would question whether players actually wanted it. As Nesmith recalled, Howard’s argument was simple: “They’re going to want something simple and more digestible here.”

Making something more accessible doesn't necessarily mean sacrificing depth.

That isn’t the same as saying RPGs should have no depth. The point is that not every interesting idea makes for an enjoyable mechanic. Sometimes players want to explore a ruin, cast a spell, or pick a fight without first consulting three menus and a spreadsheet.

Nesmith also talked about the value of competing perspectives on a development team. One artist, for instance, disliked how Oblivion’s Detect Life spell covered the creatures and characters artists had worked so hard to detail. Skyrim’s version made the effect more costly to keep active, encouraging players to use it briefly instead of walking around with everything glowing all the time. It was a small change, but one that came from listening to a different concern.

There was one disagreement Nesmith remembered particularly well. Howard wanted Skyrim’s skills and perks presented as constellations in the night sky, letting the artwork shape the way players moved through character progression. Nesmith preferred something more conventional – a list.

He was certain the constellations would be confusing. Even as development continued, he remained unconvinced. Then Skyrim came out, and players responded warmly to the presentation. Nesmith later admitted, “I was dead dog wrong on that.”

Personally, I still think Skyrim falls short of Morrowind as an RPG, but the constellation-based skill menu is one of the things it absolutely got right. I’m actually surprised to learn that it was Howard’s idea. Turning character progression into something so visually distinctive, rather than presenting players with another list of skills, was a clever decision. It’s the sort of approach I’d love to see more RPGs adopt, because making something more accessible doesn’t necessarily mean sacrificing depth.

Of course, “it just works” means something very different to anybody who has spent a few hundred hours with Bethesda’s games. We have Eurojank for ambitious, rough-edged European RPGs like the Gothic series. For all their quirks, those games can still be a lot of fun. Bethesda’s own particular flavor has sometimes been affectionately called Amerijank, and I can’t say I’ve ever found that description entirely unfair.

I can’t imagine playing one of Bethesda’s Elder Scrolls or Fallout games without mods. At the very least, I’d want an unofficial community patch, like the Unofficial Skyrim Special Edition Patch. That’s not because I dislike these games. Quite the opposite. I’ve spent enough time enjoying them to know how often the community ends up fixing things Bethesda left behind.

Some of that roughness is understandable. Skyrim and Fallout 3 give players enormous spaces to wander through, objects to mess with, systems that interact in unexpected ways, and the freedom to ignore the main story for hours. The more complex a machine is, the more ways it can fail. The same applies to video games, which is why RPGs and open-world sandbox titles are often particularly prone to bugs. It doesn’t excuse every broken quest, but it helps explain why these worlds can be both a mess and incredibly hard to leave.

Starfield made that bargain harder to accept, at least for me. Its scale was certainly ambitious, but much of the exploration lacked the density, surprises, and sense of place that made wandering through Skyrim or the Capital Wasteland so rewarding. When that urge to see what’s over the next hill is missing, familiar Bethesda quirks become a lot less charming.

That’s what makes Nesmith’s comments interesting as Bethesda continues work on The Elder Scrolls 6. Howard’s best decisions were about knowing where things belonged, and making sure the player had a reason to keep going despite everything that didn’t quite work.

Richard Lloyd, Television Guitarist, Dies at 75

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The musician was diagnosed with bile duct cancer in March 2026.

Richard Lloyd, a founding member and guitarist of influential New York rock band Television, has died. He was 75.

Lloyd died Saturday morning (Nov. 7) at his home in Chattanooga, Tennessee, according to to Rolling Stone.

“His loving wife Sheila and his dog Willie Mays were with him at the end,” his family told the publication.

His cause of death was cholangiocarcinoma, a cancer of the bile ducts. Lloyd was diagnosed with the disease in March 2026, and a GoFundMe campaign was later launched to help cover his medical expenses and other bills.

Born in Pittsburgh in 1951, Lloyd moved with his family to New York City at age 6 and grew up in Greenwich Village. He initially studied drums before turning to guitar. After spending time in Boston and Los Angeles, he returned to New York in 1973.

That year, he joined forces with Tom Verlaine, Richard Hell and drummer Billy Ficca to form Television. The band became a regular at CBGB, the Lower Manhattan club that helped launch the careers of fellow New York acts including the Ramones, the New York Dolls, Talking Heads, and Blondie.

Television released its debut album, Marquee Moon, in 1977. The album earned widespread critical acclaim for its intricate arrangements and the distinctive interplay between Lloyd’s and Verlaine’s guitars. Although it achieved limited commercial success, the album went on to become one of the most influential releases of its era. The group followed it with Adventure in 1978 before breaking up later that year.

Lloyd launched his solo career with the 1979 release of Alchemy, but struggles with substance abuse derailed his career in the years that followed. After getting sober, he returned with his second solo album, Field of Fire, in 1985. He continued releasing music over the following decades, with his final solo studio album, The Countdown, arriving in 2018.

He also worked as a session guitarist, contributing to recordings by artists including Matthew Sweet and John Doe of X, while continuing to tour and teach guitar. In 2017, he published his memoir, Everything Is Combustible: Television, CBGB’s and Five Decades of Rock and Roll.

Television reunited to record its self-titled third album, released in 1992, and continued performing periodically in the years that followed. Lloyd announced his departure from the band in 2007 to focus on his solo career.

Listed for $1.2 million, the 60-foot-high tower sits on 14.31 acres of land adjacent to the Chattahoochee National Forest with about 3,000 feet of river frontage.

"I was definitely awestruck first by the property itself, the river frontage, the waterfalls on the property, all of the hemlock trees dispersed throughout the property," Fitts says. "But then when we went to the top of the property where the watchtower was, I was in total awe."

Two shipping containers are perched atop the tower, creating the living space.

"The main structure is the bedroom efficiency with a bed, sitting area, stove, kitchenette. There's a landing between and then across is the bathroom area with a full shower, toilet, and double vanity," he explains. "Then there's a spiral staircase to the rooftop fire pit and lounge area."

And while there is a lift for things like suitcases and packages, people need to walk up 82 steel-grated stairs, which Fitts admits can be a bit daunting.

"I was actually nervous going up the tower for the first time because it's high and it is an experience. But once I did it, I said, 'This is what real mountain experiences are like. This is something that people remember for the rest of their lives.'"

"You are surrounded by just foliage, so when you're at the top, you just see a full panoramic view of the mountains and valley across one way, and you hear the roaring river on the property down below. You can actually see the waterfall," Fitts says.

"Anyone with a four-wheel-drive or some type of ground clearance should have zero issue forging the river," Fitts explains, adding that the river is shallow at the access point. "It's not scary if you've done it before, and once you do it, it's like 'OK, now I'm an adventurer.'"

While the property itself is off-grid with solar power, a well, septic system, and generator, it’s not physically far from civilization.

"Probably the property is maybe 30 minutes from downtown Blue Ridge," Fitts says, adding it just feels remote. "Once you're there, you're in a totally different world. Your neighbors are trees and wildlife."

Saving The Manuals: Porsche's Mechanical MECOSA Vs. Ferrari's Electronic Manuale Tech

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sometimes even an exotic carmaker's answer to a challenge might say something about future cars for the proletariat. Most of us probably can't even afford a replacement shift knob on the 2027 $677,600 Ferrari 12Cilindri Manuale, but how Ferrari and Porsche are adapting their automatics to fit manual transmissions could one day portend stick-shifts for us all. Here's the reason to even posit this: Carmakers want a cheaper way to build hybrids, EVs, and gas cars on the same platform and on the same assembly line. Adding or subtracting parts will make that too costly — which may result in them just ditching any manual effort, if that's the only solution.

Now that you understand the why of this reporting, we'll also acknowledge why some readers are freaking out about the fact that the 12Cilindri Manuale will simulate the experience of shifting. It's not a conventional manual, and we'll explain how it works in greater depth, but the gist is that Ferrari's linking an electric stalk and faux clutch pedal to an eight-speed dual-clutch drivetrain. It's as real (or fake) as paddle shifters, just via a dongle and pedal.

On the other hand, Porsche's stick-shift retrofit is a hack of their seven-speed PDK, which stands for Porsche dual clutch– or Doppelkupplung. It debuted on the 2020 Porsche 911 Carrera S Manual, dubbed MECOSA, which is Porsche-speak for MEchanically COnverted Shift Actuator, and is an entirely mechanical retrofit, featuring a seven-speed gated shifter and third pedal. But which is better? And which foreshadows a future joystick for the masses?

Automatics have been steadily growing in popularity since their debut in 1939, and according to the U.S. Department of Energy, by 2022, 99% of all new cars sold offered automatics. Standard automatics further evolved in 1987, with the introduction of the gearless CVT — a transmission Jalopnik readers hate in a way that borders on irrational.

The alternative? Something like Porsche's PDK, which is a dual-clutch manu-matic. Porsche began experimenting with the PDK in the 1980s. It debuted in racing in the 1986 962, and by 2008 you could get a PDK in a 911, which allowed drivers to shift using paddles for extra engagement and provided benefits such as engine braking. But it does more than just ditch the third pedal and shifter — the PDK in the 2020 Carrera S shifts at least 200% faster than a human can shift in the MECOSA, according to Car and Driver.

Despite that, at least 20% of Porsche 911 customers in America opt for a genuine manual, hence the MECOSA. Mind you, this is still a PDK underneath, and since its gears are arranged for a computer to actuate, not a human, they're not in any sane order. Instead, the pattern looks like this, starting from the top left of the gate: R,2,3,1,6,4,5,7. To translate the driver's inputs into proper shifts behind the scenes, MECOSA activates a jangle of cables and shafts so that each push or pull of the shifter engages the correct gear.

It's a solution that would make MacGyver proud. It is also not one Porsche is sticking with. The Stuttgart giant isn't abandoning its 911 customers clamoring for a manual; but now, you can get a Carrera S with a standard six-speed transmission, rather than a seven-speed with a Rube Goldberg machine tacked on.

Like Porsche, Ferrari's Manuale has a gated shifter instead of paddles, and it gains a third pedal on the floor. The transmission is physically identical to that of the paddle-shift version. Ferrari's legerdemain is to delete the paddles, insert a shift knob, and add a clutch pedal with sprung feedback. Now it feels as if your left foot is encountering a clutch spring as your ankle hinges, and Ferrari has created similar tricks in the gated shifter itself to simulate the feeling of gear shifts. Behind the scenes, however, sensors track both inputs. Your motions tell the car's computers what to do, which is identical to what happens when you pilot the 12Cilindri minus the Manuale. Except, in this one, it's possible to encounter rough shifts and even to stall the Manuale.

Keep in mind, this V12 Ferrari is carrying two extra gears you cannot joystick into. This is still actually an eight-speed DCT, not a six-speed manual, so it can also be driven like an automatic. Tap the D button on the console and the car will shift itself, and you have access to seventh and eighth gear — for lower-rpm Autobahn sprints, say.

You might cry in your pasta fagioli that Ferrari should only sell its 819-horsepower, 9,250-rpm V12 with a genuine-article manual gearbox, but Ferrari will only make 1,499 units of the Manuale. For that take rate, there was no way they were going to retrofit a manual gearbox, if for no other reason than this: It probably saves them millions on not having to develop a separate manual transmission 12Cilindri or certify two different transmissions for regulators.

Right now, the electric Porsche Taycan gets an E-Shift option. The Hyundai Ioniq 5 N offers something similar. E-Shift uses software to simulate gear changes, including simulating step-changes in acceleration, and adding a soundtrack, just like the Hyundai. Porsche copied patterns from their PDK, including adding a deceleration function during downshifts. But neither brand adds a third pedal — yet.

But the future for Porsche probably isn't either E-Shift or conventional manuals, since Porsche recently published two patents that seem to follow in Ferrari's footsteps, creating simulated shifting that could function in electric, hybrid, and gas cars. The patents also mention a fake clutch pedal, just like the one Ferrari is using, and the ability to shift sequentially, like the PDK. Porsche also describes a system that uses electrical components to provide resistance and something like progressive feel to gear swaps, to enliven driver engagement.

Considering what we found in our review of the Hyundai Ioniq 5 N, with its fake shifts and sounds, it only makes sense that blurring slushbox convenience and manual control at the flip of a switch could make any machine more entertaining — or more relaxing. Porsche's patents could also potentially smoke Ferrari's hack, because they apply regardless of powertrain.

This innovation could also get to non-Porsche EVs and hybrids via licensing. After all, Porsche isn't doing great financially. Ditto, parent company, Volkswagen — partly thanks to Porsche's struggles. A little trademark cash could definitely help that picture. And, just maybe, save the manuals for the rest of us.
