Zelensky says Putin is a 'killer' after Trump calls Russian leader a friend

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Watch: Trump defends calling Putin a 'friend'

Ukraine's president Volodymyr Zelensky has labelled Vladimir Putin as a "killer" in response to Donald Trump referring to the Russian leader as his "friend" in China this week.

Speaking to CNN, Zelensky said it was up to the US president "how to call Putin" but "for us, he is an enemy... for us, he's a killer".

On the sidelines of a historic trilateral meeting with Chinese leader Xi Jinping and Putin on Thursday, Trump called Putin his "friend" and said the two leaders have had a "great relationship for many years".

At least nine people were killed in Russian attacks targeting bridges in Kyiv and railway stations on Friday, days after Ukraine badly damaged the Crimean Bridge.

Zelensky called the attack the "biggest this year" and said the country suffered civilian deaths and injuries after an onslaught of 110 missiles, including 27 ballistic, and more than 400 drones.

Hours after the strikes on Ukraine, Trump told reporters at the White House that Putin would "love to end the war".

"They (Zelensky and Putin) both want to end it and it's a very good thing I have a relationship," he said on Friday.

Trump also doubled down on his description of the Russian leader as a friend but said he "can't speak" to whether Putin is a friend to the US.

However Zelensky said despite Trump's stance, he saw Putin as "a killer".

"I think mostly for the people in the world, he is a killer because he's killing - by the way- he's killing each day and night," he said.

Zelensky also said speaking about Putin like he is a "friend" means "he doesn't feel enough pressure".

The war of words came as neighbouring Poland said a Russian cruise missile was among three objects that violated its airspace in the southeast of the country early on Friday.

Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk said it did not appear to be an accident when Western military exercises were taking place in the region.

Nobody was hurt and no damage has been reported, but Warsaw described the incident as a "provocation" from Moscow.

Nato Secretary General Mark Rutte condemned "grave violations of Polish airspace" and said Nato jets had been scrambled in response.

Russia has made no comment about the violations, which took place during Friday's massive attack on Ukraine.

Defence Minister Wladyslaw Kosiniak-Kamysz said one missile hit a rural area at Nowy Lipowiec about 50km (31 miles) from the Ukrainian border.

The missile hit about a 90-minute drive from where European troops are training, with another missile and a jet-powered drone suspected to have hit two other locations, Polish authorities said.

Sabb, Glassboro dominate second half to earn return to state final

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

WEST DEPTFORD — It's been a tried and true formula that has enabled Glassboro's football team to make the state championship game its permanent address. The Bulldogs are known for their big-play offense and tenacious defense, and the combination earned them a fourth straight trip to a state championship game.

Xavier Sabb led the big play offensive with two touchdowns and defensive end Maurice Davis Jr. paced a defensive effort that pitched a shutout after the opening drive as Glassboro defeated West Deptford, 33-7 in a state Group 2 semifinal game.

Glassboro (12-1), the two-time defending Group 1 champion, will face Caldwell in next Saturday's 1 p.m. Group 2 state final at MetLife Stadium.

This is the fifth year that the NJSIAA has held public school state playoffs. In 2023, Glassboro lost in the Group 1 state finals and then won it the past two seasons.

"I think this is going to be the best team we had in history," said Sabb, a key member of the previous teams that reached the state final.

West Deptford had scored on the game's opening drive, with Cole Paskiewicz (18 carries, 47 yards) capping a 10-play, 64 yards drive with a 1-yard touchdown run.

Then Sabb returned the ensuing kickoff 97-yards for a touchdown. At first, it appeared as if the kick would go out of bounds, but at the last second, Sabb decided to pick it up and then he darted down the left side for the touchdown.

"Realistically, I thought the ball was going out into the end zone, so I let it go and then I picked it up late and saw a gap and took it all the way through," said Sabb, an Oregon commit. "All my teammates came and blocked for me, and without my teammates I never would have made it."

What is interesting is that Glassboro ran just one play from scrimmage in the first quarter. The Bulldogs didn't do much better in the second and trailed 7-6 at halftime.

Coach Timmy Breaker, now in his fourth season at his alma mater, made the proper halftime adjustments.

"We changed a couple of things at halftime," Breaker said. "On film when they ran the veer it was to the A gap," he said, referring to the area right by the center. "So when they came out, they changed it and ran to the B gap, the C gap and widened it, so we adjusted to the veer to their formations."

West Deptford threw for 36 yards, so once Glassboro stopped the run, the Eagles didn't have any answers.

Glassboro had the first possession of the second half and following a short West Deptford kick, the Bulldogs began pounding the ball. Columbia commit JoJo DeLeece ran three straight times for 29 yards and then Davon Barr, in his first carry of the game, finished matters with a 16-yard scoring run.

"It was an inside zone and I saw that we had numbers, and I saw No. 8 (tight end Riley Brown-Dispensa) so I ran by him and scored the touchdown," Barr said.

Glassboro scored on its next possession as a scrambling Charlie Foulke found Ausar Heard wide open in the end zone for a 25-yard scoring pass. Foulke then hit receiver Mekhi Parker for the two-point conversion and the Bulldogs had a 20-7 lead with 4:30 left in the third quarter.

Meanwhile, the defense was frustrating Deptford. Leading the way was Davis, a 6-foot-5, 235-pound senior, who had a sack, a tackle for loss and a fumble recovery.

"Maurice did an exceptional job and held his own tonight," Breaker said about Davis.

Late in the quarter Glassboro got the ball on the West Deptford 35 after Davis recovered a fourth down fumble. On the next play, the Bulldogs scored when wide receiver Parker threw an option pass to Sabb for a 35-yard touchdown strike.

According to local historian Chuck Langerman, Sabb now has 404 career points and began the fifth Glassboro player to score 400 or more in his career. The others are Corey Clement (588 points), Terrance Holmes (472 points), Amari Saab (442 points) and Sean Redman (422 points).

Sabb had three receptions for 42 yards, but he made three huge plays — the kickoff return, the 35-yard touchdown and an interception and long return that set up Glassboro final TD.

Parker, among the best juniors in the state, then ended Glassboro's scoring by hauling in a seven-yard TD pass from Foulke.

A Syracuse commit, Foulke completed 7-of-14 passes for 86 yards and despite missing the first three games due to NJSIAA transfer rules since he played at St. Joseph's Prep in Philadelphia last year, Foulke has thrown for 2,052 yards. According to Langerman, Foulke is the second person in school history to throw for 2,000 yards in a season after Jack O'Connell threw for 2,737 last year.

West Deptford (12-1) has really turned the corner in three seasons under John Emel. The Eagles went 3-8 the year before his arrival and then were 5-5, 9-2 before this season when they won the highly competitive WJFL Constitutional Division and the South Jersey Group 2 championship.

"I don't think a team can play them any better than we did in Group 2," Emel said about Glassboro. "I think they're going to ceremoniously go up north and it's just a matter of what the final score is going to be."

Emel also realized that Sabb altered the entire game. After that opening kickoff, West Deptford wouldn't kick or punt the ball to Sabb, but short kicks gave Glassboro great field position.

"He's going to Oregon for a reason, he's a five-star player and Gatorade Player of the Year last year," Emel said. "I have a ton of respect for their players. Their players are really good."

HBO Max's 4-Part Sci-Fi Epic Is One of the Best on Any Streaming Platform

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Most science fiction franchises tend to try to reinvent themselves over the years, with mixed results. Some, like Star Wars, return with new installments that often spark divisive discourse. Others, like Blade Runner or Tron, have extremely lengthy gaps between sequels. One sci-fi franchise not only reinvented itself in the early 2010s but also delivered a truly epic scale that very few blockbusters have matched. This honor goes to the Planet of the Apes reboot series, which is available to stream on Hulu and Prime Video.

Starting with Rise of the Planet of the Apes, the new films focus on the birth of the titular Planet of the Apes, especially how the apes evolve into the dominant species on the planet. It's not the first time someone has tried to reboot the series; Tim Burton launched his own version of Planet of the Apes in 2001, and James Cameron was even approached to revive the series on film before he eventually made Titanic. The new Planet of the Apes succeeds because it takes the time to show how its world is constantly evolving, offering a sense of growth that other sci-fi franchises lack. It also doesn't hurt that one of Hollywood's best directors helped take the series to a new level.

In the original Planet of the Apes, humanity has devolved into a mute class of workers while the apes lord over them. This led to arguably one of the greatest twists in science fiction history, in which George Taylor (Charlton Heston) discovers that the "planet" he's landed on is actually Earth, ravaged by nuclear war. Rise of the Planet of the Apes shifts from a nuclear threat to a viral one, as scientist Will Rodman (James Franco) creates a drug called ALZ-112 to reverse the effects of Alzheimer's disease.

While ALZ-112 increases the intelligence of the chimpanzee Caesar (Andy Serkis), it proves to be deadly to humans, triggering a global pandemic. Director Rupert Wyatt also said that future films would explore the conflict between man and ape:

"We've incorporated elements from Conquest of the Planet of the Apes, in terms of how the apes begin to revolt, but this is primarily a prequel to the 1968 film... Caesar is a revolutionary figure who will be talked about by his fellow apes for centuries... This is just the first step in the evolution of the apes, and there's a lot more stories to tell after this. I imagine the next film will be about the all-out war between the apes and humans."

True to Wyatt's words, the next two films, Dawn of the Planet of the Apes and War for the Planet of the Apes, focused on how humanity and apes came to battle. It also brought a new director into the fold, as Matt Reeves stepped up to helm both sequels. Reeves brought a truly epic scale to the Planet of the Apes reboot, showcasing just how much the world changed in the wake of humanity's decimation. Rain-soaked forests, snow-covered terrain, and a massive military fortress are given plenty of sweeping shots, with cinematographer Michael Seresin taking time to close in on both human and apes' facial expressions — letting their eyes say much more than words could. Reeves' vision led to critical and commercial success for both Apes sequels, and paved the way for his work on The Batman.

There's one person who helped make the Planet of the Apes reboot a bona fide success, and it's Andy Serkis. Serkis delivers a career-best performance as Caesar, thanks to a blend of motion capture and a truly magnetic performance. Every time Caesar is on-screen, the camera gravitates toward him, showcasing his transformation from a mere ape to a leader to a messianic figure. Even in death, Caesar continues to cast a large shadow as Kingdom of the Planet of the Apes shows how apes have taken up his words, for good and ill. It's also led the Planet of the Apes reboot to continue topping the streaming charts, showing that the new films have just as strong a hold on modern audiences as the original Planet of the Apes did on its audience.

The Planet of the Apes reboot proves that not every reboot of a popular franchise needs to rely on nostalgia or simply rehash the original story. It also showcases that there's still room for sweeping epics in the sci-fi landscape, especially as Dune: Part Three gears up to hit theaters.

A Guide to NYC’s Little Italy—and the Culinary Gems Hidden Among the Tourist Traps

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Contrary to other tourist-focused neighborhoods in New York (looking at you, Times Square), Little Italy boasts an innate charm that resonates with visitors and locals alike. Sandwiched between SoHo and the Lower East Side, this jewel box area is home to long-standing, family-owned restaurants and authentic Italian bakeries, the majority of which have been neighborhood staples for decades. Yet despite its touristy atmosphere, a unique feeling of old school New York magic hangs in the air; beyond the personality-less stands and souvenir shops, framed family photos on restaurant walls and structural imperfections of tenement buildings speak to a bygone area of the city’s past, evoking a sense of nostalgia that so many neighborhoods lack. Pop down to Mulberry Street and see for yourself—though be sure you know which spots are actually legit (that’s where we come in).

A note on how we’re defining the area: Over the years, Little Italy’s boundaries have shrunk to focus on the touristic area of Mulberry Street and its immediate surroundings. While the southern and western boundaries of Canal Street and Lafayette Streets remain unchanged, the historic easterly Bowery boundary has arguably shifted to Elizabeth Street; similarly, the northerly boundary of Kenmare has also condensed down, according to many, to Broome Street. For the sake of this piece, we’re using the boundaries of Lafayette Street, Canal Street, Elizabeth Street, and Broome Street as a happy medium.

Located in the heart of Little Italy, Umberto’s Clam House is best known for its—you guessed it, all things clams. The restaurant quickly garnered attention shortly after opening in 1972, when mobster Joe "Crazy Joe" Gallo was shot in the restaurant’s dining room. Like its mafia-related history, the food at Umberto’s is as Italian-American as it gets. Baked clams and fried calamari are the appetizer list’s biggest show-stoppers, while superbly chilled clams on the half-shell pair exquisitely with equally cold (and generously sized) cocktails from the bar. Seafood-focused mains are the name of the game here, including the garlicky linguini and clam sauce and recently resurfaced scungilli with pasta served with piping hot red sauce.

Address: 132 Mulberry Street, New York, NY 10013

For Italian-American eats in a less stereotypically Little Italy ambiance, Il Cortile is the way to go. Contrary to the old school Italian vibes found in the majority of the neighborhood’s restaurants, Il Cortile’s atrium-style glass ceiling and plant-filled room feel more like dining al fresco in Trastevere than the heart of Mulberry Street. The family-owned restaurant’s neighborhood roots date back to 1975, with a solid focus on Northern Italian cuisine. The homemade cheese ravioli and wood-fired pizzas are always a safe bet, though for meat lovers, springing for the Fettuccine di Parma made with sweet sausage and wild mushrooms won’t disappoint—and be sure to save room for the pillowy soft tiramisu made with creamy mascarpone and imported Italian ladyfingers drenched in espresso.

Address: 125 Mulberry St, New York, NY 10013

Original Vincent’s, often called The Original Vincent’s Clam Bar, is home to one of the richest restaurant success stories in Little Italy. Founded in 1904 by Giuseppe and Carmela Siano, the business began as a sidewalk street cart serving up fresh seafood and jars of homemade red sauce, available in mild, medium, and hot. Today, the restaurant sits on the corner of Mott and Hester Streets, and is still known for its signature gravies, baked clams, and linguini-based pasta dishes (don’t skip the frutti di mare). As in its earliest days, Original Vincent’s still sells jars of their red sauce to-go, making replicating the restaurant’s pasta dishes at home a breeze—simply grab a bottle of Chianti, cue up your Louis Primo playlist and voila.

Paesano of Mulberry Street is home to one of the coziest interiors in Little Italy, offering a peaceful refuge from the neighborhood bustle. Rustic twig bunches and leafy plants strung with twinkle lights hang from the ceiling, while traditional Italian frescos and framed family photographs adorn the whitewashed brick walls. Originally opened in 1961, the restaurant remains owned by the same family and continues to be known for their Southern Italian recipes, including linguini with clam sauce, wood-fired pizzas, and savory chicken marsala drenched in sautéed mushrooms. Meat lovers, don’t skip the restaurant’s signature Veal A La Paesano, featuring a juicy and tender veal base layered with thin slices of prosciutto, steamed broccoli, and gooey mozzarella cheese finished with succulent brown gravy.

Address: 136 Mulberry St, New York, NY 10013

For serious pizza lovers, a trip to Lombardi’s is a no-brainer. Tucked on the corner of Mott and Spring, this neighborhood institution is credited as America’s first pizzeria, originally founded by Gennaro Lombardi in 1905. Lombardi quickly rose to fame for his large, heavily-topped pies, which he baked in an on-site, coal-and-gas oven. The result? Smoky crusts topped with perfectly balanced tomato sauce and gobs of whole milk mozzarella—along with sweet Italian sausage and various other accoutrements. The interior oozes with classic New York charm, featuring tables clad in red-and-white checkered cloths and framed family photographs strung along the red-hued walls. Head’s up, the spot is cash only, and while there’s an ATM on site, we recommend coming prepared to avoid the withdrawal fee.

Address: 30 Spring St, New York, NY 10012

Pasquale Jones-12_2. Brussels Sprout Pizza with Red Onion, Smoked Mozzarella & Lemon

In a sea of mediocre neighborhood wine lists, Pasqule Jones offers a breath of fresh air for Little Italy exploring wine lovers. Situated on the cusp of the neighborhood’s boundaries (and easily identifiable by its rainbow neon lights), this Italian-American pizzeria first opened its doors in 2016 and quickly became a staple for industry folks and visiting oenophiles alike. Beloved for its wood-fired New York style pizzas (don’t miss the signature clam pie) and thick, homemade pastas, the restaurant’s warm-yet-casual ambiance is ideal for posting up with a pie and a bottle—especially if you’re after great Burgundy, Champagne, or Italian reds. Expect classic picks from Pierre-Yves Colin-Morey, Domaine de l’Arlot, and Vietti, as well ample fun and fresh selections from Sandlands, Chacra, and Domaine Gramenon.

Address: 187 Mulberry St, New York, NY 10012

Address: 195 Grand St, New York, NY 10013

Although less famous than nearby Ferrara, Caffé Palermo features the innate Italian charm one looks for when wandering the neighborhood. Owned and operated by Baby John DeLutro (the self-proclaimed “Cannoli King”) since 1973, the establishment lives up to its owner’s moniker, offering flavored iterations—including pistachio and hazelnut made with sweet ricotta cream, the former of which is one of the joint’s standouts—in perfectly crisp shells. What Palermo does have over Ferrara is a solid amount of outdoor seating, ideal for posting up with a cappuccino and people watching on Mulberry.

Address: 148 Mulberry St, New York, NY 10013

If you’re looking for a wine break between pizza and pastries, this is just the ticket. Located on Little Italy's western limits (Centre Street between Broome and Grand), this French-inspired wine bar offers extensive by-the-glass options and shareable bites, including cheese and charcuterie boards to larger main courses, including beef tartare and lemon spaghetti. The comprehensive wine list, which features anywhere from 750 to 1,000+ references at all times, leans unsurprisingly French, though ample selections from Italy, Spain, and the USA are also available. Our suggestion? Post up with a bottle of Grower Champagne and relish in the perfectly executed Parisian bar à vin ambiance.

Address: 249 Centre St, New York, NY 10013

Address: 409 Broome St, New York, NY 10013

Technically located on the cusp of Little Italy’s historic boundaries (Kenmare and Broome Streets), La Esquina offers a flavor-packed reprieve from the classic red-sauce joints synonymous with the neighborhood. Situated in a former counter deli, this multi-level Mexican hotspot is best known for its casual taqueria (located in ‘The Corner Deli’ space) serving tacos, tortas, and ceviche. However, for those looking for something a bit stronger, head through the secret door and down the stairs to find the restaurant’s underground speakeasy. Lit by candle and adorned with Lucha Libre posters, the bar’s high-energy ambiance is prime for enjoying queso fundido and smoky Mexican street corn (elotes callejeros) washed down with expertly crafted agave-based cocktails.

Address: 114 Kenmare St, New York, NY 10012

9 Calvin and Hobbes Comics from the 1990s that Prove Bill Watterson is a Genius

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Watterson’s work with Calvin and Hobbes was incredibly versatile

Calvin and Hobbes creator Bill Watterson is a genius. Over the course of ten years spanning more than 3,000 comic strips, he developed two truly irresistible characters that are still beloved by millions. There was a lot behind the success of Calvin and Hobbes, but I think part of the reason why Watterson is still so widely respected is that he was an incredibly versatile artist who managed to do a lot of different things in an artform that can sometimes be limiting.

While this selection only scratches the surface of what made Calvin and Hobbes such a brilliant creation, here are nine comic strips from the 1990s that illustrate Watterson’s incredible range as an artist.

One of Watterson’s recurring themes in Calvin and Hobbes was his unabashed love and admiration for the natural world, a respect he channeled right into his two leading characters. Watterson returned to this subject many times and the points he made were always thoughtful. This one, from April 1992, is especially touching, as Calvin laments that humans have lost their “awe of nature.”

Sometimes, Watterson seemed to use Calvin and Hobbes as an outlet for whatever big, philosophical questions were on his mind at the time. In this comic strip, Calvin and Hobbes have an in-depth conversation​​​​​​​ on the nature of luck. The best part about it is that they have it in a little wagon, careening down a giant hill. Calvin lands in some pricker bushes, which he seems to think is pretty unlucky.

Calvin often spoke well above the intelligence of a 6-year-old, but these comics were balanced out by adventures where Calvin is just being an imaginative little kid, like in this Sunday strip from 1995 where Calvin imagines meeting an alien who camouflages himself as Calvin. The real Calvin then goes to school pretending to be the alien, which lands him in the principal’s office.

In addition to mastering the joke-a-day format demanded of comic strips, sometimes Watterson would tell longer stories which spanned several days or even a couple of weeks. For example, when his arch-nemesis, Rosalyn the babysitter, came over, their encounters often became epic sagas full of twists and turns​​​​​​​, like the story from 1990 in which Calvin drove Rosalyn nuts​​​​​​​ pretending to be Stupendous Man.

There were many Calvin and Hobbes strips which were incredibly heartfelt, like this tender comic from 1993 that merely shows a day in the life of Calvin and Hobbes, with both longing to be reunited​​​​​​​. The best part about it is its complete lack of dialogue.

While Watterson was capable of being thoughtful and heartfelt, some of his comic strips featured a surprisingly dark sense of humor. This side of him was best captured by the strips depicting Calvin’s snowman sculptures, which always showed snowmen going through horrific things.

This strip, from Jan. 21, 1991, contains​​​​​​​ three snowman sculptures in one. The first shows a snowman being shot with a cannon, the second a snowman being buried alive, and the third a snowman being hanged by a noose.​​​​​​​ That last one is especially hard to picture alongside more child-friendly newspaper comics like Peanuts and The Family Circus.

Like most other comic strips, Calvin and Hobbes had a simple drawing style, but occasionally Watterson would find an excuse to do something in much more detail, like when Calvin would pretend he was a Tyrannosaurus rex, like he does in this Halloween-themed strip from 1993.

For the most part, Watterson worked within the traditional bounds of newspaper comic strips, but he never let those traditional rectangles hold him back from a really creative idea. His dinosaur comics were a great example of this, as he liked to make his dinosaurs as big as possible. Another example of Watterson breaking the traditional format is this August 1995 strip, in which Calvin had​​​​​​​ an extended internal monologue about his parents being aliens.

Of course, a collection of perfect Calvin and Hobbes comics from the 1990s would not be complete without Watterson’s final comic, which ran on Dec. 31, 1995. This open-ended sendoff was beautifully understated and deeply poignant. It could not have been better.
