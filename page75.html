Apple iPad Mini (2026) Review: A Dream Device, but Who Is It For?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Some thought it might be dead. Some thought it had no place in an iPhone Duo world. But the iPad Mini isn’t being put out to pasture—it’s getting a massive glow-up.

Apple’s smallest tablet has been refreshed—now in its eighth generation—and with it, nearly every aspect of the device has been upgraded. It now has an OLED screen, a faster chip, a thinner chassis, more memory, waterproofing, and much more. That all comes with a higher price, though, and raises the question of just who the iPad Mini is really for among the other choices in the iPad lineup.

I’ll come straight out with it: I love this little tablet. I’ve been using it for about a week, and I happened to take it on a short trip. The perfect time for it to really shine—and shine, it did. It was the perfect companion for long Lyft rides to the airport and wait times at my gate.

The star attraction of the new iPad Mini is its 8.5-inch display. That’s a slightly larger screen than the previous generation, yet the overall dimensions are barely bigger. But the change from IPS to OLED is what matters. It’s a Tandem OLED Super Retina XDR display, based on the same technology as the iPad Pro—though it doesn’t have the 120-Hz refresh rate. Nor is it quite as bright: It can reach 600 nits in SDR and 1,200 nits in HDR, which is 400 nits less than the iPad Pro. It’s more or less similar to the M6 MacBook Pro, though. Not the OLED MacBook Pro, but the Mini-LED model.

The iPad Mini OLED’s brightness is sufficient for most uses, even in bright lighting conditions. But you can take it to the next level by opting for a nano-texture display, which is totally worth the extra $100 it costs. (It’s the model I tested.) It’s not an antireflective coating or a matte layer; it’s actually etched into the glass. The result is a surface that almost entirely diffuses outside light without reducing the screen’s perceived brightness. Reading outside with the iPad Mini is particularly enjoyable, as the nano-texture display not only resists reflections but even gives it a paperlike crispness. This option is also available on the MacBook Pro and iPad Pro, and there’s really nothing else like it.

The iPad Mini one-ups its siblings in sharpness. Its smaller size gives it 19 percent higher pixel density than every other iPad. And yes, it really does look incredibly clear and sharp.

There’s more to it than the display. Part of the magic is how lightweight it is—barely over half a pound, and it doesn’t feel much heavier than my phone in my hand. One-handed use feels completely natural. That’s always been true of the iPad Mini, but this latest version emphasizes that even more.

Lastly, the color options have been pared down from four to just two. The lighter goldish Starlight is still available, but now there’s the introduction of the blackish, navy-blue Midnight. This is a finish carried over from the MacBook Air. The more playful Blue and Purple color options are both gone, as is Space Gray. More options are always better, but for a device that costs $700, I can see why these more professional colors make sense.

Apple has also completely redesigned the iPad Mini’s four-speaker sound system, and a couple of interesting changes are happening. As part of its process of making the device IP67 water-resistant, Apple removed the speaker grille from the sides to create a completely sealed enclosure. Instead, the tweeters sit behind two small slits on the edges of the display glass. And then bass drivers are built directly into the display. Apple calls it an Acoustic Display.

The stereo image sounds fullest when you hold the device in portrait, as long as you keep your fingers away from the display. The vibration caused by the location of the speakers isn’t terribly noticeable unless you have the volume on full blast. Overall, the audio sounds decent for a device this size, but it won’t compare well to larger devices like an iPad Air or MacBook Air. But for watching videos or the occasional movie, it suffices.

The water resistance is a first for any iPad, which is significant. You might still worry about bringing your $700 tablet poolside, but the IP67 rating means it can handle submersion for 30 minutes in about three feet of water. In comparison, iPhones have had an IP68 rating for the past eight years, meaning they can handle twice that depth for 30 minutes.

As always, it has to be said that iPads are not really designed for taking photos. That’s a bit less true with the iPad Mini, if only because it’s so easy to use one-handed. Still, I doubt you’ll use the 12-megapixel rear camera all that much, outside of the occasional document or QR code scan. It’s decent but hasn’t been changed from the previous generation. The front-facing camera, however, got a big upgrade. It’s still a 12-megapixel camera, but it has moved to the side bezel instead of the top. That location makes video calls feel more natural.

One thing the iPad Mini is still missing is Face ID. While it’s a standard iPhone feature, Apple appears to really be reserving it for only the iPad Pro. That’s a shame. This does have Touch ID built into the power button.

A number of other iPad features still apply here, though, that make the iPad Mini feel like something you could work on. It supports extended display mode, letting you connect it to an external monitor without forcing it to mirror the screen. iPadOS also now has windowing and proper cursor support. Without a keyboard, I can’t imagine you’ll use these features as much. On the other hand, the iPad Mini’s size makes it possible to type with your thumbs (like you would on a phone).

Clearly, the 2026 iPad Mini seems to have its sights set on more than just reading books and playing games. As if the OLED display and price tag weren’t already enough to give that away. Most of the demos Apple has been using to show off with the iPad Mini were more creator-focused than I expected. We’re talking running on-device AI models or creative applications.

There are three storage configurations available: 128 GB, 256 GB, and 512 GB. All come with 12 GB of unified memory, four more than the previous iPad Mini and matching the current iPad Air. You’d never know that from Apple’s own spec sheets, though, since it’s never mentioned. Regardless, it’s part of what makes the iPad Mini a more capable little tablet, whether in heavier multitasking or for on-device AI. It’s kind of absurd that this little tablet has 12 GB of memory while the MacBook Neo is stuck at 8.

The iPad Mini’s A20 Pro chip is also more powerful than the MacBook Neo’s. This is the same silicon as in the iPhone Duo, iPhone 18 Pro, and iPhone 18 Pro Max—with one caveat. For the first time, the storage options dictate which version of the A20 Pro chip you get. Only the 512-GB model gets the full six-core CPU and dual 16-core Neural Engines. I tested this six-core model, but the other configurations have nerfed performance, with one fewer CPU efficiency core and only a single Neural Engine.

Performance is really solid. The A20 Pro handles games quite well, and I tested several, including Oceanhorn 3, Fortnite, No Man’s Sky, and Assassin’s Creed Mirage. None of these games looked particularly nice here, with textures and effects turned down, but the iPad Mini played them smoothly at 60 frames per second. Visually, the most impressive was No Man’s Sky, which came to iOS and iPadOS just last week. It still feels amazing to play a game this dense on a mere iPad Mini. Of course, the iPad Pro with the M5 will be a substantial upgrade for gaming performance, but if you connect a mobile controller (we like the Backbone One), you’ll have a good time.

I also tried out some local AI in LM Studio. With 12 GB of onboard memory, you’re limited in what models you can use. LM Studio recommended the 4-billion parameter version of Qwen 3.5. This isn’t the kind of model that’s going to blow anyone away, but for simple tasks, it’s nice to run a text model completely locally. This is all GPU-dependent, so performance should be identical without the second Neural Engine that comes exclusively on the 512-GB model. Of course, Siri AI is now a significant part of this platform, and the iPad Mini can run all the advanced features, including the more expressive Siri voices and on-the-fly dictation.

The previous iPad Mini cost $499 when it came out (before the price increases), and that at least felt reasonable. At this new price of $699, it’s a little harder to justify. The iPad Mini has always been a luxe but niche device. It’s sized for reading books, watching movies, and playing games. It doesn’t have the same high-stakes use case as a phone or laptop, which makes that higher premium price harder to swallow.

If you want the most powerful iPad Mini as a professional tool, you’ll probably spend at least $999. Or $1,099 with the nano-texture display. That’s the same price as the MacBook Air! I get it: The iPad Mini is an entirely different device. But I’m wondering whether real professionals would at least prefer a larger display—not only for the extra screen real estate but also for keyboard support.

I will say: The iPad Mini does work remarkably well with the Apple Pencil. If I had to choose an iPad specifically for note-taking, it’d be the iPad Mini. On the other hand, illustrators will likely prefer a larger screen. Like previous versions of the iPad Mini, it’s only compatible with the Apple Pencil Pro and USB-C Apple Pencil. Even for those who use tablets in the workplace as a point-of-sale system or for specialized use cases, the higher price seems hard to justify.

Is it all just a plainly disguised way of justifying the higher prices Apple is paying for memory on the backend? The cynic in me says yes. It’s not unlike the direction Apple is heading with its OLED MacBook Pro. But putting the cynicism aside, there’s real quality-of-life value being added here, especially in the form of the OLED screen, waterproofing, and extra memory. For the right person, the iPad Mini will be the perfect product. It’s a dream device for casual use, whether it’s gaming, reading, or browsing. But I’m guessing that demographic has shrunk quite a bit with this increased price.

I’m a little perplexed why Apple isn’t at least offering an 8-GB RAM model that would bring the price down more. I know it goes against the focus on Siri AI, which needs at least 12 GB, but it would at least keep the core audience that would buy an iPad Mini in the first place. It would also help create some more separation between this and the iPad Air. The iPad Mini is $150 cheaper than the iPad Air despite having an OLED screen. The iPad Air, meanwhile, has a larger 11-inch screen and is big enough to use with the Magic Keyboard. You’ll have to weigh a number of trade-offs when choosing between these two.

And with the iPhone Duo’s arrival, the iPad Mini’s size feels even less unique. Sure, the Duo costs $2,000, but it solves the iPad Mini’s main problem: convenience. As your phone, the Duo fits in your pocket, goes with you everywhere, and is far more versatile as a pocket computer. The iPad Mini is less flexible and easier to forget and leave at home. And at $699, you may wonder whether you really need that fancy OLED screen and 12 GB of RAM.

Eric Dane Initially Received Different Diagnosis Before ALS Battle

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Grey’s Anatomy alum Eric Dane shared in his new autobiography My Book of Days: A Memoir of Moments, that he initially received another diagnosis prior to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS.)

Eric Dane was searching for answers before receiving his ALS diagnosis.

In the Grey’s Anatomy alum’s new autobiography My Bood of Days: A Memoir of Moments, he shared he initially received a separate diagnosis before amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).

As Dane recalled, his health battle began when he realized he was “gradually losing any use” of his right hand.

“First, I saw a hand specialist, who passed me off to another hand specialist,” Dane recalled in an excerpt from his book, per US Weekly, “who sent me to a neurologist.”

Once he saw the neurologist, his doctor posited that the Euphoria actor had “contracted some form of neuropathy” but could not determine what he was suffering from, admitting the ailment was “above his pay grade.”

“I shuttled from doctor to doctor and underwent a battery of tests, including one called an EMG, an electromyography test,” Dane recalled, noting his medical team finally confirmed a “diagnostic consensus” of multifocal motor neuropathy.

The initial diagnosis was hopeful, Dane noted, sharing that while it was a “rare autoimmune disorder causing extreme muscle weakness,” the known treatments were “90 percent effective.”

After beginning treatment for multifocal motor neuropathy, Dane’s medical team grew “scared” after he did not respond to treatment.

“‘We’ve been discussing ALS,’” Dane recalled his doctor telling him. “‘But we have to wait and see.’”

Feeling “frustrated and fed up,” The Last Ship star went on to get a lumbar puncture, spinal fluid extraction and brain scans. In 2024, he received his ALS diagnosis, calling it a “death sentence.”

But when Dane—who shared daughters Billie, 16, and Georgia, 14, with ex Rebecca Gayheart—told his family, the heartbreaking news hit him even worse than before.

“I don’t remember a single word I said after that,” Dane wrote. “I remember only feeling a violent impact, like getting the wind knocked out of me, and Rebecca wailing on the other end of the phone, and then I really lose it, and my sobs make my body shudder, and I hear her words pouring out amid her tears, ‘I’m here, we’ll fight this together, I’m not going anywhere.’”

Dane fought his ALS battle for two years before he died Feb. 19 at the age of 53.

For a deeper look into Dane’s fight, keep reading…

Opening Up About His Private Health Battle

Eric Dane shared that he was diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as Lou Gehrig's disease, in April 2025.

"I have been diagnosed with ALS,” Dane told People at the time. “I am grateful to have my loving family by my side as we navigate this next chapter.”

The Euphoria star made it clear that he wasn’t stepping away from the screen amid his diagnosis, sharing in April 2025 that he was continuing to film the final season of the HBO series.

And Dane, whose Amazon MGM Studios series Countdown debuted over the summer, admitted that acting has kept him “sharp” has he navigates his health battle.

"I feel great when I'm at work," he explained to E! News in June 2025. "Of course, there have been some sort of setbacks, but I feel pretty good. My spirit is always pretty buoyant, so at the end of the day, that's all that matters."

Two months after sharing his diagnosis, the Grey’s Anatomy alum spoke candidly about his journey, including the symptoms that led to his diagnosis.

"I started experiencing some weakness in my right hand," Dane told Diane Sawyer in a sit-down on Good Morning America in June 2025. "I didn’t really think anything of it at the time. I thought maybe I’d been texting too much or my hand was fatigued, but a few weeks later I noticed it got a little worse."

After nine months of appointments, which involved visiting two hand specialists and two neurologists, Dane was told he had ALS.

"I will never forget those three letters," the father of two explained. "It's on me the second I wake up. It's not a dream."

While sharing how the rare degenerative disease has affected him physically so far, he also detailed where he sees the progression headed.

“My left side is functioning, my right side has completely stopped working," Dane shared during his interview with Sawyer. "I feel like maybe a couple more months, and I won’t have my left hand either. It’s sobering."

As Dane goes through this health battle, his wife of over 20 years, Rebecca Gayheart, who filed to dismiss their 2018 divorce in March, has been by his side.

“I call Rebecca. I talk to her every day,” Dane shared during his interview with Sawyer. “We have managed to become better friends and better parents, and she is probably my biggest champion and my most stalwart supporter, and I lean on her."

According to Gayheart, she and Dane are the “best of friends.”

“We are really close. We are great co-parents,” the Beverly Hills, 90210 star explained to E! News April 9, 2025. “We really figured out the formula to staying a family and I think our kids are benefiting greatly from it and we are as well.”

While Dane has been marking milestones with his and Gayheart’s daughters Billie and Georgia, he’s admitted he resents how his ALS is affecting the lives of his family members.

"I'm angry, because my father was taken from me when I was young," the actor, whose father died by suicide when he was a young boy, said during his GMA interview. "And now there's a very good chance I'm going to be taken from my girls while they're very young. At the end of the day, all I want to do is spend time with my family and work a little bit, if I can."

Dane was set to present at the 2025 Emmys alongside his Grey's Anatomy costar Jesse Williams on Sept. 14 but ended up missing the award show. A few weeks after his absence, Dane shared that he had to skip the show because of a hospital visit.

“ALS is a nasty disease," he said during an interview with The Washington Post published on Oct. 2. "So I was in the hospital during the Emmys getting stitches put in my head."

"I was a really upset about it,” Dane added. “But you know, there was nothing I could do about it."

Dane met with doctors and representatives from the I Am ALS organization in September, 2025, and spoke with California congressman Eric Swalwell about federal funding towards research for the currently incurable neurological disease.

“I wanna ring every bell,” Dane told Swalwell in a TikTok video posted by the politician on Sept. 29. “I have two daughters at home. I wanna see them graduate college, get married, maybe have grandkids.”

Dane died on Feb. 19 at age 53 following his yearlong battle with ALS. His family confirmed in a statement to E! that he "spent his final days surrounded by dear friends, his devoted wife, and his two beautiful daughters."

“Throughout his journey with ALS, Eric became a passionate advocate for awareness and research, determined to make a difference for others facing the same fight,” his family's statement continued. “He will be deeply missed, and lovingly remembered always. Eric adored his fans and is forever grateful for the outpouring of love and support he’s received. The family has asked for privacy as they navigate this impossible time.”

Europe on alert after supermarket freezers hit by cyber-attacks in 10 countries

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Investigations have begun in several European countries in response to the cyber-attacks

A spate of cyber-attacks that hit supermarket chains across at least 10 European countries on Monday has prompted European Union officials to warn of a deliberate attempt to disrupt the food supply chain.

Supermarkets have reported problems in Belgium, Estonia, Finland, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Portugal, Germany and Sweden, and the significant increase in freezer temperatures has forced retailers to discard food.

Temperatures in chains across Europe reportedly rose as high as 50C (122F).

No-one has yet admitted carrying out the cyber-attacks.

Supermarket chains said the temperatures of refrigerators and freezers in some of their shops soared as high as 50C

The European Union has raised concerns about the attacks, which come three weeks after a cyber-attack disrupted as many as 700 French grocery stores, causing food losses estimated at tens of millions of euros.

The security of refrigeration systems as well as other devices that can be monitored remotely has heightened concerns about government-backed hybrid attacks.

The EU's food and agriculture commissioner, Christophe Hansen, said the bloc was taking the incidents very seriously and warned of further disruption in the future.

Commission spokesman Thomas Regnier was careful not to attribute blame, but said: "These incidents of course show once again how cyber-attacks can disrupt essential services and affect citizens' daily lives."

"These are not isolated incidents and of course we have to be collectively resilient and prepared for such cyber incidents."

In Italy, Giorgia Meloni's government called a meeting with intelligence chiefs, but said the impact was "currently very limited" and most of the food distribution chain had up-to-date security systems able to fend off such attacks.

At least four Co-op supermarkets in Sicily and one business in Tuscany were affected, according to the national cybersecurity agency.

Meloni's office said those businesses affected were "mostly still equipped with obsolete security systems, with interfaces directly exposed to the internet and inadequate authentication mechanisms".

Belgian supermarkets were among those hardest-hit, with dozens of stores reporting refrigeration failures. Federal prosecutors have opened an investigation.

UK meat industry hit by cyber-attack which targeted European supermarket freezers

Mathieu Tilman, a manager at a Delhaize supermarket in Belgium, said he was shocked by this kind of attack: "I don't even understand how anyone could do this... How can we end up throwing away so much food? I find it truly distressing. In moments like these, you just feel like quitting your job."

In Ireland, 13 SuperValu stores were impacted by what the chain's owner, Musgrave Group, called a cyber-incident. "Replenishment of chilled and frozen ranges in the affected stores is well under way," a spokesman said on Monday.

A small number of corner shops in the Netherlands also suffered disruption. Last month, supermarket chain Albert Heijn had to take several services offline because of a separate cyber-attack.

Gaming on Mac has come a long way. Gone are the days of limited releases, though not absolutely everything is playable on the platform. Thankfully, community developers are only too keen to plug the gaps.

FX Steam Launcher on GitHub brings SteamOS to Apple Silicon Macs. Specifically, this independent, open-source project is based on the ARM64 Steam Frame version of the operating system. Therefore, you're getting Windows game compatibility on Mac thanks to Proton 11 and FEX, alongside the very swish SteamOS interface (via Videocardz).

Obviously, there are a few things to bear in mind if you want to go all in as a Mac gamer. For one thing, this is not a native installation, and is instead running SteamOS by way of a virtual machine on your Mac. For another, this is a GitHub download to begin with, but once you've got it installed, the launcher directly downloads the official SteamOS image from Valve and then even handles OS updates too. Neat!

As this is an unofficial effort, there are a few more caveats still. The FX Steam Launcher itself currently only supports Apple Silicon Macs running either macOS 15 (from September, 2024) or newer. However, to actually get the latest and greatest running, you may need something much more recent than that.

Bear with me here, but Proton and FEX are essentially only the first hurdle. DXVK is required to run DirectX 9, 10, and 11 games, while DirectX 12 releases rely on VKD3D-Proton. Then either MoltenVK or KosmicKrisp is the next hurdle graphics instructions will need to pass before finally handing off the baton to Apple’s Metal API (though there's some experimental support for DXMT, which allows DirectX 10 and 11 games to run without VKD3D-Proton).

Anyway, all of that is to say KosmicKrisp, a pretty pivotal translation layer in this whole process, requires at least macOS 26 or later to run. That's a fair amount of hoops to jump through, though; if you can get it running, the launcher does allow you to run SteamOS either windowed or fullscreen, while letting you still access macOS either way.

Herniated Discs Are a Common Cycling Injury—Here’s Everything You Need to Know

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Keep your back health on track with these tips from top docs and bike-fit experts.

Sometimes it happens with a bang, like when former Tour de France pro Marty Jemison was racing Tour of Flanders in the late 1990s. His saddle slipped to be nose up. He thought “no big deal” until one of the steep cobbled climbs, where he was in the saddle cranking out maximum wattage.“And my back just popped,” he says.

Jemison finished the race and the others that followed until he retired about 18 months later, though he had pain off the bike and for the first 30 or 60 minutes of riding, and knew something was wrong. That something was a herniated disc that became nearly debilitating after retirement, eventually required two procedures, and still flares up to this day if he’s not meticulous about his bike position and on top of his core work.

Other times, it’s the proverbial straw that breaks the camel’s (or the cyclist’s) back, as was the case with avid long-distance cyclist Mark Elsasser of Philadelphia who developed some sciatica in 2018. He was able to ride through it just fine, but couldn’t sit for any length of time before the pain was unbearable. The diagnosis: a herniated disc in his low back, which he resolved with physical therapy and has kept at bay by changing his position on the bike, going from a 0-rise to 5-degree stem. “My spine is less compromised being a bit more upright rather than nose down on top of the stem,” he says.

Herniated discs are fairly common in the general population, occurring most frequently among 30- to 50-year-old adults, and men more often than women. Interestingly, you may have one right now and not even know it. “We know from radiographical studies that 50 percent of those aged 40 may have some form of disc herniation. We might consider it normal, age-related changes, and many of those won’t be symptomatic,” says bike fitter and sports physiotherapist Bianca Broadbent, co-owner of The Cycling Physios based in the United Kingdom.

In fact, one research review published in the American Journal of Neuroradiology found that degenerative changes in the discs, which include herniated discs, are present in nearly 90 percent of adults 60 and older and often require no intervention. A herniated disc only causes pain if it’s pressing or otherwise irritating a nerve root in the spine

Join Bicycling All Access for more injury-prevention tips and training advice!

Your spine is comprised of 24 vertebrae. Between those vertebrae are fluid-filled cushions known as discs, which act as shock absorbers for your spinal bones.

“Imagine your spinal discs as jelly-filled doughnuts,” says Andrew Pruitt, Ed.D., the founder of the Boulder Center for Sports Medicine and CU Sports Medicine and Performance Center. “A herniation happens when the jelly inside pops out.”

You may hear people using other terms like “bulging disc,” “ruptured disc,” or “slipped disc.” Technically, a bulging disc is just that: It’s when the jelly inside the donut is pushing against the side enough to bulge out from the vertebrae but isn’t leaking out. A ruptured or slipped disc refers to an actual herniation, where the doughnut cracks, allowing the gel-filled center to squeeze out.

A bulging and/or herniated disc can cause pain by creating pressure on the nearby nerves. There’s some evidence that the leaked fluid from herniated discs also can cause chemical irritation and inflammation of the spinal nerve roots.

Again, it’s possible to have an asymptomatic herniated disc where you feel no pain. When you do have pain from a herniated disc, however, you’ll know it.

Pain from the herniated discs is sharp and radiating. Most cyclists experience herniated discs in the lower, lumbar back (we’ll get to why in a bit). So you may feel burning pain often known as sciatica that radiates from your lower back into your glutes and down one or both legs, sometimes even into your feet. You may feel pain similar to an electric shock when you stand or sit or walk. You may also feel radiating numbness, tingling, and/or weakness in your lower extremities.

So how does the jelly get out of the doughnut and start wreaking havoc on your spinal nerves? In a word: pressure. It can be extreme and sudden, like Jemison cranking on the cobbles. Or, in many instances, it can happen gradually over time, as was the case with Elsasser.

When your spine is in its generally straight, neutral position, the pressure from the top down is uniform on your discs, so the jelly inside those cushions is evenly distributed, Pruitt explains.

“When you bend forward, the jelly is pushed back, stressing the skin of that doughnut,” he says. “If you’re sitting all day, that jelly is pressing back there for eight hours. Now jump on a bike, and you add another two hours in an even more extreme position, pressing that jelly further and further, until—pop—it comes out.”

Though herniation can happen anywhere in the spine, in cyclists it happens most frequently in the lumbar (low back) region close to the base of the spine, or the L5-S1, the bottom-most disc that sits right on top of your tailbone, because that’s where we bend the most, says Menachem Brodie, C.S.C.S., head coach at Human Vortex Training and USA Cycling expert coach.

“For many cyclists, the L5-S1 is where they’re getting their flexion, and the lumbar spine has about six degrees of movement,” Brodie says. So if you’re not hinging from the hips and keeping your spine within its safe, natural range, you’re pushing those limits. Keeping your spine under that kind of stress for prolonged periods of time pushes you closer to the failure point.

Along those lines, research shows that riders with a lower handlebar position and greater flexion in that key lower back region were more likely to have low back pain. Though, importantly, the research also suggests that poor core strength and endurance could contribute to the spinal stress and low back pain in cyclists.

Of course, the bike isn’t the only place you can stress your spine. The gym can be another place riders put their spine under unhealthy stress, says Dane DeLozier, D.P.T., the biomechanics lab director at and co-founder of Revo Physiotherapy and Sports Performance. Maintaining a stable, neutral spine is very important when resistance training, especially when doing big lifts like deadlifts and squats as well as under velocity like with kettlebell swings, DeLozier says.

“When you have your spine under load, you want it to be neutral and very stabilized. You want to be hinging through your hips, not bending and extending your spine under load,” he says.

If you’re unsure of if you’re maintaining a neutral spine, DeLozier recommends setting up your phone and recording yourself (or having a friend video record you) so you can see if you are breaking or flexing under load.

The good news is a 2020 scientific paper, “Slipped disc: Overview,” reports that about 90 percent of people will get relief from their pain and restricted movement within six weeks even if they receive no medical treatment. That’s because the body gets rid of part of the prolapsed tissue or it shifts position so that the nerves aren’t irritated anymore, according to the overview.

You can help that process along with physical therapy, DeLozier says. “The vast majority of folks feel better and have a great outcome by doing mobility exercises for the hip and low back and decompression exercises for the spine.”

The exercises you do depends on your individual situation, but your physical therapist may recommend mobility, stability, and core strengthening exercises such as:

Press Up: Lie facedown with your feet together, toes pointed, and your forearms on the floor palms. Pull your shoulder blades down and in towards your spine and press into forearms to lift your chest off of the floor, keeping your neck long and hips on the floor. If mobility allows, you can press up further by placing hands below your shoulders to come into an up-dog position. Hold for 3 to 5 seconds. Repeat about 15 times.

Hip Flexor Stretch: Kneel with the affected knee on the floor, tighten your glutes, and brace your abdominal muscles while keeping your back straight. Pressing hips forward. Hold this stretch for 30 seconds. Do 3 times.

Bird Dog: Get on your hands and knees with your palms flat on the floor, shoulder-width apart. Slowly raise and straighten your right leg and left arm at the same time. Keep your hips aligned with the torso and not tilted to one side. Hold for 10 seconds. Return to start. Repeat on the other side.

Cycling itself, when done with a healthy riding posture, can also be a great rehab tool to strengthen the discs and surrounding structures, Broadbent says. In fact, a study done on high-volume road cyclists who rode nearly 100 miles a week for five or more years had beneficial adaptations to their discs and psoas (the biggest hip flexor muscle), which were beneficial for spinal health.

If you’re riding through your rehab, Brodie recommends “crocodile breathing” when you get off the bike. To do it, lie facedown with your forehead resting on the back of your hands and let your chest and hips sink into the floor. Inhale deeply into your belly, expanding your diaphragm for four slow counts. Pause for two seconds, then exhale for six slow counts.

Finally, while many herniated discs can be treated conservatively with physical therapy, some require injection therapy like nerve blocks, if the symptoms are severe, Broadbent says. In rare cases, spinal disc herniation can cause extreme pressure and swelling of the nerves at the end of the spinal cord causing a medical emergency known as cauda equina syndrome.

“If your bladder and bowels aren’t working properly, you have saddle numbness (off the bike as well), or you have sexual dysfunction such as unable to get an erection then you should seek urgent medical investigation,” Broadbent says.

Again, cycling can actually improve spinal strength and the health and hydration of your discs, says Broadbent.

Having a good bike position and the right saddle is key. “You want your spine in a healthy position that doesn’t strain those discs in your lower back,” Pruitt says. If you ride a gravel or a mountain bike, you’re likely already in a slightly more upright, more neutral spine position, he says.

An anatomical saddle will also allow you to put your weight on your perineum without putting harmful pressure on those sensitive tissues, so you’re able to reach the bars without bending too far and putting your spine in an unhealthy position. “Saddle technology improvements have really helped reduce degenerative disc disease,” he says.

You also can protect your spine by doing core-strengthening exercises several times a week. Brodie recommends what are known as the “big three” exercises for spinal stability, developed by renowned back pain expert, Stuart McGill, Ph.D., Professor Emeritus of Spine Biomechanics at the University of Waterloo. These exercises stiffen the musculature throughout the core in a way that enhances stability in the spine.

How to do these exercises: The American Council of Exercise recommends performing three sets for each using a reverse pyramid rep/set scheme. Start with a higher number of repetitions for the first set, then decrease by 2 to 6 reps, then decrease by another 2 to 4 reps on the last set. So you might start with 8-6-4 reps and work your way up to 12-10-8 reps as you get stronger and build endurance. Hold each exercise for no more than 8 to 10 seconds.

McGill Curl-up: Lie on your back with one leg extended and the other leg bent, foot flat on the floor. Put your hands under your low back to maintain your natural arch. In one move, lift your head, shoulders, and chest off the floor, keeping your neck and back in the neutral position. Hold. Then slowly return to start. Do half a set. Then switch legs and complete the set.

Side Bridge: Lie on your right side, with your forearm on the floor and elbow underneath your shoulder. Bend your knees at a 90-degree angle. Place your left hand on the opposite shoulder for stability. Lift your hips off the floor so your body forms a straight line from your head to your knees. Hold. Then lower to the starting position and repeat to the other side. Work up to doing the move with your legs straight.

You can make these exercises part of your warm-up, along with dynamic mobility moves before you get into your bigger lifts.

Off the bike, Brodie recommends practicing good spine hygiene by avoiding hunching forward when you sit and not sitting in a static position for prolonged periods of time, but rather shifting positions in your chair every 15 minutes and getting up, moving around, and doing a little standing stretch, extending your arms, reaching overhead and taking a deep breath every 60 to 90 minutes.

Finally, don’t overlook your mattress. A firm mattress with a pillow top will help support your spine while you sleep, Brodie says. “A lot of people overlook this and it can make a big difference in your back health.”

— Newcomer organizations have names that resemble established groups

Several organizations have sprung up in recent years with names that sound very similar to long-standing established nonprofit public health and medical groups, whose science-based policies have withstood the test of time.

Here's a list of some of these organizations whose names, at first glance, may confuse prospective members or donors.

The American Public Health Association, founded in 1872, has some 25,000 individual members and more than 150,000 allied health professionals and advocates for public health science and spending. It publishes the American Journal of Public Health. Its CEO, Georges Benjamin, MD, has been a vociferous critic of the Trump administration's policies. The APHA has filed a lawsuit against its vaccination schedule policies and several others over grant funding.

The Academy of Public Health was founded in February 2025 by Jay Bhattacharya, MD, PhD, the Trump administration's NIH director, and others who questioned some of the ways the U.S. responded to the COVID pandemic, and who advocated the controversial policy of herd immunity. Its founders also criticized publication policies of many mainstream medical journals. It publishes the Journal of the Academy of Public Health.

The American Academy of Pediatrics was launched in 1930. It now has some 67,000 pediatricians as members. It has opposed Trump administration immunization policies as well as statements by HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. questioning vaccine schedules that have led to lower immunization rates among children, and more illness. It supports gender-affirming care for transgender youth.

The American College of Pediatricians was launched in 2002 by several pediatricians who left the AAP to protest the support of allowing same-sex couples to adopt children. It holds that life begins at fertilization. Its website says it is the "leading U.S. medical organization protecting the biological integrity of children against transgender ideology."

The American College of Obstetricians & Gynecologists began in 1951 to focus on issues specific to women's health, including family planning. It now focuses on advocating for maternal health as a national priority. It has about 60,000 members and publishes the journal Obstetrics & Gynecology. It has opposed several Trump administration policies, including warnings about use of antidepressants during pregnancy, suggesting these are linked with autism, miscarriage, and birth defects.

The American Association of Pro-Life Obstetricians and Gynecologists was an ACOG special interest group until 2013 when ACOG ended that designation. It opposes abortion, suggesting it is associated with health risks such as depression and substance abuse, and supports "the physician's right of conscience in medical decision making" regarding abortion.

The American Academy of Family Physicians began in 1947 and now says it is the voice of 124,500 family doctors. It publishes a journal, American Family Physician. It has opposed several Trump administration actions, such as its removal of an AAFP member and other members from the CDC's long-standing Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices.

The American College of Family Medicine began in the spring of 2023. It opposes mandatory abortion training in residencies and maintains that "abortion is never medically necessary and should not be offered or performed by any health professional."

The American College of Surgeons was founded in 1913 to improve surgical outcomes. It has launched numerous surgical quality improvement programs for surgical specialties to learn strategies and compare results, and its website has a surgical risk calculator. It claims 95,000 members globally, and publishes the Journal of the American College of Surgeons.

The Association of American Physicians and Surgeons was launched in 1943 by a conservative group of physicians. It publishes the Journal of American Physicians and Surgeons.

The Center for Science in the Public Interest began in 1971 as a consumer advocacy group and now advocates for policies that keep dangerous chemicals out of the food supply. It wants to "fix our broken food additive regulations" and improve food labeling.
