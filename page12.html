Bills who need to keep rolling and those that must step up on MNF vs. Vikings

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Buffalo Bills (6-1) travel to Minneapolis on Monday Night Football to take on a Minnesota Vikings team that is also 6-1 and coming off its first loss of the season, 19-15 at Detroit. The Bills beat the Baltimore Ravens 36-10 last Sunday, but Baltimore lost both quarterbacks in the first half, and I'm careful about using that game as a measuring stick for this one. Minnesota is a different kind of test.

Brian Flores is the main reason. His defense leads the NFL in scoring (13.3 points per game) and blitzes on roughly 70 percent of dropbacks. Carolina is next at 42.6 percent (the Bills themselves blitzed on 64.5 percent of dropbacks last Sunday, second only to Minnesota in Week 8, per Next Gen Stats). Josh Allen completes 65.7 percent of his passes against five or more rushers at 8.4 yards per attempt, so, on paper it's a great matchup but the reality is that nobody is better than Flores sending 5 or more after opposing QBs. Buffalo has the league's best offense (33.3 points per game) and the best EPA per play (0.175), and Minnesota has the best defensive mark (-0.189).

The Vikings' offense is their problem on the other side. It ranks 30th in EPA per play (-0.099), Carson Wentz starts with Kyler Murray out with a hamstring injury, and Justin Jefferson has 335 yards heading into Week 9, a lower pace than his career-low 2025, per Pro Football Talk. I think the game comes down to whether Buffalo's offense can stay on schedule against the blitz while the defense tries to not give Wentz and Jefferson easy completions. With all of this in mind, here are the Bills who need to keep rolling and those who must step up Monday night.

Zay Flowers entered Week 8 second in the NFL in receiving yards per game (101.3) and first in yards after the catch (331). He caught none of his five targets, and four came from rookie Joe Fagnano. Jim Leonhard also changed the plan: the Bills ran man coverage on 54.8 percent of dropbacks, up from 19.5 percent, per Next Gen Stats.

Benford was a big part of that. His coverage EPA of -5.8 was sixth in the NFL in Week 8, he was eighth in catch rate allowed over expected, and his tight-window percentage (50) tied Damar Hamlin for sixth. He played 45 of 51 snaps. He has found his rhythm after a slow start — Pro Football Reference has him allowing a 106.6 passer rating through his first five games (22-of-36, three touchdowns). PFF had him 72nd of 102 corners (55.4) through Week 8. Monday needs the last two games version.

Jefferson has 28 catches for 335 yards and four touchdowns. In games Wentz has played, he gets 31.5 percent of targets and 45.2 percent of air yards, and averages 2.52 yards per route run, per Sharp Football Analysis. Against Detroit, he caught five of nine targets for 33 yards and a touchdown. With the offense 30th in EPA, stopping him goes a long way toward stopping Minnesota.

In his three full games, Moore has a 28.7 percent target share and 42.3 percent air yards share, which Next Gen Stats ranks sixth and fourth in the league. Against Baltimore he caught four of seven targets for 82 yards and a touchdown. Downfield, he caught three of four for 78 yards and a score, including a 43-air-yard completion that was Allen's longest of the game by air yards. PFF had him on the field for 37 of Allen's 42 pass plays (54 snaps).

As stated above, Allen has been successful against five or more rushers. That kind of defense leaves a receiver one-on-one, and Moore is the one the offense looks to first. His AC joint sprain is the variable. He's always one bad fall over his shoulder away from leaving the game and making Josh's life tougher. Keon Colrman has stepped up when it happened and could be another key in this matchup.

Grable allowed two pressures on 42 pass-blocking snaps against Baltimore, per charting-based metrics cited by Buffalo Rumblings. Both came in true pass sets, and he had 12 of those. He played all 82 snaps, and Allen was sacked once. The numbers aren't clean, though: PFF had him at 38.8 in Week 5 after he replaced Spencer Brown, and 55.8 for the season over 133 snaps. His negatively graded run-block play rate was 13.2 percent, against 5.3 percent for Dion Dawkins.

The veteran is questionable with a rib injury and hasn't practiced, though Joe Brady said he could play anyway. If he sits, which I expect, Grable likely moves to left tackle with Brown at right. Minnesota's rush is led by Andrew Van Ginkel (6.5 sacks) and Dallas Turner (six sacks, 19 quarterback hits), per ESPN. The Bills line has allowed the third-fewest pressures (74) and seventh-fewest sacks (13). Grable helps decide whether that holds.

Cook has rushed for at least 75 yards and a touchdown in six straight games, but he got 80 yards on 21 carries (3.8 per carry) against the Ravens. On the season he has 707 yards and seven touchdowns on 138 carries. The Bills rank third in rush EPA (0.013) and second in rush success rate (49.7 percent). Excluding Week 8, Buffalo led the NFL in rush DVOA (16.1 percent).

The Vikings are beatable on the ground. They rank 17th in rush yards allowed (116.7 per game) and gave Jahmyr Gibbs 153 yards and a touchdown on 23 carries last week. Screens have produced +0.39 EPA per play on 12 attempts against Minnesota, per SIS. Cook played 44 snaps (54 percent) against Baltimore and had three targets. He needs to be both the draw and the screen outlet against the blitz. We know very well Joe Brady loves a screen opportunity.

PFF has him at a 42.6 overall grade, 96th among 103 cornerbacks. His coverage grade is 41.8 (94th), with a 100.8 passer rating allowed on 13 receptions, in addition to five missed tackles, through Week 6. An ankle injury kept him out of the game against the Ravens, and he has no designation on the final injury report for Monday.

Te'Cory Couch started at nickel last week: 36 snaps (71 percent), one reception allowed for zero yards, and one of the team's highest PFF grades (79.9). Jim Leonhard said he executed at a very high level. Jordan Hancock, who plays nickel and safety, is questionable and needs to be activated from IR. Maxwell Hairston also has snaps in three-cornerback packages, with Benford sometimes following receivers inside. If the Bills maintain the 54.8 percent man coverage from last week (Next Gen Stats), the slot is where the Vikings will test them. I think his spot is at risk, and Monday is his chance to hold onto it.

Josh Allen, QB: PFF graded him 82.3 last week. He went 6-of-10 for 157 yards and two touchdowns on throws of 10+ air yards. For the season he is fourth in EPA per play (0.22), 10th against the blitz (0.11) and third against pressure (-0.03), per ESPN. On bootlegs he has 12.15 total EPA, second among quarterbacks, per SIS.

Greg Rousseau, OLB: six of the team's 30 pressures and a 27.3 percent pressure rate, fifth among players with 10+ rushes, per Next Gen Stats. Wentz has less mobility than Murray, and Rousseau is the Bills' best answer.

Davison Igbinosun, CB: coverage EPA of -4.7 (10th) and a -74.6 percent catch rate allowed over expected, second-best among qualified defensive backs. He played 41 of 51 snaps (80 percent), PFF graded him 73.4, and he had his first career interception. Jordan Addison is his target.

Jackson Hawes, TE: 29.3 from PFF, the lowest on offense. His bobbled pass became Broderick Washington's interception and set up the Ravens' only touchdown. With the need for the run game to get going, he should see a lot of snaps on offense.

Michael Hoecht, OLB: 32.5 from PFF against the Ravens, second-lowest on the defense, and 47.9 through Week 6 (110th of 115 edge defenders). Buffalo's pressure rate was the NFL's highest in Week 8 (61.3 percent), but Rousseau had much of it, and rookie TJ Parker seem to be earning some of the snaps that used to go to Hoecht.

The SIS data points to quick throws, bootlegs, and screens as what has worked against Minnesota (+0.24, +0.37 and +0.39 EPA per play). Moore on the outside, Cook (and Ty Johnson) on the screens and Grable keeping Allen upright are how Buffalo gets there, and Benford slowing down Jefferson is how it avoids a track meet.

The Woman Who Said Yes While Shaking Her Head No

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Early on in my career, I was a research manager working on consumer health products for a pharmaceutical company. Part of my job was to develop ideas for new products by testing them in large quantitative surveys to forecast sales. We showed respondents product descriptions I had painstakingly written and asked how likely they would be to buy it.

We tabulated the results to the decimal point, giving the data an aura of accuracy. But there was one problem. What people said in surveys didn’t reliably predict what they bought in market.

I came across a clue as to why sitting behind a two-way mirror. The focus group moderator was reading a product concept we tested in an earlier survey. She asked: “If this was available, would you buy it?” Without hesitating, a woman across the table clearly said yes, while her head was already shaking no.

The woman herself and the moderator didn’t notice. And we all tallied a yes in the backroom.

But her words just didn’t match her gesture. This doesn’t mean a headshake is the same thing as a lie detector. I wasn’t sure what she might do later. But I knew at that time that her answer was less settled than she made it sound.

From then on, I would note verbal and nonverbal responses, and I kept seeing the same split. A yes would arrive with a wince, eye roll, or furrowed brow. Sometimes they would say deadpan they are excited to buy. I would ask them to write down their reasons. The written words seemed well-reasoned and coherent. Over time, the unspoken reactions helped explain what my self-reported surveys were missing. It’s not like the words were useless. But they weren’t the clear window into behavior that the market research industry was built on.

I also had been assuming that honest people could explain their answers. But I started doubting that, and it changed my career path. The pharmaceutical company offered me a paid executive MBA at Columbia. I asked if I could get a degree in psychology. They said no, so I said no. I left to work at a qualitative research firm conducting focus groups and in-depth interviews. The answers weren’t in my spreadsheets, so I’d rather be in the room with people, so I could see the real story show up before the words.

Years later, I found a different kind of research and an aha moment that helped explain the “say versus do gap” that had undermined my earlier self-reported quantitative forecasts.

Neuroscientist Michael Gazzaniga conducted fascinating studies on patients whose corpus callosum, the fibers connecting the two hemispheres of the brain, had been cut to control severe epilepsy. The surgery sharply limited communication between hemispheres, which allowed him to show patients information to one side of the brain without the other side seeing it.

A patient was shown the word “walk” to their right hemisphere and he got up and began walking and the experimenter asked him why. The left hemisphere, which controls speech, hadn’t seen the instruction, so the honest answer should have been "I don’t know." But instead, he said something completely sincere: "I was thirsty and wanted a drink."

Gazzaniga called this left hemisphere function the interpreter: a process that takes something we just did and creates the most coherent explanation based upon whatever information is available.

The woman behind the glass wasn’t a split-brain patient. Her hemispheres communicated normally. We can’t compare the two research studies literally. But what she did share with split-brain patients was the gap: a fluent verbal response that didn’t match her actions. People with intact brains base their explanations on much more information and they often get it right. But Gazzaniga argues that the interpreter is not just a quirk from surgery but something all our brains do as we attempt to make our experiences feel unified and our actions seem like our own doing. The surgery simply made the interpreter more visible; by cutting off information we would normally use.

You can see this in people with normal brains. Researchers at Lund University in Sweden conducted an experiment that let people in a supermarket taste jam. Shoppers were asked which jam they preferred and then asked to taste it a second time. But the experimenters secretly swapped out their preferred jam with another. Most of the participants didn’t even notice the swap, and when asked to describe why they preferred it, they easily explained why, even though they had just rejected it. Despite their brain being fully intact, their reasons seemed like honest reports when they may have been made up.

Have you ever bought something on a whim? You can probably think of a reason why easily. But here’s a harder question: How do you know that is the real reason? Maybe the answer arrived later based on what you know about the product, the moment you bought it, and the person you believe yourself to be?

Or how about bigger decisions, like the job that felt right, the person you just met that you trusted, or that comment from your loved one that made you snap, only to explain it after as: I was just having a bad day. I’m not saying your explanations are fictitious. But maybe it was the simplest story you could put together on such short notice, while leaving out a deeper feeling of being dismissed by them, or some other worry you felt but hadn’t yet named.

Your reasons are not necessarily lies. But alone they’re incomplete. Sometimes there’s a story, and then there’s the real story. When you close a conversation with a quick confident answer you miss an opportunity to dig deeper for a question that deserves to stay open a little longer.

If you ever find yourself doing something surprising, remember your first explanation is just the start not the verdict. Think about what was happening right before and try to parse out if you were moving away or toward something. We tend to avoid bad feelings rather than try to get what we want. We choose a more expensive bottle to avoid looking cheap, agree with someone to avoid a fight, or put off doing something because we fear what might happen. Those reasons are usually not the first things to come to mind. Make sure to check if your story fits everything, not just the things you noticed.

I never learned what that woman finally did. She said yes, but her body said no. And while both responses came from her, neither told the whole story.

Your explanation may be sincere, but it also might be incomplete.

Scientists Discover a Hidden Eye Disease That Can Steal Your Night Vision

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Researchers can spot signs of a newly identified genetic eye disease before light-sensing cells are lost by measuring how vision recovers in darkness.

People with a newly identified genetic eye condition can see well for most of their lives before gradually losing peripheral vision and struggling to see at dusk or at night. The condition is linked to a gene already associated with a different eye disease, one that affects central vision first.

The two conditions arise from different variants, or versions, of the EFEMP1 gene. The newly identified variant, p.Arg140Trp, causes abnormally thick material to build up between cells in the eye. Researchers are calling the condition EFEMP1-associated late-onset retinal degeneration, or L-ORD, although it has not been formally named.

L-ORD begins slowly at the periphery of the retina, the tissue at the back of the eye that converts light into signals the brain interprets as sight. Its gradual progression makes it difficult to detect. One useful clue is unusually slow recovery of vision when a person moves from a lighted setting into low light. This adjustment involves rod photoreceptors, the retinal cells specialized for seeing in dim conditions.

“We can detect a major abnormality in the way rod photoreceptors recover in darkness with retinas that still look structurally intact,” said Artur V. Cideciyan, PhD, an ophthalmology researcher and co-director of the Center for Hereditary Retinal Degenerations. “This gives us a functional marker of disease at a stage when photoreceptor cells have not yet been lost.”

The investigation began with a clinical description of a family in the United States in 1998.

“The different disease expression in this family was thought to be similar to a condition caused by C1QTNF5 gene variants. However, none of the variants associated with that disease were found,” Cideciyan said. “Our collaborators then discovered a new variant in the EFEMP1 gene that normally causes a different disease where the macula, which is tied to central vision, is affected first.”

The genetic finding allowed researchers to expand the investigation to multiple European families. They studied three unrelated families in different countries that shared the p.Arg140Trp variant and compared them with a separate family carrying p.Arg345Trp, the EFEMP1 variant responsible for the already-known condition affecting central vision.

Published in JAMA Ophthalmology, the research involved an international team that included scientists from the Scheie Eye Institute of the Perelman School of Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania. Researchers from the University of Edinburgh, the University of Basel, Charles University, and the University of Bonn also contributed.

The researchers do not yet know how widespread p.Arg140Trp is or how many people have this form of L-ORD. They suspect it may be relatively rare, but larger studies are needed to establish its prevalence.

“Aging is a risk factor for many medical conditions, including retinal diseases such as age-related macular degeneration, the most common retinal condition of aging,” said Tomas S. Aleman, MD, a professor of ophthalmology and the other co-director of the Center for Hereditary Retinal Degenerations. “Lessons learned from these infrequent, late-onset genetic eye diseases offer glimpses at possible mechanisms of disease for much more frequent retinal problems.”

Meatloaf Is So Much Juicier When You Add This Boxed Ingredient

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

We may receive a commission on purchases made from links.

When you want a meal that sticks to your ribs and brings a measure of comfort with every bite, you want a good, homemade meatloaf, one of America's favorite dinners. It's traditionally made with ground beef, though to keep things tender, people often include ground pork or Italian sausage. Some include a cooked-down vegetable mix to help the loaf stay moist, while others employ the use of milk. But there's a boxed (or bagged) ingredient likely already in your pantry that you might bring out next time you make meatloaf and want to avoid a crumbly, cracking mess, and it'll help create the juiciest version you've likely ever had. Instant mashed potatoes, which are great to keep on hand for soups and sauces, are about to be your secret weapon in the battle against dry meatloaf, too.

Instant mashed potatoes might sound like a strange mix-in, but hear us out. They're dehydrated and flaky, which makes them ideal when used in meatloaf because when those flakes get dampened by your other ingredients (meat, milk, egg), they thicken, adding bulk and binding the mixture together. More importantly, though, these hard-working flakes hold onto moisture, locking it inside the loaf inside instead of letting the juices run out quite so freely, so your dinner won't dry out during its long cook in the oven.

While moist meatloaf is reason enough to add instant mashed potatoes to the mix, there are a few other benefits worth considering. First and foremost: They're cheap, and they keep for a long time. You can typically get a box from Walmart for less than $4 (prices may vary), and its bags often start at just a buck apiece. Many brands can last up to a year, so you can keep them in your pantry, and they'll likely be good to go the next time you're in the mood for meatloaf (or shortcut gnocchi, for that matter).

Furthermore, most instant mashed potatoes are gluten-free. While you'll want to be sure to check the labels before using them, these potato flakes can often make meatloaf accessible to our friends and family with celiac disease or gluten sensitivities, since they can substitute for breadcrumbs.

Finally, a few tips when employing instant spuds in your dinner loaf. You might let them sit for a few minutes after combining them with the other ingredients, before you introduce the meat; this gives them the opportunity to "set up." And you're not confined to just plain buttery instant mashed potatoes. Give your supper a major upgrade with different flavors of boxed flakes, like sour cream and chives, four cheese, and more.

Is It True That Air Force One Pilots Earn Around $215K While Big 3 Widebody Captains Clear $480K?

United States Air Force VC-46A aircraft and the Air Force VC-25B "Bridge" (aka Air Force One) aircraft are military aircraft owned and operated by the United States Air Force and are crewed and piloted by USAF commissioned officers. These are paid as US military officers, and so the Air Force has little flexibility to provide extra pay to its pilots. The base pay is the same as any other US officer serving any branch (including the Coast Guard and Space Force) and doesn't change by responsibility. So an Army officer receives the same base pay as a pilot of the same rank and service.

This is problematic, as it can cost around $10-15 million to train a fast jet pilot. It has left the Air Force exposed to intense competition from commercial airlines that can offer double the total compensation with what is sometimes considered a better work-life balance. One way the Air Force (and the Marines and Navy) have sought to counter this is with retention bonuses, although these are limited, and commercial airlines do the same. Here is what to know about how much commercial widebody pilots are paid and how much Air Force One pilots are paid.

The Air Force does not pay a special "Air Force One salary." The pilots who fly these special Jumbos (and other Air Force aircraft the President flies on) are commissioned officers assigned to the 89th Airlift Wing. As with other US Air Force officers, they are paid according to their military rank and years of service. They do not get a special salary based on the prestige of the aircraft they fly.

Lieutenant Colonels with less than two years of service earn $7,295 per month in base salary, while those with six years of aviation service have a base pay of $9,250 monthly. For Colonels, the base pay is $8,751 for under two years of service and $10,284 for six years. But while six years is the highest number the US Air Force's table goes up to, in practice all Air Force One pilots have a much longer service and earn more.

Lieutenant Colonels typically have 15–17 years of commissioned service, while Colonels typically have 21–24 years of commissioned service. A Lieutenant Colonel flying VC-46s in 2026 can be expected to have a base pay of around $130,000 to $145,000, plus a Basic Allowance for Housing (BAH) of around $30,000 to $45,000, an Aviation Retention Bonus of around $35,000 to $50,000, plus other benefits for a total compensation of between $200,000 and $250,000. A Colonel can expect to earn $235,000 to $285,000 in total compensation.

The US Air Force pilots who fly the VC-46s are typically experienced Lieutenant Colonels (O-5) or Colonels (O-6). This makes them a higher rank than the typical ranks that fly fighter jets on combat missions. Most fighter jet pilots who actually carry out combat missions are First Lieutenants (O-2) and Captains (0-3). At the next rank of Major (0-4), administrative and leadership responsibilities increase, although many majors continue to fly operational and combat missions extensively.

Majors tend to carry out fewer combat missions, although they do extensively perform combat missions as required, often as squadron commanders of a strike package. It is even less common for Lieutenant Colonels and Colonels to fly in combat, although they can if a need arises. In 2026, the F-15E Strike Eagle Weapons Systems Officer that was downed over Iran is reported to have been a Colonel. On average, pilots assigned to the presidential airlift tend to be more senior in rank than pilots in operational fighter squadrons.

As such, Air Force One pilots do tend to earn more than their fighter jet (and transport) counterparts, although it is related to rank and years of service, not the aircraft per se. The 89th Airlift Wing also employs many majors and occasionally captains, although it's unclear if they fly the VC-46s. The wing has a large range of aircraft.

The 89th Airlift Wing (89 AW) is based at Joint Base Andrews, Maryland, and is responsible for operating the USAF's primary executive and special air mission (SAM) fleet. According to its website, it "provides global Special Air Mission airlift, logistics, aerial port, and communications for the President, Vice President, cabinet members and senior leaders to ensure nuclear command, control and communications, continuity of government and continuity of operations."

Its website also listed its aircraft as including two VC-25A (Air Force One) aircraft, four C-32A aircraft (often called Air Force Two), four C-40B Clippers, and eleven C-37A/Bs based on the Gulfstream business jet. This list appears somewhat out of date, as the wing now operates the VC-25B "Bridge" (ex-Qatari Boeing 747-8) Presidential aircraft. Simple Flying has previously reported that the Air Force has quietly purchased an expanded fleet of shadowy Boeing 757s; these are also operated by the 89th Airlift Wing.

89th Airlift Wing fleet (per USAF, others)

The most notable squadron is the Presidential Airlift Group / Presidential Airlift Squadron, which is primarily responsible for the VC-25s. Other notable squadrons include the 1st Airlift Squadron (operating the C-32A and the C-40B) and the 99th Airlift Squadron (operating the C-37 fleet). The wing has a "ready force" of over 1,800 personnel.

While the Air Force is heavily constrained in how much it can pay its pilots, commercial operators are not. Senior widebody captains routinely earn $400,000 to over $520,000 annually. Top widebody captains' hourly rates at all three carriers are around $465 per hour. To some extent, the pay structure in the commercial sector is similar to that in the military.

Commercial pilots are paid based on seniority and years of service with the airline, not so much the type of aircraft they fly. They are also based on their rank, although in the commercial sector, this is limited to captains and first officers. The primary reason why widebody aircraft captains are paid more than narrowbody captains is that pilots bid to fly the widebody aircraft based on their seniority and years of service.

This is somewhat similar to how it is with the prestigious Air Force One fleet. Those pilots are typically paid more than their fighter jet, transport, and other pilot counterparts, but that is due mostly to their seniority. They can only fly the VC-25s as they are very senior pilots. At the rank of Colonel, most pilots in the United States Air Force do not routinely fly military aircraft on operational missions, although there are numerous exceptions.

The Big Three US-based airlines are Delta Air Lines, American Airlines, and United Airlines. Alaska Airlines now operates a limited fleet of Boeing 787 Dreamliners, and Hawaiian Airlines has a fleet of Airbus A330s. Southwest is one of the world's largest airlines, but it exclusively operates the Boeing 737 narrowbody. Of the big three, American has the smallest and simplest widebody fleet made up of 67 Boeing 777s and 70 Boeing 787s.

Delta Air Lines has the next largest widebody fleet, with 57 legacy Boeing 767s being phased down. It also has a fleet of 81 Airbus A330s and 41 Airbus A350s. Delta has placed an order for 30 Boeing 787-10s with options for 30 more, and it has 16 outstanding orders for the A330neo and 39 orders for the A350. American's current outstanding widebody order book is for 19 Boeing 787s.

United Airlines has the largest widebody fleet, and the second largest in the world after Emirates. It operates an all-Boeing fleet of 88 Boeing 787 Dreamliners, 96 Boeing 777s, and 53 aging 767s. United has recently canceled the delayed order for Airbus A350s, meaning its widebody fleet is to stay a Boeing fleet.

It should be noted that there are other considerations when comparing income between the military and commercial sectors, although these can be difficult to simply quantify. For example, pilots in the US Air Force are guaranteed a government-backed lifetime pension of at least 20 years. With American, Delta, and United, traditional pensions are largely gone for new hires, although they contribute around 18% of pay into 401(k)-type plans.

Under the Air Force's retirement system, a pilot can retire after 20 years of service—often in their late 30s or early 40s—and begin drawing an immediate lifetime pension while pursuing a second career. There is no direct equivalent in the commercial airline industry, where pilots generally continue flying until the FAA's mandatory retirement age of 65 while building retirement savings through employer-funded defined-contribution plans.

This is also a contributing factor to the Air Force's ability to retain pilots and, later, struggle to retain pilots. The military pension provides a strong incentive for pilots to remain in uniform until they reach the 20-year milestone. A pilot can serve 20 years with the Air Force, retire, be employed by a commercial airline, and then receive those additional pension contributions. That said, it's worth noting that the Air Force pension increases if the pilot serves beyond 20 years.

Godzilla Minus Zero Opening Weekend Box Office Breaks A Foreign-Language Film Records

Godzilla Minus Zero had a great first weekend in theaters

Godzilla Minus Zero had a strong opening weekend, grossing more at the domestic box office than any live action foreign-language film before it. On top of that, a higher percentage of people sought out premium screens, like IMAX, to watch it even more than they did for The Odyssey earlier this year.

Deadline's reporting highlights that Godzilla Minus Zero is estimated to have grossed $32 million. Although American-made Godzilla movies have made more during their first weekend in theaters, this is a very impressive feat for a foreign-language film, as they rarely do this well at the domestic box office unless they're connected to an anime. As mentioned, this opening is record-breaking for a live-action one.

Godzilla Minus Zero's performance also shows the value of making movies that people want to see on the biggest screen possible, as around 74% of its gross came from IMAX or other premium large format screens. In comparison, The Odyssey got a lot of attention because around 54% of its opening weekend gross came from PLF screens. 2026 has certainly shown that there's demand for blockbusters that embrace that opulent style of filmmaking beyond a typical superhero movie.

Outside of Godzilla Minus Zero, the animated Cat in the Hat movie had a fairly underwhelming $20 million opening weekend, while You Can See Everything had an impressive hold week-over-week that makes a $100 million domestic gross more possible. Green Thumb Ended Its String of Profitable Quarters. Is the Stock a Buy?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Green Thumb Industries ended its run of four consecutive profitable quarters.

One big edge that Green Thumb Industries (GTBIF +8.48%) had until recently was that it was one of the few marijuana retailers to turn a profit. On Nov. 4, however, the Chicago-based company reported a third-quarter loss per share of $0.02, the first time since 2025's Q2 that it has lost money.

That's not the end of the world, though. The key is that Green Thumb continues to grow sales. It reported Q3 revenue of $324.4 million, up 11.3%, year over year, showing growing demand for its consumer brand portfolio.

Here are three reasons to buy Green Thumb Industries.

Green Thumb maintains a cash-generative operating profile and balance-sheet discipline. Operational cash flow remains solid at $58 million in Q3, following $76 million in Q1 and $29 million in Q2, enabling management to aggressively return capital to shareholders.

Over the first nine months of 2026, Green Thumb repurchased 13.4 million shares for $77.7 million, reducing outstanding share count from 206.6 million down to 197.5 million, and recently authorized a new $50 million share buyback program.

The primary reason for the Q3 net loss is the company's continued expansion. It opened six RISE dispensaries in the quarter: Port Charlotte and Port St. Lucie in Florida; Sparks in Nevada; and Hanover, West Mifflin, and Allentown in Pennsylvania.

The company is positioned to capture future growth as several major key states transition toward adult-use legalization or expanded access. Green Thumb holds a vertically integrated license in Virginia, where recreational retail launches in July 2027, and a conditional license under Texas' Compassionate Use Program, and continues to expand retail density in states such as New Jersey, Ohio, and Pennsylvania. Together, these expansion markets provide clear multiyear revenue runways.

Green Thumb is No. 2 in quarterly revenue among the top U.S. cannabis retailers, behind Curaleaf, which recently reported $353 million in Q3. Compared to Curaleaf and Trulieve, Green Thumb is well priced, with a price-to-sales ratio of 1.3. It also has a lower debt-to-earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA) level than Curaleaf or Trulieve because it carries less debt.

In Q3, the company reported cash of $316 million and total debt of $277.3 million. Green Thumb is already benefiting from the rescheduling of cannabis to a Schedule III substance. It spent $28.1 million on income taxes in the quarter, nearly half the $49.1 million it spent in the same period a year ago.

Its operating cash flow was $58 million in the quarter, down from $74.1 million in the same quarter in 2025, but that's still superior to the $53 million Trulieve reported in Q2 and the $22 million that Curaleaf reported in the third quarter.

The reason why some USB ports are teal

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Did you know that different colors have different meanings?

It's a fairly normal thing to look at the different slots and pieces of your computer, knowing full well what most of them do, but being somewhat confused about what one particular element is for. Not everyone can be a super genius, after all, so there's no reason to feel bad about not knowing the reason something is the way it is.

When it comes to ports on your computer, the multitude of different USB slots can sometimes feel strange to look at, considering many of them have different colors. If you have a higher-end motherboard or case, you may also have a lot of them to look at, which makes determining their exact purpose something of a mystery.

After decades of evolution, USB ports have become one of the most versatile ports you can find on your PC or laptop. When it comes to the teal port, it actually has some fascinating use cases, and we'll be breaking down exactly what that color does, as well as how you can use it to your advantage.

If you see a teal USB port on your device, you may be interested to know that the color is generally designated for USB 3.1, Gen 2 or above. After its introduction in 2013, USB 3.1 was standardized by the USB-IF, the USB Implementers Forum, with a focus on high transfer speeds and increased power output to devices connected to it. The color isn't a rule, of course, and there are a lot of 3.1 Gen 2 USB ports that have different colors.

While the higher speeds, which can reach upward of 10 Gbps, are a fantastic feature, they're also usually backward compatible with USB 3.0 and prior. You won't necessarily get the same speed benefits, but as USB is one of the most standard ports in computers, you will be able to use most previous generations of USB-A devices with it without issue.

Though it has a distinct advantage over a lot of other USB port colors, there are certain red USB ports that feature USB 3.2. The USB 3.2 ports can reach a top-end speed of 20 Gbps, and though teal-colored versions do exist, they're a bit more uncommon. If you're unsure of the exact possible speed, you can use the Device Manager on Windows or System Report on macOS.

If you have a teal USB port and can take advantage of its blazing transfer speeds, you may be able to get some spectacular usage out of it. For one thing, the high speed and power output means it can help immensely with any task you need to throw at it.

It's incredibly helpful if you're someone who does a lot of file transfers, or maybe you have a library of games you want to move around to different SSDs. Considering how well USB 3.1 works with high-speed external SSDs, you will be able to significantly cut down any transfer time that may have been slower in the past. This would also help with something like music or content creation, as sample libraries that normally take a long time to move onto SSDs or large video files can be done in minutes.

As teal USB ports tend to favor 3.1 Gen 2, the power output is also a huge benefit. While charging your phone or tablet on your computer's USB ports has usually worked, the power output has generally made it less than desirable. 3.1 Gen 2 is considerably more powerful and can charge things like phones, tablets, earbuds and other smaller electronics with no problem.
