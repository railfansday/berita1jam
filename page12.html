Love Is Blind once again proves that love is not blind, or maybe shouldn’t be, with another disastrous season. However, this may be one of the show’s most unsuccessful seasons in terms of marriages and long-term relationships. The one marriage from the season didn’t even last a year.

An interesting reunion can usually redeem a less-than-exciting season of Love is Blind, which you can watch with a Netflix subscription. We have seen many do just that. However, this reunion confirmed just how disappointing Season 11 is. It magnified Love is Blind’s issues.

Warning: Love is Blind Season 11 reunion spoilers are ahead. Proceed with caution.

I have watched all 11 seasons of Love Is Blind, and the reunions are getting worse. These are supposed to be an opportunity to dig into some of the biggest unanswered questions. They could be a chance to hold people accountable for questionable behavior from previous episodes.

Hosts Nick and Vanessa Lachey achieve some of these things, but not fully. Rob Feeley had one of the season’s biggest moments. It led to many online discussions about his and his friends’ problematic behavior. The reunion barely addresses it.

Other things aren’t fully addressed, including details of some of the breakups. For example, we’re left confused about Michaella Giorgio and Matt Waldrip’s failed relationship. We leave the season with a lot more questions than answers.

The whole point of the reunion is to get answers and closure for the viewers. Even the messiest seasons usually have some bright spots, but Love Is Blind: Boston doesn’t seem to have any. This makes us worry even more about the series’ future.

The reunion had bromance montages, an engagement announcement from someone who was barely on the season, a focus on some men who were barely on the show, a look at Blake Carver’s life in the sea, and a musical number. It seemed like the Love Is Blind Season 11 reunion wanted to do everything but focus on what viewers care about: information about the couples.

I know the show wanted to balance some lighthearted moments with the drama and heaviness. However, they just felt out of place. It took away from the really interesting parts of the reunion. Additionally, it felt extremely ironic that a show that’s supposed to focus on proving love can work under extreme circumstances chose to have “Stop the Wedding!” by Ashe as the season’s theme song.

It further emphasizes how much Love Is Blind doesn’t take itself seriously anymore. We don’t know if the producers still care at all about having successful couples.

Automatically, the Love Is Blind reunion felt uncomfortable. It just became increasingly that way as it progressed. It was an intense season, but the producers could have done more to make the tension less intense. It was either too intense or ridiculous with showing unnecessary things like montages.

Love Is Blind has had plenty of iconic moments, so the show has the capability for that. However, it’s losing its way in producing a well-executed, compelling show about relationships. The season as a whole and the reunion highlights this problem. Hopefully, they can start to pull it back to its old glory in the upcoming season.

We still think Love Is Blind, but it could use some help.

New York Times Blames Trump For Air Safety Risks—Gets Blasted By The Independent NTSB

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The National Transportation Safety Board says the New York Times distorted its comments to attack President Trump. The paper’s aviation safety story has bigger problems than just politics. It gets a major crash wrong and credits a pilot hours rule for a safety record that began years before the rule took effect. James Fallows’s “Is Air Travel Getting Less Safe?” describes real issues of overworked air traffic controllers, military aircraft operating around passenger flights, and safety recommendations that haven’t been adequately addressed. Then it tries to turn them into a story about Trump dismantling the institutions that keep us safe, even though the issues he cites have been going on far longer than the current administration. I’ve written predominantly critically about Trump since he first ran for president 11 years ago. I rejected his effort to blame diversity hiring for the American Airlines 5342 tragedy before investigators had established what happened. That doesn’t make every argument against him a good one. The Times introduces Fallows as an instrument-rated private pilot with more than 2,000 hours. That’s true but while he’s written extensively about aviation it’s an odd way to frame him as author of this story. He’s also Jimmy Carter’s former chief speechwriter, a longtime Atlantic writer and editor, and a familiar voice on NPR. His career has been about politics and public policy. His own introduction to the article frames the piece as meant to connect aviation under Trump with public health, international aid, diplomacy, higher education and the rule of law. Aviation is the vehicle for a larger political point. The piece isn’t really about aviation, it’s using aviation. The Independent NTSB Attacks The Piece The NTSB says the article: Distorts the agency’s responses with political statements intended to attack the current administration. Mischaracterizes the NTSB’s role in the 1,500 hour pilot requirement. Incorrectly implies negligence by the Black Hawk pilots involved in the Washington National Airport collision. The statement also thanks the administration for supporting the agency and providing resources. That last part is political messaging too. (Gratitude for a budget doesn’t answer address aviation safety, but is probably necessary as a defensive measure.) “We are disappointed that this article sensationalizes and politicizes tragedies.” The NTSB’s response to the New York Times Magazine. — NTSB Newsroom (@NTSB_Newsroom) November 12, 2026 Fallows interviewed NTSB Chair Jennifer Homendy. The agency argues she’s being mischaracterized, but they don’t actually say how. In the article she says, “Recommendations alone don’t save lives. Action does.” Her own July statement about the Washington crash warned that delays implementing the board’s recommendations represented a continuing, unacceptable risk. The Article Gets The Colgan Air Crash Wrong Fallows spends considerable space describing the 2009 Colgan Air crash near Buffalo – as the last U.S. airline crash with loss of life before American 5342. In fact, PenAir flight 3296 overran the runway at Unalaska in October 2019, killing a passenger. His account has the Colgan Air plane accumulating ice until it becomes a “frigid brick.” He writes that by the time the captain recognized the problem, it “might already have become unflyable.” But that isn’t what the investigation found. The FAA’s accident analysis says icing wasn’t a cause. When the stall warning activated, the airplane wasn’t yet stalled. The captain pulled back on the controls and put it into a stall. The crew had failed to monitor their airspeed and mishandled the approach. And both Colgan pilots already had more than 1,500 hours: The captain had 3,379 hours. The captain had a history of training failures despite the hours. The first officer had 2,244 hours, including 1,470 hours as a light airplane flight instructor and 774 hours in turbine aircraft. The 1,500 Hour Rule For Co-Pilots Isn’t The Reason For The Safety Improvements After Colgan, Congress and regulators made several changes. Pilot rest rules improved. Training changed. Pilot qualification requirements changed. Sharing pilot records improved. Evidence that safety has performed well after a package of changes doesn’t establish that (1) every element of those changes (2) caused the improvements. And there’s strong reason to believe the the 1,500 hour rule has the opposite effect. It makes becoming a pilot take far longer and become far more costly, which keeps talented people from becoming pilots. Those with a higher opportunity cost, or lacking access to resources needed, get excluded from the pool of potential pilots. You’re then drawing from a smaller group. The NTSB didn’t recommend the 1,500 hour number. In 2010 Senate testimony, then-chair Deborah Hersman explained that the board had investigated accidents involving both high-time and low-time pilots. It found a relationship between failed practical tests and subsequent accidents, but there wasn’t evidence that number of hours actually mattered. The higher minimum took effect in 2013. Fallows’s safety streak begins in February 2009. The first four and a half years of that streak can’t be credited to a requirement that wasn’t yet in effect. Making pilots chase hours can allow relevant skills to deteriorate and bad habits to develop before an airline has to train those habits out of them. Repeated flights in clear weather, making takeoffs and landings at the same familiar airports, aren’t equivalent to structured training for instrument flying in a jet with another pilot, airline procedures, and complex air traffic control instructions. Hours in a hot air balloon count. The hot air balloon can be tethered. European co-pilots don’t face the same restriction, but their safety record is equivalent. Sub-1,500 hour European pilots fly in the United States every day without concern (and American pilots fly in Europe without elevated risk). The argument for the rule is that experience can build judgment, and that the higher threshold was part of a successful improvement in regional airline standards. But that’s an argument for useful experience and rigorous standards. That’s not what we got. An hour spent building time isn’t as valuable as an hour spent in a supervised airline training program. And there’s zero evidence that 1,500 is the right metric. It was chosen because it’s expensive and time-consuming, restricts entry into the pilot profession and drives up wages, and was supported by the major pilots union. The Black Hawk Crew Bears Responsibility The NTSB objected to Fallows implying negligence by the helicopter pilots. But its own probable cause finding identifies the helicopter crew’s failure to maintain effective visual separation as a cause of the collision. The crew accepted responsibility for avoiding the airliner and failed to do so. The existence of systemic failures don’t eliminate pilot error. In Homendy’s testimony explaining the findings, the board concluded that the helicopter’s altimeter probably understated its altitude by about 100 feet. The crew likely believed it was below the route’s ceiling. Fallows also describes the crew flying through civilian traffic without monitoring its traffic avoidance systems. The helicopter had no integrated cockpit traffic display. The pilots had tablets capable of showing traffic, but looking down at them while flying low at night with night vision goggles was problematic. Their audio alerts weren’t integrated into the pilots’ helmets. There were failures of FAA route design, and the FAA failed to act in the face of repeated warnings. But it’s genuinely weird to pin the blame for that on the first nine days of the Trump administration before the crash. I’ve criticized efforts to preserve military exceptions around Washington National. Civilian aviation shouldn’t have to accommodate unnecessary military risks. The Pentagon needs to change, and the current administration is responsible for making that happen. But it didn’t invent the culture that allowed these risks to exist in the first place. The Article’s Own Incident Data Doesn’t Support A Partisan Cause Fallows combines airline accidents, rocket debris, military encounters, airshow incidents and presidential flyovers which generally have different causes. And a list that attributes incidents while a given President is in office to that President doesn’t do a good job of evaluating what decisions caused those incidents. But more simply, even the article’s own figures for the most serious runway incursions are problematic for the Fallows narrative: 2023: 22 incidents. 2024: 7 incidents. 2025: 14 incidents. 2026 through October: 9 incidents. The increase from 2024 to 2025 looks dramatic – a doubling! But it’s still far lower than under Biden in 2023! These are relatively small numbers, and they don’t show a steadily worsening trend. (And there are more aircraft operations than in 2023.) Air Traffic Control Was Broken Before Trump Fallows points to deregulation and falling fares as sources of overcrowding. More people being able to afford to fly is a good thing, and flying is still safer than driving. The failure is an FAA air traffic organization system that hasn’t kept up with demand. The Times has done excellent reporting on this subject. In August 2023, I covered its investigation into 46 close calls in one month and roughly 300 near collisions in a year. Staffing shortages, fatigue, outdated equipment and a culture that tolerated recurring warnings weren’t the result of the 2024 election. Fallows discusses 132 union-represented FAA employees fired during the DOGE purge. Their union announced their reinstatement in March 2025. Firing them was disruptive, but he leaves out the reversal. And recruiting gamers doesn’t establish that the FAA is lowering controller standards. The FAA has been growing the top of the funnel but there’s no indication that unqualified people are being advanced in training to a greater degree. I’ve criticized Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy for counting initial trainee hires as progress, that strikes me as juking the stats but hasn’t been a safety issue. And the Trump administration is doing modernization work that’s valuable and that’s been ignored for decades even if I’m skeptical that it’s close to enough because they don’t seem to be addressing the management problems at the FAA that created failed technology investment and decades-long controller shortages in the first place. I’ve been critical of pouring money into the existing organization. We need to address how air traffic control is managed, how it buys equipment, how it finances long projects and how it’s held accountable for performance. We’d be far better off separating the air traffic control operator from its regulator, with stable funding and professional management, as Canada has done with its nonprofit provider. The Withings app is usually one of our favorites. It allows you to customize the ScanWatch Light’s menus and offers lots of information and data based on your measurements, inputs and watch readings. I’m not a fan of the ScanWatch Light’s charging dock. It’s just an awkwardly long piece of thin, bent plastic with an ostensibly meaningless lip on one side. It doesn’t sit particularly well on my bedside table, looks ugly, and struggles to stay in position. Great.

Look, the Withings ScanWatch Light is overpriced and under-featured for the money. I can admit that. It’s not for athletes or serious fitness enthusiasts. But it isn’t meant for those people. It’s for people like me who want a stylish all-round watch that can track all the basics for general day-to-day fitness and wellbeing without getting too serious. And importantly, for people who don’t want an ugly square hunk of AMOLED display on their wrist, clashing with their outfit.

It’s also built beautifully, has solid battery life and offers lots of visualizations and information via the Withings app. And did I mention it looks fantastic? I know the ScanWatch Light isn’t perfect, but damn, do I love it.

Scientists Warn: The World Has Less Than Four Years of Carbon Budget Left

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Solar power, wind energy, and electric vehicles are reshaping global emissions trends, but a new scientific assessment reveals that the world’s remaining carbon budget is shrinking at an alarming rate.

Global fossil fuel emissions are expected to fall slightly in 2026, but the world’s total carbon dioxide output is still projected to rise. A new assessment by the Global Carbon Project estimates that fossil CO2 emissions will decline by 0.4% to 38.2 billion metric tons (42.1 billion U.S. tons), partly because of expanding clean energy and disruptions linked to the Iran conflict.

However, increasing emissions from deforestation and other changes in land use are expected to push total global CO2 emissions to 43.3 billion metric tons (47.7 billion U.S. tons), up from 42.8 billion metric tons in 2025. The findings show that renewable energy is beginning to reshape global emissions trends, but its growth has not yet produced a sustained worldwide decline.

Oil is expected to account for much of the projected reduction in fossil fuel emissions. Global CO2 emissions from oil are forecast to drop by 1.9%, largely reflecting the energy shock associated with the Iran conflict, which has increased prices and constrained supplies. Natural gas emissions are expected to decrease by 0.3%, while coal emissions could rise by 0.6%. Emissions from international aviation and shipping are each projected to fall by 1%, with reduced activity in the Middle East contributing to the aviation decline.

“A decline in fossil CO2 emissions is encouraging, but a reduction driven partly by geopolitical shocks is not a sustainable climate solution,” said Professor Pierre Friedlingstein, director of the Global Carbon Budget Office at the University of Exeter, who led the study. “The long-term answer remains the rapid deployment of clean energy technologies. While renewables and electric vehicles are increasingly offsetting growth in energy demand, further acceleration is needed if global emissions are to peak and enter a sustained decline.”

Glen Peters, Research Professor at the CICERO Center for International Climate Research, emphasized that temporary reductions should not be confused with lasting progress. “A drop in emissions in one year is not a peak unless it is driven by structural change. Accelerated deployment of solar, wind, batteries, and electric vehicles are pushing emissions down in dozens of countries, and slowing emissions growth in many more, but the changes are not sufficient to lock in a peak in global emissions.”

Solar and wind power, together with electric vehicles, are increasingly limiting emissions despite rising energy demand. According to the assessment, these technologies are meeting or avoiding roughly two-thirds of the increase in global energy demand. Solar and wind generation alone are projected to prevent 4.6 billion metric tons (5.1 billion U.S. tons) of CO2 emissions in 2026, compared with 4.1 billion metric tons in 2025 and just 0.28 billion metric tons in 2010.

China is central to this transformation. Although it produces approximately 32% of global fossil fuel emissions, it also accounts for half of the emissions avoided worldwide through renewable energy and electric vehicles. China’s emissions are projected to decline by 0.9% in 2026, helped by lower oil consumption and continued renewable electricity expansion. Coal emissions, however, are expected to increase slightly by 0.3%. China also leads the world in carbon uptake from reforestation and afforestation (new forests).

“Clean technologies are making a real difference in reducing global emissions. Solar and wind power and electric vehicles are expanding rapidly, led by China. Countries on every continent are decreasing their emissions while growing their economies. A further acceleration could turn the tide on global emissions within a few years,” said Professor Corinne Le Quéré, Royal Society Research Professor at the University of East Anglia’s School of Environmental Sciences.

Emissions are projected to decline by 1.3% in the United States, primarily because coal consumption is falling after increasing in 2025 amid high natural gas prices and cold weather. The European Union is expected to record a 1.5% reduction, while Japan’s emissions could decrease by 1%. India is moving in the opposite direction, with emissions projected to rise by 4.4%, exceeding its average annual growth rate of 3.5% over the past decade. Emissions across the rest of the world are expected to decrease by 0.7%, largely because of reduced oil consumption.

An accompanying analysis, currently in press in a separate peer-reviewed journal, identifies a broader shift in the relationship between economic growth and carbon emissions. In 59 countries, emissions were lower in 2025 than in 2019, before the COVID-19 pandemic, even as their economies expanded. Emissions growth has also slowed in regions where total emissions continue to rise, including China, India, and other emerging economies.

The decline in fossil fuel emissions is being offset by increasing carbon releases from land-use change (such as deforestation). These emissions are projected to reach 5.1 billion metric tons (5.6 billion U.S. tons) in 2026. The increase is linked partly to peat fires and forest degradation in Southeast Asia, where drier conditions associated with El Niño have heightened fire risks. Nearly all peat fires are ignited by human activities.

Natural ecosystems also influence how much CO2 remains in the atmosphere. Forests, soils, and oceans absorb substantial amounts of carbon, acting as natural carbon sinks. The Global Carbon Budget reports that land-based carbon absorption has recovered after weakening sharply during the 2023 and 2024 El Niño event. However, the powerful El Niño developing in 2026 is expected to affect this natural carbon uptake over the coming year.

The latest assessment estimates that only 150 billion metric tons (165 billion U.S. tons) of CO2 can be emitted before cumulative emissions reach the level associated with 1.5°C (2.7°F) of global warming above preindustrial levels. At current emission rates, that remaining carbon budget amounts to less than four years. Meanwhile, atmospheric CO2 concentrations are projected to reach 427.7 parts per million in 2026, approximately 54% above preindustrial levels.

Removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere is another part of the challenge. Permanent deforestation released an average of approximately 3.8 billion metric tons (4.2 billion U.S. tons) of CO2 annually between 2016 and 2025. Reforestation and afforestation offset roughly half of those emissions. China, the European Union, and the United States account for the largest combined removals through forest restoration and expansion, while deforestation outweighs these gains in countries including Brazil, Russia, and Indonesia. Technologies designed to remove atmospheric CO2 remain limited, currently capturing less than the amount released by fossil fuels in just 30 minutes.

“Alongside reducing emissions, we need to scale up CO2 removal to reach greenhouse gas neutrality. Afforestation and reforestation already remove substantial amounts of CO2, but these gains are still more than offset by emissions from deforestation, and novel carbon removal remains very limited. We need to act on all these fronts now,” said Professor Julia Pongratz of Ludwig-Maximilian-University Munich.

Despite continued increases in total emissions, their long-term growth rate has slowed substantially. Global CO2 emissions rose by an average of 0.3% annually over the past decade, compared with 1.7% annually during the preceding decade. The accompanying analysis projects that between now and the end of 2028, additional solar and wind generation and electric vehicles could prevent emissions equivalent to 64% to 79% of those associated with rising energy demand. Electric vehicles are becoming increasingly influential, although only about 10% of the global passenger car fleet was electrified in 2025 (or 6% excluding hybrids).

The researchers conclude that continued deployment of clean technologies could bring the first sustained global decline in CO2 emissions within reach. However, achieving that outcome depends on maintaining and accelerating their expansion rather than relying on temporary reductions caused by economic or geopolitical disruptions. As the authors write, “If further acceleration of clean technology deployment can be supported through time, a sustained peak and decline in global CO2 emissions could be a few years away.”
