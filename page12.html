Kanye West Settles Lawsuit Over Sample Blocked by Descendant of Holocaust Survivors

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A federal judge agreed Thursday to dismiss a copyright infringement lawsuit against Kanye West after the musician reached a settlement with Alice Merton, the German singer-songwriter who accused West of using a sample of her song “Blindside” without permission on his track “Gun to My Head.”

Merton filed the underlying copyright infringement claim last year, saying she would never grant permission to the artist now known as Ye due to his history of “antisemitic, racist remarks.”

In her lawsuit, Merton described herself as a direct descendant of holocaust survivors who refused to clear the sample because she was “unwilling to compromise her personal beliefs.” Merton said she was “understandably shocked and humiliated” when several websites reported in December 2023 that she appeared to be a willing collaborator on Ye’s song, which featured Kid Cudi and Ty Dolla $ign.

Terms of the new settlement were not disclosed in the order dismissing the action signed by U.S. District Court Judge Fernando L. Aenlle-Rocha and obtained by Rolling Stone. In a Nov. 6 court filing, lawyers for both sides said they had agreed to resolve the dispute and expected to seek dismissal of the lawsuit once the settlement terms had been fulfilled. Attempts to reach the lawyers were not immediately successful Friday.

Ye first teased “Gun to My Head” at an early Vultures listening event in December 2023. The song garnered particular attention because it marked Ye and Cudi’s first collaboration in several years.

According to the lawsuit, “Gun to My Head” sampled a segment of “Blindside” that “spans most of the entire song’s baseline melody.” It also featured a vocal recording of the opening lyric line from Merton’s song, “I sat down with a gun to my head,” which became “the dominant lyric line” in Ye’s song, the complaint said.

Merton said Ye had not contacted her to clear the sample before the listening event, and the song did not appear on the first Vultures album released Feb. 8, 2024. But on Feb. 15, 2024, Ye’s clearance company allegedly reached out to clear the sample, and a few weeks later, Merton denied the request, saying Ye’s “values are contrary to our values,” the lawsuit said. Netflix's 8-Part Female John Wick Series Is One Of Its Best Binges

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

It’s rare for any thriller to live up to the John Wick franchise, let alone a small-screen show, but one Netflix series with a single season managed this feat. It is tough to think of an action thriller movie from the last 15 years that was more influential than 2014’s John Wick. In terms of pure plot, the Keanu Reeves vehicle was the simple story of a retired assassin killing every criminal involved in his dog’s death.

However, in visual and aesthetic terms, John Wick spelled the death knell for the shaky cam style that dominated action thrillers in the 2000s and early 2010s thanks to the popularity of director Paul Greengrass’s groundbreaking Bourne sequels. After John Wick’s release, the movie’s sequels popularized a hyper-smooth, slick action filmmaking style that prioritized gliding, unbroken Steadicam shots over fast-cutting.

Soon enough, Atomic Blonde, Bullet Train, Extraction, the Nobody movies, Kate, Gunpowder Milkshake, Pretty Lethal, Violent Night, and almost every other action thriller movie had embraced this CGI-assisted filmmaking style. Few of these efforts could match the John Wick sequels, and even John Wick’s own streaming spinoff The Continental ended up feeling like a knock off rather than a legitimate franchise entry. This is why it is so impressive that Netflix’s under-seen South Korean thriller series My Name recaptured John Wick’s entire appeal perfectly.

As an action thriller about revenge, My Name sounds straightforwardly similar to John Wick in terms of plot. However, the complicated lore of the show’s story makes My Name closer in story terms to the Internal Affairs movies or their Americanized re-imagining, The Departed. Han So-hee’s Yoon Ji-woo is the daughter of a powerful mob member who joins the city's police so she can act as a mole and pass on information to the Dongcheon drug cartel.

Yoon Ji-woo’s boss, the kingpin Choi Mu-jin, encourages her to spy on the police, meaning the show’s heroine soon lands a spot on the very task force that is investigating the cartel. However, once there, she learns from her new detective partner that Choi Mu-jin is suspected to have killed her father. With split loyalties, Yoon Ji-woo has no one to trust and every reason to sharpen her combat skills. While My Name’s plot is as complex as any crime drama like Love/Hate, this show is no slow burn, realistic character study.

Instead, Netflix’s hyper-stylized My Name is a neon-drenched thrill ride, as Yoon Ji-woo battles her way through the city’s criminal underworld while trying to solve the mystery of her father’s sudden death. Gritty, dark, and fast-paced, the action show is a must-watch for fans of revenge thrillers.

Every action show needs to find the right balance between constant fights, chases, and gun battles, but My Name’s tense premise gives the story an incredible head start in this regard. My Name’s twisty plot and the complicated machinations of its heroine’s plan mean that even the show’s quiet dialogue scenes crackle with constant tension.

There is always a risk that Yoon Ji-woo will be exposed as a mole in either organization, which ramps up tension during quiet scenes and makes the action all the more hard hitting. Even compared to dark crime thrillers like This City Is Ours, My Name’s one-season story is a masterclass in tension building.

On that note, the show’s one-season status actually works for, rather than against, My Name as a series on the whole. While it would have been hard to drag My Name’s story out for more than one season, Netflix’s killer John Wick replacement wisely limited its story to just one perfectly paced outing instead.

Rinnai’s new heat pump water heaters get smarter – and easier to install

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Rinnai has launched a new lineup of electric heat pump water heaters that adds wifi controls and more installation options to an appliance that can cut the energy needed for hot showers.

The REHPW series comes in 50-, 65-, and 80-gallon sizes. It builds on Rinnai’s existing REHP lineup, with changes designed to make the units easier to install, monitor, and maintain.

Heat pump water heaters pull heat from the surrounding air and transfer it into the tank. Moving that heat requires less electricity than producing it with conventional electric resistance elements. There’s no fuel combustion at the appliance, although emissions from its electricity supply depend on the grid.

Rinnai lists a Uniform Energy Factor, or UEF, of 3.75 for the 50-gallon model and 4.0 for the two larger versions. Higher numbers mean greater efficiency under standardized testing; actual bills depend on electricity rates, hot water use, and installation conditions.

The lineup delivers up to 91 gallons of hot water in the first hour, depending on tank size. That rating includes both stored hot water and what the unit can heat during that hour – useful context when several people take consecutive showers.

There are five operating modes, 4.5 kW backup heating elements, and a variable-speed fan designed to keep noise down. The heat pump can operate with ambient air as low as 37°F.

Installation is where some of the more practical upgrades show up.

The new Rinnai heat pump water heater units have water connections on both the top and side, giving installers more options for connecting existing plumbing. Both the air intake and exhaust are on top, allowing zero side clearance where Rinnai’s installation requirements permit.

An optional ducting kit adds flexibility for tighter spaces. Carrying handles help with moving the tank into place, and the air filter slides out horizontally from the side for cleaning.

Rinnai has also added a third tank temperature sensor to the 50- and 80-gallon models to help manage hot water delivery.

Wifi adds remote monitoring and control. Rinnai’s updated Central app supports the REHPW series, including scheduling and tools to use time-of-use electricity rates to manage energy use.

The units also carry CTA-2045 AC Level 2 certification for demand response. That gives compatible utility programs a way to communicate with the water heater and adjust its electricity use. Rinnai says the certification can help qualify homeowners for local and state utility demand-response rebates; availability depends on the program.

The series is Energy Star certified, meets NEEA Tier 4 requirements, and complies with California’s Title 24 requirements, including JA13, according to Rinnai. Warranty coverage includes 10 years for the tank and other parts, plus one year for labor.

Rinnai directs buyers to an installer for pricing, so the upfront cost – and how much a local rebate might offset it – will require a quote. However, the REHP series price on Amazon hovers around the $2,000 mark.

Painter Sascha Braunig Pushes Through the Void

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Sascha Braunig’s paintings have a tendency to produce visceral reactions: a pit in the stomach, an eyebrow raise, a need to move closer. This is not unintended.

“I want the viewer to have a bodily experience,” Braunig says.

The paintings are bright, smooth, candy-colored—lighthearted contrasts to the psychologically intense shapes: luminously rendered heads and torsos often bound by wiry tentacles. At times the figures recede into their matching optic backgrounds, as if hiding in plain sight.

Braunig, who is 43 and splits her time between New York and Maine, has been painting with this funky verve for close to two decades. Not long after she got her MFA from Yale’s painting program in 2008, she was having solo exhibitions of her small paintings of heads at the now-defunct Foxy Production in Manhattan. Inclusions at the New Museum Triennial and MoMA PS1 soon followed. (Attention from critics came quickly as well; one called her an “inspired reanimator of Surrealism,” another a “phenom.”)

Braunig is excellent at capturing the inherent tension of living in a body—especially one that is subjected to societal expectations of femininity. She’s playing with the ways women have been contorted and cinched and squeezed for time immemorial, asked to fit into archetypes that seem at turns glamorous or sinister. Often they are both.

“Our aesthetic, our personhood is formed in relation to these cultural forces and imagery,” she says. “You can’t separate yourself out from what you’ve seen throughout your whole life.”

These themes of gender performance and corporeal control permeate “Wrist Painter,” Braunig’s most extensive exhibition to date, which opens November 13 at the Portland Art Museum. The show brings together some 45 works, including paintings, sculptures, works on paper, and sketchbooks. It will be her first solo institutional show in Maine. (A special occasion, as almost all of the pieces in the show were made a block and a half away, in her studio at the Portland arts nonprofit SPACE, over the last 15 years.)

“Sascha really stands out here in this arts ecosystem. She’s doing something really singular,” says Sayantan Mukhopadhyay, a curator at PMA who organized the show. When people think of Maine painters, they usually think of landscapes. But Braunig leans toward the cerebral, with a bit more bite. “The paradoxes are what really excite me about the work,” Mukhopadhyay says.

And the dichotomies are many. “Sascha has a way of presenting an abstraction that can read both soft and strong, feminine and violent,” says Olivia Smith, whose New York City gallery, Magenta Plains, now represents Braunig. “I think that’s what I’ve always loved: that she offers a way for the viewer to re-associate, which ends up telling them more about themselves than about Sascha.”

One of the earliest artworks in “Wrist Painter” is Sequins, from 2010. It’s from Braunig’s head series, which she began in grad school. Her process starts with a drawing, then she brings her sketch to life by making an embellished three-dimensional model. Then she’ll light the model and paint what she observes. She applies her lustrous oil paints with such precision that these strange bodily shapes start to look “real.”

She continued this system as she moved on to her bodies series a few years later, using clay resin, wire, and paper to make her maquettes. This haptic process is why works like Warm Leatherette, a 2015 painting at PMA that looks like a snatched Michelin Man encased in chartreuse padding, seem so tactile. (She has often described her choice of colors as “seasonal” or “dietary”—she finds a palette that works for one show and then cycles to a new one for the next.)

Her maquette process also lends a certain theatricality. She is literally setting up a scene, as if the entire idea of femininity were on a velvet-curtained stage, the whole specter aglow under klieg lights. What is gender if not a performance?

Braunig, who grew up in British Columbia, Canada, liked drawing and painting from a young age. Her father (a dentist) and mother (a nurse) enjoyed creative pursuits outside of work, and the house was full of art books. She loved watching Pee-wee’s Playhouse, which she credits as being just as formative, even more so, than going to museums.

Her parents encouraged her to attend an intensive summer art program at Skidmore College in upstate New York when she was 16. It was there that she first learned about Cooper Union, the New York City school where artists Lee Krasner and Wangechi Mutu got their start. Braunig decided it was the place for her.

New York City was the perfect place for the budding artist. “I was totally in love. It maybe ruined me forever,” she jokes now. She went to Yale right after Cooper, and then she moved to Maine in 2010, in her mid-20s. “I was really fortunate to have a New York gallery as I left the city, because I was able to have shows and have my work represented here even though I wasn’t here.”

Living somewhere far less expensive was also a game changer—she didn’t need to pile on jobs to make ends meet, and could instead focus on building her deeply personal style. “I had been exposed to so many influences coming to New York. And maybe those first five years in Maine were a time when I was able to process them alone.”

Braunig knows her art history, and she gets why people connect her style back to Surrealism. But her artistic comrades are more like French self-portraitist Claude Cahun or even Frida Kahlo, she says, “who’s really making work that is influenced by events of her own life.” Her art might feel dreamy (or perhaps like an acid trip), but “it’s not a dream space. I really am thinking about actual forces in the actual world.”

For many years Braunig used her work to illustrate broader ideas around culture and gender, but her most recent output has taken a turn toward the autobiographical. A few years ago she went through a creative block that left her stranded from her art practice, unable to make anything. As she emerged from this period last year, she started making her most personal paintings yet.

That work went into her radiant show “Cauldron” at Magenta Plains this past spring, its title a reference to the messy, transformative stew of midlife. Its anchor painting was My Death (2025), which, along with several others from the show, will travel to Portland for “Wrist Painter.”

Sascha Braunig, My Death, 2025, oil on linen over panel.

She calls My Death an “explicit self-portrait” about her own reanimation—an emergence through the void of creative block. It’s juicy and haunting and beautiful. She employed those tentacles again, but this time they are like a life force attached to a sleeping Braunig. A mysterious figure made from strips of vermilion orange pulls on them like marionette strings, bringing the artist back to life.

“I’ve come to understand that painting is a survival practice,” she says. “That can be a framing practice where you’re using it to talk about ideas in the world, or it can be a practice where you’re directly processing your own experiences. Maybe those two things aren’t that different.”

For the PMA show, Braunig has made one brand-new work, titled Torsion. At 78 inches tall by 60 inches wide, it is the largest painting of her career. She previously used the title for a museum show in Norway, but realized she’d never given it to an individual painting. The term, in physics, refers to the twisting of an object around an axis: a towel wrung out to dry.

This new Torsion is a painting of a fleshy pink figure with a tiny, twisted torso standing on tiptoes within a gray box. The figure’s muscular back is to the viewer, its knees fanning out in a plié. Dramatic tentacles in orange and yellow trace its exaggerated outline.

The figure’s head is cut off by the box’s ceiling, and its arms stop around the elbows, where they encounter the side walls. Maybe the figure’s been blocked, held back by the confines of this shadowy encasement. Or maybe, somehow, it has broken free.

“Sascha Braunig: Wrist Painter” is on view at the Portland Art Museum in Maine from November 13, 2026, through March 14, 2027.

NiCE’s next-generation Cognigy bets the hard part of AI agents is running them, not building them

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

GUEST COLUMN by Zeus Kerravala

The contact center has been the low-hanging fruit for the artificial intelligence industry. Ask any information technology or business leader what they want to use agentic AI for, and they will say it’s to improve the customer experience, which starts with the contact center.

However, to date, the industry has spent the past two years arguing over which AI agent is the smartest. That’s the wrong debate. Model capabilities are improving so quickly and are getting so much cheaper that raw intelligence and reasoning are becoming the least differentiated part of the stack.

The real challenge for enterprises is operating a fleet of AI agents reliably across complete customer journeys, with the visibility and control a large business requires. Without that, the human, or worse, the customer, is left connecting the dots among the outputs of the growing number of agents they are communicating with.

That’s the thesis behind the next generation of NiCE Cognigy, which NiCE Ltd. launched this week. The release includes a redesigned Agentic Experience Studio, an AI Assist companion spanning the building, testing and improvement of agents, reusable “skills” that package instructions and tools, turn-by-turn tracing and native voice-to-voice model support.

Default speech-to-text, text-to-speech and large language models are now included in the per-conversation price, with premium models from Deepgram, ElevenLabs and Anthropic available with a few clicks and billed by usage.

Ahead of the launch, I attended an analyst briefing with Nhu Ho, senior product marketing manager at NiCE Cognigy, and with Clement Boutier, director of product management, who leads the team that built the release and owns the platform roadmap.

Here are the important points from this release:

Ho opened with a chart showing how long AI can work on its own before a human has to step in. In 2020, it was seconds; in 2024, it was minutes; and this year it’s roughly 16 hours. The footnote was interesting, as it calculated that figure at a 50% success rate. “Capable and dependable do not always mean the same thing,” Ho said. “That is exactly where the platform matters.”

For CX leaders, that’s a solid benchmark to consider. A 50% success rate is a research milestone. In a contact center, it’s a churn event or a compliance violation. As models commoditize, value shifts to the system around them: testing, tracing, guardrails and deterministic fallbacks. NiCE doesn’t build frontier models, and frontier model builders don’t run contact centers. Positioning Cognigy as the layer that makes AI dependable is a smart place to plant a flag.

Cognigy earned its pre-acquisition leadership position by handling complex production deployments for large brands such as Lufthansa, Mercedes and DHL. But that flexibility and customizability added complexity. Boutier put it this way: “The large number of options when configuring an agent creates friction to get started, which slows things down.” The new studio lets a builder stand up an agent in a few clicks, with models already provisioned, while power-user tools, including full access to the agent’s system prompt, are available in advanced sections.

This is important because NiCE aims to create a “1+1=3” value proposition for its current customers. NiCE needs Cognigy to expand broadly across the CXone installed base, not just among expert teams at the largest enterprises. Boutier described three builder personas: smaller businesses building simple agents, contact center administrators and team leads, and engineers at technically sophisticated companies. AI Assist is aimed squarely at the first two.

In the demo, it built an agent, tested it, fixed the problems it found, and deployed it to WhatsApp. The flip side is that lowering the barrier means more agents built by less experienced people, which makes the governance features the real story of this release.

Despite all the agentic hype, most enterprises aren’t ready to hand over entire journeys to an autonomous agent. Boutier outlined three modes: traditional natural language understanding, hybrid and fully agentic. Hybrid “is actually where most of our customers are,” he said, while fully agentic deployments are at the “beginning of the adoption curve.” This approach lets customers ease into agentic, build confidence and move to fully autonomous at their own pace.

NiCE designed the product for that approach. An agent can serve as the orchestration layer, calling existing flows as tools. Flows can also be placed in front of agents, for example, to scrub credit card numbers or other personal data before any data reaches a model. Ho described the release as “an evolution, not a replacement,” with production deployments, application programming interfaces and integrations continuing to run unchanged.

That’s a meaningful advantage over AI-native challengers whose pitch effectively requires a rebuild. Contact centers are the fastest-moving environments, and deterministic workflows aren’t legacy baggage. They’re how a business enforces what a large language model shouldn’t be deciding.

“You can have the best builder experience, but if you don’t have any feedback loop on what you’re building, you’re never going to build a great agent,” Boutier said. The new tracing shows each turn: which skill loaded, which tool was called and with what arguments, and when the agent handed off to a flow.

The Simulator now supports multivariate testing of up to 10 variants, using an LLM-as-judge to run synthetic customers against an agent. Boutier ran the same agent on two models and found that one was faster and consumed fewer tokens. This shifts model selection from a preference to a data-backed economic decision.

Voice-to-voice support may be the first feature customers deploy. Boutier noted that buyers are open to using a speech-to-text and text-to-speech cascade, “but what they realize is that voice-to-voice models sound just better.” In the demo, the agent delegated tool calls to a separate model while continuing to talk, making the conversation sound far more natural.

Cognigy now serves as both a Model Context Protocol client and server and supports agent-to-agent connections, including one customer linking Cognigy to agents built with Microsoft’s tooling. NiCE is also co-developing the Personal Agent Protocol with Meta and Sierra, which isn’t live yet. Boutier described Cognigy as “the front door” of the contact center, where a consumer’s personal agent must authenticate before it can proceed. It’s great to see NiCE collaborating with Sierra, a direct competitor, on this, as it makes things easier for a customer.

The industry views agent identity as a shared responsibility. But as Sierra outlines, companies can also let personal agents work through their websites and APIs, so the front-door position depends on enterprises routing that traffic through Cognigy. This lets brands interact with personal agents safely.

There has been a wave of companies banning personal agents, which I think is a mistake. If something is good for a consumer, it will happen, so it’s better to be on the leading edge of enabling it rather than fighting it.

This release is less about new capabilities and more about NiCE betting on where value will accrue as AI agents mature. Intelligence is getting cheaper. Dependability, governance and the ability to blend autonomous reasoning with deterministic control are not.

NiCE is the market leader in contact center as a service, as Cognigy was in conversational AI. Now the challenge is to get an easier-to-work-with Cognigy into the hands of thousands of CXone customers without creating a sprawl of individually governed agents. This will allow NiCE to maintain its industry leadership as personal agents arrive. As it does that, the Cognigy acquisition will move from an add-on to a core component of the NiCE platform.

Starship flight 15 powers to orbit—setting up a possible catch on Saturday

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

One way or another, the company will get some more data on Saturday.

STARBASE, Texas—Starship lifted off right on schedule Friday from South Texas, making its 15th test flight in more than three years.

The largely successful launch took place in the early evening; as the massive rocket rose into the atmosphere, the Sun sank toward the western horizon. Conditions were ideal for the launch, with the atmospheric winds so calm the rocket’s white plume did not dissipate for many minutes. The weather in South Texas is not always comfortable; summers are long and hot. Friday evening was perfect at Starbase.

The first stage ascended toward space flawlessly, releasing the upper stage above the Gulf of Mexico. From there, the Starship upper stage ignited its Raptor engines and powered its way into a nominal orbit. This was the second time SpaceX has flown Starship into low-Earth orbit.

When it came time for the first stage to return to the ground, it dropped like a rock. However, due to an unspecified issue, the vehicle was commanded to divert into the Gulf rather than return to the Starbase facility to be caught by the launch tower. It created a fantastically powerful explosion, even for observers more than 10 miles away.

The primary aim of Friday’s launch was the deployment of 26 Starlink V3 satellites from a narrow door at the base of the spacecraft. This occurred about 40 minutes into Starship’s flight, and all 26 satellites—which have solar panels that stretch 60 meters end to end when fully deployed—safely made it into orbit, where they will now use onboard propulsion to raise their altitude.

This means that Starship is now an operational, orbital rocket. It is the largest and most powerful machine humans have ever launched into space. The challenge for the company now is to make Starship reusable and dramatically increase its flight rate.

In that sense, the first part of Friday’s mission was not a success. SpaceX had hoped to return the first-stage booster, refurbish it, and fly it again. The company has previously landed and reflown Starship boosters but has yet to do so with the latest iteration of the vehicle, version 3.

However, SpaceX still has a chance to make history on Saturday with the potential return of the Starship upper stage. SpaceX has never attempted to catch the vehicle’s upper stage.

Doing so will require Starship to pass a series of tests in orbit and subsequently during its return to Earth to ensure it is fully healthy. This is critical because Starship will be passing over parts of Mexico and the United States before returning to Starbase, and SpaceX does not want to endanger people on the ground. Additionally, it does not want to risk damage to its launch towers.

If Starship passes these health checks, it would land at about 3:15 pm local time in Texas (21:15 UTC). If it does not, the company has earlier options to de-orbit the vehicle into an ocean.

Successfully returning Starship’s upper stage would be historic, as well as significant for SpaceX’s efforts to reuse the massive rocket. It would also give the company valuable information about the vehicle’s performance during reentry.

One of the biggest hurdles left in Starship’s development is the vehicle’s heat shield, which must protect the upper stage during its return through Earth’s atmosphere. More than that, SpaceX aims to make Starship rapidly reusable, and to do this, Starship’s tiles must be light and resilient.

SpaceX has been experimenting with tiles since the beginning of the Starship program, trying to find the right combination of low mass, heat resistance, and durability so the tiles require minimal refurbishment between launches. For this flight, SpaceX said it is applying learnings from the previous test flight in September, including additional retention mechanisms on tiles in areas deemed to be at the highest risk of falling off during ascent and addressing areas with a flow path for plasma to get behind the tiles.

Another problem on recent flights has been heat incursion into the small gaps between the edges of the tiles. According to SpaceX, this Starship has a large portion of the heat shield equipped with a curved tile design that has shown the ability to reduce heating in the gaps between tiles.
