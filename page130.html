Matt Damon Reveals the One Strict Rule He Makes His Kids Follow on Movie Nights at Home

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Movie star Matt Damon has revealed he doesn't fool around when it comes time for his family to watch movies at their sprawling property.

While speaking to People, the actor, 56, opened up about their at-home rules he ensures his wife, Luciana Damon, and their daughters Alexia, Isabella, Gia, and Stella follow.

“I tell my kids, it’s the same rules as when we go to the theater: no phones, no talking. I try to create the movie experience as much as I can in my house. That’s not easy to do. ... In our living room, behind us is a dining room and a kitchen. It’s an open floor plan. So there’s a lot of light. So it’s like, you get all the lights off, and you get it dark," he told the outlet.

The Hollywood icon revealed his kids adore films just as much as he does.

"This generation is really coming back to theaters, which is wonderful," he said. "So I just try to instill in them that it can be like that at home, too. If you give it that same kind of reverence, it can be just as fun.

"Because being on this side of it and knowing what it takes to make every shot and how much love and care and work is put into every single shot that you’re seeing, you want to give the director and the team your complete attention as much as you can."

He also opened up about how fans should stream his hit film "The Odyssey," telling moviegoers that they should re-create the experience at home.

Damon's co-star John Leguizamo told the outlet, “You want to immerse yourself because it’s a community experience you hope to have at home as well as going to the theater. You want to see people’s reactions, and you want them to feel the things you felt.”

Leguizamo admitted that he and his wife, Justine Maurer, sobbed during their first viewing of the film, adding, "You live vicariously through her experience, and it means something different.”

Meanwhile, producer Emma Thomas told the outlet about how she and her husband, writer-director Christopher Nolan, transformed their garage into a home movie room.

“It’s actually also where Chris and our editor edit the film. We have a very large collection of physical media, and we watch films that way a great deal. ... It is nice because you can have a cup of tea and the dog can be sitting at your feet," Thomas told People.

“I’m a big fan of darkening a room and getting a projector up on a wall. You know, I might try to twist their arms into doing that. I think they’ll probably watch the behind-the-scenes [features] to see if they made it in," Nolan added.

Israel launches new ground operation in Gaza

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Israel has said its military is targeting Hamas infrastructure in central Gaza and urged civilians to avoid the area. The fight is unfolding against the backdrop of a dire food and shelter situation for Palestinians.

Israel says its military is involved in operations on Wednesday in central Gaza in an effort to dislodge Hamas militants.

France's AFP news agency reported "intense Israeli artillery shelling amid ongoing gunfire and airstrikes."

A statement from the Israeli military (IDF) said, "The IDF launched a precise operation within Gaza earlier today west of the Yellow Line to remove a threat posed to IDF troops operating in the Gaza Strip."

The Yellow Line in Gaza was established in an October 2025 ceasefire between Israel and Hamas, and separates Israeli and Hamas-controlled territory.

On Wednesday, COGAT, an Israeli military body responsible for civilian matters in the Palestinian territories warned international aid agencies and Palestinian civilians to avoid areas near the Al-Bureij and Nuseirat refugee camps and Gaza's main thoroughfare, the Salah al-Din road.

Palestinian medics told Reuters news agency that one person had been killed in an Israeli airstrike in central Gaza and that two more had been killed in separate incidents unfolding in Deir al-Balah and Gaza City.

Hamas — which is listed as a terrorist organization by Israel, the US, Germany, the EU and others — blasted the Israeli operation, calling it "a flagrant violation of the ceasefire agreement and confirmation that the occupation has reneged on its obligations."

Israel's military countered that it was reacting to Hamas ceasefire violations and the group's attempts to rearm.

The October 2025 ceasefire halted major operations, but both sides have continued to carry out limited attacks. Gaza health authorities report at least 1,600 Palestinian deaths and Israel reports the loss of fewer than 10 soldiers since the ceasefire went into effect.

In all, Palestinian authorities say more than 74,000 people have been killed inside Gaza during Israel's retaliation for Hamas' October 7, 2023, assault — which killed roughly 1,200 Israelis, most of whom were civilians.

Beyond the threat of violent gunfire and airstrikes, civilians in Gaza continue to face ongoing food insecurity and lack of adequate shelter as winter approaches.

On Tuesday, the International Organization for Migration (IOM) and the UN's World Food Programme (WFP) warned that funding shortfalls and access restrictions put untold numbers of Palestinian lives at risk.

Currently, more than 1.7 million Gazans are displaced, with IOM and WFP saying 1.4 million face acute hunger and 1.98 million require shelter assistance.

With Gaza's infrastructure decimated by years of Hamas mismanagement and pounding Israeli attacks, more than 80% of Palestinians are unemployed, meaning they cannot afford food and have been forced to burn garbage to try to keep warm.

"Families in Gaza are facing their first sleepless nights of a very long winter ahead," said WFP Palestine Country Director Shaun Hughes. "Unless we get immediate support to scale up the humanitarian response, we risk this becoming not only a shelter crisis but also a deeper food security crisis."

MARA prices $185M debt offering after $1.1B Bitcoin sale

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Bitcoin miner is taking on new debt to finance its AI infrastructure expansion, with the Long Ridge acquisition adding nearly $69 million in annual interest costs.

Bitcoin miner MARA Holdings has priced a $185 million secured debt offering through its US subsidiary to help finance its $1.5 billion acquisition of Long Ridge Energy & Power.

According to a Tuesday filing with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, the new notes carry an 8.75% interest rate and mature in 2032. They will be added to $600 million in existing Long Ridge notes carrying the same rate, bringing the combined principal to $785 million and annual interest obligations to around $68.7 million.

The offering is expected to close on Nov. 27, alongside MARA’s acquisition of Long Ridge, which operates a 485-megawatt natural gas power plant in Ohio. MARA announced the $1.5 billion acquisition on April 30 as part of its expansion into AI and high-performance computing infrastructure. The Long Ridge property includes land that MARA says could support more than 1 gigawatt of data center capacity, although the company has yet to announce a major AI tenant for the proposed development.

The financing follows MARA’s Nov. 9 disclosure that it sold 13,597 Bitcoin after quarter-end for approximately $1.1 billion. It used $750 million to repay Bitcoin (BTC)-backed loans, releasing 17,477 BTC previously pledged as collateral.

MARA has sold billions of dollars in Bitcoin this year to reduce its debt. In March, the company sold 15,133 BTC for approximately $1.1 billion, using the proceeds to help repurchase $1 billion in convertible notes at a roughly 9% discount, saving approximately $88 million before transaction costs.

In its Nov. 9 shareholder letter, MARA said it had reduced debt by approximately $1.4 billion, or 37%, since the beginning of the year. However, the Long Ridge acquisition will bring additional debt onto its consolidated balance sheet.

According to MARA’s April 30 acquisition announcement, Long Ridge generated approximately $144 million in annualized adjusted EBITDA, extrapolated from the second half of 2025. The roughly $68.7 million in annual interest payments on the secured notes would equal nearly half that figure.

MARA also faces potential debt repayments next year. According to its third-quarter 10-Q, holders of approximately $924 million in convertible notes have the right to demand repayment in 2027, potentially creating another cash requirement as the company finances its AI expansion.

The future of tech will not be inclusive by default

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ten years ago, QueerTech began with a feeling that too many 2SLGBTQI+ people in the technology sector know well: not belonging.

My co-founder Andy Saldaña and I were working at different companies in tech and kept running into the same frustration. We could see the possibility of the sector around us, had the skills to succeed, and believed technology, when stewarded responsibly, could change lives. Yet we could not see a place for ourselves, as queer professionals, within it.

I grew up in Morocco, where being queer was, and continues to be, illegal. In 2007, I left for New York City in pursuit of the "American Dream." There, I quickly realized this dream was based on a contradiction — opportunity without equal rights. Finally, I moved to Montreal, drawn to Canada’s reputation for respecting people across cultures, religions, and identities.

Across this journey, I expected geography to change my sense of belonging. It never did. So in 2016, Andy and I started small. QueerTech was a volunteer-run community group built around a simple need: to find one another, create connections, and make space for queer people in an industry where many of us felt invisible.

Today, QueerTech is a national nonprofit working across workforce development, entrepreneurship, research, responsible technology, and community. Our scale has changed, but the need has not. What has evolved is our understanding of what meaningful inclusion requires: not only creating spaces where queer people belong, but breaking down the systemic barriers that determine who gets access to opportunities, who advances into leadership, and who has the power to shape the future of technology.

The scope of our mission, spanning inclusive technology and professional wellbeing, demands ambitious goals and innovative programming grounded in collaboration and practical impact. Our community-first approach supports 2SLGBTQI+ professionals from industry entry through career advancement and entrepreneurship, while driving systemic change through partnerships with industry, investors, regulators, and community organizations.

QueerTech’s goals for the Canadian tech ecosystem are:

Your Favorite Coffee Shop Is Probably Streaming Music Illegally. It’s the Music Industry’s Fault, Too (Guest Column)

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A new study found 81% of U.S. businesses don't properly license music, costing creators and other rightsholders. The fix starts with education — not lawsuits.

Walk into almost any shop, gym, salon or restaurant in America, and you’ll hear music. Ask the owner whether it’s licensed and you will likely get a confident “yes.” But in most cases, that answer is, unfortunately, wrong.

Soundtrack recently surveyed 1,525 decision-makers responsible for nearly 15,000 business locations across 10 customer-facing industries to find out how U.S. businesses actually play music. Though the law is clear that businesses need specific licenses to play music in public, the results of the survey describe a market that loves music, depends on it, and, overwhelmingly, does not properly pay for it.

Ninety-seven percent of the businesses we surveyed play music for customers, and 77% do it every day. Adjusted for the size of the U.S., that means roughly 3.8 million U.S. business locations play music in their stores.

Nine in 10 of those surveyed said music boosts employee motivation and shapes the energy of their space, while more than eight in 10 called it an essential part of the business and said it has to fit their brand. Rather than merely being a background expense, businesses see music as part of the product.

Yet despite this overwhelming demand, the way music played in businesses is paid for has not evolved. A striking 79% of businesses illicitly use a consumer streaming service, such as a personal Spotify, Pandora or YouTube account, to play music for customers. Only 28% of businesses use a service built and licensed for business. This is one of the biggest failures, and also one of the biggest opportunities, for the music industry today.

Aside from the scale of misuse, it is significant how many businesses are sure they’re doing it right. Seventy-eight percent of respondents were confident the music in their business is legally compliant. Eighty-three percent said they’re familiar with the rules. Yet 81% are misusing music, and 58% believe it’s legal to stream a personal account in their business.

Even I struggle to distill the U.S. licensing rules into just a few sentences; such is the unnecessary complexity surrounding them. Put simply, businesses require both a music service licensed for commercial use and licenses with all four U.S. performing rights organizations (PROs) — ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR — that cover the music they play.

Some licensed music services bundle performance rights, while others require businesses to secure separate public performance licenses from U.S. PROs. For a business owner, working out exactly what’s included can be a job in itself.

Too often I speak to business owners who believe they’re fully licensed but are missing key PRO licenses or still playing music via their personal streaming account. The survey reflects this, finding only 5% of businesses hold licenses from all four U.S. PROs.

However, this isn’t a story about bad actors. Nine in 10 businesses said compliance matters to them, and most said it matters that artists are paid fairly. The businesses using personal streaming accounts are doing it because it’s what they know. It’s easy, familiar and often free. It’s not a problem of intent; it’s a lack of information and a non-user-friendly system.

The cost to music creators and other rightsholders is real. Combining what businesses told us they spend on music with the number of establishments recorded by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, we estimate that annual spending on music would rise by about $1.4 billion per year if every business was properly licensed through a compliant business music service. That money should flow to songwriters, artists, publishers, labels and the services that license them legally.

The music industry shares the blame. No one proactively educates the market or gives a business owner a clear answer about what they need to be fully covered. Consumer platforms offer free tiers, teaching a generation of shop owners that music costs nothing. And business music services, mine included, have not explained the difference loudly or simply enough.

Enforcement alone won’t close a gap this wide. You can’t sue three million locations into compliance, and you shouldn’t want to. These are the customers who say music is essential to them. The answer is a coordinated push across trade organisations, PROs, publishers, labels, consumer platforms and business services to create one plain-language message about what is legal, provide simpler routes to full licensing and build services businesses actually want to use.

That last part matters. Businesses told us what they look for in streaming services designed for businesses: ease-of-use, price and good playlists (the main complaint about business music services is that their music selections are limited and repetitive). The music offered by these services has to be at least as good as the consumer app on the owner’s smartphone — or the consumer app will keep winning.

Streaming rebuilt the recorded music industry by making the legal option easier for individuals than piracy. Now, the same needs to happen for businesses. The demand is already there, and most want to do it right. What’s missing is clarity, and a legal option that’s simple to use and offers better selections.

That $1.4 billion isn’t lost. It’s waiting for the industry to make doing the right thing the easy thing.

Ola Sars is the co-founder and CEO of Soundtrack, the music streaming service for businesses. The study was conducted by Soundtrack and the NMPA in 2025, surveying 1,525 U.S. music decision-makers via Sago’s opt-in B2B panel.
