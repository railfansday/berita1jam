How long will your NVIDIA GPU be supported?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NVIDIA can support GeForce GPUs for years, but Game Ready updates, security patches and OS support don't all end at once.

Graphics cards do not have an expiration date on the box. That would make it easier to upgrade, but much more depressing.

NVIDIA also does not guarantee a specific number of years of driver support for all GeForce GPUs. Rather, support is largely dependent on the architecture your card uses. At time of writing, Turing, Ampere, Ada and Blackwell GPUs are still on the normal Game Ready Driver track, which includes new features, game optimizations, performance improvements and bug fixes. That means that even though the RTX 2060 was released in 2019, it continues to receive regular driver updates.

Older cards have started moving down the ladder. In October 2025, Maxwell, Pascal and Volta GPUs stopped receiving Game Ready updates, but NVIDIA will continue to offer critical security updates until October 2028. Pascal and Volta had nine years of Game Ready support, whereas Maxwell lasted for 11 years.

That's a pretty respectable run, but what comes next and how could it impact your system?

NVIDIA does have a neatly defined three-phase software support lifecycle. Unfortunately, it's about NVIDIA vGPU software; not about the GeForce card in your gaming PC.

NVIDIA vGPU is a virtualization solution, mostly for enterprise use (data centers and professional deployments), that requires an active software or support subscription to receive software support. Phase I, also known as Full Support, includes new features, performance enhancements, security patches and bug fixes. Phase II is the next three years of Extended Full Support, with the same coverage, and Phase III is the final three years of Maintenance Support, where the GPU remains on its last supported software branch and receives maintenance, security fixes and bug fixes as NVIDIA sees fit.

GeForce support is far less ceremonial. NVIDIA has moved older architectures off its primary Game Ready branch, but doesn't publish a single lifespan for all generations.

That can also make model names confusing. The GTX 750 Ti is a Maxwell card and will continue to receive security updates until October 2028, whereas the more powerful GTX 780 Ti is based on the older Kepler architecture, whose published security-update window ended in September 2024. A good rule of thumb: bigger number equals older architecture. GPU naming is weird that way.

The end of Game Ready support sounds a lot more dramatic than it normally is.

Your graphics card does not suddenly refuse to boot up Windows or stop running all of the games you already have installed. Existing drivers continue to work. What goes away is the constant flow of optimizations for new games, feature additions, performance tweaks and regular bug fixes, which can become more noticeable as newer software expects newer drivers.

Security-only support is still meaningful though. NVIDIA has already issued dedicated Maxwell, Pascal and Volta security drivers during 2026, so these cards haven't simply been abandoned.

Another clock is ticking too: your operating system. The last full Game Ready and Studio driver for Windows 10 will be available in October 2026, according to NVIDIA. Windows 10 systems will get quarterly security updates and critical bug fixes until October 2029. So even a newer RTX GPU might lose the normal Game Ready update stream due to Windows and not the hardware.

For an RTX 2060 owner, driver support alone is not much of a reason to start looking for a new GPU. Performance and VRAM limits or a lack of access to newer features may become bigger issues first. NVIDIA hasn't confirmed a new GeForce generation for 2027 either, despite reports previously pointing to late-2027 mass production for its next architecture.

China’s electric airliner era starts with a 19-seat plane and 400 km of range

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

COMAC’s CE-25A, China’s 19-seat battery-electric commuter plane, flew with a crew for the first time on Saturday in Binzhou, Shandong. Two pilots and a flight test engineer were aboard the roughly 10-tonne demonstrator, and COMAC says every system worked normally.

It wasn’t the plane’s first flight. The CE-25A had already completed 19 autonomous test flights with nobody on board since February 2, and China’s aviation regulator is now reviewing it for a type certificate.

The CE-25A is a T-tail commuter plane with 10 electric ducted fans on the wings, five per side, instead of propellers. COMAC lists a 300 km/h cruise speed and a 400 km (250-mile) range.

The CAAC accepting COMAC’s type certificate application starts the airworthiness review. It doesn’t mean paying passengers are close, and COMAC hasn’t given a date for commercial service.

The program has been quiet for a long time. COMAC showed a CE-25A model at the China International Import Expo in Shanghai in November 2023, with a placard listing 400 to 500 km of range and a 400 km/h top cruise speed. The full-size aircraft, registered B-0EFH, only became public when photos leaked on Chinese social media on October 28.

The obvious battery candidate is CATL. In July 2023, COMAC, CATL, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University formed a joint venture with CNY 600 million in registered capital to develop electric aircraft, as we reported when CATL launched its aviation unit.

CATL had just unveiled its “condensed” battery at the time, claiming up to 500 Wh/kg at the cell level, and said it was testing the cells to aviation standards. Neither company has confirmed what’s inside the CE-25A, though.

~500 Wh/kg has long been seen as the target for battery energy density to enable electric flight on a commercial scale.

COMAC isn’t alone in flying a large electric demonstrator. In August, Heart Aerospace flew its X1 in Plattsburgh, New York, for 27 minutes. It’s a 25,000-lb aircraft with a 106-foot wingspan, which Heart calls the largest battery-electric aircraft ever flown.

Eviation flew its 9-passenger Alice back in September 2022, then laid off most of its staff in early 2025.

Heart is the closest Western comparison. It dropped its 19-seat ES-19 for the 30-seat ES-30 back in 2022, and the ES-30 now relies on reserve turbogenerators to stretch its range, with BAE Systems working on the battery. Heart is targeting 2031 for service and claims “more than 40%” lower operating costs.

For years, the go-to excuse in the West for doing nothing about emissions was China. Why should we bother, the argument went, if China won’t? Now China is pulling ahead, and it’s doing it by cutting its dependence on fossil fuels.

Look at trucking. Electric trucks hit 50.3% of China’s heavy truck sales in September, and they’re already eating into the country’s diesel demand. In the US, electric models were about 0.3% of heavy-truck sales last year, according to the ICCT.

Now it’s planes, starting where batteries make sense today: short regional routes. The CE-25A’s 400 km shrinks once you keep the reserves regulators require, and a Beechcraft 1900 turboprop in the same class covers well over 1,000 km. So it’s not replacing turboprops anytime soon. But that’s how trucking started too. Short-haul first, then long-haul tractors once the batteries caught up.

And this couldn’t happen fast enough. We’re in the middle of a diesel and jet fuel crisis. Jet fuel averaged $3.50 a gallon in early August, up 63% from a year earlier, according to Heart Aerospace. Every truck and plane China moves to batteries is one less exposed to that.

China also has the batteries to pull it off, with CATL in the joint venture. Meanwhile, Heart has pushed to 2031 and added a fuel-burning backup, and Eviation is a cautionary tale.

Robots now run AI-written code without an AI calling the shots, succeeding 92% of the time

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Robots completed 221 of 240 trials using Claude-written programs for tasks such as uncapping bottles. Human help was available during development, not execution.

Robot programs developed by Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5.5 coding agent completed 221 of 240 physical evaluation trials, ACE-CaP-0 researchers reported in a study posted November 12. The result shows that task-specific skills can persist in code and run without the coding agent choosing each action.

The researchers behind ACE-CaP-0, a method for developing robot control programs, reported a 92.1% success rate across eight tasks. Each program was frozen before 30 separate evaluation trials, with no further updates. Human guidance and demonstrations were available during development.

The experiment separates program development from repeated execution. The agent's work produced a program that could be used repeatedly. During evaluation, that program still used perception and control systems to respond to observations, while its decision rules stayed fixed. Neither a human nor the coding agent remained in the execution loop.

During development, the coding agent wrote, ran, inspected and revised its code using physical feedback. It could ask for textual advice or demonstrations in which a person physically guided the robot arms. Those demonstrations supplied video, arm trajectories and key joint poses.

Development allowed at most ten human-assisted scene resets per task. Resets used to collect demonstrations were excluded, and the budget counted scene resets rather than all robot executions or demonstrations.

The agent could also use simulated experiments when they helped resolve a development question. In a charger-insertion comparison, on-demand simulation produced a program with 100% evaluation success, versus 86.7% for development without simulation. The comparison used one development run per condition.

That comparison also illustrates a cost trade-off. Development took 2.6 hours with on-demand simulation versus 3.7 hours without it, while output-token usage increased from 730,000 to 1,090,000. The time and token figures leave total development costs unresolved; the researchers call for accounting that includes demonstration effort.

Repeating evaluation trials measures how the resulting program performs. Establishing how consistently the development process produces an equally capable program requires separate development runs across tasks.

ACE-CaP-0 leaves behind task-specific programs that can execute repeatedly with fresh observations and fixed decision rules. The study provides evidence for that form of skill reuse after human-assisted development. Testing those routines under new conditions is the next step toward establishing how broadly they can be reused.

Polish activists face opposition as they make abortion more accessible

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Abortion laws in Poland are very restrictive. Activists there have found a new way to help women wanting to terminate a pregnancy: the "Abortomat," a locker that dispenses abortion pills. But they face fierce opposition.

Women's rights activists in Poland have opened what they say is the world's first delivery locker supplying abortion pills in a country with one of Europe's most restrictive abortion laws.

Known as the "Abortomat," the locker is in a building opposite the Polish parliament, in what activists say is a clear message to politicians like Donald Tusk. The Polish prime minister pledged to introduce abortion on demand up to the 12th week of pregnancy before the 2023 parliamentary election, but his coalition has been unable to agree on a common position.

Only two European Union member states, Poland and Malta, do not allow abortion on request.

In Poland, abortion is only allowed when the pregnancy is the result of a crime — rape or incest — or when it endangers the health or life of the woman.

Justyna Wydrzynska, an activist from a nonprofit organization called Abortion Dream Team, showed DW the "Abortomat" in a small room behind a tasseled portiere at Abotak, Poland's first abortion center.

Inside, the pink lighting is subdued. Glitter tassels, baubles and spotlights make it feel a bit like a disco, she said.

The "Abortomat," which opened in late September, has 22 pink lockers. To open one, a woman receives a PIN code from the organization Women Help Women after completing a medical questionnaire, which is then checked by a doctor.

Parcel lockers have sprung up like mushrooms after rain in Poland in recent years. Even the smallest villages have them.

"When you walk around Poland, vending machines are everywhere. This is our culture. People in the US have Amazon, we have vending machines for everything," said Wydrzynska.

Women in Poland who have an abortion are not criminalized. But anyone who helps them terminate a pregnancy risks up to three years in prison.

In 2023, Wydrzynska was the first activist to be convicted in Poland of helping a woman to have an abortion by sending her pills. The conviction was later overturned, and the court ordered a retrial.

Inside each compartment at the "Abortomat" is a package that normally contains two drugs, mifepristone and misoprostol, usually taken up to the 14th week of pregnancy.

Because mifepristone is not registered in Poland, the drug must be ordered from abroad where it is legal. Information on how to do this is not easy to come by, which is where Abortion Dream Team and Abotak come in. They help women find information about accessing medication, which is sent from abroad by Women Help Women, or help them to travel abroad for a termination.

Wydrzynska said she believes the "Abortomat" does not break the law because it is a machine, not a person, that is delivering the pills, although she admits she is not sure.

Dr. Sabrina Mana-Walasek, a reproductive rights lawyer representing Wydrzynska, said the legal environment for abortion pills is "complicated."

She said that although mifepristone is not registered in Poland, it is registered in the European Union and, as EU citizens, Polish women can order it and take it.

"If a woman orders pills for herself and pays for them herself, it's not a crime," she said.

The ultra-Catholic legal group Ordo Iuris disagrees and has notified the prosecutor's office that the activists are committing a crime.

"In the case of an 'Abortomat,' the actions enabling a woman to have an abortion are theoretically performed by the woman herself using the device, but the reality is that this machine is operated by people — they send the code required to open the compartment containing abortion pills, they order and procure the pills, and they restock the compartments," said Katarzyna Gesiak, director of the Ordo Iuris Center for Medical Law and Bioethics.

Wydrzynska said anti-abortion activists regularly demonstrate outside Abotak, using horns and loudspeakers to generate loud noise to annoy the center's residential neighbors and pressure the landlord to revoke their rental agreement.

At one demonstration, a woman holding an image of the Virgin Mary told DW she believes abortion "is the work of Satan."

"Abortion is evil, it's demonic, it's against the fifth Commandment, 'Thou shalt not kill,'" she said.

Earlier this year, Diana, a 25-year-old Ukrainian woman living in Warsaw, used abortion pills during her sixth week of pregnancy before the "Abortomat" started up. She got the pills using information provided by Abortion Dream Team.

Diana told DW that she has never wanted to have children.

"I was expecting something like a headache, stomachache, maybe vomiting, but I had nothing like that. It was just like a normal period. I didn't feel any pain. It was really easy for me," she said.

"I felt relief because I knew that it was the right choice for me. I knew that I can live my life," she added.

Another Abortion Dream Team activist, Natalia Broniarczyk, said they help about 130 women daily in Poland. About 125 of these women use pills that cost about €75 ($83), while the remainder travel to a clinic abroad.

In contrast, just 851 abortions were performed in Polish hospitals last year.

Broniarczyk is concerned about the rising support for right-wing parties ahead of next year's parliamentary election, but she's also furious at the inactivity of liberals in Tusk's coalition.

"I'm mad at the liberals because for three years, they did nothing to protect us. They lost three years and now, when we're close to an election, they are fighting for our voices and respect. People are more progressive than politicians," she said.

After taking office in 2023, Tusk's coalition, which includes left-wing and conservative partners, were unable to agree a common position and their separate bills failed to obtain parliamentary approval in 2024.

Afterwards, Tusk said abortion on request would not be possible during the current parliamentary term.

In October, however, two bills were resubmitted to the Sejm, the lower house of parliament, and were narrowly approved.

One reinstates severe irreversible fetal defects as a justification for a legal termination. This justification had been ruled illegal by the Constitutional Court in 2020 when the previous right-wing Law and Justice (PiS)-led government was in power.

The 2020 ruling led to tens of thousands of people, mostly women, taking part in street protests in towns and cities across the country, some of the largest since communism fell in 1989.

Lawmakers also approved a bill partially decriminalizing anyone who helps a woman terminate her pregnancy.

According to the results of a recent poll conducted by SW Research for Onet.pl, almost 61% of respondents said they want the country's right-wing president, Karol Nawrocki, who is backed by PiS, to sign into law the bill on the fetal defect justification.

Nawrocki opposes abortion, and both Wydrzynska and Broniarczyk think he will use his power of veto to block the bills, preventing them from becoming law.

Wu-Tang!” repeatedly as the mighty crew wielded their lyrical swords.

But then came the Red Hot Chili Peppers, who were part of Rick Rubin’s induction. Cosplaying Rubin (who, after an incredibly theatrical induction speech by Neil Young, accepted his honor in shorts, sandals and rose-tinted sunglasses), Anthony Kiedis, John Frusciante, Flea and Chad Smith erupted into their Blood Sugar Sex Magik single “Give It Away” donning fake white beards. After a short technical issue with Flea’s instrument, the ever-limber bassist and Kiedis continued to somehow defy aging and rocked it like it was 1991.

Why do birds flap before landing? It may be about more than slowing down

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

New calculations suggest birds flap during landing not just to control speed, but to stop within limited space.

Most birds flap their wings as they come in to land, but those final wingbeats may do more than slow them down. New calculations suggest they also help birds stop within a tight space.

A bird the size of a pigeon could glide to a complete stop with its wings held still.

To manage it, though, the bird would have to begin its landing maneuver at about 80 mph (36 meters per second). It would also have to climb roughly 65 feet (20 meters), the height of a six-story building.

Flapping, the researchers propose, lets a bird stop inside the space it has.

Tom Rottier carried out the study at the University of Manchester and the University of Toronto Institute for Aerospace Studies. He worked with Philippe Lavoie of Toronto and Ben Parslew of Manchester.

No birds were flown for the work. Instead, equations were written for a bird gliding on fixed wings.

Only gravity, lift, and air resistance acted on this model bird. The team then solved the equations for the slowest possible touchdown.

The forces on a wing depend on how fast the wing moves through the air. Those forces shrink as a bird slows down. Its control over where it goes shrinks with them.

Arriving fast is no better. Whatever speed is left at touchdown is usually absorbed by the legs. A bad landing can damage muscle and bone, or it can kill the bird.

The difficulty, said Parslew, an aerospace engineering lecturer at Manchester, is that “landing isn’t as simple as just slowing down.”

A wing also has a minimum working speed. In level flight, the bird’s whole weight has to be carried by lift. A wing that moves too slowly cannot make enough of it. Engineers call that minimum the stall speed.

The stall speed is not fixed, however, as the team’s equations show. A bird that climbs more steeply puts less of its weight on its wings. The stall speed drops as a result. In a climb straight up, it is zero.

A climb that ends straight up is how a gliding bird could stop without flapping. The bird comes in fast and pulls up. It then climbs more and more steeply while gravity and air resistance slow it down.

At the top, the bird is pointed straight up and has no speed left.

More lift also means more drag, and drag takes away the speed the climb depends on. For that reason, the tilt of the wings has to keep changing on the way up. By the end, the wings are turned broadside to the air.

The team calculated what such a landing would take for a rock pigeon weighing 14 ounces (400 grams).

Starting from level flight, the bird would need that 80 mph speed. This is more than double a pigeon’s typical cruising speed.

The bird would travel about 200 feet (60 meters) horizontally, roughly two-thirds of a football field, while climbing 65 feet.

City pigeons fly between buildings. A landing of that size is not feasible in many of those places, the team wrote. Bigger birds would need even more room.

The researchers therefore propose that flapping is not simply a way to slow down. A glide can do that. Flapping does it in a small space.

A perch can be approached from below, but the ground cannot. That changes the answer.

The researchers then tested landings in which the model bird could not climb at any point. No path brought it to a stop.

The slowest landing allowed by the equations came in at an angle of about 34 degrees below level.

A bird that glides down to the ground therefore arrives with some speed left. That speed has to be removed by flapping, by braking with the legs, or by both.

The legs need enough muscle and bone to absorb the impact. But that adds weight the bird must carry in flight.

The researchers suggest this trade-off may have shaped how birds evolved. The need to withstand landing impacts may have set a minimum strength for their legs.

Leftover speed may also help explain why ducks, geese, and swans land differently on water than on solid ground.

More of a bird’s energy is absorbed by water than by the ground. The bird can therefore arrive faster.

Some birds of prey do finish a landing without flapping.

A 2022 study observed Harris’s hawks flying between two perches. They learned to dive low and then glide up to the perch.

A wing that is tilted very steeply is stalled, meaning air no longer flows smoothly over it.

The authors of that study proposed an explanation for the swoop. In their view, it keeps the distance a hawk flies with stalled wings as short as possible.

Rottier’s team tested the idea by giving their model bird one goal at a time. For this test, the model bird was allowed to flap during the dive, as the hawks did.

First, it was told only to spend as little time stalled as possible. The model bird climbed and then came down onto the perch. It arrived at speeds that would harm a real hawk, the team wrote.

Next, the only goal was to land as slowly as possible. This time the model bird swooped deep.

A mix of the two goals generally matched the real hawks’ paths best. Touchdown speed did more than stall time to produce the swoop.

Diving builds speed, and speed allows a steeper climb. A steeper climb ends in a slower arrival.

The pigeon figures come from equations, not from a bird in the air. In its main form, the model has no wind, no body or tail, and no flapping.

A wing gets a brief boost in lift and drag when it tilts up quickly. The wings of landing birds do this, yet the boost is also left out of the model. To see how much it could matter, the team doubled the model’s maximum lift.

For a slow touchdown, the starting speed the bird needed fell by 44%. The forward distance fell by 65% and the climb by 55%. When the maximum drag was doubled instead, the speed needed went up.

A living bird may therefore be able to glide to a stop in less room than the pigeon numbers suggest. How much less cannot be said from the model.

The equations were written so that wind can be added later. The team also described the model as a base for studies of single species.

The same physics may apply to animals that never flap, such as flying squirrels and flying snakes. It may also apply to drones built to land on perches.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the Royal Society A.
