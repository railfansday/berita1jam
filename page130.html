Salon, Digital News Trailblazer, Folds After 31 Years

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The website said its "publishing model is no longer sustainable" given the "business realities of independent digital journalism."

Salon, a left-leaning publication known for its sharp political and cultural commentary that showed what online journalism could look like before most mainstream outlets entered the space, is closing up shop after 31 years.

In an unsigned farewell announcement on Thursday, Salon pointed to the profound changes in the economics of independent digital journalism and said its publishing model is no longer sustainable.

“Salon was never just a website,” the announcement said. “It was an idea about what journalism could be, born at a moment when the internet was still a largely unexplored frontier.”

The shuttering highlights the rapidly-shifting economics of digital media, where even established publications are contending with massive declines in traffic. Search and social media have upended how outlets attract readers. Artificial intelligence search overviews, which allow audiences to bypass clicking links, are now doing the same. In this landscape, longevity and editorial influence no longer guarantee financial survival.

Launched in 1995 by then-San Francisco Examiner editor David Talbot, Salon was an early experiment in digital publishing without a print offering. It went public four years later and went on to cultivate a sizable readership. Prominent journalists who got a foothold in media through the website include CNN anchor Jake Tapper, NBC News’ Steve Kornacki, The Intercept founder Glenn Greenwald, The Nation national affairs correspondent Joan Walsh, who served as editor-in-chief of Salon for five years.

The site also played a starring role in Nora Ephron’s 2009 feature Julie & Julia as the home blog of Amy Adams’ Julie, who pledged to cook all of Julia Child’s cookbook in a year.

Like many other media outlets, the site saw a massive decline in traffic in 2016 after then-Facebook changed the social media platform’s news feed algorithm to prioritize posts from family and friends over publishers. In 2023, Find.co acquired the website from its previous owners, Drew Schoentrup and Chris Richmond. The privately held media company bills itself as a marketing operation revolving around crypto brands. Its portfolio includes CCN, Webopedia and CryptoManiaks.

Salon’s Thursday post didn’t mention what will happen to its immense archive. On Bluesky, Salon chief content officer Erin Keane said it will “stay live for now.”

Melania Trump touts rare agreement she brokered between Russia and Ukraine

WASHINGTON, DC - NOVEMBER 05: First lady Melania Trump gives hand written letters to Russian special envoy and head of the Russian Direct Investment Fund Kirill Dmitrievon (L) and Ukrainian human rights Ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets (C) to deliver to their presidents during an event on a temporary Russia-Ukraine ceasefire at the White House on November 5, 2026 in Washington, DC. Samuel Corum/Getty Images hide caption

First lady Melania Trump hosted a rare meeting between Russian and Ukrainian officials at the White House to recognize a temporary ceasefire in southeastern Ukraine.

The six-day pause from the Russian-occupied city of Oleshky comes as the administration ramps up efforts to secure an agreement to end the war in Ukraine.

"We have chosen humanity over war," the first lady said. "Undeniably, the lives of innocent people must transcend the divisions of war. I hope Oleshky inspires greater peace."

Trump has accomplished something that has so far eluded her husband - helping broker a temporary ceasefire and bringing together representatives of the two sides in acknowledgement of the human costs of the war.

The White House sees it as a diplomatic breakthrough, but some European analysts worry the efforts can backfire.

The temporary reprieve, which expired on Sunday, enabled humanitarian aid to reach the city while allowing hundreds of residents to be evacuated.

Kirill Dmitriev, Russian President Vladimir Putin's special envoy, thanked the first lady. He said Putin personally gave the instructions to ensure the successful implementation of the ceasefire.

"On behalf of President Putin, on behalf of the people of the Russian Federation, we would like to thank first lady Melania Trump for her distinguished humanitarian role in resolving a key situation and resolving this humanitarian issue," he said.

Ukrainian human rights ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets presented Melania Trump with an award for her humanitarian work. The Order of Princess Olha, 1st Class, is Ukraine's highest award for women.

"I have only one request from all Ukrainian nation," Lubinets said, "please continue doing your wonderful work and support people."

The event comes just days before the U.S. leads a new round of peace talks between Russia and Ukraine in Abu Dhabi next week.

President Trump also met with Dmitriev at the White House.

Trump told reporters that the sides were close to reaching a deal.

"It's going to be an agreement. It's going to be a big agreement, ‌and ⁠it's going to involve more than Ukraine," he said. "I could even say it might involve all of Europe. It's ⁠going to be a big, beautiful agreement, and everyone's going to be thrilled."

Heather Conley, who worked on European issues in President George W. Bush's State Department, supports all efforts to help suffering Ukrainians, but she also said the U.S. must be careful not to normalize Russia's bad behavior.

"In this White House ceremony, we were honoring the Russians that were the cause of both the occupation of Oleshky, the starvation of Oleshky, and now we're honoring them for allowing aid to come into Oleshky," said Conley, who is now a senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute. "The more we create this equivalency where the Russians and the Ukrainians are equal in this crisis, it is putting this thing upside down."

Letterboxd Finalizing $330 Million Sale to The New York Times

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Letterboxd, the social networking platform for cinephiles, will soon have new owners. The website is finalizing a sale to The New York Times Company in a deal valuing the website at $330 million, according to a report from the NYT on Thursday.

An agreement has not yet been reached and could still fall apart, but the companies have entered into exclusive negotiations. The $330 million price tag is more than 20 times Letterboxd’s projected earnings in 2026 of about $15 million, the Times reported.

The New York Times would acquire the controlling stake in Letterboxd from Tiny, a Canadian-based public company, which originally picked up a 60 percent stake in 2023 when the website was valued at just $50 million. Tiny has been exploring a sale since April; a deal has finally emerged after film studios, tech executives, and private equity companies all kicked the tires. IndieWire understands that A24 was also in late-stage talks to acquire Letterboxd, with the studio promising Letterboxd’s founders to maintain its independence and its business model unchanged. Letterboxd has grown significantly since being acquired by Tiny, now reaching 26 million users (though the company does not disclose how many of them are on paid subscriptions.) OpenAI makes new data center commitments, AI policy recommendations

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

OpenAI Group PBC today made a set of commitments designed to ensure that its data centers benefit the communities where they’re built.

It also proposed that lawmakers make some of the commitments mandatory for artificial intelligence companies.

The move comes two days after Democrats won both the House and the Senate on a campaign backing data center construction curbs. Additionally, voters approved dozens of ballot measures designed to scale back or limit AI infrastructure projects.

OpenAI today reiterated a commitment it made in January to fully pay for the electricity and grid upgrades that its data centers require. That covers items such as the construction of new overhead power lines. Additionally, the company pledged to provide “financial assurances” that will protect other grid users from potential project cancellations and delays.

OpenAI will also take steps to reduce grid strain. In particular, it will scale back its data centers’ power draw at times when energy demand is particularly high. That approach, which is known as a flexible data center model, has also drawn the interest of other industry players as Nvidia Corp. and Google LLC.

Some data center builders sign non-disclosure agreements with local officials to keep construction details under wraps. OpenAI today pledged not to use NDAs. It will instead only seek “narrow protections covering legitimate security information, trade secrets, or proprietary or competitively sensitive information.”

OpenAI will publicly share construction plans and noise projections before construction of a new facility begins. Additionally, it will provide information about the jobs that each data center is expected to create. The goal is to help local unions and training providers prepare residents for the positions.

OpenAI plans to tailor its commitments to each data center. It will publish the customized project terms in a document it calls a local compact. Once construction is underway, the company will provide annual updates and designate a local contact to field residents’ questions.

OpenAI hopes to codify some of the best practices that it published today into law. The company released a policy paper that calls on lawmakers to limit data center developers’ use of NDAs and require them to cover their electricity costs. Furthermore, OpenAI argues that AI companies should be offered incentives to lower their power usage at times of peak grid stress.

Some of the best practices that the ChatGPT developer is prompting are already being adopted by other industry players. Google, for example, today pledged to fully cover the electricity costs of a new $17 billion data center that it plans to build in Wyoming. It will also finance related grid upgrades. Anthropic PBC made a similar commitment in February.

Amnesiac Surgeon Confronts His Organ-Trafficking Past in Taiwan Creative Content Fest Series ‘Hannibal Inside’

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

An amnesiac sashimi chef learns he was once the star surgeon of an organ-trafficking syndicate in “Hannibal Inside,” a crime thriller series from Studio76 adapted from “The Monster Community,” a novel the producers picked up from Kadokawa Taiwan.

The project is at the Taiwan Creative Content Fest (TCCF) in Taipei.

Two years after a car crash erased his memory, Owen works the knife at a fishing port in Keelung, Taiwan. The only person he remembers is his girlfriend Xi-xi. When he steps in to save a stranger’s life on the street, footage of the rescue spreads online and his old associates spot him. Owen, it turns out, was the chief surgeon of Undercurrent, a syndicate that sells organs to the global rich. He was second in command only to its shadowy leader, known as The Professor. Undercurrent assassin Jiang Xu sets out to force Owen’s memories back to the surface. Meanwhile Detective Lin, whose son needs a heart transplant, pegs Owen as the prime suspect in a string of murders in which the victims’ organs are removed. After Xi-xi is killed, Owen heads to his hometown of Songkhla, Thailand, to take revenge. There he crosses paths with local police captain Xu Zhi-rou, whose father he once saved. The road leads to a luxury cruise ship where billionaires bid on living people according to their organ matches.

Producer Dennis Yang says the team took the serial killer plot of the book and wove in real news reports of black-market organ trading, “a high-risk underworld that is rarely explored with real emotional depth in Asian series.”

For director Ray Jiang, crime and action shows are plentiful, but few of them dig into what makes people tick or treat life with respect.

“I want to ask: ‘Who defines the value of a life? Who decides who gets to live and who dies?’” Jiang says. “Society often judges people by whether they are good or bad, or how much they contribute – as if criminals deserve to die, while good people should live forever. But I believe every life is valuable because of what a person achieves on their own journey. No one has the right to judge someone else’s worth.”

The series is in pre-development. Full episode outlines are complete and the writing team is working on scripts.

“This project blends commercial entertainment with a thrilling pace, while exploring universal questions of justice and survival. It is an ambitious crime thriller with great potential for the global market,” Yang says.

Yang calls the project’s selection for TCCF this year a major milestone. Two earlier Studio76 projects, “The Brides Town” and “The Swamp Thing,” were both picked to pitch in person at Series Mania in Lille, France, and Yang says “Hannibal Inside” marks the company’s third time.

At TCCF, the team hopes to meet IP investors and streamers from around the world, as well as distributors in Europe and co-production partners in Asia. “Ultimately, through TCCF and its bridge to Series Mania, we hope to bring our story to a wider global audience and maximize the project’s international IP potential,” Yang says.

The Taiwan Creative Content Fest, organized by the Taiwan Creative Content Agency (TAICCA), runs Nov. 6–9 at the Taipei Nangang Exhibition Center.

Google, Meta, TikTok sue over social media law

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Three of the biggest social media companies want to block a California law designed to reduce youth social media addiction from going into law.

On Wednesday, Google, Meta, and TikTok each filed lawsuits against California's attorney general, Rob Bonta.

Beginning on Jan. 1, 2027, the bill (AB 1709) would prohibit platforms including Google, Meta, and TikTok from providing "an addictive feature" to users under 16. The law describes such features as "psychologically exploitative" and intended to maximize engagement in a way that leads to compulsive use. This includes infinite scroll, autoplay, and an "addictive" feed.

The law also requires platforms to "implement reasonable measures" so that young users aren't offered such features.

The companies argue that the law infringes on their free speech rights, as well as Section 230, a provision of a 1996 federal law designed to shield online platforms from liability related to user content. Critics of Section 230 say it wrongly provides online platforms with broad immunity.

Google claimed in its lawsuit that its subsidiary, YouTube, personalizes recommendations to ensure that young users receive content relevant to their interests and appropriate for their age and developmental needs. The company defended autoplay as helping users enjoy content uninterrupted and sequentially, like a playlist for educational videos.

"AB 1709's bans on personalized recommendations and autoplay would therefore frustrate YouTube's efforts to provide under-16 users with safe, responsible, and enriching experiences on the platform," the lawsuit said. "They're framing this as a free speech fight, but what's really at stake is a business model that profits from keeping kids scrolling, even when it puts them at risk," Torres said. (Torres is a Mashable contributor.)

California's Attorney General's office told Reuters that it was reviewing the filings.

"Our office is committed to defending California's commonsense laws to protect kids online," the office said in a statement to Reuters.

In August, Meta settled a youth social media safety and addiction case for $17 billion, though the company did not admit guilt or liability. As part of that settlement, Meta agreed to limit teens' daily time spent on Instagram and Facebook to two hours; block access to both platforms overnight and during school hours; give teens the option to create a default non-algorithmic feed; and allow teens to turn off autoplay.

Meta also issued a "call to action" to YouTube and TikTok to adopt what the company describes as a new "industry standard." Neither company joined the settlement terms.

Despite the Will Ferrell flick being over 20 years old, there are still so many stories coming out that have never been told before. Now, even more stories are coming out thanks to a new behind-the-scenes book co-written by Elf costar Andy Richter, titled Elf Unwrapped: Behind the Scenes of a Modern Christmas Classic.

Back in 2020, the classic Christmas film was featured on an episode of the Netflix docuseries The Christmas Movies That Made Us. Fans learned a lot of behind-the-scenes facts about the Jon Favreau-directed flick, including the dark ending the film almost received. Elf super fans already know that a large chunk of the interior scenes of the film were shot at an abandoned mental institution. Part of this was to keep costs down since the film was made on a $33 million budget. Richter talked about it with Us Weekly:

There was this old mental hospital that had a couple of empty wings, and that’s where the offices are, the Hobbs’ home, the mailroom; almost all the interiors of the movie are in a mental hospital that was still functioning a little bit. The studio and soundstage space was limited due to a“big vampire movie” that was filming at the same time, so the production design team behind Elf made the unusual filming location work. It turned the space into the Gimbels department store, Greenway Press, and the Hobbs’ apartment. As magical as the locations looked on screen, some actors still found filming at the semi-functioning mental hospital unsettling, including Richter, as he said in the interview:

I wasn’t wandering around.. I went to the set. I went to the trailer. I got in the van. I went back to the hotel.

While filming aimed to stay on the abandoned side of the hospital, portions of the location were still occupied by patients. It’s unclear if any patients actually saw Ferrell traipsing around in his iconic green costume inspired by Rankin/Bass’s elves in Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer, but Ritcher did speculate about what they might have thought if they caught a glimpse of the SNL alum:

Will was all over that place for a month or two, like what people were thinking? Patients were looking out the window and seeing this guy in February wandering around in an elf costume, and maybe recognizing, ‘Hey, isn’t that Will Ferrell?’

Ferrell himself had some apprehensions about the costume the first time he tried it on, so it’s valid for Richter to consider what outsiders who had no idea a Christmas movie was being filmed next door might have thought. Thankfully, it all worked out, and Elf continues to be a holiday movie must-watch for families.

Palantir stock is selling off despite another round of strong quarterly results. Where does the stock go from here?

Palantir Technologies' (PLTR -9.73%) strong business results have supported significant valuation gains for the stock. The third quarter saw the company's revenue increase 96% year over year to $2.31 billion, and its net income surged 137% from the prior-year period to roughly $1.13 billion.

Despite the strong overall results, the company's stock has pulled back in subsequent trading due to concerns about slowing growth in commercial contract values, slower expansion in international markets, and bearish pressure on companies in the broader artificial intelligence (AI) space.

Palantir stock is down roughly 1% year to date, and the company's share price is down 16% from the lifetime high it reached last month. What does history suggest happens next for the stock?

In its recent quarterly report, Palantir guided for sales between $2.649 billion and $2.653 billion in the current quarter. Meanwhile, non-GAAP (generally accepted accounting principles) adjusted operating income is projected to be between $1.64 billion and $1.644 billion.

The company has built a very strong track record of surpassing its own guidance and Wall Street estimates, and this outperformance has generally helped generate wins for investors who bought Palantir stock and took a buy-and-hold approach.

Of course, investors have to keep in mind that past performance does not dictate future results. While Palantir has been a publicly traded company for a little over six years, that's still a relatively short period in the grand scheme of things.

The company has gone through periods when sentiment toward tech stocks turned negative. Still, its overall history as a publicly traded company has been characterized by a sector valuation backdrop that has generally been very favorable. For context, the S&P 500 is up roughly 125% since Palantir went public, and the even more tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite is up 127% over the same period.

Palantir looks poised to continue delivering results that could support substantial valuation gains. The company is growing sales at a highly impressive clip, and its asset-light software business is serving up margins that are nothing short of incredible. Thus far, the incredible margin strength has also shown a high degree of sustainability.

On the other hand, it's important to understand that Palantir will not be immune to broader market pressures, even if overall business performance remains quite robust. Investors who follow the market actively are almost certainly aware of concerns that valuations for AI stocks are in a bubble, as well as a slew of macroeconomic risk factors, including relatively high inflation, elevated bond yields, and other dynamics that threaten bullish momentum.

Trading at roughly 109 times this year's expected earnings and 51 times expected sales, Palantir's heavily growth-dependent valuation profile comes with substantial risk. However, the company is a clear category leader in vital corners of the AI software market — and there's a good chance that long-term investors will score wins with the stock even if near-term market pressures roil the market.
