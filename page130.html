Moderator Matthew Galsky, MD, of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai in New York City, is joined by David Aggen, MD, PhD, of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York City, and Stephanie Berg, DO, of the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute in Boston.

In this second of four episodes, they examine the disappointing results from the KEYNOTE-866 and ENERGIZE trials, how those findings compare with the positive NIAGARA trial, and what they mean for perioperative treatment decisions in muscle-invasive bladder cancer.

Cologne Auction House Moves Forward with Sale of Gothic Paintings as Jewish Dealer’s Heirs Claim They Were ‘Stolen’

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Cologne-based Lempertz auction house said it still planned to sell two works by Gothic painter Johann Koerbecke after the heirs of a German Jewish art dealer who was persecuted by the Nazis claimed the pieces were “stolen.”

Dealer Max Stern held the paintings in Düsseldorf between 1936 and 1937, the year the Nazis shut down his gallery. Per a provenance supplied by Lempertz, both paintings were then held by Düsseldorf’s Galerie Hans Bammann, which appears to have then sold them. Related Articles Celebrating the Inaugural Art Week NYC, Organized by ARTnews, Art in America, and Artforum New Museum Inks Multiyear Partnership with Video Art-Focused Vega Foundation

On Monday, the Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation issued a release in which it called on Lempertz to stop the sale of the two works planned for Saturday. According to the foundation, such a sale should not occur “until two distinct ownership claims of different victims are justly resolved according to the Washington Principles on Nazi-Confiscated Art.”

Both paintings—The Annunciation and The Vision of Saint Bernard—are likely from Marienfeld high altarpiece, which was completed in 1457. They are being auctioned in a sale of Old Masters art. They carry high estimates of around 300,000 euros each, or about $329,000 apiece, making them the auction’s most expensive works. “For art historical reasons,” the Lempertz lot entries read, “the two important panels (Lots 1005 and 1006) will first be offered individually and then together.”

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation pointed out that both works appear in several databases of missing artworks, including the Interpol Database of Stolen Art since 2021. On the German Lost Art Database, both are reported as “Nazi-confiscated property”; the foundation registered that claim with the organization in 2018.

The foundation has demanded the return of multiple artworks belonging to Stern over the past decade, and in some cases, it has been successful. In 2017, for example, the FBI returned a painting by Jan Franse Verzijl that had been on view at an US art fair two years earlier.

Potentially complicating the matter is the fact that, prior to Stern’s possession of the Koerbecke paintings, they were owned by Ellen Funke, who, during the Nazi era, was classified as a Mischling, or “mongrel,” because she had Jewish ancestry through her mother. She sold her collection in 1936 to fund her emigration from Germany, and works she once owned have also been the subject of restitution claims—two of which happen to involve the Koerbecke works.

In 2025, Bavaria’s state museums restituted Koerbecke’s Vision of Saint Bernard to Funke’s heirs. At the time, the Bayerische Staatsgemäldesammlungen noted that The Vision of Saint Bernard and The Annunciation were at one point part of a single work, with both images appearing on either side of the same panel, and that Stern had taken them apart with the hope of selling them both. Funke’s heirs are listed as the sellers of The Vision of Saint Bernard on the auction house’s website.

Similarly, Funke’s heirs are also listed as the sellers of the The Annunciation, which entered the Kolumba Art Museum of the Archdiocese of Cologne as a donation in 1996. The museum restituted the work in 2025.

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation’s release notably does not mention Funke by name. But it does state that the foundation “maintains that the rights and histories of both Jewish victims must be taken into account, and neither claim should be privileged at the expense of the other.” In an email to ARTnews, a spokesperson for the foundation said “the museums restituted the paintings to Ellen Funke’s heirs in accordance with the priority principle set forth in the guidelines established in Germany to apply the Washington Principles,” adding that that restitution ”did not result in a ‘just and fair solution’ in accordance with the Washington Principles.”

In a statement to ARTnews, Lempertz said it would not cancel its planned sale of the paintings by Koerbecke, who is widely considered one of the great painters of the Gothic era in Germany.

“We adhere to current restitution practices and follow the recommendations of the Federal Government,” a representative for the auction house said, claiming that the Stern “had the panels sawn in two” and went on to sell one of them for “double the price.”

“These magnificent paintings were not taken away from Max Stern,” the Lempertz representative said, adding, “Max Stern received compensation after the war; the heirs of the Funke family did not.”

The Stern Foundation spokesperson said the “view that the paintings were not ‘seized’ from Max Stern has long been refuted,” citing a 2019 statement from the now-defunct German Advisory Commission that the persecution of Jewish art dealers began at latest by August 1933, “so that from that point on at the latest, it can in no way be assumed that ‘proper, customary business transactions’ took place.” The spokesperson added that the claim that Max Stern was compensated for the paintings is “incorrect“ and has “no legal basis.”

Lempertz did not dispute the listings for both paintings on the Art Loss Register, but the representative also noted that the listings contain details of the works’ respective restitutions.

Peanut Peptide Injections Promising for Allergy Immunotherapy

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

— A novel route to immunotherapy for peanut allergy passes phase II test

A novel peptide-based immunotherapy for peanut allergy appeared safe and modestly efficacious for reducing children's symptoms over the first 12 months, a phase II trial showed.

For the primary efficacy endpoint, the higher of two monthly PVX108 shot dosages significantly boosted the maximum tolerated dose of peanut from baseline to month 12 by 2.4-fold, compared with a 1.2-fold change in the placebo group, reported Brian P. Vickery, MD, of Emory University and chief of the Division of Allergy/Immunology at Children's Healthcare of Atlanta, at the American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology annual meeting.

In terms of safety, there were no cases of anaphylaxis or other treatment-related allergic or severe adverse events with the shot. Only six of the 71 trial participants reported any adverse event (13% with the higher dose tested), largely local administration site reactions.

At an oral food challenge at 1 year, 39% of patients on the higher dose tolerated at least 300 mg of peanut protein compared with 17% in the placebo group (the equivalent of one peanut). "This is more than double, on average, what causes accidental reactions in the field when people are exposed accidentally while they're trying to avoid peanut. And it's been shown in previous work that ... is probably clinically meaningful for most patients with peanut allergy," Vickery said. But he acknowledged it "is probably less than what you'd expect in a study of oral immunotherapy, where the participants are swallowing about 300 mg of protein daily as part of their treatment."

While efficacy needs to be confirmed in a larger trial, "there's also a big safety difference and a difference in burden in terms of daily versus monthly therapy," he added, pointing to adverse events in most immunotherapy patients with other routes of administration and anaphylaxis in 10-15% of trial participants in some studies.

Immunotherapy shots offer an advantage of fewer office visits than the typically once-every-2-week schedule for oral immunotherapy up-dosing and also not requiring kids to consume peanut products daily, commented Jay Lieberman, MD, of the University of Tennessee Health Science Center in Memphis. With peanut protein, though, "you can't do it the same way we do allergy shots to cat or oak tree pollen, because it is too dangerous. It caused too many reactions," he said.

PVX108 comprises seven short peptides representing immunodominant T-cell epitopes of major peanut allergens rather than intact peanut proteins that interact with the immune system and can lead to reactions. "This is designed to get around that problem by formulating fragments of peanut proteins that are too small to interact with IgE [immunoglobulin E] and the allergic components of the immune system and instead interact directly with T cells, which are thought to be kind of the driving force behind the allergy," Vickery said.

"We have made some progress in being able to treat peanut allergy with oral immunotherapy and now FDA-approved omalizumab [Xolair] as the two most commonly used options," Vickery said in an interview monitored by media relations for drug developer Aravax. "But there are millions of patients out there with peanut allergy, and most of them are not being treated due to limitations in care and also limitations in access to these treatments and because of limitations in the treatments themselves."

"The scientific community believes that there is plenty of room for new forms of immunotherapy to be developed," he added, "because we don't think of peanut allergy as a one-size-fits-all condition where one treatment will solve the problem."

While the only FDA-approved peanut oral immunotherapy product recently left the market as clinicians rely on off-the-shelf commercial products, Lieberman agreed that PVX108 could find a place: "Bringing an [oral immunotherapy] FDA-approved thing to the market when people are already just using PB2 powder [commercially available peanut flour], that was the problem."

The trial included 71 peanut allergy patients ages 4 to 11 years at 14 trial sites in the U.S. and Australia in the primary efficacy and safety cohort. They were randomized to double-blind treatment with 5-nmol or 50-nmol PVX108 or placebo given monthly by intradermal administration over 12 months. At the end of that period, a double-blind placebo-controlled food challenge was done following the PRACTALL guidelines.

After 6 months off treatment, a follow-up food challenge was conducted at month 18. It showed evidence of maintenance or improvement in clinical effects compared with month 12, indicating disease-modifying potential, Vickery said, although this needs further confirmation.

The optimal length of treatment isn't clear yet, but immunotherapy shots for other allergens typically take 3 to 5 years of treatment, Vickery noted. "So it's likely that longer-term treatment might be necessary or even might be more optimal."

How Does The REO Runabout Stack Up Against Slate?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

Like the idea of a Slate but don't want an EV? Well, an upstart from Dallas, Texas has just the thing for you. That is, if it ever becomes a reality. Startup company REO recently unveiled renderings of its new truck, dubbed the Runabout. It's a similar idea to Slate, with just one basic frame, three different body variants to choose from, no frills, a bunch of customization, and a sub-$30,000 price tag. The main difference is that that Runabout uses a four-cylinder gas engine, rather than electric motors. So how does it compare to Slate?

First, let's look at price. The most basic Slate you can get—cheekily called "The Blank Slate"—costs $24,950, and it's a two-door pickup with nothing more than some seats, a steering wheel, a battery, and a single rear electric motor to get you around. There's no radio, power windows, or screens to speak of.

The cheapest REO you can get will be the Runabout T4X, which costs $21,500*. Note the asterisk, as REO says the current listed price is an estimate, and the company has no factory or clear plans for getting it to market. For that shockingly low price, it's estimated that you'll get a four-cylinder engine, a choice of either a six-speed manual or automatic transmission, and mechanical four-wheel drive. It's a two-door, two-seat pickup with drop-down sides for the bed, like a Kei truck.

What four-cylinder engine will the Runabout have and which manufacturer will supply it? REO isn't saying. There are far more questions than answers so far. REO does say that it has a "targeted powertrain life of 500,000 miles." To quote myself reading that in the press release: "lol."

While Slate uses a hybrid body-on-frame style chassis, the REO has an old-school ladder frame, like a classic pickup. It's similar to slate in that it offers modular body styles, but with key differences. There's a two-door pickup and an SUV version, like Slate, but there's also a four-door pickup, which Slate doesn't have. REO claims its truck has solid rear axles, too, to give it more off-road credibility.

The design isn't bad, even if it looks like a Grand Theft Auto version of an Australian-market Land Cruiser, but that's to be expected. REO hired longtime automotive designer Frank Stephenson, whose previous work includes the first BMW-owned Mini Cooper, the Maserati MC12, and the McLaren P1. Overall, it looks handsome enough and utilitarian, even if all the photos are just renders at this point.

Inside, REO's truck is said to be pretty barebones (it hasn't been revealed yet), but not as barebones as Slate's. There's a digital screen for gauges and one for navigation, but everything else has a physical control. You're probably wondering if it has power or hand-crank windows. REO used its press release to throw a little shade: " Each model will feature power windows for convenience: hand-cranks are best left to others."

The entire idea behind REO is to offer the sort of basic pickups customers loved in the '80s and '90s, trucks that were simple utilitarian workhorses and nothing else. It's an admirable goal, especially given how expensive pickups have become. But do customers want to go backward in powertrain technology, especially with affordable mainstream electric trucks like the Ford Fathom coming to market soon?

So for less money, you can get a potentially more capable truck, in a similar configuration as the Slate, with a bit of extra interior comfort. But you lose the electric powertrain, which reduces maintenance and fuel costs. Instead, you'll have to make due with a generic four-cylinder of some sort. There is one big "if," though. You can only buy one if REO ever actually makes it. Sure, having a design by Frank Stephenson helps it feel more real, but just because they found the money to pay him to design something doesn't mean they'll actually get around to building it.

Slate is already here, it's ready to launch production vehicles soon. We've seen them in person, touched them, and tested them. Until REO can actually muster a viable product, it should probably avoid throwing anymore shade in its press releases.

Sotheby's makes last-minute deal to sell $180m Van Gogh painting following dispute

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Blaquier family, from Argentina, bought Blossoming Chestnut Trees (Châtaigniers en fleurs) (May 1890) in 1975

Sotheby’s today announced that a settlement has been reached over the sale of Van Gogh’s Blossoming Chestnut Trees (Châtaigniers en fleurs) (May 1890). The work has been poised to achieve a record price for the artist but became the subject of a claim by the heirs of its Jewish former owners, who called for the work to be withdrawn from the 18 November auction in New York.

The Sotheby’s statement records that the auction house is “pleased to confirm that an agreement has been reached between the Blaquier-Arrieta family and all of the legal descendants of Hedwig and Samuel Fischer regarding the sale of Vincent van Gogh’s Châtaigniers en fleurs”. The sale will now proceed as planned.

The work is estimated at around $180m ($207m with fees) and has been guaranteed by Sotheby's to sell. This is far in excess of the previous record of $117m, paid at Christie’s in 2022 for Van Gogh’s Orchard with Cypresses (April 1888).

The Blaquier-Arrieta family, from Argentina, bought Blossoming Chestnut Trees in 1975. As the Sotheby’s statement explains: “In recognition of the painting’s complex Nazi-era history and the suffering endured by the Fischer family during the Holocaust, the two families have reached a mutually satisfactory resolution and the work will be sold pursuant to their agreement.”

The provenance in the Sotheby’s catalogue entry notes, as it has done since last month, that the painting was acquired by the Berlin publisher Samuel Fischer and his wife Hedwig in 1909. Their son-in-law, Gottfried Bermann Fischer, deposited the Van Gogh with the Amsterdam gallery Paul Cassirer in 1932 or 1933—and it was then bought by Paris collectors Henriette and Marcel Kapferer in 1936.

Last Thursday (12 November), in a surprise move, the Berlin-based restitution researcher Gunnar Schnabel sent a letter to Sotheby’s, on behalf of the Fischer heirs. He questioned the 1936 sale—which he says took place three months after the family had left Berlin for Vienna—asking Sotheby’s to prove that the picture had been sold at its market value and that its sale was not forced. In the meantime, Schnabel asked for this week’s sale to be delayed.

In a statement, the Fischer heirs commented: “We are grateful for the trusting and fair negotiation and collaboration with Sotheby’s and the Blaquier-Arrieta family, which make it possible to acknowledge the suffering of the Fischer family during the Nazi era and honour Gisela Berman Fischer's [granddaughter of Samuel and Hedwig] history of seeking justice for her family.”

The Blaquier-Arrieta family commented: “We are pleased to have reached an amicable resolution with the Fischer heirs. We appreciate the constructive dialogue that made this resolution possible and look forward to an extraordinary result and to passing along this masterpiece to a new generation of collectors.”

No details have been released on the nature of the agreement and whether the Fischer family will receive financial compensation.

What impact, if any, the short-lived dispute will have on the sale will only become apparent with the bidding.
