"I think Kojima is a pioneer, and keeps going out to untrodden waters."

Hideo Kojima always brings a certain sense of mystery and allure to his work, as is currently the case with the OD KNOCK and PHYSINT projects. For quite some time, speculation over who would star in the latter threw all sorts of crazy names into the mixer, before Kojima revealed that Pennywise and Dune actor Bill Skarsgård had got the job.

Skarsgård recently appeared on the Happy Sad Confused podcast, where he spoke about how working with Hideo Kojima is a "big deal" for him. He also admitted that he grew up playing all the Metal Gear Solid games and is a big fan of Kojima's process.

"I played every single one [Metal Gear Solid], at least the ones on platform. So, I was very enamoured by him and the sort of the mythology or the universes that he creates, but also the sort of mystique and allure about him as well."

As the actor continued to praise the Metal Gear creator, Skarsgård seemed to specifically be impressed by Kojima's fluent knowledge of filmmaking despite being a video game developer. He even recalled a moment where he asked Kojima if he would ever make a film:

"[Kojima] is an auteur, but he's a video game auteur. He's fluent in filmmaking, in film, and in cinema. I remember at one point, way back 20 years ago, there was talk about doing a Metal Gear Solid movie, and, is gonna Hideo gonna direct it? I asked him, 'would you ever do a film?' and he's like 'no'. At this point, what he's doing is inventing a new medium."

Skarsgård touched briefly on his performance capture in PHYSINT and suggested he's working on eight hours of narrative storytelling.

"What we're gonna do is eight hours worth of narrative, right? But it's also an entire universe that you play, so it truly is quite literally world building. But now with technology, we're getting closer and closer to something that's photorealistic, but it's also interactive when you play it. I think he's a pioneer, and keeps going out to untrodden waters."

PHYSINT is a return to tactical espionage for Kojima since departing Konami and Metal Gear Solid in 2015. We're very excited to see what he's been cooking up with Bill and the rest of the cast when we eventually learn more. Xbox has also remained fairly quiet on how PHYSINT fits into Microsoft's exclusivity plans.

‘Werwulf,’ ‘Cliff Booth,’ ‘The Odyssey’ and More Costume Designers Show Exclusive Sketches From the Year’s Biggest Movies

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ruffled gowns, sequins and a werewolf hunter dressed in layers of wool are among this year’s costume designs audiences will see on screen.

Contemporary designs are seen in Olivia Wilde in “The Invite,” while a nod to the Regency era is made in “Sense and Sensibility.”

And there are numbers. Marci Rodgers delivered over 3,000 looks for the “Michael” biopic starring Jaafar Jackson, while Ellen Mirojnick created over 5,300 designs for Christopher Nolan’s epic, “The Odyssey.” Related Stories Jimmy Kimmel Calls for Donald Trump to Resign Amid Republicans’ Midterm Elections Losses: ‘Quit Already … Nobody Wants You’ Trump Says 'Artificial Intelligence' Is 'a Very Bad and Inaccurate Name,' and That He Wants to Officially Ban the Term 'AI'

Below, Mirojnick, Rodgers and more costume designers share exclusive looks at their concepts for costumes which reflect character arcs and show how clothing can tell a lot about a person before they have even spoken a single line.

Costume designer Ellen Mirojnick says that though Anne Hathaway’s Penelope had inner strength at the start of Christopher Nolan’s “The Odyssey,” “it was important that she never appear queen-like.”

As the film opens, Penelope’s “dresses were more vulnerable, simpler, not at all decorative. Her work at the loom was softer — it was artistic in a way.” By the time she sets forth to challenge her suitors, though, her gown is a “magnificent blue.” “That was quite intentional,” Mirojnick says. “It was to be the period at the end of the sentence. She needed to look armor-like.”

One key scene in Antoine Fuqua’s biopic about Michael Jackson was his performance of “Billie Jean” at the “Motown 25” concert in 1983. There, Jackson debuted not only his moonwalk but his signature outfit: a black sequined jacket, a fedora, a single glove, white socks, cropped pants and black loafers.

Costume designer Marci Rodgers says she approached recreating the look “from the feet up.”

She used the same shoe Jackson wore: a Florsheim Como Imperial loafer. The pants “were a version of the tuxedo pants that we see when he’s singing ‘Off the Wall’ — it’s the same style.”

It turns out that the jacket Jackson wore during the performance belonged to his mother, Katherine. “To me, it was like his cape,” Rodgers says. “He was wearing a piece of clothing that was from his mother’s closet, and that, by far, transformed everything.”

Jaafar Jackson, who plays Michael, had a similar jacket and lent it to Rodgers to study. “It was in my possession, so I was able to get as close as possible to re-creating the sequins of Michael’s mother’sjacket.”

In “Wicker,” a spinster fisherwoman (Olivia Colman) in a medieval community, mocked by villagers for her independence, asks the local basket weaver (Peter Dinklage) to make her a husband (Alexander Skarsgård) out of wicker.

Writer-directors Alex Huston Fischer and Eleanor Wilson wanted the fisherwoman’s husband to be created practically, rather than with special effects. So prosthetic makeup designer Joe Dunckley and lead makeup FX artist Sarah Rubano spent 18 months developing Skarsgård’s costume. It started with a full-body scan of the actor’s frame so they could sculpt pieces specific to him.

The intricate weave was created using a relatively new powder-bed printing process. Dunckley explains, “It’s a volume of thermoplastic elastomer. You shoot a laser into it, and where that focal point of thelaser is, it fuses the powder and you can build intricate weave and complex geometry.”

In the end, 95% of the costume was made using 50 3D-printed appliances painted to look like wicker and stitched to a spandex suit. The key, Dunckley says, was not to lose Skarsgård under the makeup.

At the start of Georgia Oakley’s adaptation of Jane Austen’s classic novel, the Dashwood women (Daisy Edgar-Jones, Esmé Creed-Miles and Caitríona Balfe) are mourning the loss of their father and husband, which meant that there were rules that costume designer Grace Snell had to follow: no shiny buttons, the fabric had to be dull and black was one of the few acceptable colors, along with gray and lavender.

“I wanted Mrs. Dashwood to be the deepest shade of grief,” Snell says. So the designer fashioned a black taffeta dress. “What’s great about this black is it’s almost got this stripe of dark green in it, so it looks quite reflective. It felt like it could have been from any time.”

Mrs. Dashwood stays in mourning for most of the film, but as the story progresses, Snell began introducing beige into her palette.

Willoughby (Frank Dillane), who toys with young Marianne, “has a real interest in fashion,” Snell says “and wants to be seen.” For the London hop scene between Willoughby and Marianne, Snell put the young man in a rust-red moleskin coat. Opposite Marianne’s pink dress, “they look wonderful, but it looks explosive.” She completed Willoughby’s outfit with silk pantaloons. “They were only just coming into fashion, so he’s one of the first to bring them over from France,” Snell says, adding, “He’s a little bit of a rock star; he’s unattainable.”

This companion piece to Robert Eggers’ 2024 “Nosferatu” tells the tale of a 14th-century town beset by a werewolf. Willem Dafoe plays the landowner-hunter hired by the king to lead the charge against the beast.

What sets Dafoe’s character apart from the film’s victims is the amount of wool he wears. “His is the character that really denotes wealth,” says costume designer Linda Muir. Because wool was a scarce commodity at the time, Muir put him in double-layered wool coats and linen shirts. His boots, too, are higher than those of anyone else in the film to emphasize his station. The props department made him a family signet ring, and to complete the look, Muir gave him a bycocket, which is commonly associated with Robin Hood.

David Fincher’s follow-up to “Once Upon a Time in Hollywood,” starring Brad Pitt, is no longer about a cocky stunt double, Cliff Booth — now the stunt man is tending bar.

Fincher’s instructions to costume designer Trish Summerville were to keep things authentic and grounded. “Cliff’s character is very lived in,” Summerville says. So she retained elements of Cliff’s character from the first film, like his love for Hawaiian shirts. “Back then, people didn’t have disposable fashion; you kept your clothes for quite a while. Cliff still has his favorite jeans — Levi’s — moccasins and work shirts.”

In one scene, Cliff wears a Hawaiian shirt featuring beer bottles. Searching for the perfect shirt, Summerville found various rayon numbers from the ’50s through the ’70s printed with images of bottles of booze.

But she needed multiples of the garment for Pitt and his own stunt double. “So I had an artist put them all together, and we printed the graphics on [new] shirts.” She adds, “It made me laugh. Cliff’s a chill rascal, so I thought the shirt was just great for him.”

When Angela (Olivia Wilde) and her husband, Joe (Seth Rogen) — a couple on edge — invite their loud upstairs neighbors (Penélope Cruz and Edward Norton) to an intimate dinner party, things take an unexpected turn.

In early meetings with Wilde, costume designer Arianne Phillips came to understand how the doorways and windows of Angela’s apartment created “beautiful framing” — so much so that Phillips thought of the costumes like portraits or photographs.

“Olivia really loved the idea that Angela’s creative identity is so absorbed into the apartment that she basically blends into the walls,” Phillips says. “There’s a funny scene with her and Joe in the bedroom when she’s asking him about the paint color, and all the colors are so close, you can’t tell them apart. That was reflected in her blouse too.”

Giants’ offense vs. Eagles’ defense: The Vic Fangio factor

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The New York Giants emerge as winners from their Week 8 BYE.

They're (relatively) rested and still riding the emotional wave of a stunning overtime win in Week 7. They return to action this week as 3.5-point favorites (per FanDuel) on the road against the Philadelphia Eagles. The Eagles have been in a tailspin over the last several weeks, losing the last six games. They've lost each of the last three games by at least 11 points, including a deflating 27-10 loss to the Washington Commanders last week.

The Giants are looking to build on the momentum of their upset win over the Houston Texans, while the Eagles are just hoping to stop the skid.

What can J.J. McCarthy and the Giants' offense expect from the Eagles' defense?

The last six (counting overtime) quarters of Giants' football have turned former 10th overall pick J.J. McCarthy into a national story. Most had written McCarthy off after his rookie year was upended by injury, his sophomore year was a disappointment, and he fell to QB3 in his third year.

Now? McCarthy has catapulted himself back into the "QB1" conversation with an electric 11 minutes against the New Orleans Saints and a game full of explosive plays against the Houston Texans. This of course, begs the question: Can he do it again?

The Eagles now have about a game and a half worth of tape on the McCarthy-led Giants. That isn't much to go on, but it may be enough to form a more effective gameplan.

The good news is that McCarthy also has two more weeks in the Giants' offense and more tme practicing with the Giants' starters. Not to mention, McCarthy will get to enjoy the presence of a (hopefully) healthier Andrew Thomas and Jermaine Eluemunor. It seems likely that Eagles defensive coordinator Vic Fangio will look to contain the explosive passing plays that defined his performance against the Texans. McCarthy excelled at throwing deep against Houston, delivering some legitimate "WOW" throws. He'll need to capitalize on the opportunities to do so against the Eagles, but can't count on them being regular occurrences. Instead, the Giants' intermediate passing game will need to be more consistent on a down-to-down basis while they work to create opportunities for explosive plays.

If there was a blemish on McCarthy's game against the Texans, it's that his passing success rate* was 25th out of 25 qualifying quarterbacks.

*Success rate is defined as the rate at which a play that gains a percentage of the yards needed to convert a first down. A "successful" first down picks up 40 percent of the yards to convert, a successful second down picks up 60 percent of the yards to convert, and a successful third down picks up 100 percent of the yards to convert.

Explosive plays are great and go a long way toward making it more likely that a team scores on a given drive. However, a team can't rely on explosive plays to consistently move the ball down the field. While the Giants did an excellent job of scheming 1-on-1 opportunities and good receiving windows downfield for McCarthy, it's not something they can live on. McCarthy will need to be more consistent in hitting underneath passes to keep the offense moving and on schedule. That will be particularly true if the Giants' run game struggles with both Cam Skattebo and Tyrone Tracy injured.

The Giants finally got their run game going against the Texans. While the Texans' defense isn't what it was in 2025, they still filed one of the NFL's best run defenses, which makes the Giants' 180-yard, 3-touchdown performance all the more impressive.

The Eagles' run defense has decidedly mediocre this year. And their high Run Stop Win Rate but average YPC and high rushing EPA allowed (+11.45, 28th in the NFL) suggest that they're prone to giving up explosive plays on the ground. That would be good news for the Giants in light of their performance against the Texans, but there are suddenly doubts about the Giants' backfield.

Tyrone Tracy landed on injured reserve with a hamstring injury suffered early in the game, while Cam Skattebo is currently not practicing with a hamstring injury of his own. Skattebo's injury was suffered at the end of his final carry of the game, the 42-yard run which set up Devin Singletary's 30-yard touchdown run to win the game. Skattebo ended that run early when he felt his hamstring tighten, and while he didn't seem overly concerned after the game, he has yet to practice.

That leaves Singletary and the newly-added Greg Desrosiers as the Giants' healthy backfield, with practice squad backs Phil Mafah and Eric Gray as possible additions.

The Giants offensive line will need to play well. While the Eagles' defense has struggled this year, the trio of Jalen Carter, Jordan Davis, and Moro Ojomo remains dangerous.

The Giants will also need to depend on McCarthy's play to help unlock their running game. Not only will more consistent passing success help take the pressure off the run game, but the threat of McCarthy's legs will help unlock the running game. The Giants used a slow mesh to simulate a read-option play on Tyrone Tracy's touchdown, freezing a key Texans' defender. The Giants could make (judicious) use of the read-option with McCarthy in this game — as they did with Jaxson Dart in Week 1 against Fangio acolyte Christian Parker — to help supplement their rushing attack.

The Giants could also make use of Darnell Mooney as a pseudo-running back with sweeps and screen plays. Mooney has been disappointing as a receiver, but his athleticism and explosiveness have been apparent when he gets the ball in his hands. Doing so on schemed touches could be a way the Giants look to augment their rushing attack and adding an explosive element on the ground.

The Giants have spent most of the 2026 season facing defenses we've called "Fangio Adjacent". That is, defenses designed and called by coaches who have either been inspired by Vic Fangio, or his acolytes.

We've already been over the iterations enough to know the general shape of the "Fangio" defense.

It's based around a 4-2-5 nickel formation under a quarters coverage. He makes heavy use of pattern matching principles to add man coverage to his zone schemes, as well as using post-snap rotations to create ambiguity and doubt in quarterbacks' reads. While his defenses frequently start out in a Cover-4 look, they often rotate to Cover 3, adding a defender to the box, or a hybrid coverage like Cover-6 or Cover-8* to give them a numbers advantage to the passing strength.

*I go into more detail on Cover-6 and Cover-8 in our Week 1 preview of the Cowboys' defense.

Fangio doesn't blitz all that often, just 22.5% of the time (26th in the NFL), instead trusting his defensive front to get home.

While the details may shift with different coordinators and defensive rosters, the basic shape of the "Fangio" defense remains consistent and has become one of the most popular philosophies in the NFL. However, the biggest advantage that Fangio himself has always been that it's his. More than any other coach, Fangio not only knows how is defense is able to attack offenses, but is also intimately aware of it's weaknesses and how offenses go about attacking his scheme.

We saw that first-hand last year when Fangio deviated from his scheme on Thursday Night Football. That game he opted to blitz Dart heavily in his second start.
