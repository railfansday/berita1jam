The Shenyang Aircraft Design and Research Institute, the developers of China’s J-35 fighter jet platform, used metal 3D printing to create large primary load-bearing structures for military aircraft applications. Global Times reports the structure is over 16.4 feet (five meters) in length, using segmented printing and fusion techniques.

Shenyang Aircraft Design and Research Institute, a research organization under the Aviation Industry Corporation of China (AVIC), has been working on 3D-printed aircraft components since 2003. The impressively large 3D-printed components are the culmination of decades of research and development of various metal additive manufacturing techniques.

The engineers are no strangers to large 3D-printed components: in 2005, they achieved secondary load-bearing structures; then, in 2012, large primary load-bearing titanium alloy structures, earning additive manufacturing expert Wang Huaming and his team China’s National Technological Invention Award.

Recently, however, the team created a primary load-bearing structure even larger, at over 16.4 feet. The massive component is not printed in one piece, as printing such a large structure faces warping and cracking issues, as stated in Progress and Challenges of Laser Direct Manufacturing of Large Titanium Structural Components, published in the Chinese Journal of Lasers. To avoid these problems, two techniques were employed: Segmented Additive Manufacturing (SAM) and additive fusion joining. It sounds like fancy words to say it was printed separately and welded, and it technically wouldn’t be wrong — but the key distinction is that it is part of the whole workflow; basically, metal 3D printing into the intentionally designed gaps between the components and creating one whole printed piece.

Additive manufacturing eliminates the need for expensive molds and multi-part construction. Titanium alloys are notoriously difficult to machine, cast components are usually weak, and joining parts using bolts and screws is additional labor — metal 3D printing solves all of this by creating a strong printed part in the desired shape in one large piece, minimizing the need for additional fasteners. By eliminating molds, the team can iterate much faster and create more complex structures that would otherwise not be possible with traditional manufacturing techniques.

The Grammy Awards are presented by the Recording Academy, a membership organization of music professionals. Its voting members include performers, songwriters, producers and engineers. The MTV Video Music Awards start from a different place. They honor music videos. The competing work is the visual piece built around a song, not the recording alone. MTV has described its voting as a blend of professional and fan input. Fans have voted online in many categories.

The Billboard Music Awards have been tied to Billboard's charts, including the Hot 100 and the Billboard 200. In chart-driven years, winners were based on measured performance such as sales, streaming, radio airplay and touring. Fan voting has also appeared in some categories in some years.

Any comparison of Grammys vs. VMAs vs. Billboard Music Awards comes down to three inputs: peer judgment, chart data and a mix of fan and professional votes. Each method captures something different. A peer vote reflects the judgment of colleagues in the industry. A fan vote reflects the preferences of the people who cast ballots. A chart reflects measured commercial activity.

None of these systems has stood still. The Recording Academy has revised its rules over time, including how nominations are decided. Bosses can set strict office schedules if they respect staff's family time

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Many leaders decide alone which days staff come into the office. Fewer people quit when managers also ask when their workday should end

A version of this article originally appeared in Quartz’s Leadership newsletter. Sign up here to get the latest leadership news and insights straight to your inbox.

Some of the country's largest companies have spent the past few years ordering people back to their desks. At the end of 2024, Amazon $AMZN's chief executive, Andy Jassy, told corporate staff to come in five days a week. In the throes of the COVID-19 pandemic, Goldman Sachs $GS CEO David Solomon said working from home was "an aberration that we're going to correct as soon as possible." Many workers protested. But both men were determined to have employees working the same way they had before the pandemic.

Millions of office workers spent more than a year working from home. Many of them stopped working at the same hour every day and stopped answering messages late at night. Many more found meaning in their jobs. Once they were ordered back to the office, though, they had to decide whether they could maintain the same schedule without looking like they were slacking off.

Employees put up with return-to-office mandates if their manager accepts the schedule they built at home, according to Harvard Business School's Grace Mele-Cormier and colleagues. The problem is less about the time they have to come in and more about settling what time people can leave.

Mele-Cormier and his team found that employees tend to quit less often and earn slightly higher performance reviews when they're allowed to maintain the same working schedules as before. But many employees still fear that their boss will think they care less about the job if they go home at the same hour every day. They become less tense once they know their manager is on board, according to HBR's researchers.

The problem is that many managers never tell employees which hours they're expected to work. Their silence on the topic forces staff to assume they have to continue answering emails and messages at all hours of the day and night, just as they had done before the pandemic. Some employees end up leaving their roles for ones that have a clearer schedule.

Executives see the resignations, but they rarely learn that employees quit over their working hours. To executives, some teams seem to lose people for no reason while other teams don't.

Many managers never ask employees what time they need to leave each day. They assume people coming back to the office want one fixed schedule from their boss, with the same start and end time for everyone. If a manager asks each person what hours they need, the team ends up with different schedules for different people, and many managers worry they'll lose control of the team's day.

They also fear their staff will think the boss has no plan for how the team should work. Asking, though, actually leaves a manager with a better plan, according to Dan Cable, a London Business School professor. By hearing employees out, managers can spot the people most likely to quit over their working hours while there is still time to change their schedules. But Cable warns against giving employees decision-making powers. "You get a voice, not a vote," Cable told an audience at the school. "Listening shapes better decisions. It does not remove the leader's responsibility to decide."

Acting on what employees say doesn't always mean giving them what they asked for. That applies in almost every situation. After Jennifer Dulski and her leadership team laid off employees at Change.org, Dulski, then its president and chief operating officer, met with the remaining employees in several countries, according to the Society for Human Resource Management. She couldn't bring back the jobs the company cut. So her team started showing employees the company's finances instead. Once employees saw the numbers, Dulski said, they understood why their leaders had cut staff.

Other leaders change a plan or cover a cost after employees speak up. Geno Cutolo spent his first 90 days as president of staffing firm Adecco North America meeting employees across the business. He then redesigned a reorganization Adecco had already planned, to try to remove many of the frustrations and obstacles employees described to him. "It's important to act on what you hear," Cutolo said.

At Safeguard Global, the HR team heard that European employees were worried about winter heating bills, so the company paid them a monthly stipend through the winter, chief people officer Katherine Loranger said. In each case, employees could point to something that changed because they spoke up.

Employees expect the same proof after a manager asks about their hours. A manager can approve working hours, move team meetings to times everyone can make, or explain why a schedule can't change. If a manager asks and then changes and explains nothing, employees conclude the manager never had any intention to listen in the first place. Employees then stop telling managers what they need to know, and managers can't possibly tell which employees are thinking about leaving until they hand in their notice.

Can America Build More Housing Without Gutting Historic Protections? Inside the Senate’s Permitting Battle

A sweeping permitting reform package moved closer to a final Senate vote Monday, when lawmakers voted 70–27 to limit debate on legislation that could weaken a federal safeguard for historic places.

At issue is Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act, which requires federal agencies to consider how projects they fund, approve, or permit might affect historic properties—and consult with those affected before proceeding.

The proposed changes would exempt most projects whose only federal connection is financial assistance, while imposing tighter deadlines and restricting legal challenges.

As the nation confronts a shortage of 4.03 million homes and an aging energy grid, supporters argue that reducing regulatory hurdles will accelerate infrastructure development. But preservation advocates warn the changes could leave communities with less influence over what gets built—and what gets lost.

"Preservation is not a hindrance to progress," Heather Veneziano, director of Tulane University's Historic Preservation Program, told Realtor.com® during a recent walking interview through Bayou St. John, one of New Orleans' oldest neighborhoods.

Rather than keeping neighborhoods frozen in time, she argued, preservation can help communities determine how they evolve.

"Keeping things static is impossible," Veneziano said.

New Orleans provides a particularly salient standpoint from which to understand Veneziano’s perspective.

The city is home to 26 nationally recognized historic districts encompassing more than 45,000 properties and roughly 40% of its urbanized area, according to the city. Indeed, its architectural heritage is so extensive that it became the only entire city included on the World Monuments Fund's Irreplaceable America list.

Veneziano helped lead the nomination, informed in part by the aftermath of Hurricane Katrina and the connections to place that could not be rebuilt elsewhere.

"You lose all your really strong ties and connections to place, but also to people," she said of the communities uprooted after the devastating 2005 superstorm. "And places don't matter without people."

But Veneziano sees the buildings themselves as part of what makes those communities viable.

"It's an economic driver for the community, it's housing at a basic level for the community," she said. "Those are the containers for all of the services that happen in your community."

Along Bayou St. John, Veneziano pointed to the Pitot House, built in 1799, as an example of another kind of value older buildings retain.

Its elevated living quarters reflect a time when residents built knowing water would eventually come, while its surviving materials offer lessons in constructing homes suited to Louisiana's flood risks.

Yet, those lessons might have been erased had preservationists not found a way to accommodate another pressing community need.

In 1964, the religious order that owned the property needed land for a girls' school. Rather than allow its demolition, preservationists raised money to move the house 220 feet down the street on flatbed trucks.

The compromise allowed both the school and the house to continue serving the community. And today, the deal serves as a reminder of how preservation can accommodate development rather than prevent it.

Not every historic property can be relocated, however, and the expense of finding alternatives can test even the most committed to protecting a neighborhood's housing resources.

In March, Jane Place Neighborhood Sustainability Initiative, a community land trust dedicated to permanently affordable housing, sought permission to demolish a century-old shotgun house in Mid-City.

The nonprofit argued that rehabilitation would be prohibitively expensive and higher-density construction would better serve the property.

Preservation authorities disagreed, finding insufficient evidence that the house was beyond repair. Jane Place eventually withdrew its appeal without a finalized replacement development plan.

The spat concerned local demolition rules, not Section 106. But it exposed competing interpretations of how best to protect a neighborhood's housing resources: Preserve an existing home or make room for more?

Veneziano acknowledged that unclear local requirements can frustrate homeowners trying to maintain or renovate their properties.

"If your municipality doesn't have their act together and isn't clear about what their guidelines are," she said, "it can be very challenging to homeowners."

In a city facing an estimated shortage of 44,000 affordable rental units, the case for preserving old buildings can be difficult to reconcile with the urgent need for new homes. But elsewhere in the Big Easy, a long-vacant public building offers another approach.

In September, a century-old firehouse reopened after decades of vacancy, transformed into seven affordable apartments above an early-childhood education center.

The mixed-use redevelopment cost approximately $8 million, including $1.35 million in state historic tax credits and $725,000 in federal disaster recovery funds.

"The Louisiana Avenue Firehouse shows us that demolition of historic buildings is not always the answer," Louisiana Housing Corporation Executive Director Kevin Delahoussaye said.

But the very public investment that helped save the building also triggered Section 106 review—and that additional requirement is what housing advocates argue can make federally assisted developments more expensive and difficult to finance.

Their argument is simple: More reviews take more time. More time costs more money. And combined, that can make a project not worth it.

YIMBY Action, which supports the proposed Section 106 exemption, estimates that at least $10 billion annually in federal housing and community development funding could move faster under the broader permitting package, citing delays that can jeopardize financing or discourage developers from accepting public money altogether.

Veneziano emphasized that preservationists aren't simply trying to "save this fancy house." Their work, she explained, is "really community engagement"—helping residents recognize and protect histories that might otherwise be overlooked.

That concern brought hundreds of preservationists, civil rights advocates, and tribal representatives to the U.S. Capitol on Nov. 9 to oppose changes to Section 106.

"Our sacred places are living places of prayer," Harry Antonio Jr., governor of the Pueblo of Laguna in New Mexico, told the crowd.

The Senate proposal would narrow consideration of certain effects on historic places, including noise and visual impacts, raising concerns about the protection of sacred landscapes, burial grounds, and other culturally significant sites.

Veneziano pointed to a conflict farther upriver in Wallace, LA, where federal scrutiny exposed problems with a developer's assessment of a proposed grain terminal.

There, descendants of enslaved residents opposed the project, which would have been built near historic plantations, burial grounds, and their predominantly Black community.

A ProPublica investigation revealed that an archaeological consultant's original findings about potential harm to historic sites had been stripped from a report prepared for the developer. The Army Corps of Engineers subsequently rejected the assessment as insufficient and demanded further investigation.

In April 2024, the Corps determined that noise, light pollution, and the terminal's industrial appearance would adversely affect Wallace's historic character—precisely the kinds of impacts the Senate proposal would subject to a narrower standard of review.

The company behind the terminal abandoned the project later that year, blaming prolonged permitting delays. Residents celebrated the outcome as a victory.

Such lengthy reviews, however, may be the exception rather than the rule.

An October analysis by Virginia's Department of Historic Resources found that 85% of nearly 9,900 projects completed Section 106 review within 30 days. Just 6% took longer than 90 days, primarily because they required additional surveys or involved potential harm to historic properties.

Those figures aren't necessarily representative nationwide, nor do they establish whether reviews actually delayed construction.

"If you're trying to get a project through, yeah, it's annoying," Veneziano acknowledged.

Still, Section 106 doesn't prohibit development. It requires agencies to consider the consequences and potential alternatives before proceeding.

And as Congress weighs how to accelerate construction, lawmakers will also confront a more fundamental question: Who gets to determine what a community can afford to lose?

Veneziano posed a question of her own: "How do we take the best of what we have, and build upon it into the future?"

Teacher prep programs probed in Education Department’s latest anti-DEI push

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The agency said diversification efforts disregard “merit,” but critics say the programs level the playing field for historically excluded candidates.

The U.S. Department of Education opened a string of investigations into teacher preparation programs on Nov. 13, alleging that they discriminate on the basis of race under Title VI or sex under Title IX.

The investigations mark one of the first times the department's Office for Civil Rights has used civil rights protections to challenge diversification efforts in teacher scholarships, hiring, training and recruitment practices.

The programs at the center of the investigations are aimed at recruiting historically underrepresented groups in the teaching profession, such as men and teachers of color.

“Race, national origin, or sex should not decide who will shape America’s next generation, yet many states and universities seem to prioritize recruiting and training ‘minority’ candidates rather than evaluating candidates based solely on merit,” said Assistant Secretary for Civil Rights Kimberly Richey in a Nov. 13 statement.

“Education agencies and institutions that consider race, national origin, or sex as a means to withhold opportunities in selecting, supporting, and preparing teaching candidates violate federal law and compromise education excellence."

Richey concluded that students should be "judged on their hard work and merit, not prejudged by the color of their skin or their sex.”

EdTrust, a nonprofit that advocates for racial and economic equity in American education, said in a statement to K-12 Dive that the Trump administration is using civil rights protections meant to "dismantle discrimination and expand opportunity" to instead "undermine pathways into teaching for people historically excluded from the profession betrays that purpose."

“The administration’s false divide between ‘merit’ and diversity perpetuates the damaging suggestion that teachers of color are less qualified or less deserving," said Eric Duncan, director of Pre-K-12 policy of EdTrust, in the Nov. 16 statement. "It discounts their skills, expertise, and contributions while ignoring the systemic barriers that have denied talented people opportunities for generations. Scholarships that address those barriers help ensure that family wealth does not determine who can become a teacher."

The Education Department's investigations, Duncan said, "risk turning civil rights enforcement into a tool for preserving the very inequities it should confront.”

CUNY, which houses the NYC Men Teach Program being investigated by the department, said in a statement to K-12 Dive that it was "proud of its role educating the teachers of tomorrow and our programs are open to all students regardless of race, gender or national origin."

According to the city's website, the program was created around 2015 in collaboration with the federal Education Department and then-Mayor Bill de Blasio with the goal of putting an additional 1,000 men of color into the city’s teacher pipeline.

A March 2026 evaluation of the program conducted for the NYC mayor's office by Westat, which provides research services for government agencies, shows that the program contributed over 3,100 new male teachers of color to New York City Public Schools between school years 2015–16 and 2023–24.

The American Association of Colleges for Teacher Education said in a Nov. 16 email to K-12 Dive, “Strengthening and diversifying the educator pipeline is essential to addressing persistent teacher shortages and ensuring every PK-12 student has access to well-prepared, effective teachers. Decades of research confirm that a diverse educator workforce benefits all students academically, socially, and emotionally." AACTE added that it does not comment on active federal inquiries involving specific institutions or state agencies.

While the investigations are some of the first of their kind by OCR, they build on previous efforts by the Trump administration to weed out DEI in teacher preparation programs — especially in the wake of the U.S. Supreme Court’s 2023 ruling in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard.

Some of the earliest federal grants terminated in 2025 by the administration were under the Supporting Effective Educator Development Grant Program, the Teacher Quality Partnership Program and the Teacher and School Leader Incentive Program, due to what it said was the promotion of "divisive ideologies". The department admitted in a March court filing that it had cancelled 104 out of 109 active TQP and SEED grants.

The loss of over 100 federal teacher training grants last year caused at least 127 teacher candidates to drop out of their programs and another 259 accepted candidates to not enroll after all, according to survey findings released in October.

Those cuts were preceded by an anti-DEI directive that has since been heavily litigated and was most recently blocked in court in September.

That anti-DEI letter specifically called for agency personnel to review all new grant awards and issued grants to ensure those federal dollars “do not fund discriminatory practices — including in the form of DEI.” The directive added that “Illegal DEI policies and practices can violate both the letter and purpose of Federal civil rights law and conflict with the Department’s policy of prioritizing merit, fairness, and excellence in education.”

The Justice Department in August 2025 also warned districts they could be exposed to legal liability by asking job applicants how their “cultural background informs their teaching,” using recruitment strategies targeting candidates from specific geographic areas or racial backgrounds, and asking job candidates to describe how they overcame obstacles.

Since the Supreme Court’s decision in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard, civil rights groups have raised concerns that it would set back educational equity efforts even outside of race-conscious admissions. That decision undid race-conscious policies in college admissions, and the Education Department under Trump has used it to justify scaling back other diversity, equity and inclusion initiatives in education.

Scholarship availability, teacher pipelines and student affinity groups were among the top areas beyond college access that advocates were concerned could be impacted in the wake of the ruling.

Aaron Connolly speaks to Sky Sports about his breakthrough at Brighton and the pitfalls and setbacks that followed on his journey in the game. He is now rebuilding in League One at Leyton Orient and determined to forge a career he can be proud of.

It is easy to forget that Aaron Connolly is still only 26, considering everything he has already experienced.

Just over seven years have passed since that magical full Premier League debut, scoring twice for Brighton against Spurs at the age of 19 in 2019.

In the time since, there have been injuries that have damaged his progress in football and obstacles in his life beyond the pitch.

"It's been tough," Connolly - now at Leyton Orient - tells Sky Sports. "It's probably been well documented that I haven't dealt with setbacks very well before.

"I love playing football. It's what I've done my whole life. So when I can't, it's obviously difficult.

"I've had a spell of injuries and interruptions. It seems to have been the way for the last three or four years of my career.

"At Hull, I was doing well, scoring goals, then got an injury and didn't really get back in.

"Then I left Hull, went to Sunderland, started one of my first games against Millwall, scored and then picked up a little injury and missed the next game. It's just that momentum I never seemed to get.

"Then obviously last year [at Orient] I had that momentum for about 15 to 20 games. I started scoring and assisting, and then tore my quad. I was in a bit of a rush to come back and then obviously did my shoulder and needed surgery again.

"Hopefully, I'm doing everything I can to stay injury-free right now, but with football you never know. There could just be that one late tackle or slip or something. Hopefully, fingers crossed, I'm past that stage now."

Connolly knows, injuries aside, he hasn't always helped himself. Temptations and distractions away from the pitch halted the progress in his late teens and early 20s.

A reputation was built that he is now working hard to shake off.

"There's always going to be football fans who have a certain opinion of you without knowing you," he admits. "I burst onto the scene a few years ago, and it feels like a long time ago now.

"There are people at the highest level because they live and breathe football and do everything properly. I didn't do that.

"I probably worked as hard as I could to get to that position to score those goals against Tottenham. After that, I kind of came away from everything I'd done to get myself in that position.

"Now, obviously, I've realised, and I'm hoping it's not too late to have realised, that kind of lifestyle and that attitude towards the game isn't going to get me very far.

"I probably believed the hype straight away and thought I'd scored two on my full debut, so I'm going to score 30 in the Premier League without dedicating myself to it, being in the gym, being first in and last to leave.

"I let myself down a lot, and that's probably why I ended up getting injured.

"I'd be late into training, be the first one to leave, never really stay and work on my craft or anything. I kind of thought I'd signed a long-term deal here now, so I've got five years to play with. That five years just went, and every footballer will tell you it goes quickly. It does.

"I've realised now that I need to dedicate myself to football. Sometimes when I'm at the training ground now, I come in first and leave last, and I'm sitting there thinking, 'why has it only clicked for me now that that's what you need to do in football?'

"Probably from 23 to 25 years old, I was just looking back on when I was 19, thinking, 'what did I do? Why did I do it for so long?'

"Even when I was injured, I still wanted to do the same thing because when I was at Brighton, if I got a little injury, that injury would take six weeks when it should have been two because of the way I was doing things. I wasn't doing everything properly.

"But listen, that's just the journey. If every player had the same journey, it would be a boring sport.

"I can never look back and say I wish I hadn't scored those two goals straight away, because I might not have ended up at Sunderland, for example, without that kind of CV behind me because of the impact I had early on at Brighton.

"It is difficult to think that I wish it hadn't happened. I do agree it probably was the worst thing that could have happened, looking back now, but at the time it felt like the best thing ever. And some of the issues probably would have come about eventually anyway."

Part of Connolly's journey has been overcoming an alcohol addiction, something he opened up about a couple of years ago on World Mental Health Day, while he was with Sunderland.

"I don't want to be that kind of player that's known for these types of issues, but also the reason I need to remember why I did the interview a few years ago was just to help as many people as possible," he says. "I know in my family my mum's dad struggled a lot with it, so I'd always happily speak about it.

"I'm in a good place with all of it. Obviously, it is a difficult thing to wake up and realise I can't do what a lot of my team-mates can do, which is go out on a Saturday, have maybe a couple of sociable drinks and go home. I know that's not me.

"Trying to find different things at the weekend to entertain myself when all I knew before was Saturday game, Saturday out, Sunday out. That was kind of what I'd do.

"I don't miss it, that's for sure. I have a better life and better relationships with people now without it.

"And my football, if I was drinking and going out, I wouldn't be able to do any of it, at any level.

"I wouldn't have the relationships I have with people. I always have time for everybody now, whereas before I was quite selfish and didn't have time for many people.

"I was probably miserable a lot in different clubs and different scenarios. It would be the only thing on my mind.

"I can't think about whether people like me or like what I've done or opened up about that situation. But for me, it helped, and all the messages and responses I received helped a lot of other people as well. That's what I've done it for."

Connolly is keen to now try and help younger players learn from his own pathway, even if those at Orient aren't quite exposed to what he was in the Premier League.

"Everyone needs to make mistakes," he says. "I'm not going to know all the answers straight away when I was 19 and getting paid all this money and playing in front of 50,000 every week.

"People are texting you saying, 'I can sort you this, I can do this for you', the minute you start playing in the Premier League.

"It took a lot of adjusting and getting used to that. I'm probably still not used to that kind of stuff because when you're growing up in Ireland, you don't get that kind of special treatment with things like that.

"But I always sit with the academy players in the physio room, and if they ever want to come and ask me something, they know they can. I'd like to think I'd give them as much advice as I can.

"They all want to play in the first team, but they're currently on loan in the Isthmian Premier, and they're thinking it's the end of the world. Why am I here at 18 or 19?

"I'll remind them that some journeys are completely different. I was in the Premier League at 19 and now I'm in League One. You might be in the Isthmian at 19 and could be in the Premier League by 26. There are different journeys.

"I always used to have a little bit of back and forth with Adam Webster at Brighton when I'd say, 'where were you at 19?' He was playing for Portsmouth in League One and Two. He'd say, 'well, I'm here at 26. Let's see where you are at 26'.

"He was right. They probably saw it coming before I did.

"I always try to give them that kind of advice. I know they do come in and sit with me and ask me questions, so I just want to be that kind of open space for them as well."

For Connolly, there is a lot of time to get back to where he believes he can be. Right now, he just wants to get a run of fitness and form with Leyton Orient.

Most importantly, he is determined to forge a career with no more regrets.

"A lot of people when I was 19 probably wouldn't have thought I'd be in League One, but that was probably one of the reasons why I'd end up dropping down the leagues to where I am now." he says.

"Like I said before, if this is going to be my level and this is where I end up at 35 or 36, then that's fine. At least I know I've given it everything, and then it's down to whether there are better players than me and better players in my position going ahead of me. I can accept that.

"But if I'd kept living the way I was and not appreciating the game, I'd probably look back when I'm 34 or 35 thinking, I wish I'd realised this 10 years earlier.

"I don't want to look back on my career with regret. You always hear that when I was 18, there used to be people coming into the training ground for educational talks and saying that.

"I'm just looking forward to seeing what I can do at 100 per cent fitness."
