The move comes amid a broader strategy shift at JLR, Jaguar and Land Rover’s parent company. In the past year, both brands have focused on launching electric-powered flagships, the most buzzworthy of which is the Jaguar Type 01 sedan.

Range Rover has launched the Range Rover Electric and is preparing a Range Rover Sport Electric, effectively electrifying its core lineup. A new entry-level Range Rover GT is also on the way and will effectively replace the Evoque and Velar once it hits production as a 2028 model.

Like the Jaguar Type 01, the Range Rover GT takes the shape of a long and low sedan-like vehicle with a minimalist interior and an all-wheel-drive electric powertrain. The Range Rover GT will launch first as an EV, but the company says the platform is capable of hosting gas and hybrid powertrains as well. Crypto giant Binance says North Korean hackers used fake social media profiles and the lure of a potential job offer as part of a months-long scheme aimed at tricking an employee into downloading malicious software onto their laptop.

Researchers have repeatedly warned about long-running social engineering attacks that can go on for months.

This latest attack allowed the hackers to patiently worm their way into Binance’s systems to steal a record $6.3 billion in crypto last week, the company has now confirmed. The hack, disclosed in an updated statement posted on X on Tuesday, prompted the world’s largest crypto exchange to suspend customer withdrawals across its network.

The FBI and blockchain investigators have blamed North Korea for the heist. The country has been long accused of carrying out massive cryptocurrency thefts in recent years to fund its internationally sanctioned nuclear weapons program.

In its latest statement, Binance said that North Korean hackers, posing as recruiters hiring for a job, contacted a software engineer who works on wallets at the company. Following two interviews with the purported company, the engineer was instructed to download an apparent coding assignment as part of the interview process. When opened, the assignment contained malicious code that compromised the engineer’s work-issued laptop. Binance said its security tools did not detect the malware.

That foothold in Binance’s network allowed the hackers to access an internal tool capable of taking over the work computers of two other engineers on the wallet team. One of those engineers had unwittingly downloaded a tampered app containing password-stealing malware months earlier after searching for the software in search engine results. While Binance replaced that compromised engineer’s laptop, the hackers used the engineer’s stolen credentials to break into the computer of a third engineer, whose job allowed them access to systems capable of controlling billions of dollars in crypto.

Binance said the attack “should not have happened,” and that it had improved its network security following the theft.

The hack underscores an ongoing tactic in the North Korean regime’s repertoire of attacks targeting the crypto industry and high-net worth individuals. Researchers have warned for years that North Korean hackers pose as venture capitalists, investors, and recruiters using dossiers of stolen American identities to trick employees into granting access to their systems.

These attacks are widely known across the crypto industry and have been the subject of repeated warnings from the U.S. government and cybersecurity giants in recent years. Some half of all intrusions at U.S. tech companies last year were attributed to North Koreans posing as remote IT workers and fake recruiters.

By Binance’s own account, the company faced at least 57 suspected attacks from North Korea, which it said reflect the “persistent threat posed by state-sponsored actors targeting the cryptocurrency industry.”

But how the hackers were able to convince the engineer into downloading malware given the highly publicized threat, and bypass security weaknesses in Binance’s own network, will likely be the focus of investigators’ attention.

While the case exposes how North Korean hackers use social engineering, such as offers of employment, to trick employees into granting access to their systems, it’s ultimately the responsibility of the company to ensure its systems cannot run unauthorized or untrusted software that could cause a compromise.

Binance spokesperson Ross O’Leary referred to the company’s public statement but declined to answer TechCrunch’s questions on the record about the incident. Those questions included how Binance prevents unauthorized or malicious code from running on its employees’ laptops, if at all, and whether any third parties had performed cybersecurity audits of Binance’s network prior to the hack.

The company said it would bring in cybersecurity experts to review the incident and has put new security measures in place to prevent a repeat incident.

The heist remains the largest theft of crypto in history.

Google is cutting schools off from Search if they restrict AI

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Google is turning off Gemini and Search by default for K-12 student accounts until school administrators enable them

David Paul Morris/Bloomberg via Getty Images

Google $GOOGL is cutting off K-12 student access to Google Search at schools that do not enable its Gemini AI products, according to NBC News. The company announced the changes to its education products on Tuesday, including updated controls for Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search.

Chris Phillips, Google's vice president and general manager of education, wrote in a blog post that access to those products "will be entirely off for K-12 student accounts until school administrators explicitly turn them on," according to NBC News. Before Tuesday's change, Gemini was switched on by default in any school that used Google Classroom.

"We believe schools should have control over AI in their classrooms and in providing administrators with controls to help them manage their approach," Phillips wrote, according to NBC News.

Google said it is bringing Guided Learning to Google Classroom globally and adding Guided Learning capabilities to Google Search. Administrators who activate Search can also enable a "guided learning mode" that recognizes when a student drops homework questions into the search box, according to The Wall Street Journal. Under that setting, the chatbot is built to walk students through a problem one stage at a time without handing over the final answer.

Districts that offer Search cannot fully turn off its AI Overview and AI Mode features, according to The Wall Street Journal.

Randi Weingarten, president of the American Federation of Teachers, criticized the decision. "It is unfortunate that Google has prioritized its business interests over the education of our children," she told NBC News. Josh Golin, executive director of the advocacy group Fairplay, described Google's approach in a statement as an effort to "strong-arm schools into using its AI products," according to NBC News.

The change follows pressure over Google's AI tools in classrooms. Sen. Richard Blumenthal, a Connecticut Democrat, and Sen. Josh Hawley, a Missouri Republican, sent a letter to Google CEO Sundar Pichai asking what the company knows about how its AI products affect children's mental health and learning. They set a Nov. 2 deadline for a reply.

The senators cited a September investigation by The Wall Street Journal, which relied on more than 60 interviews with clinicians, parents, students, school officials and educators. It found that many students had come to lean on Google's AI tools for both their schoolwork and their emotional needs.

In early October, 13 parent advocacy organizations, including the Digital Childhood Institute and Schools Beyond Screens, sent an open letter urging districts to disable student use of both Gemini and YouTube, according to NBC News.
