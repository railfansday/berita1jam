JetBlue is raising checked bag fees again — to as much as $70 for a first bag

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The airline cited higher fuel and operating costs; the new fees apply to tickets booked on or after Nov. 3

JetBlue Airways raised its checked bag fees on Tuesday, with a first bag costing as much as $70 on peak travel dates when the fee is paid less than a day before the flight leaves, according to CNBC.

For a non-peak period, a first bag in economy costs $50 if paid for more than a day ahead of the flight and $60 if paid inside that 24-hour window, according to CNBC. During peak periods such as the holidays and summer, the fee is $55 in advance and $70 within 24 hours, up from $49 for the advance fee.

The new rates apply to any ticket purchased starting Nov. 3, according to Thrifty Traveler. Customers who booked earlier pay the old rates. A second bag costs $70 off-peak and $75 at peak when paid in advance, and a third bag costs $200 regardless of travel dates.

The first bag fee climbed from $60 to $80 each way for travelers buying the lowest-priced basic economy ticket on a transatlantic trip to Europe, according to Thrifty Traveler.

Customers who carry JetBlue's co-branded credit card or have elite frequent flyer status are exempt from the fees, according to CNBC. JetBlue Mint and BlueFirst passengers also receive a minimum of one complimentary checked bag, according to Thrifty Traveler.

"We're adjusting checked bag fees to help offset rising fuel and operating costs," JetBlue said in a statement, according to CNBC. "This helps us keep our base fares competitive while giving customers the choice to pay for a checked bag only if they need one."

The cost of jet fuel has approximately doubled this year, ever since the war in Iran began in February. Jet fuel was priced at $4.44 per gallon in the U.S. Gulf of Mexico market on Tuesday.

JetBlue was the first major U.S. airline to increase baggage charges following the outbreak of the war in late March, according to Thrifty Traveler. Every U.S. carrier matched that increase within weeks.

Dominic Fike Might Be Growing Up, But He’s Angsty as Ever

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Across How to Quit Smoking, it’s clear that Dominic Fike has mastered the art of a distinctively Dominic Fike sound. It’s in his distorted, sunburned guitars, in the quake of his voice as he sing-raps, in all of the Florida nostalgia baked into his lyrics. This is his third album, but it doesn’t feel so far removed from the 22-year-old kid who sparked a label war after uploading low-fi songs to SoundCloud in the late 2010s — and who went wildly viral for hits like “3 Nights” and “Babydoll,” the latter of which is still skyrocketing up the charts. The angst and anguish and the ability to come up with a damn good hook are still there, even if the pressures and the stress of adulthood have taken on aching new heights.

How to Quit Smoking finds Fike at 30, now a dad and still figuring a lot of things out. Throughout his past albums, he’s detailed pretty clearly what he’s gone through: Fike recorded his first EP while under house arrest for battery of a police officer; when it started blowing up, he was in jail for violating his parole. Once Columbia won the label bidding war and signed Fike for a reported $4 million, he used a lot of that money to bail his mom out of prison, where she was serving time on drug charges. (“Everybody looking at me like I’m not invested/Bitch, you know how hard I was stressing?” he raps on “How Much Is Weed,” the jittery track that opened 2023’s excellent Sunburn.)

He’s outlined his journey with sobriety and addiction in his music, all while dealing with loneliness and some highly public breakups. Amazon rewrites its software engineering playbook for an era when AI writes the code

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

by Todd Bishop on Nov 3, 2026 at 12:26 pmNovember 3, 2026 at 12:36 pm

Last month, a small team inside Amazon Web Services called Velocity Labs went from concept to the public release of a new open-source AI model in less than a week.

The group, whose existence hasn’t been previously reported, was formed in April. Velocity Labs has about 35 people, most of them in Seattle. The idea is to think beyond Amazon’s normal product-oriented approach, building quick prototypes in areas of AI that the company sees as underexplored and increasingly important to customers.

“We have noticed how the open-source community, and some of our competitors, experiment with new ideas, and deliver initial products fast,” the company says in job listings. “With Velocity Labs we are building an organization that can move faster, explore more, and make sure AWS is at the cutting edge of innovation in agentic AI.”

Inside the group, scientists and engineers work together, and they’re encouraged to cross over: engineers engage in research, and scientists do engineering work.

Velocity Labs is an example of the ways Amazon is trying “to create organizations that we think are going to be better suited to working with this new set of tools,” said Marc Brooker, an AWS vice president and distinguished engineer who leads the team.

For the vast majority of Amazon’s developers, he said, AI agents now write almost all the code that would have previously been written by hand.

Redefining the role: The team is part of Amazon’s larger rethinking of software development for the AI era, laid out in revised guidelines that the company’s engineering leaders sent to its developers earlier this year.

A letter that accompanied the guidelines, published last week under the byline of Michael “Terk” Terkowitz, a VP and distinguished engineer in Amazon’s stores business, advises engineers to learn AI now and to stay close to their customers and products.

The letter acknowledges that as Amazon has grown, some engineers and managers have become “less connected to their customers and business.”

Amazon has never published its underlying role guidelines for software engineers, and it declined to provide a copy of the revised version for this article. Brooker, one of the authors of the accompanying letter, said in an interview that the update emphasizes measuring engineers and teams by outcomes rather than inputs.

“We had seen these anti-patterns in the industry of folks really focusing on how many tokens are you spending, how many lines of code are you committing,” Brooker said, explaining that those kinds of measures don’t equate to customer value.

Asked whether AI use is part of performance reviews or promotions, Brooker said, “Not directly in a metrics sense.” It could factor in, he said, to the extent that it makes someone a stronger performer.

The letter doesn’t dismiss usage metrics entirely. Amazon has found that they “correlate well with the outputs that matter” and expects that to continue “for some time,” it says.

But what’s important, the letter says, is to use them “with care and judgement.”

Amazon engineers have described pressure to use AI tools. Three developers told The New York Times last year that managers were pushing them to use AI, raising output goals and becoming less forgiving about deadlines, with some coders saying their jobs had begun to resemble warehouse work. Amazon has set a goal for more than 80% of its developers to use AI each week, the Financial Times reported.

In May, Amazon shut down an internal leaderboard called KiroRank — which ranked employees by how much they used Kiro, the company’s AI coding tool — after finding that some were using the tool excessively just to climb the rankings, the FT reported.

Asked whether that episode was part of the impetus for the revised guidelines, Brooker said he didn’t recall the leaderboard or the details surrounding it.

Letter accompanying the revised SDE role guidelines

How the work has changed: Two years ago, most Amazon developers were still writing their code by hand, Brooker said. Today, “for the vast majority of our developer population, almost all of their implementation code is agent generated rather than human written,” he said.

“That is a massive change in the way of working for software engineers,” he said.

Brooker said he used to spend much of his time in a traditional code editor or the command line, and now spends a lot more of it working with agent tools such as Kiro, Kiro Crew and Anthropic’s Claude Code, calling it “a complete change of tools.”

For the company, the transition hasn’t been seamless. In March, Amazon acknowledged that an engineer who followed inaccurate advice from an AI agent contributed to one of several outages on its retail site, though the company disputed a Financial Times report that junior and mid-level engineers would need senior engineers to sign off on AI-assisted changes.

Amazon also pushed back on an earlier FT report that a 13-hour disruption to an AWS service in December happened after engineers let its Kiro AI coding tool make changes, and the tool decided to “delete and recreate the environment.” Amazon blamed user error — a misconfigured role — and said it has since added safeguards, including mandatory peer review for production access.

Still, the letter acknowledges that today’s AI tools won’t help in every situation, and it says that’s OK as long as engineers can explain why.

“If your work does not benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing,” the letter says. “An engineer who can explain where AI does not yet help their work is demonstrating the judgement we are asking for.”

The model that Velocity Labs released last month, Strands Decider 2B, is designed to make quick, narrow decisions inside AI agents, such as which tool to use or where to route a request. It’s based on Qwen, an open model from Alibaba, and was released Oct. 1 along with the recipe used to train it.

Brooker built the original version on his own GPU at home after the startup TypeSafe AI unveiled Jev, a similar decision model, on Sept. 15, he wrote on his blog. “Every line of code was written by an agent,” he wrote, though he tried to make sure “the core ideas and insights were mine, or at least I understood them.”

Asked whether AI will mean fewer engineers at Amazon, Brooker said, “I don’t think it changes the number of engineers.” The company wants engineers who take ownership, are curious and can learn quickly outside their areas of expertise, he said.

AI isn’t the right answer for every problem, Brooker said.

As the election season comes to a merciful close, maybe the real treasure is the absurd spots we met along the way.

There are many things the country can’t agree on, as will be evident when results for a series of close Congressional races begin rolling in Tuesday night.But there is one thing we can agree on: the campaign ads need to stop. They are everywhere, like ants at a picnic, or aunts in a political WhatsApp chat. And they need to go.On Wednesday they will, but not before putting a big dent in our collective psyche. Republicans and Democrats combined to spend $11.6 billion on campaign ads, a record-breaking sum for any cycle. (Elon Musk himself spent nearly half a billion dollars on GOP candidates, which may or may not turn out to be a better investment than the $1 billion SpaceX spent on Skydance to maybe not distribute a documentary.) Imagine if that $11.6 billion was spent on, like, going to the movies. Actually, that figure is literally $3 billion more than Americans shelled out at the box office last year and on par with the all-time $11 billion-plus highs of the late 2010s.Of course all that money led to studios producing some pretty cool movies. Did this cash yield any meaningful cultural output? Is there some hidden masterpiece, some Madison Avenue Black Panther, to show for all the outlays?Well, not exactly. But we do have Obama shooting hoops in a gym, a senior citizen shooting an intruder in her home and Steve Hilton going softcore. Here are the six wild anti-classics this cycle yielded, and what you might have missed if you weren’t in these localities/was watching anything more sane including a Jets game.—1. Shooting Cranks

“Four score and seven years ago.” Ask not what your country can you do for you.” “You break into my house, you’re gonna get shot.” The North Carolina state legislator Jennifer Balkcom wasn’t terribly well-known before this election cycle. Then she ran for the competitive House seat in a district centered on Asheville in the western part of the state. And thanks to going full Lowry Gang, and her mother going full Ma Barker, we now can’t not know her. Apparently Balkcom’s nice white-haired mom was home once, someone broke in, and she shot the intruder, as a smiling and nodding Jennifer recalls sitting next to her storytelling mother over a kitchen-table cup of coffee. “Mom’s a fighter,” Balkcom smiles and shrugs. We’re not sure why any of this would be a reason to vote for her, but it does make you rethink that family vacation to the Blue Ridge Mountains.

2. Don’t Even Ask About The Lunch Special

Barack Obama was on the campaign trail a lot this season, but perhaps his most notable stop was in an Iowa gym with Democratic Senate candidate (and former Paralympian Gold medalist) Josh Turek to shoot hoops to support the candidate’s surprisingly viable challenge to turn the seat blue. You won’t learn much about the candidate’s policy views, but you will learn that Turek has perimeter game, that Mr. President still loves a good matchup of H-O-R-S-E (or, I-O-W-A) and where each of the two men ranks Kareem all time. (Obama has him higher.) They both put Jordan ahead of LeBron, an empirically wrong take, but otherwise this ad is pretty much harmless. The subtle message is that Turek is a doer, and that he hangs out with Obama. There are worse codes to transmit — the former president won the state handily in both 2008 and 2012, the last time a Democrat did.

Speaking of Iowa, is it particularly cinema-obsessed? Turek’s opponent Ashley Hinson did a whole number in a movie theater with an awkward “working across the aisle” metaphor,” in which she sits in a multiplex and hands popcorn and candy to an unseen denim-shirted hand reaching out creepily from another section (is this supposed to be indicative of what we do in a theater?) while talking about her allegedly bipartisan legislative efforts. She says, for instance, that she collaborated with local Democrats to expand benefits to veterans (this is challenged by Democrats) and that she also worked “with President Trump to take on health-insurance companies to lower your costs.” What such work entails is unclear; the closest that can be surmised is that Hinson supported Trump’s notorious Medicaid cuts as well his notion of moving ACA tax credits into an HSA account — an idea often discredited for putting millions into debt. Neither of which one might be expected to brag about. The ad ends with someone spilling popcorn in Hinson’s lap as they’re trying to get to their seat, which seems metaphoric, or at least a lot more indicative of what actually happens in a movie theater.

5. At Least It Was Good Product Placement?Back in 2012, in an incident you might want to Google, Ken Paxton was entering a Dallas-area courthouse when he spied a $1,000 Montblanc pen that had been left behind the security tray by an attorney named Joe Joplin entering the courthouse, prompting Paxton to rummage around and quietly put it in his pocket, with surveillance footage capturing the whole episode. The ad asks: “Ken Paxton steals pens. What will he steal from you?” Not sure. But at least he gave us this cultural treasure.

Not sure the “Now lick it” double-entendre at the end will win any subtlety awards (it was the mail-in ballot). But Hilton did prove an important point: when you have no chance of winning, go for the most shameless and laugh-inducing form of virality. Hilton is extremely unlikely to win, but he did move the needle by proposing an eye-popping 60 percent tax credit. Thanks to this ad, we now know the kind of mise-en-scene it might have been used for.

CFTC clears stock perpetual futures for trading on US exchanges

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

US regulators are opening the door to stock-based perpetual futures, extending a popular crypto trading product into traditional markets amid ongoing legal disputes.

Perpetual futures contracts tied to individual stocks can begin trading on regulated US exchanges for the first time, according to the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), extending a derivatives product popularized in cryptocurrency markets into traditional equities.

CFTC Chairman Michael Selig said in a Tuesday statement that the contracts were deemed approved under CFTC Regulation 40.3 and could be offered on exchanges registered with both the CFTC and Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to trade security futures products.

Selig said bringing the products into regulated US markets would allow American traders to access perpetual futures under US regulatory safeguards rather than being “forced to turn to offshore, and often unregulated, venues.”

Perpetual futures allow traders to take positions on an asset’s price without owning the underlying asset or facing an expiration date. Crypto exchange BitMEX helped popularize the instruments, commonly referred to as perps, in 2016.

CFTC announces approval of equity perpetual futures trading. Source: CFTC

The announcement follows a wave of regulatory filings in September, when Coinbase, Kalshi and Kraken parent Payward, through its Bitnomial exchange, sought approval to offer perpetual futures tied to individual US stocks. OG.com joined the push days later, proposing contracts that could trade around the clock on weekdays.

The filings followed the CFTC’s May approval of Bitcoin perpetual futures contracts for Kalshi and separate regulatory relief allowing Coinbase to provide access to certain cryptocurrency perpetual futures listed on an affiliated foreign exchange.

Selig did not identify which exchanges would offer the contracts or provide a timeline for when trading would begin.

Related: CFTC sues Utah to block action against prediction markets

The announcement also comes nearly three months after President Donald Trump said the CFTC was working to bring Hyperliquid, a decentralized crypto exchange best known for its perpetual futures trading, into the US in a “fully compliant and legal fashion.”

Trump made the remarks during an Aug. 19 White House event. At the time, neither the CFTC nor Hyperliquid had announced a formal plan for bringing the platform to US markets.

However, the CFTC’s approach to perpetual futures has faced legal opposition. In June, CME Group’s Chicago Mercantile Exchange sued the regulator over its approval of cryptocurrency perpetual futures, arguing that the contracts should be regulated as swaps rather than futures and that the agency had exceeded its authority. The distinction matters because swaps are subject to different regulatory requirements, potentially including stricter reporting, margin and registration rules, while futures can trade under the existing futures exchange framework.

The CFTC moved to dismiss the lawsuit in September, arguing that CME lacked standing because it had not demonstrated financial harm and could list similar contracts itself.
