They say to write what you know. For better or worse, I know this topic from both sides of the couch.

A 2025 YouGov survey found that 38 percent of American adults reported being estranged from at least one immediate family member (Orth, 2025). When you hear the word estrangement, you may think of Hollywood-style relational pyrotechnics, with family members storming out of the house, never to speak to one another again.

To be fair, that does happen. But estrangement isn’t always about screaming fights and punishing silence. Researcher Kristina Scharp (2019) describes family estrangement as a continuum involving multiple dimensions, including emotional closeness, communication, physical distance, and the desire for reconciliation.

Here are four ways family estrangement can show up.

Proximity is a factor that can contribute to closeness, but is insufficient by itself. You can live near your family, see them every week, or even reside under the same roof, but physical proximity doesn’t guarantee emotional intimacy. More simply put, seeing your family regularly doesn’t mean that you share emotional closeness with them.

With structured contact, the relationship remains intact, but you have a non-negotiable list of rules that need to be in place in order to avoid conflict either interpersonally or intrapsychically. You still maintain contact, but you’ve established limits around when, where, and how interactions happen. Structured contact may look like time limits when attending family gatherings, staying in a hotel instead of with your family, or limiting conversations to topics that don’t escalate. Structured contact can provide a way to maintain a connection while reducing exposure to harmful dynamics.

With low contact, you may have rules about how and when to engage, or you may find yourself stuck in the same conversational loops on repeat. With low contact, you limit your exposure to family to only the minimum amount necessary to “check the box” of spending time with family. Low contact can often be a healthy way to preserve what remains available in a family, or it can be a way to mitigate the guilt and shame that come when you go full no contact.

No contact is exactly what it sounds like. There are no phone calls, emails, texts, or visits. No contact with family can run counter to deeply held beliefs about family connection, and many people agonize for years or even decades before they do it. Contrary to public opinion, going no-contact with family is not simply a trend, and ethical psychotherapy involves carefully exploring the reasons for considering estrangement rather than encouraging it without thoughtful consideration.

Research by Reczek et al. (2023) found that periods of parent-child estrangement can be followed by renewed connection. When all parties involved are committed to the change process, even long-standing periods of estrangement may find their way toward repair. Often, when repair fails to be an option, it’s because one or more parties may be unwilling or unable to participate in the process.

The decision to estrange yourself from family, regardless of which level of estrangement is appropriate, is one that is best made with informed supporters. Sometimes, estrangement isn’t even a choice, but the consequence of another family member’s rejection or withdrawal. While it can be a grief-laden experience, and one that should never be taken lightly, estrangement can also open up possibilities for loving and reciprocal relationships that do not cost you your energy or well-being.

Angelina Jolie Feels ‘At Home’ in Cambodia as She Expands Business Overseas—Inside Her Plans to Be ‘a Bit Nomadic’ After Ditching Los Angeles

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Angelina Jolie is ushering in a new era after quitting Los Angeles.

In the wake of selling the $24.75 million California estate she snapped up less than a year after filing for divorce from Brad Pitt in 2017, the actress has revealed plans to enjoy more time overseas and less time tied down to a singular location.

Jolie recently visited Cambodia, where local outlets reported she met with religious leaders. The “Maleficent” star reportedly “paid her respects and presented religious offerings” to Samdech Preah Maha Sumeatheathibodi Am Limheng, supreme patriarch of the Mohanikay Buddhist Order.

She also visited a traditional craft village on Cambodia's Koh Dach, which is known as Silk Island, to observe local artisans.

The mom of six detailed her special connection to the country during earlier travels to the Philippines, where she spoke on a Nov. 11 panel alongside Cambodian filmmaker Rithy Pahn.

“I found this place and the people to be so warm and so open, and I couldn’t believe their spirits were so strong after all they have suffered,” Jolie said of Cambodia. “You go to certain places, and you just feel at home.”

According to People magazine, the actress plans to expand her creative collective, Atelier Jolie, with a residency in Cambodia in 2017.

The effort will be a partnership between Pahn’s Bophana Center and the Maddox Foundation, which Jolie founded in 2003 and named for her eldest child, Maddox Jolie, whom she adopted from Battambang, Cambodia in 2002.

According to the Atelier Jolie website, the business is focused on “the discovery of art, craft and design work from cultures around the world.” She founded the certified B Corporation business in 2023, with a headquarters in Manhattan’s NoHo neighborhood that serves as a retail store, art space, and cultural center.

Jolie spoke about her plans for the future in a September 2026 interview with L’Officiel, noting that she plans to spend more time “out of the States.”

“The hope for the Atelier was always to be global,” she said. “The idea would be to visit the different locations around the world and meet local artists and makers.”

She noted a desire to expand the Atelier to Brazil, as well as “get back to doing more hands-on work” with the Maddox Foundation in Cambodia.

She also expressed a desire to live a life that’s less tied down to one specific locale.

“I don’t really plan to have a base,” Jolie admitted. “I’d like to have a home base for my family, a place where some of the mom stuff and the family photos are. And then I’d like to be a bit nomadic for a while.”

The star also noted that she has a home in Cambodia, which “will also be a residency a few months out of the year for the Khmer culture.”

Jolie’s appreciation for the Southeast Asian country began when she first visited to film the 2001 movie, “Lara Croft: Tomb Raider.” She returned a year later to adopt her son, and has since spent lots of time traveling back and forth with her entire family.

In 2017, she directed “First They Killed My Father,” a Khmer-language biographical thriller based on a memoir of the same name by Cambodian human rights activist Luong Ung.

In a People magazine interview, Jolie revealed Maddox—who was 15 at the time—was deeply involved in the creation of the film, and helped with research.

Jole revealed her son spent “over four months” in the country at that time, “really reading, listening, learning, and absorbing all things about his culture and his country, including the very, very dark parts.”

Although Jolie was born and raised in Los Angeles, she has long expressed her desire to leave the city.

“I grew up in this town. I am here because I have to be here from a divorce,” she told The Hollywood Reporter in 2024. She explained that once her youngest children—twins Knox Leon and Vivienne Marcheline—turned 18, she’d be “able to leave.”

The milestone birthday passed this July, paving the way for a new chapter for Jolie.

“It’s the end of a certain time for me,” she told L’Officiel weeks later.

Jolie made moves quickly, putting her idyllic Los Angeles compound on the market for $29.9 million in May, seemingly in anticipation of Knox and Vivienne’s 18th.

The six-bedroom, 10-bathroom abode found a buyer in September, selling for $24.75 million. Jolie had originally purchased the home for $24.5 million in 2017, and it served as her primary residence for nearly a decade.

While Jolie plans to spend the bulk of her time jetsetting, she hasn’t said goodbye to the United States for good, and has reportedly recently purchased a Connecticut mansion, according to Page Six Hollywood.

10 worst prisons in the world: crowding, isolation and gang rule behind the list

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Security Journal UK ranks 10 prisons as the world's worst. See which conditions still hold today and who has answered for them

Krzysztof Popławski / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)

Ask most people to picture the world's worst prison and they will describe a single building, a fortress with a cruel warden and a name everyone has heard. The reality is usually broader than that. In the Indian state of Maharashtra, home to Mumbai, the prison system as a whole holds far more people than it was built for and runs at nearly 173% of capacity, according to Mumbai Live. In 2010, almost half of all inmates in Russia were ill with diseases such as HIV and tuberculosis, according to federal prison service figures reported by Reuters. Neither problem was confined to one notorious jail: the crowding in India spans a state's whole prison system, and the illness in Russia was counted across the country's inmates. So when a single prison becomes infamous, it is usually the place where a nationwide shortage of space, staff and medical care is easiest to see. It is rarely an exception. That changes the question worth asking. How bad one jail is matters less than what keeps it that way.

Crowding is not simply a measure of how much crime a country has, because courts, budgets and government policy decide who stays inside and for how long. Money decides a good deal of it. Mumbai Live reports that in Maharashtra, many people held while they await trial stay locked up for long periods because they cannot afford a lawyer or bail. Many of the people in those cells are there because they are poor, not only because of what they are accused of. That is why officials who build new space or move inmates between jails rarely end the problem, since courts keep sending new people in. Not every harsh prison is a crowded one, though. Some governments impose isolation or detention without end on purpose. Those prisons raise a different question: who made that decision, and can anyone challenge it?

Security Journal UK set out to identify the prisons with the most inhumane, overcrowded and abusive conditions in the world, looking at places as far apart as an overflowing jail in Mumbai, Butyrka Prison in Moscow, a supermax in the Colorado Rockies and the U.S. detention camp at Guantanamo Bay, Cuba.

Crowding at Mumbai Central Jail is a daily condition that runs through the whole jail, not one bad stretch or a single notorious event. By Security Journal UK's count, more than 2,000 inmates live in a jail built for around 800, so they share beds, toilets and guards with far more people than the building was meant to hold. Crowding is one of the reasons the jail is ranked No. 10, along with disease, bribery and violence. Officials have few good places to move some of those inmates.

Mumbai Live reports that nearby prisons are overcrowded too and that options for relocating inmates are limited. Officials hold nearly 2,936 inmates in Thane Central Jail, near Mumbai, which was designed for 1,086. Sending inmates there would trade one overfilled jail for another. The state has allocated an 11-acre plot in Mankhurd for a proposed new prison. A plot of land offers the people inside Mumbai Central Jail no relief until a prison stands on it.

A packed jail is most dangerous because of what spreads and festers inside it. Tuberculosis and COVID-19 are among the illnesses recorded at the jail. Mumbai Live reports concerns about sanitation, water, healthcare and disease outbreaks across Maharashtra's prisons, along with a shortage of manpower. In a jail this crowded, inmates face those diseases with few defenses. The bribery and violence cited against the jail also take place in a system with too few staff to run it. The worst violence on record came in 2015, when fights between inmate groups left people injured and dead. Disease is the more recent mark against the jail.

Mumbai Central Jail's problems start with too many people in too little space, and disease and violence are harder to control in a jail so full.

Mendoza Prison in Mendoza, Argentina, is known for holding far more people than it was built for, and that reputation was earned. Security Journal UK includes the prison because it is filled to triple its capacity, with malnutrition and gang violence among its inmates. An outside count found crowding on the same scale: in May 2005, more than 1,600 inmates were registered in a prison built for 600, according to Amnesty International. The crowding is the most solid part of the case against Mendoza, though the only dated figures behind it come from 2005.

The most vivid detail about Mendoza is also the hardest to measure. The prison is on the list partly because a cell built for four sometimes holds more than 20 inmates. That claim covers only some cells, and no count says how many. Readers can hold the 20-to-a-cell image loosely, because the documented conditions are damning without it.

Provincial authorities knew how bad Mendoza was. Mendoza Province declared a prison emergency in 1999, 2000, 2001, 2003 and 2004, according to Amnesty International, so the emergency had become the ordinary state of affairs. Declaring the problem took the place of solving it. When the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights reviewed the prison in 2005, the damage went beyond the headcount to the building itself. "We couldn't work out where the toilets were, there's no water or electricity and the sewers have collapsed; it is in a total state of neglect and isn't even fit for animals," said Florentín Meléndez, a commissioner with the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights.

The last change on record was a reshuffle rather than a repair. Later in 2005 fewer inmates were held there, and officials made more room by using units left empty after transfers, according to Amnesty International. Nothing on the record describes the prison after that year. Mendoza's place among the world's worst is therefore a verdict on a crisis documented in 2005, and whether the prison fits that verdict today is unknown.

Federal Bureau of Prisons / Wikimedia Commons

The harm at ADX Florence, the federal supermax in Florence, Colo., comes from isolation rather than crowding. Each inmate is locked by himself in a cell for 23 hours of every day, according to Security Journal UK. That confinement is the prison's ordinary routine, not a penalty for breaking its rules. The prison ranks eighth among the world's worst largely because that near-total isolation causes mental illness among the men held there. The isolation is the purpose of the prison, not a side effect of a shortage of money or space.

When isolation is the purpose of a prison, the decision about who goes there is itself a punishment. Biden spared the men on federal death row from execution in December 2024, and Trump, once back in office, signed an order instructing Attorney General Pam Bondi on how they should be housed, according to The Associated Press. The push to move some of those men to ADX came from that order, which treated them as one group rather than one prisoner at a time. U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly, whom Trump nominated, temporarily blocked the transfer of 20 of them, which makes his ruling hard to dismiss as partisan. Kelly drew his line at the process, not at the prisoners' crimes, even though ADX's inmates have included Ted Kaczynski, Dzhokhar Tsarnaev and Joaquín "El Chapo" Guzmán. "But the Constitution requires that whenever the government seeks to deprive a person of a liberty or property interest that the Due Process Clause protects — whether that person is a notorious prisoner or a law-abiding citizen — the process it provides cannot be a sham," Kelly said.

The ruling is a temporary block, and it makes no finding about what life inside ADX does to people. A class action filed in 2012 challenged the effects of the isolation. The Associated Press reports that Kelly based his ruling on the Fifth Amendment's guarantee of due process. The legal fight over ADX now centers on who can be sent there rather than on what its cells do to prisoners. The two sides still disagree about the cells themselves. The inmates' lawyers argue their clients face years in a small cell cut off from other people, while government lawyers argue the conditions are not unusual for prisoners such as these.

ADX clearly meets the ranking's test for inhumane isolation imposed on purpose, but whether the law will ever call that isolation cruel remains open. So far a judge has only paused the transfer of 20 men until the government gives them a fair process.

Bang Kwang Central Prison in Thailand is known for its chains, but its lasting problem is the number of people inside it. Security Journal UK reports that as many as 50 people have been packed into some of its cells, and it names that crowding as one reason the prison ranks among the world's worst. That crowding is also the part of Bang Kwang's reputation that the prison's recent reforms left untouched.

The prison's treatment of inmates has changed in ways its crowding has not. Prisoners at Bang Kwang have spent months in leg irons, another reason the prison is counted among the worst, but Thaiger reports that recent reforms ended routine shackling and introduced video calls. Those changes show that Thai prison authorities can alter how inmates are treated when they choose to. They changed the rules inside the walls, not the number of people living within them. Bang Kwang is best judged today by its crowded cells and by the prisoners waiting to learn whether they will be executed.

For the condemned, the heaviest burden is not knowing what comes next, the third reason the prison is ranked among the worst. Thailand does not announce executions in advance, according to Thaiger, so a prisoner on death row has no way of knowing which day might be the last. That fear is concentrated at Bang Kwang, where Thailand sends people facing death, life behind bars or a term of at least 25 years. The country's most recent execution took place in June 2018, and the years without one since then have stretched the waiting out rather than ended it. Most of the hundreds of people Thailand has condemned to death were found guilty of drug crimes.

Whatever has changed about the chains, Bang Kwang's prisoners are left with the crowding and the waiting.

Butyrka Prison in Russia has been crowded on a scale that no cell was built for. Cells designed for 10 prisoners frequently hold as many as 100, according to Security Journal UK. At Butyrka, cells packed that tightly are a regular condition rather than a rare emergency. Tuberculosis and reports of torture are also among the charges against Butyrka, and the case rests on all three together: too many people, sickness and abuse. With crowding, disease and abuse in the same cells, the obvious question is what happens to a prisoner who falls ill there.

The Russian state has taken part of the blame for the death that defines Butyrka's reputation. Sergei Magnitsky, a lawyer, died in custody there in November 2009, and by 2010 Russia's federal prison service had admitted partial responsibility for his death, according to Reuters. Critics did not have to prove this charge, because the agency that ran the prison accepted some of the blame itself. Once officials concede that the system failed one prisoner, the reports about packed cells and disease are harder to dismiss. An admission is not a repair, however. The real test is what the prison did next.

Within about a year of Magnitsky's death, Butyrka's management was promising comforts rather than space. "We are developing additional medical services... and even sunbeds will be put in place," Sergei Telyatnikov, Butyrka's head, said in 2010, as reported by Reuters. The sunbeds were due by the end of 2010, and the plan also called for ultrasound systems, possibly mud baths, and permission for inmates to use Skype. Better medical equipment may help sick prisoners. Still, none of these additions creates a single extra place to sleep in a cell built for 10.

Butyrka shows a state that admitted failure and then answered with amenities, while the crowding stayed in place.

Kamiti Maximum Security Prison in Kenya is accused of failing its inmates in nearly every way at once. Security Journal UK ranks it fifth among the world's worst prisons, citing malnutrition, rampant disease, abuse by guards, bribery and torture cases. These harms come from many directions, so fixing any one of them would leave the prison largely as it is. The hunger comes from the daily routine rather than from an occasional shortage, with maize porridge as the typical meal. An outsider could check that charge by looking at a plate. Only someone who gets inside could confirm the gravest charges, torture and abuse by guards.

The UN official whose job was to investigate torture tried to see Kamiti and could not get in. Nigel Rodley, the UN Special Rapporteur for Torture, moved freely through Kenya's police stations on his 1999 mission, The New Humanitarian reports, but the prison stayed closed to him on Sept. 29 of that year. The refusal applied to this prison in particular, not to his mission as a whole. "It is hard to imagine that if the authorities did not have something to hide they would have denied me access," Rodley said. Those words came from the UN's own torture investigator, and they turned a refused visit into a suspicion about what the prison held.

Kenya's government gave an innocent explanation. Joshua Terer, permanent secretary of Kenya's home affairs ministry, blamed the refusal on a failure of communication, according to The New Humanitarian. In his account, the refusal was a mix-up, not an attempt to hide anything. Neither account was proven, and under either one, no independent inspector got inside. Kamiti's reputation therefore rests partly on what its accusers describe and partly on the inspection that never took place.

Kenyan officials kept the UN's torture investigator out of Kamiti, so nobody independent has been able to test the worst accounts of the prison.

Prisoners at the prison in Muhanga, Rwanda, have slept standing up, and the reason is the number of people inside. Security Journal UK includes the prison because its inmates sleep on their feet for lack of room. With 6,441 people held in a building designed for 3,063, according to IGIHE, many inmates have no floor left to lie on. Once people have to sleep upright, every other problem inside gets worse with the crowding, from the spread of disease to fights between inmates to the guards' job of keeping order.

Muhanga's crowding is not just a leftover from the genocide. The cells first filled when Rwanda jailed suspects after the 1994 genocide, and the country's prisons have not emptied since. Rwanda's prison system has an overcrowding rate of 124%, according to the latest Human Rights Commission figures reported by IGIHE, and Muhanga's own rate is far higher. Crowding is a problem across Rwanda's prisons, but Muhanga carries much more of it than the system as a whole. The genocide explains how Muhanga filled up, but it does not explain why the prison is still full.

Abroad, Muhanga is better known for claims that inmates ate one another. IGIHE reports that the claim first ran on the Indian outlet Zeenews in 2015 and resurfaced in the British tabloid The Mirror in 2022. The story has been repeated rather than proven. "Prison overcrowding did exist, it is true, but no prisoner has ever eaten another," Sheikh Abdul Karim Harelimana, Rwanda's interior minister in 1996, said. Even while denying the rumor, the former minister did not dispute the crowding. The rumor remains in dispute, while the crowding does not, so the crowding is the part of Muhanga's record a reader should weigh.

Rwanda's government treats the crowding as a problem of how many people it locks up. Its plans include new facilities, alternative sentences and electronic bracelets, according to IGIHE. Only the first of those adds room. The other two would keep some people out of prison altogether.

The real test for Muhanga is not the next rumor but whether the number of people inside ever falls toward what the building was made to hold.

Diyarbakır Prison in Turkey owes its notoriety to the torture of the political prisoners held there, not to crowding. Security Journal UK ranks it third in the world, largely for the systematic torture of political prisoners that began in the 1980s. Most of those prisoners were Kurdish, according to bianet, so the abuse fell on a political and ethnic group rather than on inmates chosen at random.

Turkey's military kept its own count of the dead. In April 1984, the General Staff put the number of deaths inside the prison at 53, according to bianet. That count came at the end of a period of torture that ran from 1980, the year the prison was built, through 1984. The abuse went on for several years rather than in a single episode.

The charge that overcrowding and violence continue at Diyarbakır fits the prison's recent past better than its present. That charge rests on the years after the 2016 coup attempt, when many people detained in its aftermath were held there. Authorities have since emptied the prison, which held 270 prisoners at the end, according to bianet. The government has also placed the site under Minister of Culture and Tourism Nuri Ersoy to become a museum. With no one held inside, the building can no longer be the scene of the crowding and violence it is charged with.

Survivors tried to have the torture judged as a crime, and no court ever ruled on it. Over a thousand people once jailed there for political reasons brought criminal complaints, according to bianet. The U.S. detention camp at Guantanamo Bay, Cuba, is notorious for how long it holds people and on what grounds, not for how many people it holds. Security Journal UK ranks the camp second among the world's worst prisons because it detains people indefinitely without charge. Some detainees have been confined there for more than a decade without any court finding that they committed a crime. For those men, the waiting itself serves as the punishment.

The camp has shrunk to a small group of men, and the people running it have not closed it. Since 2002, roughly 780 detainees have been held at the camp, PBS NewsHour reports, and by June 2023 just 30 were left. Sixteen of those 30 had already been cleared for release, yet they stayed in custody. President Biden had set the goal of shutting Guantanamo before he left office. As of February 2025, the camp was still operating with fewer detainees.

The torture charge against Guantanamo concerns the camp's present conditions as well as its early years. The allegations behind its ranking go back to the camp's opening, and for most of its existence no independent United Nations investigator went inside. In 2023, Fionnuala Ní Aoláin, the UN Special Rapporteur on Human Rights and Counterterrorism, made the first such visit in the camp's history, PBS NewsHour reports, and she found detainees shackled whenever they moved within the facility. Inmates at Ciudad Barrios Prison in El Salvador face two harms at once: far too many people share the space, and a gang controls much of it. Security Journal UK ranks the prison first among the world's worst for that combination of overcrowding and gang rule. When one gang makes up most of an overfilled prison, its members are in a position to run daily life, and the guards are not. That is the gang rule behind the prison's ranking. The crowding and the gang make each other worse. The more inmates the staff must handle, the less control the staff keep, and gang members take up the authority the staff lose.

Fixing Ciudad Barrios would take more than easing the crowding. Officials who move a few inmates elsewhere will not fix a prison this far past its limits and this dominated by one gang. Whoever takes responsibility for Ciudad Barrios faces a problem of control as much as one of space.

Why "Face-to-Face Only" Might Be Costing You Good Therapy

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

I’ve been noticing a trend, a shift, across psychology and therapy platforms. It’s the “only face-to-face” request. “In person only,” “No telehealth,” from referral requests to consumers, I see it everywhere.

Look, I get it. I truly get it. Prior to COVID, I swore I would never do telehealth. Only in rare cases, where it was the only way someone would get support, would I even consider it.

So, understandably, the start of COVID was not an easy transition for me. Lockdowns meant that those rare cases where I would consider it now applied to everyone, and people needed support more than ever. So, like many of my colleagues, I did the training, dived deep on what was possible, and did the best I could. And what I found were... results. It was not the terrible outcome I had anticipated, but more than that, it was not just something to hold the fort until I could get back to ‘real therapy’. It worked. So, post-COVID, I started doing more, until now, full disclaimer: I run a telehealth-only practice, so it’s fair to say I may hold some bias in this space.

I also get why the pendulum has swung. Post-COVID, we're living in a world where a lot of us are lacking face-to-face connections. We spent years on screens, we worked on screens, we saw our families on screens, our kids went to school on screens, we threw our birthday parties on, yup, screens. So it makes sense that we've come out the other side and decided, maybe rightly so, that online interactions just aren't as good. Here’s this thing, I back this, we absolutely do need face-to-face interactions. But this is where we get stuck in black-and-white thinking. Online bad; in-person good. Rather than leveraging screen time to buy us more face-to-face in the spaces that matter most.

But you folks know me, let’s check what the evidence actually says about it all.

Telehealth therapy has actually been shown to be as effective as face-to-face therapy. A 2021 meta-analysis comparing live video therapy with in-person therapy found that therapy is no less effective when it's delivered by video, with the strongest results for CBT for depression and anxiety. The authors concluded that video therapy isn't just the convenient choice, it's one now backed by evidence. A 2025 meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials in children, adolescents and their parents found the same thing. Video therapy was as effective as in-person treatment, both straight after treatment and at follow-up.

This was seen in couples' work too. A study of over 1,100 married clients in couple therapy found teletherapy was as effective as in-person therapy for outcomes including relationship satisfaction, sexual satisfaction and the therapeutic alliance.

The big worry I hear, from clinicians especially, is about the therapeutic relationship. Can you really connect through a screen? It's a fair question, and the honest answer from what I have found is that the alliance holds up well. A review of studies on this found a good working alliance and good outcomes across all of them, and outcomes that were equivalent to face-to-face. Now, the alliance scores sat a touch lower on average, and the research isn't conclusive yet that they're fully equal. But the outcomes held up.

This is the big bit I think we're missing. If what we're craving is more in-person connection, telehealth might actually be part of how we get it. It potentially allows us more time to engage in person in the situations that are more meaningful.

We can fit our therapy around our work and our lives, and save that important face-to-face time for family, friends, and community. The hour you'd have spent driving across town, finding parking, sitting in a waiting room and driving home again? That could be dinner with your kids. A walk with a friend. Actually turning up to the thing you keep saying you'll go to. This is no way going to be the case for everyone, but again, if we get stuck in that black-and-white thinking that one is superior to the other, then those who would actually benefit end up missing out.

The other thing to consider, particularly when we look at sex therapy, is that there's some emerging evidence it might actually be easier for us to talk about things like sex, and those topics that feel vulnerable and exposing, when we have this perceived barrier or distance between us.

Psychologist John Suler described this back in 2004 as the online disinhibition effect: some people self-disclose more often, or more intensely, online than they would in person. We usually talk about the toxic side of that, the trolls and the pile-ons. But Suler also described the flip side, where people share more openly and ask for help more freely.

We see a version of this in sexual health research, too. Studies comparing computer-based questionnaires with face-to-face interviews consistently find people report more sensitive sexual behaviours when there's a screen rather than a person in front of them. In one survey from the Cleveland Clinic, 44% of men said they'd prefer to talk to their doctor about sexual health problems online or by phone because they were too embarrassed to do it face-to-face.

Back to COVID, when sexologists in Portugal were surveyed about moving their work online during lockdown, many reported that their clients felt less exposed, with fewer psychological and emotional barriers to get past. So being at home could ease some of the tension about opening up and remove some of the barriers when it comes to getting help for sexual issues. Some also said it made it easier for clients to talk about gender and sexual orientation, with less stigma. That tracks with what I see in my day-to-day too.

Absolute disclaimer: None of this means telehealth is right for everyone, or every time. Some people don't have a private space at home. Some couples find it hard to get uninterrupted time together without kids. Some people need the ritual of leaving the house and going somewhere. And yes, sometimes that disinhibition means people share a lot, quickly, and, as therapists, we need to pace that with care.

But "face-to-face only" as a blanket rule? That's the black-and-white thinking I’m not so sure about. It might be ruling out a therapist who's exactly right for you. It might be ruling out the very format that would make it easier to say the hard thing. Like most things when it comes to psychology, I suggest we move into a space of curiosity rather than good/bad and make a choice that fits best for individual needs.

A Party to End All Parties—Fashionable London Fetes the Life of Antony Price

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Antony Price always said that if anyone should ever think about giving him a memorial, “Make it a fabulous party!” The legendary and beloved British designer’s wishes were honored by his friends in London on Wednesday night. With glamour on the invitation and his gasp-making shows and images playing on vast screens, the man who forged the look of Roxy Music and Duran Duran and conjured couture dreams for decades was fully celebrated in an epic Antony Price appreciation night thrown for him at 180 Strand.

Tributes flowed from his multi-generational fans and closest people. Among them were models who’d walked his London shows, or rather stalked and prowled them, per his direction: Marie Helvin and Yasmin Le Bon, as well as Marilyn Cole, who brought along a glorious reminder of her role on the cover of Roxy Music’s 1973 album Stranded, printed on a t-shirt. There she was, lying looking seductively sweaty in a jungle wearing an artfully shredded red dress that Antony Price had concocted. Thanks to Price’s creative direction and styling (terms unheard-of then), the lives of a generation of British teenagers were changed, just from staring at the fantasies of Jerry Hall as a blue-painted mermaid, Amanda Lear as a gleaming patent-leather dominatrix, and Kari-Ann Muller as a 1950s pin-up on Roxy album covers. It went from scruffy long-haired hippy to sexy slicked-back glamour overnight.

Thirty years later, Alexander Fury discovered the credit ‘Antony Price: clothes, makeup and hair’ on the back of the Roxy albums as a student at Central Saint Martin’s. “I didn’t care about the music—it was the clothes, the look! Who was this genius? ” It started an obsession that led Fury to fashion journalism; he also became an Antony Price collector and friend. From the podium, he contextualized Price’s contribution as the first London designer to fuse fashion, music, and spectacle together in live events—selling tickets to the public. His 1970s fashion shows, dramatically stylized productions staged with supermodels in music venues like Camden Palace and the Hippodrome, “were pinnacles of that approach, early examples of fashion breaking out of its niche to be truly democratic. He was a revolutionary, a pioneer,” Fury said, but added, “yet he was also, and he remains, the most criminally overlooked designer in British fashion history.”

Nick Rhodes of Duran Duran was celebrating his friend’s life in the crowd, while his fellow band members Simon Le Bon and John Taylor circulated elsewhere. Rhodes remembered how he and Taylor came across Price’s wildly stylish store Plaza in the King’s Road, and how that led to Price making the band the vividly colored suits they wore on a yacht slicing through the Caribbean on the video for their massive 1982 hit “Rio.”

“ John and I went in there and blew our entire EMI clothes budget for our whole album on one suit. As soon as we told him we were planning a shoot in the Caribbean, he said, “Oh, all those beautiful pastel colors! And he started dyeing things in his bath. Everything was hand-made with Antony. When you hear people saying, he was a true artist—well he was. He did everything, could do everything. He was up there with Thierry Mugler in Paris, and John Galliano or Alexander McQueen, after him.”

Price was an ace tailor, sculpting broad-shouldered, narrow-hipped suits based on his own rediscovery of matinee idols and screen goddesses in 1940s Hollywood movies when he was a Royal College of Art fashion student in the 1960s. Born in Keighley in the idyllic Yorkshire countryside in 1945, he went to Bradford school of art, just after David Hockney, then followed him to the Royal College of Art at the time when London was swinging. He was the son of “a Spitfire pilot who left my mother with me and my sister and twins,” he once told me. “We were not a straight-up-and-down family. My sister was a poor man’s Sophia Loren. I’ve always loved that curvaceous type.” A lot of that background stuck with him and imprinted his tastes for life. One thing was his liking for uniform; he’d style himself “like a Star Captain beamed down to earth for an evening’s rock ’n’ roll, often in a military cap ,” as I wrote once in a British Vogue profile.

Then there was his passion for birds, keeping and breeding peacocks and fancifully-feathered chickens. He never said whether his idea of molding clients’ torsos from chicken-wire (as couture dress-forms) had cross-fertilized from this practice, though it was a suspicion. Philip Treacy saluted Price, who sculpted bombshell dresses in black velvet for his millinery shows, remembering just what a brilliant collaborator he was. One season, the young Treacy talked about his idea to use feathers to finish an upcoming collection. “So Antony bred a hundred white Yokohama chickens—from the egg. Then brought them live to my studio so I could clip the tail-feathers off before putting them on the hats,” he laughed, adding, “no chickens died for fashion.”

Price delighted in collaborating with younger creative people, who adored him back for his genius, his delight in making women look more amazing than they’d ever felt, his mind-blowing techniques, and, yes, his “lethal mix of humor and pessimism,” as Treacy put it. There were, indeed, decades when—though he was still operating a couture service for private clients—Price would moan at all and sundry that he’d be forgotten. That was not to be. In what was to be the last year of his life, Marco Capaldo, the young designer of 16Arlington, a fan who idolized Antony Price’s glamour, asked him to collaborate on a show of 16 curvy demi-couture looks. On November 17 last year, they were flamboyantly posed in high-1980s style by a whole new generation, including Lily Allen, wearing the ‘Avenge’ dress, Lila Moss in the feathered ‘Pollo,’ and Lara Stone in the zebra-stripe ‘Jerry.’ The warmth of the ovation as Capaldo brought him out at the end finally showed Price just how much he was adulated and loved.

Lily Allen in 16Arlington and Antony Price

Kit Butler in 16Arlington and Antony Price

Isaac Ferry, son of Bryan, grew up seeing melees of creative people coming in and out of the house. Price was his godfather. Four years ago, while Price was living in a cottage in Sussex with Isaac, his now wife Elizabeth and brother Tara, he witnessed the couturier’s tireless routine in action. “He’d rise at five, go straight down to the workroom, ‘because I’ve got to get on,’ and he’d be down there, watching the early birds at the feeders. He’d sit at his machine or cutting table, working away while watching Cleopatra, Spartacus, or some other Hollywood spectacular.”

Shortly after the 16Arlington show, Antony Price passed away at his sewing machine at the age of 80. Wednesday’s party was an emblematic finale to a legendary life, toasted with oceans of Moet & Chandon by the constellation of stars of pop, London club-life of the high glam years, clients of two generations who partied for him in exactly the way he might have dreamed up himself. Now, just one thing remains to memorialize the full stature of Antony Price: An exhibition and/or a documentary about him are long overdue.
