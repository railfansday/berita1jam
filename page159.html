Brian Flores: Vikings plan to rotate players in order to deal with altitude in Mexico City

📅 2026-11-17  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The 49ers are spending the week in Colorado in order to acclimate to the altitude at which their Week 11 game in Mexico City will be played. The Vikings are not.

But the Vikings have a plan for ensuring that their players are ready for the conditions.

“It’s a tough place to play,” Vikings defensive coordinator Brian Flores told reporters on Tuesday regarding the game in Mexico. “I think it’s certainly taxing, you know, I think you’re — I think as a staff, we’re already talking about, ‘Hey, we’re gonna have to rotate these guys and get, you know, everybody’s gonna have to be ready to play.’ And so, yeah, I mean, it’s certainly something that we’re going to have to deal with.

“But there’s always, you know, elements that we’re dealing with, whether it’s cold or rain or things like that. Obviously, we’re indoors, so we don’t deal with much of that. But that’s the environment we’re gonna be in. I think we prepare them to play in that environment, but I’m not gonna be silly and say, ‘Hey, everybody, hey, you’ve got to go in there for 12 straight plays.’ I mean, we’re not doing that.

“So we’ll have rotations, we’ll have a plan in place. Inevitably, we’re gonna have to fight through some of that. It’s not gonna be your average game where, you know, altitude, you just have — you literally have less oxygen. So we’ve got to do a great job as a staff of making sure — watching guys, and making sure we got guys in and out, you know, as much as we can.”

His broader message to the defense is clear: “We’re gonna need everybody.”

Regardless of the plan to rotate players, the question becomes whether the 49ers will be able to rely more heavily on their best players because they’re properly acclimated to the thinner air. The Vikings are choosing a different strategy, and it’s going to create an obvious apples to apples comparison between the approaches that the two teams have taken — especially if the 49ers players seem to be fresh and the Vikings players seem to be gassed.

Why iPhone Duo wasn’t for me (but might be for you)

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

I’ve had the iPhone Duo for nearly two weeks now, and have officially decided to return it. Here are the three main reasons why the Duo didn’t work out for me, but might for you.

I use my iPhone one-handed all the time, and based on my experience, iPhone Duo is clearly intended to be used with two hands. You can use it one-handed, but for me it’s not nearly as comfortable as the iPhone 18 Pro. It’s too wide and too heavy to be used for long.

Perhaps over time I’d get used to needing two hands for most iPhone interactions. But that would be a pretty significant shift for me, as there are so many situations when I don’t have both hands free.

If you already use two hands with your iPhone regularly—perhaps because you have a Pro Max model—then the Duo’s one-handed experience likely won’t bother you. But the more I used the Duo, the more I found myself missing my iPhone 18 Pro.

The biggest value proposition of a foldable is that it’s a 2-in-1 product, letting you have a standard phone but also a tablet-sized display that fits in your pocket.

I totally get the appeal, and fully expected to love that hybrid design. But after two weeks of trying, I still struggled to find times when I actually wanted to unfold the Duo. And to be clear, that’s a me problem, not an issue with the Duo itself.

I tried a bunch of apps on the inner display, and struggled to find use cases that—for me—made it worthwhile to open the Duo. But based on the feedback I’ve seen from most Duo owners, I’m very much in the minority.

I know lots of people love the Duo for video, but I don’t watch much video on my iPhone. I almost never watch YouTube, TikTok, or Instagram Reels. And for shows and movies, I stick with my TV screen and an Apple TV 4K.

Multitasking support on iPhone Duo works really well. But when I found myself needing to multitask, I still reached for my iPad Pro with Magic Keyboard attached. A keyboard-free product can’t replace my main computer (nor would I expect it to), and the Duo screen was too small to comfortably work on it.

Last year I bought an iPhone Air and absolutely loved it. Initially, the Air’s less capable camera system didn’t bother me, it was “good enough.”

After a few months though, I started missing the Pro camera system more. Then I learned that my wife was pregnant with twins, and I knew I wanted the best possible iPhone cameras to capture their early weeks and months. So I bought an iPhone 18 Pro in September.

I’ve been very impressed by iPhone 18 Pro’s Main camera upgrades, and it’s great having a Telephoto lens again too. Since having the best camera system is so crucial for me right now, I knew iPhone Duo couldn’t be my only iPhone.

I initially planned to dual-carry the two models, using iPhone 18 Pro solely as a camera. But over and over I found myself wishing it was my only iPhone.

Is the iPhone Duo camera good enough for most people? Probably so. But for this year at least, with my twins aging rapidly, it’s not quite right for me. If I loved the Duo for other reasons, I would just use both models. But since I didn’t, I’m happy to stick with the iPhone 18 Pro.

In closing, I want to explicitly state that I don’t think the iPhone Duo is a bad product, and this isn’t a “bad review.”

The iPhone Duo just isn’t for me. I get the sense, though, that it’s lots of people’s favorite iPhone in years. Apple created its most unique new iPhone model since the original, and there’s plenty to love about it. I’m very curious to see how popular foldables become over time compared to slab models, because each clearly offers pros and cons. But for me, at least right now, the iPhone 18 Pro is my model of choice.

Google to keep Gemini chats for 2 months, up from 72 hours, even with history off

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google is extending how long it keeps temporary Gemini chats and conversations made with chat history turned off to around two months, saying the change aims to improve safety and security as AI systems become more capable.

The change, which will roll out globally in the Gemini app over the coming weeks, marks a significant increase from the previous 72-hour retention period mentioned in Google’s Gemini Apps Privacy Hub, according to an archived version from November 12.

OpenAI, by comparison, says it may keep temporary chats in ChatGPT for up to 30 days, whereas Anthropic retains incognito chats in Claude for 30 days by default. However, companies may keep some data longer for legal or safety reasons, or to investigate policy violations.

Google did not answer questions about what prompted the change and whether it would affect how conversations are accessed or reviewed, instead pointing to a blog post that says chats are kept longer to respond to users, process feedback, and protect its services and users. Its updated Gemini Apps Privacy Hub also cites potential system failures as a reason for retaining chats and says temporary conversations are not used to train AI models.

The longer retention period comes as Google introduces separate settings for Gemini’s chat history and AI training. Until now, both were tied to a single “Keep Activity” setting. Users will now be able to keep their conversations in their chat history without using them to train Google’s generative AI models.

Google says both new settings will be turned off by default for users who previously disabled Keep Activity. For those who had Keep Activity turned on, their chat history will remain enabled. They can, though, decide separately whether Google can use their conversations to train AI models.

The failure of a Vulcan Centaur rocket carrying a full load of internet satellites for Amazon was caused by an anomaly in its first-stage booster, rocket maker United Launch Alliance said today (Nov. 17).

That failure — which led to the loss of 40 Amazon Leo internet satellites — was recognized about 10 minutes after liftoff, but the problems began much earlier than that, ULA said in a statement.

"While we appear to have lost telemetry and the mission at approximately 10 minutes into flight, early indications are that just after liftoff the launch vehicle experienced low booster performance, resulting in stage separation occurring when the vehicle was low in altitude and below minimal velocity to reach orbit," ULA wrote in today's update.

The two-stage Vulcan Centaur is the successor to ULA's workhorse Atlas V rocket, which will retire after just six more flights. Vulcan debuted in January 2024 and now has a total of five launches under its belt.

The new rocket has experienced problems on three of those missions. On both its second and fourth flights — which launched in October 2024 and February of this year, respectively — the nozzle of one of Vulcan's strap-on solid rocket boosters (SRBs) fell off about 30 seconds after liftoff.

On both occasions, the rocket overcame the problem and successfully delivered its payloads to orbit. However, after the second occurrence, the U.S. Space Force paused national-security launches with Vulcan until ULA addressed the SRB nozzle issue. (The February 2026 flight was a Space Force mission.)

That work led to "design and process improvements to the nozzle," ULA said in an Oct. 28 update. And those tweaks apparently worked.

"All indications are that the solid rocket booster performance was nominal, following nozzle improvements implemented for this launch," the company wrote in today's update.

Vulcan's Centaur V upper stage, which features two RL-10C engines, did its job as well "but was unable to recover from the [booster] performance loss, resulting in loss of mission," ULA added.

Vulcan's first-stage booster is powered by two BE-4 engines, which are built by Amazon founder Jeff Bezos' Blue Origin. Today's update did not identify the exact problem with the booster; doing so will require more time, as the investigation has just begun.

If that investigation finds a serious issue with the BE-4, there could be broad ripple effects across the American space program. After all, that engine also powers the first stage of Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket, which is a key piece of hardware for NASA's Artemis program of moon exploration. (And New Glenn is currently recovering from a failure of its own — a massive explosion on the test stand that occurred on May 28.)

NASA plans to integrate the Centaur V upper stage into its Space Launch System moon rocket, the vehicle that launches Artemis astronauts to space. So the fact that the Centaur performed nominally on Sunday is a bit of good news for the space agency.

Vulcan's grounding is a big deal, no matter what the anomaly investigation ends up finding. The U.S. doesn't have a surfeit of powerful rockets, after all, and two of them — Vulcan and New Glenn — are out of commission at the moment.

There's considerable pressure on both Vulcan and New Glenn to bounce back strong, for the nation faces an impending launch crunch in the next few years. SpaceX plans to retire its Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy rockets and shift its launch load over to the fully reusable Starship megarocket. SpaceX is expected to prioritize its own needs in Starship's early days, using the vehicle primarily to build out its next-gen Starlink broadband constellation and Starmind AI data center network.

The Vulcan failure also impacts Amazon — specifically, the company's plan to build out Amazon Leo, which will compete with SpaceX's Starlink. Amazon has booked about 100 launches on a variety of rockets to build its constellation, which will eventually consist of more than 3,200 satellites. Thirty-eight of those bookings are for Vulcan; Sunday's launch was the first of those.

The other rockets Amazon has booked are the Atlas V, New Glenn, Falcon 9 and Arianespace's Ariane 6. About 400 operational Amazon Leo satellites have reached orbit to date across 14 launches (not including Sunday's failure).

The Cologne-based Lempertz auction house said it still planned to sell two works by Gothic painter Johann Koerbecke after the heirs of a German Jewish art dealer who was persecuted by the Nazis claimed the pieces were “stolen.”

Dealer Max Stern held the paintings in Düsseldorf between 1936 and 1937, the year the Nazis shut down his gallery. Per a provenance supplied by Lempertz, both paintings were then held by Düsseldorf’s Galerie Hans Bammann, which appears to have then sold them. On Monday, the Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation issued a release in which it called on Lempertz to stop the sale of the two works planned for Saturday. According to the foundation, such a sale should not occur “until two distinct ownership claims of different victims are justly resolved according to the Washington Principles on Nazi-Confiscated Art.”

Both paintings—The Annunciation and The Vision of Saint Bernard—are likely from Marienfeld high altarpiece, which was completed in 1457. They are being auctioned in a sale of Old Masters art. They carry high estimates of around 300,000 euros each, or about $329,000 apiece, making them the auction’s most expensive works. “For art historical reasons,” the Lempertz lot entries read, “the two important panels (Lots 1005 and 1006) will first be offered individually and then together.”

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation pointed out that both works appear in several databases of missing artworks, including the Interpol Database of Stolen Art since 2021. On the German Lost Art Database, both are reported as “Nazi-confiscated property”; the foundation registered that claim with the organization in 2018.

The foundation has demanded the return of multiple artworks belonging to Stern over the past decade, and in some cases, it has been successful. In 2017, for example, the FBI returned a painting by Jan Franse Verzijl that had been on view at an US art fair two years earlier.

Potentially complicating the matter is the fact that, prior to Stern’s possession of the Koerbecke paintings, they were owned by Ellen Funke, who, during the Nazi era, was classified as a Mischling, or “mongrel,” because she had Jewish ancestry through her mother. She sold her collection in 1936 to fund her emigration from Germany, and works she once owned have also been the subject of restitution claims—two of which happen to involve the Koerbecke works.

In 2025, Bavaria’s state museums restituted Koerbecke’s Vision of Saint Bernard to Funke’s heirs. At the time, the Bayerische Staatsgemäldesammlungen noted that The Vision of Saint Bernard and The Annunciation were at one point part of a single work, with both images appearing on either side of the same panel, and that Stern had taken them apart with the hope of selling them both. Funke’s heirs are listed as the sellers of The Vision of Saint Bernard on the auction house’s website.

Similarly, Funke’s heirs are also listed as the sellers of the The Annunciation, which entered the Kolumba Art Museum of the Archdiocese of Cologne as a donation in 1996. The museum restituted the work in 2025.

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation’s release notably does not mention Funke by name. But it does state that the foundation “maintains that the rights and histories of both Jewish victims must be taken into account, and neither claim should be privileged at the expense of the other.” In an email to ARTnews, a spokesperson for the foundation said “the museums restituted the paintings to Ellen Funke’s heirs in accordance with the priority principle set forth in the guidelines established in Germany to apply the Washington Principles,” adding that that restitution ”did not result in a ‘just and fair solution’ in accordance with the Washington Principles.”

In a statement to ARTnews, Lempertz said it would not cancel its planned sale of the paintings by Koerbecke, who is widely considered one of the great painters of the Gothic era in Germany.

“We adhere to current restitution practices and follow the recommendations of the Federal Government,” a representative for the auction house said, claiming that the Stern “had the panels sawn in two” and went on to sell one of them for “double the price.”

“These magnificent paintings were not taken away from Max Stern,” the Lempertz representative said, adding, “Max Stern received compensation after the war; the heirs of the Funke family did not.”

The Stern Foundation spokesperson said the “view that the paintings were not ‘seized’ from Max Stern has long been refuted,” citing a 2019 statement from the now-defunct German Advisory Commission that the persecution of Jewish art dealers began at latest by August 1933, “so that from that point on at the latest, it can in no way be assumed that ‘proper, customary business transactions’ took place.” The spokesperson added that the claim that Max Stern was compensated for the paintings is “incorrect“ and has “no legal basis.”

Lempertz did not dispute the listings for both paintings on the Art Loss Register, but the representative also noted that the listings contain details of the works’ respective restitutions.

Peanut Peptide Injections Promising for Allergy Immunotherapy

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

— A novel route to immunotherapy for peanut allergy passes phase II test

A novel peptide-based immunotherapy for peanut allergy appeared safe and modestly efficacious for reducing children's symptoms over the first 12 months, a phase II trial showed.

For the primary efficacy endpoint, the higher of two monthly PVX108 shot dosages significantly boosted the maximum tolerated dose of peanut from baseline to month 12 by 2.4-fold, compared with a 1.2-fold change in the placebo group, reported Brian P. Vickery, MD, of Emory University and chief of the Division of Allergy/Immunology at Children's Healthcare of Atlanta, at the American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology annual meeting.

In terms of safety, there were no cases of anaphylaxis or other treatment-related allergic or severe adverse events with the shot. Only six of the 71 trial participants reported any adverse event (13% with the higher dose tested), largely local administration site reactions.

At an oral food challenge at 1 year, 39% of patients on the higher dose tolerated at least 300 mg of peanut protein compared with 17% in the placebo group (the equivalent of one peanut). "This is more than double, on average, what causes accidental reactions in the field when people are exposed accidentally while they're trying to avoid peanut. And it's been shown in previous work that ... is probably clinically meaningful for most patients with peanut allergy," Vickery said. But he acknowledged it "is probably less than what you'd expect in a study of oral immunotherapy, where the participants are swallowing about 300 mg of protein daily as part of their treatment."

While efficacy needs to be confirmed in a larger trial, "there's also a big safety difference and a difference in burden in terms of daily versus monthly therapy," he added, pointing to adverse events in most immunotherapy patients with other routes of administration and anaphylaxis in 10-15% of trial participants in some studies.

Immunotherapy shots offer an advantage of fewer office visits than the typically once-every-2-week schedule for oral immunotherapy up-dosing and also not requiring kids to consume peanut products daily, commented Jay Lieberman, MD, of the University of Tennessee Health Science Center in Memphis. With peanut protein, though, "you can't do it the same way we do allergy shots to cat or oak tree pollen, because it is too dangerous. It caused too many reactions," he said.

PVX108 comprises seven short peptides representing immunodominant T-cell epitopes of major peanut allergens rather than intact peanut proteins that interact with the immune system and can lead to reactions. "This is designed to get around that problem by formulating fragments of peanut proteins that are too small to interact with IgE [immunoglobulin E] and the allergic components of the immune system and instead interact directly with T cells, which are thought to be kind of the driving force behind the allergy," Vickery said.

"We have made some progress in being able to treat peanut allergy with oral immunotherapy and now FDA-approved omalizumab [Xolair] as the two most commonly used options," Vickery said in an interview monitored by media relations for drug developer Aravax. "But there are millions of patients out there with peanut allergy, and most of them are not being treated due to limitations in care and also limitations in access to these treatments and because of limitations in the treatments themselves."

"The scientific community believes that there is plenty of room for new forms of immunotherapy to be developed," he added, "because we don't think of peanut allergy as a one-size-fits-all condition where one treatment will solve the problem."

While the only FDA-approved peanut oral immunotherapy product recently left the market as clinicians rely on off-the-shelf commercial products, Lieberman agreed that PVX108 could find a place: "Bringing an [oral immunotherapy] FDA-approved thing to the market when people are already just using PB2 powder [commercially available peanut flour], that was the problem."

The trial included 71 peanut allergy patients ages 4 to 11 years at 14 trial sites in the U.S. and Australia in the primary efficacy and safety cohort. They were randomized to double-blind treatment with 5-nmol or 50-nmol PVX108 or placebo given monthly by intradermal administration over 12 months. At the end of that period, a double-blind placebo-controlled food challenge was done following the PRACTALL guidelines.

After 6 months off treatment, a follow-up food challenge was conducted at month 18. It showed evidence of maintenance or improvement in clinical effects compared with month 12, indicating disease-modifying potential, Vickery said, although this needs further confirmation.

The optimal length of treatment isn't clear yet, but immunotherapy shots for other allergens typically take 3 to 5 years of treatment, Vickery noted. "So it's likely that longer-term treatment might be necessary or even might be more optimal."

SAG-AFTRA has tentatively re-upped its solo contract with Netflix.

The guild revealed Tuesday that a tentative agreement had been reached on a new bargaining agreement, which is now subject to approval by the national board.

That vote is expected “in the near future,” per the union, and no details from the deal are expected in advance of that review.

SAG-AFTRA and Netflix Reach Tentative AgreementDeal Goes to SAG-AFTRA National Board for Review and Approval pic.twitter.com/RnRiwMPzh4

The performers’ union has had a standalone contract with Netflix Studios since 2019. Before that, the streaming giant dealt with the guild on a production-by-production basis. Guild officials said at the time that, with few exceptions, the contract’s terms followed the guild’s Codified Basic Agreement and Television Agreements.

The Netflix agreement applies to scripted, dramatic episodic and feature-length productions made by Netflix Studios, LLC. Watch on Deadline
