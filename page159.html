The World Of Hyatt Credit Card (See rates and fees) has a new initial bonus: earn 4 free nights at any Category 1-4 Hyatt hotel or resort after spending $5,000 on purchases in the first 3 months from account opening. That’s an improved offer from a card whose bonuses have usually been more modest than competing travel cards.

I have this card, and I put actual spending on it. Hyatt points are still valuable, but what gets me to use the card is the credit toward elite status and the rewards earned along the way. Hyatt status does more to improve my hotel stays than status with other major chains.

These are four separate certificates, so you can use them for one trip or spread them across different stays. You’ll need standard room award availability, and the certificates expire 12 months after they’re issued. The terms require checkout before the expiration date. Under Hyatt’s current award chart, a Category 4 standard room can cost as much as 25,000 points per night. Four certificates could therefore cover nights that would otherwise require 100,000 points. Their category restrictions and expiration date make them less flexible than having those points in your account. The card has a $95 annual fee. Chase excludes current holders of this card and previous cardmembers who received its initial bonus within the past 24 months. The usual Chase restriction on applicants who’ve opened five or more personal cards in the past 24 months also generally applies. Why I Keep The Card And Spend On It The annual Category 1-4 free night after each cardmember anniversary makes the $95 fee easy to justify. There’s also a second Category 1-4 night after $15,000 in purchases during a calendar year which makes that spending more rewarding. And these nights are transferable to another World of Hyatt member. If I won’t use one, I can give it to someone who will. For spending, the card earns 4 Hyatt points per dollar on qualifying Hyatt purchases; 2 on restaurants, airline tickets bought directly from airlines, local transit and commuting, and fitness club and gym memberships; and 1 on other purchases. More significantly, it comes with 5 qualifying nights each year, plus 2 more qualifying nights for every $5,000 spent. Those nights count toward both status and Milestone Rewards. Put $15,000 of otherwise unbonused spending on the card during a calendar year and you earn: 15,000 Hyatt points. An additional Category 1-4 free night certificate. 6 qualifying nights from spending, giving you 11 including the card’s annual 5. That means 49 qualifying nights actually spent in hotels, plus the card’s 5 annual nights and 6 from spending, gets you to the 60 needed for Globalist. And the $5,000 spent earning the initial bonus counts toward the $15,000 free-night threshold in the calendar year when you spend it. Milestone Rewards start at 20 qualifying nights and continue every 10 nights through 150. Star Wars’ Thrawn Creator Is Bringing a Murder Mystery to Star Trek

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Hannah covers horror, sci-fi, and games at Collider, where she writes news and features, reviews new releases, and interviews the people who make them. She’s helping build out Collider’s gaming coverage, with Pokémon as one of her specialties. If she isn’t writing about a monster, she’s probably watching one on a tape from her VHS collection.

Timothy Zahn built one of Star Wars’ most enduring villains around the pleasure of watching someone notice what everybody else has missed. Thrawn can make a conversation about culture feel like the beginning of a military disaster, especially when the people across the table haven’t realized how much they’ve already told him. That makes his creator an intriguing person to put in charge of a murder mystery.

Zahn introduced Thrawn in Heir to the Empire in 1991, giving Luke, Han, and Leia a formidable new opponent after Return of the Jedi. Thirty-five years later, he’s taking that interest in secrets and competing agendas to another corner of science fiction. His destination is Deep Space Nine, a station where a valuable object changing hands is usually enough to get several people interested in who’s buying, who’s selling, and what neither side wants anyone else to know.

Zahn is writing Star Trek: Roc’s Egg, an original graphic novel releasing from IDW Publishing in October 2027, with art by Ángel Hernández. The story begins when an artifact arrives aboard Deep Space Nine and appears to be destroyed in transit, and a Federation JAG investigation follows, uncovering a secret auction, wealthy collectors, dangerous criminals, and a murder waiting to happen. Hernández’s previous Trek work includes Voyager — Homecoming and Deep Space Nine — The Dog of War.

Zahn’s relationship with Star Trek actually predates his work in Star Wars. In IDW’s announcement, he traced it back to watching the original series in 1966, describing the opportunity to work on the graphic novel now as “a huge honor and privilege,” making this a chance to contribute to a franchise he’s followed since its beginning.

The station is a promising place to start. A mystery aboard a starship puts suspects among people who generally have a reason to trust one another. On Deep Space Nine, the investigators have to contend with visitors whose business may already depend on keeping Starfleet at a distance. Add a private auction, and an apparently destroyed artifact becomes a much more interesting problem. For readers who know Zahn through Thrawn, having multiple angles to look at this story from is the draw. His most famous character wins by understanding what other people value and how far they’ll go to protect it, while Roc’s Egg gives him a station full of people with something to hide, and an investigation that could make those secrets expensive.

Star Trek: Roc’s Egg releases in October 2027.

Anthropic has launched Linked billing in beta, letting apps built on Claude bill API usage to a customer’s own Claude organisation. Customers can set a spend cap or unlink the app, but only organisations that pay Anthropic directly qualify, and their own data protections do not carry over.

Apps built on Claude can now charge their AI usage to their customers’ own Claude accounts. The feature, called Linked billing, launched in beta on Tuesday, according to Anthropic’s release notes.

A customer first links its Claude organisation to the app and approves it. The developer then chooses, on each request, whether the customer or the developer pays. The request still runs on the developer’s account, with its usage limits and model access.

Customers can set a monthly spending cap for each app in the Claude Console, or unlink it at any time. If Anthropic cannot bill the customer, the request is refused rather than charged to the developer. Anthropic is strict on limits elsewhere too: it ends enterprise discounts once customers use up their tokens.

Not every customer qualifies. It only works for organisations that pay Anthropic directly, not those billed through AWS, Google Cloud or Azure, or with only a Claude subscription. Credits from Claude plans and Anthropic’s startup programmes cannot be used either.

There is also a privacy catch. A customer’s own data protections, such as zero data retention and HIPAA agreements, do not apply to these requests.

The club later brought in consultants to train staff on AI, but the effort never quite gained momentum, he said.

Talks about a bigger role started early this year and came together by midyear. “Adrian and I talked about it, and we agreed for me to come on board and take an operational role and hack on some code,” Chew said.

The bigger challenge is getting people to change how they work, Chew said. At Zapier, he saw many companies struggle to get results from AI, with some spending “massive token budgets” without a big win. He said the answer is blending AI with traditional automation and fitting it into how people already work.

“It has to be approachable for folks,” he said. “You can’t go in and just throw everything up and start from scratch.”

All the Bonds have their virtues, but Connery benefited from being there first — for anyone who discovered the series in its initial heyday, Connery simply is James Bond. Yet Connery actually wasn’t the first actor to play Bond. That honor goes to journeyman MGM contract player Barry Nelson, who played Bond in a “Casino Royale” adaptation eight years before Connery made the character famous in “Dr. No.” Connery may have been the first 007 to grace the big screen, but Nelson established Bond on the small screen in an episode of the CBS anthology series “Climax!” opposite Peter Lorre in the Le Chiffre role that would later be inhabited by Orson Welles (in the goofy 1967 “Casino Royale”) and Mads Mikkelsen (in the 2006 adaptation that introduced Craig to the series).

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is a fascinating artifact that includes elements that would go on to characterize future James Bond movies, while also going in a few other very different directions. The key difference is in the scale, stripped down by necessity for television production. The hour-long “Climax!” episode is all interiors, taking place entirely in the hotel and casino of the title. It’s more a James Bond play than a movie, with Bond and Le Chiffre facing off in a series of verbal — and ultimately physical — jousts in confined spaces, and it’s surprisingly effective.

It’s also a very strange beginning for a franchise that would come to be known for its globetrotting locales and elaborate action set pieces. Not that this “Casino Royale” was officially part of the James Bond franchise as we know it. In fact, although there have been three adaptations of Ian Fleming’s first James Bond novel, only the third incarnation with Daniel Craig was from EON Productions, the company that controlled the character from “Dr. No” through the Craig era before selling the rights to Amazon.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” came about when producer Gregory Ratoff optioned the rights to Fleming’s novel in May of that year. The novel wasn’t yet a hit, and Fleming was sorely in need of cash, which led him to make one of the worst deals in movie-rights history when he sold the book outright to Ratoff for $6,000 after the TV episode aired. This left “Casino Royale” out of the deal when EON secured the screen rights to James Bond as a character, so Bond producers Albert Broccoli and Harry Saltzman could only sit back and grit their teeth when Ratoff turned “Casino Royale” into a comedy in 1967 and cast Peter Sellers, David Niven, Woody Allen and others as rotating Bonds.

EON ultimately made a deal to bring “Casino Royale” under their umbrella and used the novel to reboot the franchise with Craig, but in 1954 it served as the source for a much more modest affair. The biggest similarities between the 1954 “Climax!” episode and the Daniel Craig movie come in the time spent on extended gambling sequences, and the centering of a brutal torture scene. Given censorship restrictions of the time, Nelson’s torture in a bathtub is considerably less graphic than the mallet to the testicles inflicted on Craig, but it’s rough enough stuff for a 1950s TV production.

Certain aspects of Nelson’s characterization, like Bond’s mix of sexual swagger and icy indifference, would later be adopted by most future 007s, to varying degrees. The biggest difference between Nelson and future Bonds is a simple one: He’s American. Although Broccoli and Saltzman flirted with the idea of an American Bond throughout the series’ history (going so far as to cast James Brolin in “Octopussy” before Moore decided to return at the last minute), to date they’ve never actually pulled the trigger on casting outside the UK.

Nelson is probably best known to modern audiences as the affable hotel manager Stuart Ullman in Stanley Kubrick’s “The Shining,” but in the 1950s he was a sturdy character actor and occasional leading man who bounced around television after an initial sojourn as a studio contract player. As a young man, he appeared in several important MGM films, including “Shadow of the Thin Man,” “Bataan,” and “A Guy Named Joe” (the source material for Steven Spielberg’s “Always”). Later on, he popped up in “Airport” and had a successful Broadway career. He was nominated for a Tony acting opposite Liza Minnelli in Martin Scorsese’s 1977 production “The Act.”

In 1954, though, he was in the middle of a prolific and productive era as a TV stalwart, both as a series regular (on “The Hunter” and “My Favorite Husband”) and a frequent guest star. He doesn’t exhibit a ton of range as American “Combined Intelligence” agent “Jimmy” Bond, but that too is in keeping with the overall conception of the character, at least until Craig tried to bring a little more emotional depth to the role.

In Americanizing Bond, the “Climax!” edition of “Casino Royale” ends up making a number of related changes that are small but make the film feel just a bit off for fans of the EON productions. American Felix Leiter (later played by Jeffrey Wright) becomes Brit Clarence Leiter, for example. Although there’s none of the Bond gadgetry that would become famous later, there are hints of what’s to come in the form of props like a villain’s cane that hides a gun, and the opening credits for the “Climax!” series itself feature a gun barrel pointed at the viewer that predicts the iconic graphics of later Bond title sequences.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is noteworthy for another reason: its composer, Jerry Goldsmith, who would ultimately score hundreds of major films, including “Planet of the Apes,” “Chinatown,” “Basic Instinct,” and most of Joe Dante’s work. Yet in some ways what’s fascinating about “Casino Royale” is how unremarkable it is — it’s a decent, solid piece of entertainment from an era when proliferating anthology shows provided similar programs every week.

If Fleming had his way, it would have only been the start for 007 on television; he wrote several outlines for episodes of a James Bond series before CBS canceled the project. Luckily, he repurposed most of what he wrote as short stories for his 1960 book “For Your Eyes Only,” and television’s loss was cinema’s gain. By 1962, James Bond belonged to the movies — and to Sean Connery.

“We're not saying they're perfect” says architect fighting to save Melbourne's public housing blocks

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Photography by Ben Hosking, Michael Pham

Architect Simon Robinson has been leading calls for the Victorian state government to rethink its plan to demolish all of Melbourne’s public housing towers. In this interview, he sets out why he believes retrofit is the smarter approach.

Announced by the Labor-run state government in 2023, Homes Victoria’s High-Rise Redevelopment Program (HRRP) will see all 44 of Melbourne’s public housing towers and many other lower-rise blocks razed and replaced with new housing by 2051.

Officials argue that the buildings, constructed between 1962 and 1975, have reached the end of their useful lives. They claim the towers do not meet modern standards and cost too much to maintain, and that the redevelopment programme will triple housing capacity and improve the quality of homes.

But the policy has proved highly controversial, with critics particularly angry about a lack of transparency or consultation with residents, who are being relocated.

It is now subject to an ongoing legal dispute, has prompted concern from the UN’s special rapporteur on housing, and become a political issue in the build-up to the Victorian state election later this month.

Among the voices leading calls for a change of course is Melbourne not-for-profit research and design practice Office.

Office has worked pro bono to make the case for refurbishing the towers, after learning from residents that the government has not published feasibility studies for keeping any of the condemned estates.

“We thought that was pretty odd that you wouldn’t do a refurbishment study on these estates,” Office director Simon Robinson told Dezeen.

“So we took it upon ourselves to start to do these feasibility studies, along with public housing residents.”

“We’re not saying that they’re in perfect condition,” he added. “We’re also not saying that all of them should be refurbished – perhaps some are beyond repair. All we are saying is that these rapprochement studies need to occur.”

In fact, feasibility studies have been conducted by the government but not been made public, despite requests by the Victorian Legislative Council.

By avoiding the relocation of residents, Office estimates that a retrofit and infill approach would cost around $300 million less than demolition and rebuild.

“As all the estates are occupied, the cost of relocating and housing the residents during construction work needs to be included in the project cost; this is consistently excluded and never disclosed,” said Robinson.

“We have estimated that these costs are very significant, with reports indicating that the government is spot purchasing or head-leasing properties from the private market.”

The practice has put together a counter-proposal based on a strategy that it calls Retain Repair Reinvest (RRR).

RRR focuses on retaining buildings without displacing residents, carrying out renovation work to improve housing comfort and energy efficiency while reducing construction waste and carbon impact.

Robinson agrees that most of Victoria’s existing public housing buildings are currently not fit for purpose – buildings are rundown, cold in winter, and sweltering in summer. But he believes comfortable living standards can be achieved through renovations.

Office has applied its RRR strategy to three Melbourne estates in hypothetical case studies – Ascot Vale Estate, Barak Beacon Estate and the Flemington Estate.

Formerly home to approximately 10,000 people, Flemington Estate’s four towers make up about 10 per cent of Victoria’s public housing stock.

Only about 32 households remain, though an injunction prevents Homes Victoria from serving further eviction notices while legal challenges are pending.

Office has proposed retaining the estate’s 720 dwellings and building an additional 577 infill social housing units to increase density.

New facades and private balconies would be added, with other upgrades including integrated underground parking, a central communal green space, new heating and cooling systems, improved insulation, new kitchens and bathrooms.

Energy efficiency is another key focus. Double-glazed windows, photovoltaic cells, and water conservation systems would provide a 55 percent reduction of global warming potential compared to the demolition approach, according to Office.

Despite the seemingly obvious benefits, the Victorian government has so far been unmoved by the studies presented by the practice.

Robinson believes the reason lies in the fact that the state government no longer wants to manage public housing.

“When the state government manages public housing, they are responsible for maintaining the buildings and organising new tenants – all the admin that comes with owning a building,” he said.

As well as the demolitions, also attracting concern is the type of housing proposed for redevelopment under the HRRP.

Rather than like-for-like public housing, the new homes will be “community housing” – developed by private developers, and split between below-market and market-rate rental homes.

“The government is now calling everything social housing, because they don’t want the public to know that it’s no longer public housing,” he continued.

“Victoria has the lowest percentage of public housing stock in Australia, and we’re currently demolishing it all and turning it into private and community housing.”

The issue is particularly charged in the context of Australia‘s housing-affordability crisis. Now the fate of Flemington Tower awaits a ruling from the High Court of Australia following a hearing last month.

“The papers we produced are quite dense and not for the general public. So what we’re now doing is translating that work into a series of publications, putting it in bookshops, and making it more accessible for the general public.”

“We continue interviews with different residents, different advocates, different professionals, speaking with journalists and doing lectures. The idea is to bring awareness to what’s occurring, rather than us doing more reports.”

He is also calling for a frank discussion in the architecture community about the redevelopment projects, which many of Australia’s prominent studios are working on.

“All the big-name architects are working on these estate renewals, some have even won awards as exemplar projects for these architects to promote their practice in terms of doing public good,” said Robinson.

“It’s really hard to find anyone who’s willing to talk about why they’re doing it, or is it good, the displacement and environmental issues. We’re alone.”

T-19A Kestrel: Meet The US Air Force Academy's New Unlimited Aerobatic Aircraft For Cadets

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

On November 12, four new composite planes swept low over the Cadet Wing assembled at the US Air Force Academy in Colorado. The flight marked the official arrival of the T-19A Kestrel light trainer at Davis Airfield in Colorado Springs. Instead of wading through decades of defense bureaucracy and drawn-out congressional appropriations, USAFA secured its next-generation aerobatic aircraft in just 10 months, cutting through red tape with the help of a private donor group led by Andrew McKenna.

The T-19A is a derivative of the commercial GB1 GameBird. By skipping the traditional procurement, leadership bypassed the budget overruns and timeline slips that have paralyzed programs like the Boeing T-7A Red Hawk. The lightweight plane is purpose-built for agility thanks to its designer, German aerobatic champion Philipp Steinbach. He is a co-founder of Game Aerospace Composites in Bentonville, Arkansas, which produces the off-the-shelf civilian variant certified to pull a staggering load of ten G-forces.

The Kestrel went from concept to delivery in 10 months using $3 million in private donations, whereas the Red Hawk has spent over a decade in development using billions in taxpayer funds and still faces multi-year delays. The T-19A will fill a major gap in modern USAFA flight training by giving cadets early exposure to high-G, unlimited aerobatics and complex energy management before they ever step into Undergraduate Pilot Training.

The T-19A, or GB1 GameBird, is the only American-built two-seat aircraft approved for Unlimited-category competition aerobatics. Its all-carbon monocoque airframe lacks traditional mechanical wing-attach joints, relying on a continuous composite spar cured inside an autoclave. This gives the plane an operating empty weight of roughly 1,290 pounds (585 kg) while enabling symmetric flight loads of up to 10-G at its aerobatic maximum limit.

Under the hood, a 583-cubic-inch Lycoming AEIO-580-B1A flat-six piston engine delivers 303 horsepower (226 kW) to a four-blade composite MT-Propeller system. An inverted oil and fuel system keeps the engine running under sustained negative-g flight and zero-g push-overs. Cadets command the airframe from tandem seats equipped with Garmin G3X Touch glass displays, combining edge-of-envelope stick-and-rudder forces with modern electronic flight screens before stepping into standard turboprops.

To make the transition, USAFA pulled six active-duty instructor pilots from the 306th Flying Training Group to stand up the first training cadre. These officers will shape a new syllabus to train cadets and establish a demonstration and competition team. The initiative comes as the wider military struggles with a persistent shortfall of roughly 2,000 operational pilots, forcing leadership to seek shortcuts to build stick-and-rudder skills earlier in pilot development.

The Academy's existing fleet jumps from unpowered TG-16A gliders and basic T-53A/T-41D piston trainers straight to the UPT's T-6A Texan II turboprops after graduation. USAF Chief of Staff General Ken Wilsbach and Academy Superintendent Lt. Gen. Paul Moga secured the four airframes thanks to a $3 million donation coordinated by philanthropist Andrew McKenna. Meanwhile, the USAFA paid $400,000 in operational funds for initial tooling, spares, and flight gear, along with a projected annual operating budget of $1 million to maintain the fleet.

The Kestrel will act as an intermediate high-performance step, teaching cadets how to manage high power-to-weight ratios, rapid throttle response, and aggressive energy management. Standard trainers are built for stability and prohibit extreme maneuvers. Because the Kestrel is certified for unlimited aerobatics, instructors can deliberately put cadets into inverted spins, vertical stalls, and disorienting attitudes, teaching them instinctive recovery skills that directly prevent real-world loss-of-control accidents.

The Air Force is facing a persistent pilot deficit and needs to push students through UPT faster without lowering standards. By giving top cadets advanced stick-and-rudder proficiency during their junior and senior years at the Academy, they arrive at UPT needing fewer remedial rides and moving through the syllabus faster. Additionally, building an aerobatic competition team around the Kestrel will push the selected cadets to fly under public scrutiny with the precision and discipline they will further refine in operational flying squadrons.

Operating the Kestrel at the USAFA in Colorado Springs comes with real performance trade-offs. The Academy's Davis Airfield sits at an elevation of 6,572 feet (2,003 meters), and summer heat often makes the air feel like it is over 9,000 feet (2,743 meters). That means the T-19A's non-turbocharged engine loses roughly 22% to 26% of its power. That thinner mountain air requires longer takeoff runs and leaves pilots with less excess engine power during demanding maneuvers.

Weight limits create another hurdle for daily operations, as the Kestrel can weigh up to 2,200 pounds (998 kg) for standard point-to-point flights. Its maximum takeoff rating for extreme 10-G aerobatics only applies at 1,940 pounds (880 kg) or less. Subtracting the plane's 1,290-pound (585 kg) empty weight leaves just 650 pounds (295 kg) for people, gear, and fuel. Once an instructor and a cadet strap in, only enough weight allowance remains for 25 to 30 gallons (95 to 114 liters) of fuel.

That limits intense flights to roughly 45 minutes, requiring ground crews to refuel and turn the planes around quickly between sorties. Moving cadets from unpowered gliders into a 200-knot (370 km/h) airplane is also a major operational adjustment. Because rapid spins and heavy G-forces can easily cause motion sickness or make new pilots lose track of which way is up, instructors must design a step-by-step program that gradually introduces cadets to the plane's capabilities.

Built entirely from carbon fiber, the T-19A brings modern composite design to a general aviation field long dominated by traditional steel-tube frames. Even compared to the newer carbon-fiber Extra NG, the Kestrel weighs about 110 pounds (50 kg) less at an empty weight of 1,290 pounds (585 kg). Its wing and fuselage tanks also hold 81 gallons (307 liters) of fuel, giving it a cross-country range of over 1,000 nautical miles (1,852 kilometers).

In the air, the Kestrel reacts far faster than almost all other military trainers. The USAF's primary turboprop trainer, the Beechcraft T-6A Texan II, rolls at 180 degrees per second and handles forces between positive 7.0-G and negative 3.5-G. The Kestrel more than doubles that agility, rolling at 400 degrees per second while withstanding up to positive and negative 10-G.

Unlike older aerobatic planes, the Kestrel avoids the sluggish handling caused by a heavy nose-mounted radial engine. Its horizontally opposed Lycoming responds instantly, without the twisting-torque side effects of a radial engine during aggressive maneuvers. That instant throttle response also translates into blistering acceleration and vertical climb performance. Its power-to-weight ratio allows the Kestrel to take off in just 680 feet (207 meters) and climb at 4,200 feet per minute (1,280 meters per minute).

This sharp handling marks a major departure from the Academy’s existing T-53As, which are militarized Cirrus SR20s built to train for instrument flying rather than agility and dogfighting. The Kestrel bridges that gap, letting cadets practice inverted flight, vertical spins, and high-G recovery before they arrive at active-duty pilot training bases to begin the pipeline to the cockpit of a warplane.

The T-19A's performance derives from its lightweight carbon-fiber fuselage built around a single, continuous spar that runs straight through the cabin floor. By dropping heavy metal wing joints, Game Aerospace saved weight while also making the 25.26-foot (7.70-meter) span wings strong enough to withstand extreme loads up to 15-G.

Up front, the engine spins a 74.8-inch (1,900 mm) German-made MT-Propeller built from a compressed wood core wrapped in a tough composite shell with stainless-steel leading edges. Because standard engines lose oil pressure when flying upside down, the Kestrel uses a specialized inverted oil system that keeps oil flowing smoothly between 55 and 95 psi (3.8 and 6.5 bar) at temperatures up to 245°F (118°C), even during violent tumbling maneuvers.

Instead of flexible steel cables that can stretch under heavy loads, the pilot's control stick connects to the wings and tail via rigid metal pushrods mounted on sealed bearings for instant, slop-free response. Small aerodynamic paddles beneath the wings help balance the large carbon ailerons so the stick stays light at speeds up to 175 knots (324 km/h), while tiny fins along the wing surface keep airflow smooth and prevent sudden wing stalls down to 60 knots (111 km/h).
