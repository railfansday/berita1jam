Petered (what happens after the Peter Principle)

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Peter Principle posits that people are promoted until they reach their level of incompetence. What happens after that point? The implicit assumption is that they stop getting promoted and linger at whatever level they’ve arrived at, and surely all of us can point to mediocre leaders who seem to exhibit exactly that circumstance. But in fact, that situation is not stable but subject to career pressure, as Christian Gläßel and Adam Scharpf define it in Making a Career in Dictatorship. Unable to move up, and very possibly unable to meet expectations, the person who is subject to the Peter Principle is at risk of losing their job, or even their entire career. Which means they might take an even greater risk to save it. Gläßel and Scharpf write:

The logic of career pressure also offers a compelling twist to the Peter Principle. The Principle posits that individuals in hierarchical organizations are promoted until they reach their level of incompetence, where they can no longer perform effectively and cease to advance. In contrast, our logic shows how these individuals may surpass this point and climb the hierarchy even further. Career-pressured individuals often take on high-risk, labor-intensive, or morally contentious tasks. By loyally executing the organization’s dirty work, they can reestablish their value, regain competitiveness, and even outpace peers who were previously ahead of them. Alternatively, they may resort to sabotaging superiors or higher-ups by making false allegations or revealing secrets to create vacancies above them and forcibly advance their careers.

Gläßel and Scharpf, Making a Career in Dictatorship, page 182

In other words, someone promoted up to their level of incompetence, sensing a threat to their own livelihood, becomes a threat to everyone else. I’m reminded here of David Graeber’s theory in Bullshit Jobs, that a great many management jobs are basically feudal retainers—people kept around in order to make the folks above them look good. Very often, making someone else look good may well mean tackling the dirty work for them so that they can keep their hands clean.

The petered leader has in effect been made an imposter—a real imposter, not a “syndromed” or perceived one. They must seem competent, to preserve the appearance that they belong in the role; while in fact they are not capable of delivering on their actual responsibilities and may have been quietly tasked with unrelated services both illegal and unethical. As a result, one of the primary tools of the real imposter is obscurity. Imposters cloak themselves in a fog of obfuscation and busyness, so that the people around them can never say for sure what it is they are doing, or how well (or not) they are doing it.

What we call imposter syndrome was originally termed imposter phenomena, a term that makes plain that it is something that can be observed, not merely felt. The transformation into a syndrome shifted the experience of being impostered by a patriarchal, white supremacist society into a medicalized disorder, something for which the impostered person must be treated. That’s a neat trick: to shift the locus of treatment to the mistreated person, rather than the system that caused her harm. But perhaps there’s even another, more diabolical move here. What if the popularity of imposter syndrome is, in part, a means of obscuring the actual imposters in our midst? What are we missing when we look inward instead of around?

It is right that the murder of many people be mourned and lamented.

I Tried the Nike Air Zoom Hyperslide Every Day for a Month and Now I’m Spoiled

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

If you buy through our links, we may earn an affiliate commission. This supports our mission to get more people active and outside.Learn about Outside Online's affiliate link policy

The Hyperice pod hidden in the straps elevates the decadent coddling the Nike Air Zoom Hyperslide delivers. (Photo: 101 Degrees West)

For more than a decade, every morning while I’m hand-grinding coffee beans for my moka pot, I do 15 to 20 squats. It’s a comforting morning routine, but it is mostly a discipline, a way to ensure I don’t let busy days keep me from working on my glutes to counteract hours of sitting.

A month ago, however, I added decadence to my discipline, making me look forward to the coffee-grinding routine (almost) as much as I do the coffee. That decadence comes in the form of a comfy pair of slides and a hot foot massage, bundled together as the Nike x Hyperice Air Zoom Hyperslide.

Is this $250 recovery slide an expensive gimmick? Well . . . yes. But an oh-so-comfortable one that I’m now reluctant to give up.

The slide itself is a slab of supportive foam topped by responsive, low-profile Air Zoom units (the same pressurized-air and trampoline-like tensile-fiber pods used in Nike’s high-end running shoes, here tuned more for comfort than performance). The Air Zoom cushioning feels both plush and lightly bouncy underfoot, and the wide platform provides reassuring stability, making my feet feel coddled and supported while doing my squats, walking around my office, or sitting at my desk.

But that coddling is just the beginning. Hidden beneath the Velcro top straps are a pair of Hyperice pods that magnetically attach to the main instep straps and transmit heat and vibration to the tops of my feet. Buttons on each side of the pods cycle through three levels of vibration (50, 75, and 100 Hz) and three levels of heat (109°F, 113°F, and 117°F). You can also use the Bluetooth-connected phone app, but as far as I can tell, its main benefit is only letting you set the run time longer than the default fifteen minutes.

The first time I used the Hyperslides, I dutifully wore socks and selected the lowest settings, as recommended. Quickly, however, I was cranking them up to high on both heat and vibration. I have, however, kept wearing the socks, because they seem to help wrap my foot and transmit the sensations farther around it rather than having them isolated in one spot on top.

It didn’t take long before I craved the buzzing warmth radiating through my feet each morning, although the sensation felt simply pleasurable, not therapeutic. That was before the day I woke up with a sharp pain between two metatarsal heads that I traced back to a twisting foot plant on a trail run the day before. When I slid my foot into the slide and started the massage, it initially felt a bit intense on the sore spot, but as the heat and vibrations loosened the stiffness, the pain all but disappeared and stayed away all day.

The Hyperslides do what they do better than I ever imagined (having never imagined a heated, vibrating slide), but after using them for a while, I have a couple of nitpicky wishes.

The batteries are supposed to last for an hour on full power, which was pretty close to what I get. Because I almost always restart them after the first fifteen minutes, I run out of juice after two days. They usually sputter out of power before the full 30 minutes on the second day, which I prefer to having them complete the cycle because the dying pods prompt me to plug them in. There’s no battery-level indicator, so if they turn off without dying, I don’t know if I need to charge them unless I remember how many days it’s been since they were charged and how many cycles I’ve run since (I don’t have the mindspace for that). The worst is when I turn them on thinking they are charged, and the battery runs out in a few short minutes—then I have to wait three hours for them to charge. I wish they had some way to let me know how much juice was left.

My only other wish is that I’d like the heat and vibration to reach down to my arch better. But that would require a bigger, more encompassing device, like a boot. Oh! Nike and Hyperice made one of those too. I may have to try them someday, but for now I’ll luxuriate in the easy, slip-on, push-button pleasure that the Hyperslides add to my mornings.

Mammograms May Offer Early Insight Into Cardiovascular Disease

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Mammograms are widely used to help detect breast cancer, but they may also offer insight into heart health.

The routine X-ray screening may identify breast arterial calcification (BAC), which is the presence of calcium deposits within the walls of the small arteries in the breast.

On a mammogram, BAC is commonly identified as thin, white, parallel lines that give a “tram-track” appearance.

While BAC is harmless to the breasts, it is associated with a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular disease. A 2022 study found women with BAC were 51% more likely to develop heart disease or experience a stroke than those without it.

Laxmi Mehta, MD, a cardiologist at The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center, said BAC is associated with cardiovascular risk factors, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol. Additionally, BAC has been connected to:

“The reasons for BAC resulting in higher risks for cardiovascular disease are not definitely determined; however, there are some potential mechanisms, one being that they share similar risk factors that result in vascular injury or metabolic stress that result in vascular calcification and atherosclerosis,” Mehta told Healthline.

Cheng-Han Chen, MD, interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center, added that because BAC and heart disease share many of the same risk factors, “calcium in breast arteries therefore suggests disease in the heart (coronary) and brain arteries as well,” he told Healthline.

While 1 in 39 females in the United States dies from breast cancer each year, cardiovascular disease causes one out of every three deaths, resulting in 1 death per minute.

“Cardiovascular disease is still the most common cause of death in premenopausal women,” Richard Reitherman, MD, PhD, a radiologist and medical director of breast imaging at MemorialCare Breast Center at Orange Coast Medical Center, told Healthline.

“The presentation of cardiovascular disease in premenopausal women is notoriously different than in postmenopausal women, primarily [because] there are no typical symptoms such as chest pain.”

Reitherman said that a prevalent and common health screening procedure such as a mammogram can lead to the early identification of women with silent cardiovascular disease.

“Cardiovascular outcome studies that have included breast arterial calcifications as a risk factor are increasingly reported in the medical literature,” he said.

“The hope is that identifying younger women with silent cardiovascular risks [like BAC along with] appropriate integrated risk identification, analysis, and interventions can significantly reduce mortality in this age group.”

Mehta said there are several reasons BAC has been overlooked as a risk factor for heart disease.

“Mammograms are completed for assessment of breast cancer, and BAC has been historically an ‘incidental’ finding, which is not clinically relevant from an oncologic standpoint,” she said.

Additionally, there is no standardization on quantification of BAC severity, nor is it a required component of mammogram reports, said Mehta.

However, routine reporting of BAC on mammograms is an urgent goal of the American College of Radiology, Society of Breast Imaging, and Medicare, said Reitherman.

In fact, it is gaining traction in the United States. A new Maryland law, passed on October 1, requires mammography centers to notify women when a BAC is detected on their mammogram.

“Automated identification and reporting of these calcifications detected on screening mammograms will become increasingly prevalent in the coming months [and] years,” said Reitherman.

The next step is to identify this group of women to primary care physicians so that appropriate risk evaluation can take place, Reitherman added.

In addition to doctors routinely screening for conditions related to heart disease, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol, he hopes BAC will also be routinely considered.

“Under current development are protocols to identify patients needing a cardiologist’s training and expertise to appropriately stratify this group of patients,” said Reitherman.

“The common healthcare behavior [of] having regular mammograms not only reduces risk of morbidity and mortality from breast cancer but will hopefully contribute to the reduction of morbidity and mortality from cardiovascular disease in younger women.”

He said this type of integrated healthcare approach demonstrates cross-specialty integration and coordination among women, radiologists, and their healthcare providers to improve women’s lives.

“This is an amazing game-changing opportunity,” Reitherman said.

Mehta said it’s reasonable for women to ask if BAC is present on their mammogram report.

“But if it is not uniformly reported by the radiologist, the individual clinician may not have the answer,” she said.

More research is needed before BAC on mammograms can be used routinely to predict heart disease.

Finding out your BAC could provide additional information to help you and your doctors decide how aggressively to address your cardiovascular risk factors, Chen said.

Reitherman agreed. “The goal of reducing morbidity and mortality from any cause such as cardiovascular disease is based on recognition of risk factors, followed by appropriate lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions,” he said.

sweeping concrete cutouts shape ZAO’s sculptural art center in rizhao

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

eye-shaped openings carve through the concrete structure to create an open-air cultural space beside a wooded hillside and lake.

ZAO / Zhang Ke Architecture Office has completed the Rizhao Community Art Center in China‘s Shandong province, where a sculptural concrete structure rises from a wooded hillside beside a small lake. The building appears to hover above the landscape, its heavy upper volume supported at three corners while sweeping, eye-shaped openings carve through the level beneath. From a distance, its curved surfaces seem to extend the contours of the surrounding terrain.

Located in Bailuwan, also known as Egret Bay, the project forms part of a larger residential and resort development along China’s Yellow Sea coast. Conceived as a cultural destination for the surrounding community, the center brings exhibition spaces together with areas for public gatherings and performances. Its unusual arrangement places the galleries underground, leaves the central level open to the landscape, and introduces a series of courtyards above.

A network of deep, curving concrete beams supports the upper level, transferring its weight through three structural cores that also accommodate the stairways. This allows the architects to leave much of the ground floor open, with broad openings extending toward the grassy slopes. The concrete ceiling rises and dips overhead, creating an expansive sheltered space that changes in height as visitors move through it.

Footbridges connect this central level with the surrounding roads and pathways, while its northeastern side opens toward the lake. Designed to accommodate outdoor cinema, theater performances, and community events, the space can also be used simply as a place to pass through or gather. With access from several directions, the building becomes part of the landscape’s circulation, offering a public space beneath its substantial concrete roof.

The building’s exterior is formed from cast-in-place concrete, with insulated double-layer walls that retain the grain of their timber formwork. The horizontal impressions lend texture to the large curved surfaces, particularly along the underside of the structure. At the junction between the upper and lower volumes, a narrow opening allows glimpses of the surrounding greenery to enter the otherwise enclosed concrete stairwells.

Moving upstairs, visitors encounter a very different spatial experience. Here, the structural framework defines a sequence of trapezoidal courtyards and smaller community rooms, arranged along winding passages. Inspired by the inward-facing spaces of traditional Chinese gardens, the upper level alternates between enclosed interiors and pockets of open sky. An accessible rooftop terrace eventually opens the building back toward the surrounding forest and lake.

Beneath the grassy hillside, the center’s exhibition spaces are reached by a gently sloping concrete ramp. The gallery level follows the existing topography, with a sculptural ceiling that echoes the changing contours of the ground above. Structural elements divide the interior into interconnected spaces, allowing visitors to move between more enclosed rooms and wider areas for displaying art.

Across these different levels, ZAO uses the same concrete structure to create distinctly different encounters with the landscape. The upper courtyards offer sheltered spaces for reflection, while the central floor remains exposed to the surrounding terrain. Below, the galleries turn visitors’ attention toward art within the hillside itself. Together, these spaces give the community a building that can be explored as readily as the landscape around it.

Digital Approval Workflow: How to Audit Your Current Setup

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A digital approval workflow allows businesses to streamline documentation processes and ensures the right people are approving requests. But an approval workflow doesn’t necessarily operate efficiently in practice.

By carrying out an audit, organizations will have a better understanding of how their approval process is operating. This will allow you to identify any inefficiencies in your approval process.

What is a Digital Approval Workflow Audit? It is a structured process to review your digital approval processes. It can help identify whether you are making sure requests are being approved correctly and efficiently.

It can also help you identify any redundant processes, delays, missing documentation and lack of ownership. A typical digital approval workflow audit will look at:

Whether your approval workflow is structured, Who is responsible for approving requests, Whether requests are being approved efficiently, Are there any documents in place to document the process, Is there an audit trail, Are sensitive documents only being accessed by authorized users and can managers see how approvals are happening.

The aim is not to look for errors in your process but understand how your process is operating and whether it is meeting your operational requirements.

Why Should Businesses Audit Their Digital Approval Workflow? Regular audits will help ensure your approval process is running efficiently.

One of the most common issues with an approval workflow is requests getting stuck in limbo as nobody has approved them. By carrying out an audit you can identify where delays are happening and whether certain stages are causing unnecessary delays.

For example, purchase requests may need to go through 3 managers when they just require 1 or 2.

Every approval should have an owner. If you don’t have clear owners it can cause confusion amongst staff members as they won’t know who should be approving a document.

By carrying out an audit you can identify whether every stage has an owner and whether decisions are being documented.

You need to ensure you have reliable documentation in place for any important decisions. An approval audit trail should include all the relevant details such as the request, supporting documents, who approved, time stamps, comments and what the outcome was.

Having this information will make it easier to review previous decisions and identify any discrepancies. Structured approval processes will make it more useful for you than emails.

4.Reduce Manual Processes By carrying out an audit you can identify any repetitive processes. For example, staff may be manually sending requests, sending reminders and updating spreadsheets.

This will help you identify which processes you can automate.

Different departments may have different approval standards. This can lead to inconsistencies in decisions.

By reviewing your approval workflows you can ensure they are operating using standard processes. Whilst still allowing specific requirements for different types of requests.

A successful audit should cover the whole approval process.

For example, a purchase approval may follow this process:

Request has been made by an employee -> Reviewed by the department manager -> Checked by the Finance team for budget-> Final approval by Senior Manager -> Decision has been received by employee.

Make a note of all the different stages, including any manual processes and how information is being communicated. This will help you to identify if there is a structured process in place.

Ensure everyone has an owner for each approval.

Having approval tied to roles will help stop any disruption to your workflow if employees change roles.

Ensure you have rules in place for where requests are going.

Are approvals in the correct order for the type of document? Some processes may require sequential approvals whilst others may be able to have multiple people reviewing at once.

Are there any extra approvals being triggered based on criteria? For example, purchase value, type of document or risk.

Having a clear structure will help reduce any unnecessary approvals.

Review how long requests are taking to go through each approval stage.

Look for any patterns such as: Requests remaining pending for a few days, Repeated follow-up from employees, Approval stages always causing delays, Requests being sent back due to missing information, Unnecessary review steps.

Useful metrics to track include average approval time, number of requests in limbo, rejection rate and the number of times requests have been revised.

This will help you identify if delays are caused by the approval workflow, unclear ownership or incomplete submissions.

A good digital approval workflow should have a clear audit trail.

Name of requester and submission date, Details of request, Supporting documents, Names/roles of approvers, Approval/rejection, Time stamps, Comments, Reassignments, escalations and status, Final approval.

Ensure users can view previous versions of documents. Your audit trail should give enough information so you can see what happened during the approval process.

Approval workflows often include confidential financial, operational and business information. Therefore you should review your security.

Check If: Employees can only see relevant information, Approvals are in line with organizational authority, Sensitive documents are secure, Previous employees/inactive accounts have the correct restrictions, Important actions are being documented, Backup processes are in place.

You should also ensure your processes are in line with any internal policies and legal/regulatory requirements.

Notifications will help your approvers know when they need to do something. However, if they aren’t managed well it can cause unnecessary communication.

During your audit check whether notifications being sent at the right point and pending requests are being escalated.

For example, if a manager isn’t responding within the set time frame the request can be sent to an authorized backup approver.

See if your notification settings useful, deadlines are realistic and escalation rules are clearly communicated.

Those who use the approval process regularly can provide useful feedback on any issues.

Ask your team members whether the process is easy to understand, or requests are often delayed, whether they experience having issues accessing documents?

Once you have completed your audit you should split your findings into 3 categories:

Critical issues – Security issues, unauthorized approvals, missing documentation or important compliance requirements.

Operational issues – Delays, lack of ownership, continual requests for information and unnecessary approvals.

Improvement Opportunities – Better reporting, automated reminders, simpler forms and improved integration.

Ensure each issue has an owner and set a realistic timeframe for resolving. Prioritize any issues based on how much they could impact your organization.

Carrying out a digital approval workflow audit will help you understand whether your approval process is efficient, secure and has good ownership. By reviewing your approval stages, ownership, documentation, audit trail and performance you can identify any issues.

Using a audit checklist will help make the process easier to manage. You should also review real examples of approvals, ask for staff feedback and create an improvement plan.

Most importantly you should regularly carry out audits. This will help ensure you have consistent approvals, reduce delays and create adaptable workflows.
