Spain 2-3 England: Thomas Tuchel’s men clung on despite going down to 10 men to end Spain’s 43-game unbeaten run

England hold out, for a raucous win that will rightly be held up. Sure, it might not have been what it could have been as Ben White surged in for a sensational third in Madrid… but that last word remains key.

England beat the world and European champions 3-2 in their own capital, to end the longest unbeaten run in international history, that stretched back to March 2024.

Spain couldn’t make it 44 games, as England just about made it to 94 minutes. That was ultimately down to a brilliant double save from James Trafford, who was one of many positives on another Nations League night of enjoyably entertaining drama. Might Jordan Pickford’s first place have gone with Spain’s unbeaten run?

Trafford had to show his best. This was never going to be easy, no matter how Thomas Tuchel’s side made it look for 63 minutes - until Elliot Anderson’s red card. That will be the manager’s main takeaway from the game, the “proof of concept” as he put it the night before. With 11 against 11, England were denying this Spanish team the ball and creating some swift chances that the world champions were struggling to live with.

That was what the game-opening goal came from. Even if there was also debate about Anderson’s follow-through in that instance, England had opened another gap in the Spanish structure while looking so more cohesive and coruscating themselves.

This is a team that can sustain attacking waves much better than the one who mostly forced their way through the summer on individual impact. White’s supreme goal, that had the world champions looking at each other and pointing fingers, was the grand exhibition of that.

Just as crucially, it was so different to what Spain do. It was something of England's own. In between, Ezri Konsa scored the plundered set-piece header that his recent performances have deserved. Jude Bellingham was at that point bossing the Spanish midfield, in the kind of way people have long imagined as his career has only escalated.

This, and the very confidence from such a marker of a win, are what England have to take through to finally win that trophy at Euro 2028.

So many positives, so many solutions… until Spain asked the most severe questions, and Tuchel didn’t always have the answers. That is where there is obvious context to a win that should still inspire confidence.

It clearly meant much more to England given the fact Spain are the world champions who had won at Wembley. They're the ones who have done it, the benchmark. Tuchel and all the players were aware they had to do something more, to close this year with a performance that offered more for the future; that they had to stand up.

They had admittedly felt frustration from that Wembley defeat; that they could have won. As a consequence, England just looked sharper for pretty much all of the first hour. Spain didn’t even have a shot on target in the first half, as Unai Simon immediately had to withstand an England flurry.

The world champions weren’t counter-pressing in the way they were in the summer. England were starting to overrun them. The ultimate expression of that, in White’s goal, did seem to spark something in response.

It is possible that England themselves even got a bit overconfident. In what has been a theme in this double of games - that ended 3-2 apiece but see England top the group on goal difference - Tuchel’s side gave the ball away for a Spanish goal.

This time it was Anthony Gordon, who seemed to forget what Lamine Yamal can do. Even the Barcelona playmaker took a poor touch from there, leading to what felt like a needless challenge from Anderson. He was sent off, and Spain were set off.

It was immediately a different game. Spain were doing everything they weren’t in the first half. A change of midfield helped, with Fermin Lopez offering the impetus that Luis De La Fuente’s midfield had been missing. Nico Williams’s brilliant finish from an utterly sensational Yamal pass was then a goal to match White’s.

And so, in what was almost humorous irony, England ended 2026 in the way they’d ended its defining games: backs to the wall, a backline of nine, Tuchel hauling off his best attackers, and questions about the subs.

Spain really pressed. Yamal was at the heart of every move, creating almost every chance. The game then seemed to just constantly be the ball being fired across the England box. England were probably one bad bounce away from another inquest about Tuchel’s substitutions, and how he manages these matches.

But that bounce didn’t come. The equaliser didn't come. England stood up.

And that might be because of a decision Tuchel doesn’t even have to make. Trafford made his claim. England made their mark.

Tuchel has ultimately overseen their most significant win for some time, at least as regards what it means for finally winning that trophy. That’s what all this is about, after all.

Spain can fairly say that what really matters is what happens in tournaments, but it didn’t feel meaningless as they toiled to keep that unbeaten record and the Madrid crowd willed them on. That run is now over. Might that finally signal that this could be England’s time?

It is far too early to say, but this was the end to the year Tuchel wanted. It was the performance England needed.

Arsenal 1-1 London City Lionesses: The Gunners drew at the Emirates for the fifth game in a row as Izzy Goodwin cancelled out Frida Maanum’s opener after just 98 seconds

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Alone in an empty penalty box, Mary Earps dropped to her knees and yelled into the cold night air. With an eight-save, player-of-the-match display, the ‘Queen of Stops’ properly marked the end of her exile and return to the Women’s Super League with an outstanding performance between the posts for the London City Lionesses in their 1-1 draw with Arsenal, a reminder to anyone who needed it that the former England No 1 is not done yet.

“Tiring,” Earps exhaled when asked to sum up a night where she did it all, including a sensational late stop to tip Frida Maanum’s dipping strike onto the bar and earn her side a point. There was agility, presence, bravery, but how about the timing, as well, to produce such a performance in front of the biggest crowd of the season at the Emirates and keep the disrupters London City above Arsenal in the race for the Champions League positions. In front of Sarina Wiegman, too.

But Earps and London City also managed to ensure that Arsenal’s miserable wait without a home win this season continued, with five successive draws at the Emirates to begin their campaign. It will be little consolation to Renee Slegers that her side showed more intent and direction that in their previous draws against Crystal Palace, Manchester United, Birmingham City and Aston Villa this season: this was billed as a must-win for Arsenal and the sight of the goalkeeper Misa Rodriguez going forward for a corner in the final seconds of stoppage time, at 1-1, revealed as much.

That Arsenal’s corner failed to even reach the box, with Smilla Holmberg’s outswinging delivery instead drifting out of play, summed up how perplexing their season has become. A team who started the season with title aspirations, to go along with the club’s wider ambitions, are left sitting sixth in the WSL after 10 games, 12 points off leaders Chelsea and six points off London City, the holders of the final Champions League place. Arsenal are simultaneously a team capable of going to Barcelona and the Camp Nou on Wednesday and drawing 1-1, while also failing to register a victory in front of the biggest women’s football crowd in the country in the last six months.

In a way, London City were the sort of opponents Arsenal should have enjoyed facing. The plucky upstarts, with the billionaire owner and Ballon d’Or holder in midfield, are expansive in possession and have interesting ideas about what to do on the ball. They arrived at the Emirates with a plan and ambition to do more than just nullify their opponents and it was a measure of their ability to dictate that Arsenal, at times, did not even attempt to press. London City had more possession and more passes, which for Arsenal at least allowed them to break out of the sterile, side-to-side patterns of play that had defined their previous stalemates here.

And there was a reminder, too, that Arsenal can be dangerous when they are presented with space, to attack in a way where they can move up the pitch and have a shot within seconds. Earps was kept busy, after all, and a lot of Arsenal managed to find made sense, at last: using Alessia Russo’s ability to work the channels and her awareness to bring others into play, looking to release Lisa Baum on the right and instructing her to be direct when she had a clear line to goal.

Maanum’s goal, after just 10 minutes, was an example of that. Emily Fox was invited to drive forwards and was directed to find Russo’s bending run off the shoulder of the last defender. There was a synchronisation to the way Russo didn’t even need to look up to know that Maanum would be arriving on the edge of the box, if she could find the right angle on the pull-back. There was a sense to Slegers leaving Olivia Smith and many of her summer signings on the bench and trusting the understanding between players like Russo and Maanum.

But then Arsenal this season have usually displayed a way of shooting themselves in the foot, too. As Maanum celebrated and her team-mates huddled around her, Leah Williamson was pointing to her head and telling them they still needed to stay switched on. Yet within 98 seconds London City were level due to a moment, a glitch in the system. It came from Williamson, failing to match Izzy Goodwin’s run, stepping in at the wrong time in the box. The England captain was horribly bypassed by a centre-forward who offered a sharp contrast by simply being in total control of what she was doing.

The frustration from Arsenal is their errors keep piling on top of each other, the scrutiny too. It is now just three wins from 10 in the WSL. Earps, in her defiance, sensed her opportunity to ensure there would be no respite.

Syfy’s 5-Season Zombie Series Breaks All The Storytelling Rules

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Even the most diehard fans of zombie stories will struggle to deny that the genre can get repetitive. Across zombie TV shows, movies, books, games, and graphic novels, survivors tend to face the same exhausting routine: find safety and shelter, watch it eventually fall to the undead, then hit the road to find another refuge and begin the cycle anew. The specifics may change from story to story, but the genre still over relies on this tried-and-tested narrative loop.

From George A. Romero’s Night of the Living Dead in 1968 to modern hits like The Walking Dead, zombie entertainment has repeatedly built suspense around supposedly secure shelters becoming death traps. It's an effective setup, but one that can soon feel stale to experienced genre fans. That’s why one Syfy series should be celebrated for finding so many new ways to keep its undead apocalypse moving. Z Nation, the five-season saga that made zombies fun again, throws storytelling tradition out the window and gives its survivors much bigger problems than finding their next temporary sanctuary.

Following survivors escorting Murphy (Keith Allan), a potential source of a zombie cure, across America, Z Nation turns saving humanity from the undead into a spectacularly messy road trip. The cult classic series offers a refreshing alternative to the likes of The Walking Dead and The Last of Us, especially for viewers exhausted by the relentless misery that tends to define the genre’s small-screen entries. While its campy performances and B-movie sensibilities might initially discourage viewers more used to prestige TV, beneath the cheap thrills sits one of the most subversive zombie shows ever made.

Initially, Z Nation looks ready to follow The Walking Dead’s familiar blueprint. After an opening involving Murphy receiving an experimental vaccine before getting bitten by zombies overrunning a military facility, the Syfy show drops viewers into a familiar-feeling survivor camp in upstate New York that feels plucked out of literally any zombie TV show. Murphy’s arrival and the reveal of his apparent immunity creates a central mystery from the same trope The Last of Us is built on, and the immediate setting suggest a The Walking Dead-like lengthy struggle to protect a precarious home. That’s when Z Nation pulls out the rug from under smug viewers schooled in zombie stories.

The camp falls almost immediately, and its survivors inherit a military mission to transport humanity’s potential salvation to California. This kicks off a cross-country zombie TV show built around an action-filled road trip rather than finding another home isn’t the main objective. The central characters in Z Nation have somewhere to go and a reason to get there. They’re never simply escaping whatever destroyed their previous shelter. Obstacles like the need to find food, water, and ammo in an apocalyptic world defined by scarcity still remain. However, these take a backseat to the roller coaster plot and the larger-than-life communities and dangerous survivors Murphy and his escort encounter on the way to the West coast.

That structure gives Z Nation room to keep changing, and it makes full use of the narrative wiggle room. The original delivery mission eventually gives way to competing visions of humanity’s future, including efforts to build a society accommodating intelligent undead. Survival remains at the core, just like any great zombie TV series, but the scope means that survival in this instance comes to encompass humanity as a whole. What’s more, thanks to the show’s willingness to embrace wild plot twists and shocking narrative developments, even the equally familiar setup of a savior figure’s journey across an apocalyptic wasteland never feels predictable.

Unlike many of its contemporaries across its 2014-2018 run, as well as many zombie series that followed, Z Nation never feels trapped in the narrative loop of waiting for a fragile period of safety to suddenly collapse. Its forward momentum is relentlessly fast-paced and the trajectory of its narrative arcs are impossible to predict. Every episode can bring a different kind of story and setting, making it the perfect zombie TV show for anyone sick of the formulaicness that often plagues the genre.

Breaking the usual survival narrative cycle most zombie TV shows follow would already make Z Nation refreshing for experienced fans of the genre. However, its storytelling freedom extends well beyond ditching the familiar search-for-safety loop. The cross-country road trip setup would promise incredible variety even in a grounded post-apocalyptic series, but Z Nation is perhaps as far from grounded as it’s possible to be. To say that Z Nation isn’t afraid to get wild is an understatement.

This is a series where a tornado full of zombies is treated as a perfectly reasonable plot development. Elsewhere, a giant cheese wheel becomes a downhill zombie disposal system, and botanical researchers produce a strain of undead-fertilized cannabis whose plant is connected to a plant-zombie hybrid. There’s a zombie tsunami, a giant rolling ball of animated corpses, and even the birth of a zombie baby that parodies Biblical Nativity scenes (complete with undead wise men following a star). These aren’t isolated jokes in an otherwise gritty survival thriller, either. The absurdity is part of Z Nation’s central appeal.

Even Murphy’s immunity refuses to remain a straightforward cure storyline. As Z Nation continues, his changing condition leads to developing the power to control other zombies, blue skin, and a host of other strange abilities. Humanity’s supposed salvation even eventually starts pursuing the creation of his own zombie-hybrid society. The familiar immune-survivor premise becomes a story about a deeply unreliable man deciding whether saving the species is worth it, and if he can really still consider himself human at all.

It doesn’t have grounded horror or the restrained, brooding atmosphere associated with many post-apocalyptic prestige TV shows, but that’s part of Z Nation’s charm. While the effects can be scrappy, and plausibility is something it lets other shows worry about, the Syfy series is still inventive, ambitious, and surprisingly coherent. What’s more, for all its absurdity, it’s not silly. The show is more than capable of taking itself seriously when it needs to. For viewers bored with watching another settlement slowly disintegrate, few zombie shows offer a better antidote than Z Nation.
