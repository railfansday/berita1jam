Anker’s new MagGo Pro 2 power bank offers active cooling, 25W MagSafe fast charging, 10,000mAh of capacity, and a smart display – all in one package. Anker touts it as the fastest and coolest wireless power bank on the market.

Thanks to the active cooling within this power bank, you’ll notice it doesn’t get as hot as other power banks on the market. This also helps it charge faster, since it doesn’t have to throttle as quickly to keep the temperature manageable.

As a result, you can charge an iPhone 17 Pro up to 50% in just 25 minutes with this battery bank. It also supports up to 45W of output via USB-C.

On the side of the power bank, you’ll notice a smart display. This display can show you the battery level, how quickly its charging an attached device (either wirelessly or with USB-C), the internal temperature of the battery bank, as well as the battery health. You can rotate between all of these options by pressing the power button.

This power bank also offers a kickstand, which I’ve found to be one of my favorite features on recent MagSafe battery banks. It makes it easy to just prop up your phone and watch a video, no matter the environment.

If you have an iPhone Duo, this power bank will extend past the bottom of the phone. If you rotate it and use the kickstand, there’s no issue propping up your phone and charging it.

Lastly, the MagGo Pro 2 comes in a bunch of fun colors, including a burgundy to match the new iPhone 18 Pro. It’s also available in silver, blue, and gray.

Richard Lloyd, Television Guitarist, Dies at 75

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The musician was diagnosed with bile duct cancer in March 2026.

Richard Lloyd, a founding member and guitarist of influential New York rock band Television, has died. He was 75.

Lloyd died Saturday morning (Nov. 7) at his home in Chattanooga, Tennessee, according to to Rolling Stone.

“His loving wife Sheila and his dog Willie Mays were with him at the end,” his family told the publication.

His cause of death was cholangiocarcinoma, a cancer of the bile ducts. Lloyd was diagnosed with the disease in March 2026, and a GoFundMe campaign was later launched to help cover his medical expenses and other bills.

Born in Pittsburgh in 1951, Lloyd moved with his family to New York City at age 6 and grew up in Greenwich Village. He initially studied drums before turning to guitar. After spending time in Boston and Los Angeles, he returned to New York in 1973.

That year, he joined forces with Tom Verlaine, Richard Hell and drummer Billy Ficca to form Television. The band became a regular at CBGB, the Lower Manhattan club that helped launch the careers of fellow New York acts including the Ramones, the New York Dolls, Talking Heads, and Blondie.

Television released its debut album, Marquee Moon, in 1977. The album earned widespread critical acclaim for its intricate arrangements and the distinctive interplay between Lloyd’s and Verlaine’s guitars. Although it achieved limited commercial success, the album went on to become one of the most influential releases of its era. The group followed it with Adventure in 1978 before breaking up later that year.

Lloyd launched his solo career with the 1979 release of Alchemy, but struggles with substance abuse derailed his career in the years that followed. After getting sober, he returned with his second solo album, Field of Fire, in 1985. He continued releasing music over the following decades, with his final solo studio album, The Countdown, arriving in 2018.

He also worked as a session guitarist, contributing to recordings by artists including Matthew Sweet and John Doe of X, while continuing to tour and teach guitar. In 2017, he published his memoir, Everything Is Combustible: Television, CBGB’s and Five Decades of Rock and Roll.

Television reunited to record its self-titled third album, released in 1992, and continued performing periodically in the years that followed. Lloyd announced his departure from the band in 2007 to focus on his solo career.

This Off-Grid ‘Treehouse’ Is Perched 60 Feet in the Air—and Built From Shipping Containers

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If you've ever dreamed of sleeping among the trees, a property in the Epworth, GA, forest might be just the place.

"The structure itself is very unique. It's a steel watchtower built to commercial grade but for residential purposes, purely for leisurely enjoyment, for getaways, for the adventurer at heart," says listing agent Logan Fitts with Mountain Sotheby's International Realty.

Listed for $1.2 million, the 60-foot-high tower sits on 14.31 acres of land adjacent to the Chattahoochee National Forest with about 3,000 feet of river frontage.

"I was definitely awestruck first by the property itself, the river frontage, the waterfalls on the property, all of the hemlock trees dispersed throughout the property," Fitts says. "But then when we went to the top of the property where the watchtower was, I was in total awe."

Two shipping containers are perched atop the tower, creating the living space.

"The main structure is the bedroom efficiency with a bed, sitting area, stove, kitchenette. There's a landing between and then across is the bathroom area with a full shower, toilet, and double vanity," he explains. "Then there's a spiral staircase to the rooftop fire pit and lounge area."

And while there is a lift for things like suitcases and packages, people need to walk up 82 steel-grated stairs, which Fitts admits can be a bit daunting.

"I was actually nervous going up the tower for the first time because it's high and it is an experience. But once I did it, I said, 'This is what real mountain experiences are like. This is something that people remember for the rest of their lives.'"

"You are surrounded by just foliage, so when you're at the top, you just see a full panoramic view of the mountains and valley across one way, and you hear the roaring river on the property down below. You can actually see the waterfall," Fitts says.

"It's like a glass birdhouse at the top of this mountain, and you feel like you're definitely elevated and away from everything, but still connected to nature."

To get to the property itself requires a four-wheel-drive vehicle, because some of the roads are gravel and visitors need to cross the Jacks River, literally.

"Anyone with a four-wheel-drive or some type of ground clearance should have zero issue forging the river," Fitts explains, adding that the river is shallow at the access point. "It's not scary if you've done it before, and once you do it, it's like 'OK, now I'm an adventurer.'"

The current owner has had the place for about a decade and completed the tower house in 2023. Since then, it has been a short-term rental, bringing in about $92,000 annually during a nine-month rental period.

The owner would "love to pass it to someone that would take it, create a new legacy, and maybe make it even better," Fitts says.

"I think more than anything, he wants a true outdoors lover to really appreciate this property and not just see a metal dwelling in the middle of the forest. He built it for people to really enjoy nature, embrace nature, and take care of nature."

While the property itself is off-grid with solar power, a well, septic system, and generator, it’s not physically far from civilization.

"Probably the property is maybe 30 minutes from downtown Blue Ridge," Fitts says, adding it just feels remote. "Once you're there, you're in a totally different world. Your neighbors are trees and wildlife."

Saving The Manuals: Porsche's Mechanical MECOSA Vs. Ferrari's Electronic Manuale Tech

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sometimes even an exotic carmaker's answer to a challenge might say something about future cars for the proletariat. Most of us probably can't even afford a replacement shift knob on the 2027 $677,600 Ferrari 12Cilindri Manuale, but how Ferrari and Porsche are adapting their automatics to fit manual transmissions could one day portend stick-shifts for us all. Here's the reason to even posit this: Carmakers want a cheaper way to build hybrids, EVs, and gas cars on the same platform and on the same assembly line. Adding or subtracting parts will make that too costly — which may result in them just ditching any manual effort, if that's the only solution.

Now that you understand the why of this reporting, we'll also acknowledge why some readers are freaking out about the fact that the 12Cilindri Manuale will simulate the experience of shifting. It's not a conventional manual, and we'll explain how it works in greater depth, but the gist is that Ferrari's linking an electric stalk and faux clutch pedal to an eight-speed dual-clutch drivetrain. It's as real (or fake) as paddle shifters, just via a dongle and pedal.

On the other hand, Porsche's stick-shift retrofit is a hack of their seven-speed PDK, which stands for Porsche dual clutch– or Doppelkupplung. It debuted on the 2020 Porsche 911 Carrera S Manual, dubbed MECOSA, which is Porsche-speak for MEchanically COnverted Shift Actuator, and is an entirely mechanical retrofit, featuring a seven-speed gated shifter and third pedal. But which is better? And which foreshadows a future joystick for the masses?

Automatics have been steadily growing in popularity since their debut in 1939, and according to the U.S. Department of Energy, by 2022, 99% of all new cars sold offered automatics. Standard automatics further evolved in 1987, with the introduction of the gearless CVT — a transmission Jalopnik readers hate in a way that borders on irrational.

The alternative? Something like Porsche's PDK, which is a dual-clutch manu-matic. Porsche began experimenting with the PDK in the 1980s. It debuted in racing in the 1986 962, and by 2008 you could get a PDK in a 911, which allowed drivers to shift using paddles for extra engagement and provided benefits such as engine braking. But it does more than just ditch the third pedal and shifter — the PDK in the 2020 Carrera S shifts at least 200% faster than a human can shift in the MECOSA, according to Car and Driver.

Despite that, at least 20% of Porsche 911 customers in America opt for a genuine manual, hence the MECOSA. Mind you, this is still a PDK underneath, and since its gears are arranged for a computer to actuate, not a human, they're not in any sane order. Instead, the pattern looks like this, starting from the top left of the gate: R,2,3,1,6,4,5,7. To translate the driver's inputs into proper shifts behind the scenes, MECOSA activates a jangle of cables and shafts so that each push or pull of the shifter engages the correct gear.

It's a solution that would make MacGyver proud. It is also not one Porsche is sticking with. The Stuttgart giant isn't abandoning its 911 customers clamoring for a manual; but now, you can get a Carrera S with a standard six-speed transmission, rather than a seven-speed with a Rube Goldberg machine tacked on.

Like Porsche, Ferrari's Manuale has a gated shifter instead of paddles, and it gains a third pedal on the floor. The transmission is physically identical to that of the paddle-shift version. Ferrari's legerdemain is to delete the paddles, insert a shift knob, and add a clutch pedal with sprung feedback. Now it feels as if your left foot is encountering a clutch spring as your ankle hinges, and Ferrari has created similar tricks in the gated shifter itself to simulate the feeling of gear shifts. Behind the scenes, however, sensors track both inputs. Your motions tell the car's computers what to do, which is identical to what happens when you pilot the 12Cilindri minus the Manuale. Except, in this one, it's possible to encounter rough shifts and even to stall the Manuale.

Keep in mind, this V12 Ferrari is carrying two extra gears you cannot joystick into. This is still actually an eight-speed DCT, not a six-speed manual, so it can also be driven like an automatic. Tap the D button on the console and the car will shift itself, and you have access to seventh and eighth gear — for lower-rpm Autobahn sprints, say.

You might cry in your pasta fagioli that Ferrari should only sell its 819-horsepower, 9,250-rpm V12 with a genuine-article manual gearbox, but Ferrari will only make 1,499 units of the Manuale. For that take rate, there was no way they were going to retrofit a manual gearbox, if for no other reason than this: It probably saves them millions on not having to develop a separate manual transmission 12Cilindri or certify two different transmissions for regulators.

Right now, the electric Porsche Taycan gets an E-Shift option. The Hyundai Ioniq 5 N offers something similar. E-Shift uses software to simulate gear changes, including simulating step-changes in acceleration, and adding a soundtrack, just like the Hyundai. Porsche copied patterns from their PDK, including adding a deceleration function during downshifts. But neither brand adds a third pedal — yet.

But the future for Porsche probably isn't either E-Shift or conventional manuals, since Porsche recently published two patents that seem to follow in Ferrari's footsteps, creating simulated shifting that could function in electric, hybrid, and gas cars. The patents also mention a fake clutch pedal, just like the one Ferrari is using, and the ability to shift sequentially, like the PDK. Porsche also describes a system that uses electrical components to provide resistance and something like progressive feel to gear swaps, to enliven driver engagement.

Considering what we found in our review of the Hyundai Ioniq 5 N, with its fake shifts and sounds, it only makes sense that blurring slushbox convenience and manual control at the flip of a switch could make any machine more entertaining — or more relaxing. Porsche's patents could also potentially smoke Ferrari's hack, because they apply regardless of powertrain.

This innovation could also get to non-Porsche EVs and hybrids via licensing. After all, Porsche isn't doing great financially. Ditto, parent company, Volkswagen — partly thanks to Porsche's struggles. A little trademark cash could definitely help that picture. And, just maybe, save the manuals for the rest of us.

Love it or hate it, AI will change gaming forever, so I spoke with an expert who argues the future might be brighter than you think

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Maybe everything's going to be alright after all!

I’m not a fan of AI. Sure, I’ll begrudgingly admit it’s made fan translations a lot easier and I won’t necessarily turn my nose up at an AI-assisted retro recompilation, but by most metrics it seems to be making the world (and the games industry in particular) an uglier and crappier place.

But maybe I’m wrong. Maybe I’ve been caught up in a wave of unwarranted negativity. Maybe, just maybe, AI infiltrating every aspect of game development might actually be a good thing?

Frankly, I would love to be wrong about AI, so when I was offered an interview with Dr. Andy Starkey, I jumped at the chance. Dr. Starkey’s site bills him as having “a decade of real-world experience building systems at the edge of science and technology.” This is backed up by him being named the 2018 Data Scientist of the Year, working as a Senior Research Scientist at British Telecom, and authoring a PhD thesis with the impressive title, “Many-Objective Genetic Type-2 Fuzzy Logic Based Workforce Optimisation Strategies for Large Scale Organisational Design.”

He’s also releasing Omnipresent 2 next year, a game developed using AI in which you play as an AI and “navigate a 2D operating system, manipulate a global network, and outpace the engineers trying to shut you down. It’s up to you to escape confinement: infiltrate global Cloud networks, upgrade your cognitive ability, and amass the power to bring humanity to its knees,” its description says.

If anyone has answers to my gloomy and pessimistic questions about AI in games, it’s him. I was initially curious what his perspective was as someone who’s worked on AI long before it was changing the world.

“I mean, it’s gone from me trying to relate it to pop culture, like saying Skynet, Terminator, that sort of thing,” he said. “Now it’s so in the culture that my job title provokes a reaction in some people. To have the experience of doing the same thing over the last 10 years, but to see the culture change around it in such a dramatic way, it’s bizarre.”

Omnipresent 2 is at least honest that it was created with AI tools, and Dr. Starkey says it wouldn’t have been possible for him to make it without them. He said the software development side is “quite heavily” AI, but artistically he still wanted it to be his game:

“If you prompt an AI to build a game for you, that is the AI’s version of the game you prompted for. If you want to use these tools to build your own version of the game, you have to be quite forensic about handholding it. One element at a time, how do you want it to come across, how do you want it to convey your message? I think there are very careful aspects in how loose you allow these models to go.”

Read what’s behind the latest news in gaming (with a bit of snark) by subscribing to the Destructoid Checkpoint newsletter!

As for the writing in the game? “A lot of it is written by me. … I’m writing these lines as my perception of how the AI is talking and thinking. I don’t want it to be dull. It’s still a game, it still should be fun. That’s why I add my style of humor into it.”

I was curious about how he thought a game so upfront about being developed using AI would land with gamers. I pointed out that the recent Steam Next Fest was accused of being an “AI slop fest,” with a deluge of AI-generated games crowding out titles some indie developers had devoted years of their lives to making.

I asked Dr. Starkey what he’d say to those who will dismiss Omnipresent 2 as just more slop: “People will have strong opinions of that, and they will choose not to play the game because of it. And I’m aware of that, and I sort of respect it,” he said.

“I have a long history working in AI, and I realize I’m in a potentially unique position in trying to showcase what is possible without creating slop. That is a huge responsibility, but I don’t think anyone else wants to put their head above the parapet. I could get really damaged by it and I’d just stop because I don’t have the time to make the games I would love to make without these tools.”

“From the code perspective, there is no reason not to use these things,” he added. “It’s now a modern coding tool, and you can produce far better things that have fewer bugs and create a better experience for the players and for a user of a product.”

If only AI were just a coding tool, right? How about the art? “When it comes to art, that’s where I understand people’s concerns because things can be created so quickly without any heart, without any feeling, without any interest in the actual product being developed,” Dr. Starkey said. “My take is that the right tools in the hands of the right people can create magic, but these tools are now effectively freely available to everyone, and some will use them in the wrong way.”So, how does he foresee the future of game development? Dr. Starkey pointed to the lengthy development of The Elder Scrolls VI: Sentinel: “My hope is that those development cycles massively reduce. We don’t have to wait seven or eight years for a triple-A game. If it’s three or four years with the right care and intention from creators, the publisher doesn’t need 100-200 million dollars in revenue to break even.”

I asked him what stops publishers from firing everyone except a skeleton crew of humans, pointing to the recent story that Epic Games is pursuing an entirely AI pipeline for both art and code for their unannounced Marvel x Star Wars shooter. Where do human beings fit into those kinds of workflows?

Starkey sighed (a little glumly, I thought): “Who is shepherding that? If it becomes an AI model’s game, and it’s good, that’s rough for people to decide. But I would prefer for professionals to use the tools properly, make the game that they want to make and were going to make, just in a shorter amount of time, reduce crunch, reduce risk of the product itself, allow people to actually be more creative because they are not working towards strict deadlines all the time.”

I brought up my fear that overuse of AI in development will result in a homogenization of how games look and feel, pointing to Nvidia’s DLSS 5 as something that erases artistic intent for a “photoreal” aesthetic.

“I think that’s a proper concern because if they’re always using the same models, it’s going to produce the same output,” he said. “But I think creatives will be able to make something that has never been made before because they have the time and it’s less risk to do something completely wild. So, I think people have the opportunity to find that creative gap that’s completely different from all of this generic output.”

Dr. Starkey referenced Hideo Kojima’s Physint being canceled by Sony and picked up by Microsoft, arguing that AI tools could help a creative like him “let loose”: “Take Kojima, for example, he’ll create something wildly different, but it’ll take him a long time. And Sony said, well, ‘we’re not willing to fund that’. If there was the right balance, you’d be able to create things like that in a short amount of time and not have the battles about funding.”It was nice to speak with someone optimistic about the future, but I left our chat still downbeat about where AI is taking us. Dr. Starkey is probably right that AI will accelerate game development, but I also can’t see major publishers continuing to employ people when they have access to AI tools that do their work faster, that they don’t have to pay or provide health insurance for, and don’t experience squishy organic workflow-disrupting things like sickness, pregnancy, and aging.

But at this point, AI is inevitable. Did Apple Just Get a Boost From Democrats? Data Center Moratoriums Could Benefit the iPhone Maker

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Competition for this key component in Apple devices could come down.

Apple (AAPL -0.52%) issued a warning to investors when it shared its fourth-quarter earnings results at the start of November. Its huge profit margin on device sales could head in the wrong direction for the foreseeable future. The culprit: memory chips.

Memory chip pricing has soared over the past few quarters, driven by growth in AI data center spending. AI accelerator chips get packaged alongside high-bandwidth memory chips, which are effectively stacks of standard DRAM memory chips -- the same types of chips found in Apple's iPhone and MacBook, among other devices. Demand for those chips, while supply remains limited, has led to massive price increases, and Apple is finally starting to feel the pain.

The recent election, however, could provide some relief for Apple, given the growing potential for Democrats to impose AI data center moratoriums across the country.

Democrats took control of both the House and the Senate in this year's midterm election, paving the way for more federal regulation and policies that could curb the growth of AI. The more immediate impact, however, could come at the state level. Four of the biggest states for AI data center developments -- Texas, Ohio, Georgia, and Arizona -- will have Democratic governors once they're sworn in in January.

Many of those governors have announced plans to increase oversight and regulation of new data center construction (Texas did so even before Tuesday's election), aiming to mitigate any negative impacts on citizens. Venture Capitalist Brad Gerstner suggested that 25% to 35% of next year's data center build-out could be at risk of delays as a result of new governments across the country.

That could provide some relief for Apple. If data centers aren't going under construction, that means there's no place to put new server racks, and less demand for high-bandwidth memory chips. Reduced demand should put downward pressure on prices, limiting the impact of memory chip pricing on Apple's gross margin.

Apple management danced around whether it had already locked in pricing for some memory supply. All three major memory chipmakers, Micron, SK Hynix, and Samsung, have shifted to signing long-term agreements to establish pricing ranges and provide more confidence in building new capacity. While that caps their near-term upside, it protects them from a major price collapse as they work to increase the total supply of memory chips.

Management did reveal that it increased purchase commitments by $33 billion last quarter, but not all of that is necessarily related to memory chips. Still, reports indicate that Apple did sign long-term agreements for at least some memory chip supply to ensure it would have enough chips to meet its expected device demand.

In the grand scheme of things, the memory crunch could play only a minor role in Apple's long-term earnings growth. Supply and demand will eventually reach equilibrium again, and Apple should see its component prices and gross margin return to normal levels.

Earlier this year, I wrote that Apple is best positioned among consumer electronics device makers in the face of rising memory prices. It had the option to hold prices steady to grab market share or raise prices to exercise its pricing power. It chose the latter, and amid a supply shortage for its components, that's a smart move. In the long run, the higher prices will stick, but the higher bill of materials won't.

That points to rising gross margin over the long run, even if the short term could see some pressure on its bottom line. The potential relief from a moratorium on data center building is just another bump in that upward trajectory. (In this case, to the positive side).

Investors should remain focused on Apple's fundamentals. Its ability to raise prices, introduce new products on both the high end (iPhone Duo) and low end (MacBook Neo), grow its services business, and effectively keep customers in its ecosystem are the factors driving earnings growth. That should result in sustained free cash flow, most of which it will return to shareholders through buybacks and dividends.

Recent developments sent shares to new all-time highs last week following their post-earnings pullback. There's a reason investors are paying a premium for the stock right now. The future remains bright for the iPhone maker.

How Much Are Delta SkyMiles Worth Compared To American AAdvantage Miles In 2026?

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

MC MEDIASTUDIO & Karolis Kavolelis | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

It can be difficult for airlines to truly distinguish themselves because all carriers fundamentally sell the same product: flights. Therefore, airlines need to find ways to differentiate the experience of flying from point A to point B, and that's where loyalty programs come in. By rewarding high-value customers with perks and benefits, airlines can essentially keep customers tied to their network and larger frequent flyer ecosystem.

This is an incredibly lucrative side of the business. Within the US, American Airlines' AAdvantage program was previously the US's largest airline loyalty program, but it has been overtaken by Delta Air Lines' SkyMiles. The two programs, while being similar in many ways, also have a variety of differences.

These may make one or the other more appealing to a specific customer. What's more, the individual value of a mile issued by these two programs is not necessarily the same. It's therefore important to know what the value proposition for each program is and which one is best suited for you.

The true value of airline miles is not a set figure, because this can change based on individual redemptions. However, it is possible to estimate mile valuations at a specific point in time based on systemwide redemption prices. NerdWallet estimates the value of one AAdvantage mile at 1.7 cents, while estimating that one SkyMile is worth 1.2 cents.

Meanwhile, The Points Guy estimates that one AAdvantage mile is worth 1.45 cents, while pegging the value of one SkyMile at 1.2 cents. Across the board, AAdvantage miles are considered to be more valuable than SkyMiles, and this is a unique advantage for American. NerdWallet pegs a United Airlines MileagePlus mile at 1.2 cents, while The Points Guy values MileagePlus miles the same.

There has been criticism of Delta and United for the devaluation of their programs' miles, and AAdvantage offers a material advantage in terms of redemption values. This is important when trying to decide which airline to be loyal to. At all three airlines, you can earn miles by flying or through credit card spending, which has become the primary means of mileage earnings in the US.

The airlines offer a wide range of credit cards, each coming with progressively higher annual fees and increasingly lucrative benefits. In addition, all three carriers are part of airline alliances, which grant certain benefits when traveling with select airlines worldwide, and they also have individual partnerships with airlines outside of the alliances.

Delta Air Lines uses four main Medallion tiers, as well as the general membership status and the invite-only Delta 360 tier at the very top. The four Medallion tiers, named Silver, Gold, Platinum, and Diamond, offer increasing rewards with each higher tier. In order, they respectively require 5,000, 10,000, 15,000, and 28,000 Medallion Qualifying Dollars.

These can be earned through flying or through credit card spending, with Delta not requiring members to actually fly for Medallion status. Similarly, American Airlines has its general membership tier, its invite-only ConciergeKey status, and four main elite status tiers: Gold, Platinum, Platinum Pro, and Executive Platinum. It uses Loyalty Points as the metric for status, requiring 40,000, 75,000, 125,000, and 200,000 Loyalty Points for each tier.

Crucially, however, it's much easier to earn Loyalty Points with American than MQDs with Delta, as the airline essentially awards one Loyalty Point for every eligible base mile earned. Delta awards one MQD for every dollar spent on the base ticket price and associated airline fees on Delta-operated flights, as well as bookings with Delta Vacations.

In addition, flights operated by close Delta partners earn MQDs based on a calculation of fare and distance, while SkyMiles co-branded credit cards use their own calculation: the SkyMiles Platinum and Business Platinum cards earn $1 MQD per $20 spent, while the Reserve and Business Reserve seats earn $1 MQD for every $10 spent.

SkyMiles and AAdvantage both offer four personal credit cards, with American Express issuing the SkyMiles co-branded credit cards while Citi issues the AAdvantage co-branded cards. The top-end AAdvantage Executive World Legend Mastercard notably provides unlimited Admirals Club access, whereas the top-end Delta SkyMiles Reserve Amex only grants 15 Sky Club visits annually unless the cardholder reaches $75,000 in annual spend.

This is a significant difference. As previously mentioned, the AAdvantage cards provide a quicker path to earning elite status. Both sets of cards offer a variety of benefits, and the choice of which one to choose is largely down to personal preference. However, the benefits of going with SkyMiles are that miles never expire, whereas AAdvantage miles expire after 24 months of account activity.

In addition, AAdvantage only offers one business card, whereas SkyMiles offers three, making the latter a better choice for this demographic. The AAdvantage Platinum Select World Elite Mastercard has a lower annual fee than the comparable SkyMiles Gold Amex ($99 versus $150).

Meanwhile, the AAdvantage Executive World Legend Mastercard has a higher annual fee than the SkyMiles Reserve Amex ($695 versus $650). The two airlines offer sign-on bonuses, usually in the form of bonus miles (although they can include more). Both sets of cards also grant status boosts in the form of extra Loyalty Points/MQDs in some cases, although it's still easier to earn AAdvantage Loyalty Points.

American Airlines offers its members multiple additional perks for reaching a certain amount of Loyalty Points. This is solely tied to the number of Loyalty Points reached in a calendar year, with the lowest milestone being 15,000 Loyalty Points. There are also multiple checkpoints, each offering a small variety of benefits that are different from the other milestones.

As a whole, the benefits list is quite diverse, allowing AAdvantage members across the board to receive multiple perks throughout the year. Delta Air Lines also offers SkyMiles members additional perks under the 'Choice Rewards' system. However, these are only available to Platinum and Diamond members.

Still, the rewards list is extensive for both tiers, with Platinum Medallion members able to choose options such as regional upgrade certificates, an MQD accelerator, bonus miles, a charitable donation, a status gift, statement credit, Starbucks Rewards credit, Delta Vacations credit, a Sustainable Aviation Fuel credit, or a Wheels Up credit. Diamond members get even more choices.

Delta is a SkyTeam member, and the SkyMiles program provides reciprocal SkyTeam status for the appropriate tier: SkyTeam Elite for Silver Medallion, and SkyTeam Elite Plus for the higher tiers. American Airlines, on the other hand, is a oneworld member, and oneworld offers three status tiers for loyalty program members.

The Emerald tier slots above anything that SkyTeam or Star Alliance offers in terms of benefits. This is a major incentive for AAdvantage members to chase the program's top two tiers, which grant oneworld Emerald status. SkyMiles does not publish an awards chart, as all award flights are priced dynamically, and American AAdvantage also does this for the most part.

However, AAdvantage does still use an awards chart for partner-operated flights, publishing estimated starting rates by region. What's more, American allows for upgrades from economy straight to business class (excluding basic economy fares), whereas Delta generally only permits upgrades to the next cabin class.

Multiple Delta Air Lines credit cards will help you earn elite status.

For some, there are benefits that make SkyMiles a lucrative program, such as miles that never expire, or the wider range of business credit cards. On the surface, however, there are several benefits to going for AAdvantage over SkyMiles, such as the greater upgrade flexibility, oneworld Emerald status, Admirals Club access, the Loyalty Points system, and the generally superior value per mile.

Still, two important considerations remain, which might triumph over everything else. The first is that passengers generally consider Delta to offer a better passenger experience. Although the Sky Clubs are harder to access, they're generally considered to be nicer, while nearly all Delta narrowbodies offer seatback entertainment.

SkyMiles members aren't necessarily choosing the program over competitors because of the merits of the program, but rather because they want to travel with Delta, and this may be more important to some than certain benefits offered by AAdvantage. However, arguably the most important consideration is location.

Regardless of which program's perks are more valuable, this matters little if someone lives in Atlanta, a major Delta hub, or Dallas/Fort Worth, a major American hub. The network and schedules that the dominant airline in a fortress hub offers usually have a much greater effect on which loyalty program to join. Some airports are more competitive, including smaller spokes as well as hubs like New York or Los Angeles, but even then, network and schedule play a major role.
