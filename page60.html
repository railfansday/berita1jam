Delta Weight-And-Balance Outage Triggers Atlanta Ground Stop Days Before Thanksgiving

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Delta Air Lines flights bound for Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (ATL) were held at their departure airports on Sunday morning after an outage affected the carrier's weight-and-balance calculations. The issue led the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) to issue a ground stop at Delta's request, affecting hundreds of flights and causing mounting delays.

Delta said it was using "manual workarounds" while its teams worked to restore the automated system. While the ground stop was later downgraded to a ground delay program, the disruption came on the Sunday before Thanksgiving, with the holiday travel rush already underway.

According to the FAA's National Airspace System (NAS) status page, the ground stop on Sunday morning was imposed on Delta flights bound for Atlanta. It was initially scheduled to remain in place until at least 9:00 AM ET, but was downgraded to a ground delay program shortly after 8:40 AM. The change allowed flights to resume operating under restrictions, although delays are expected to continue as the carrier worked through its backlog.

FAA data showed inbound delays averaging 57 minutes, while flight tracking data shows that over 400 flights have been delayed at ATL, accounting for almost 20% of all inbound and outbound flights at the country's busiest airport. According to Atlanta News First, Delta passengers have also reported significant delays at airports including Newark, New York LaGuardia, Reagan National, Washington Dulles, Boston, Baltimore, Miami and Minneapolis.

The issue affected Delta's Automated Load Instruction System (ALIS), which handles loading information used in aircraft weight-and-balance calculations. At the time of writing, Delta had not disclosed what caused Sunday's outage, or whether any flights had been canceled as a result. Simple Flying contacted the airline for comment and received the following statement:

"Delta teams are using manual workarounds this morning to safely get flights departed while work continues to restore automation. We thank our customers for their patience and our people for their professionalism."

In its statement, Delta said its teams were working to restore automated weight-and-balance calculations for departing flights and that it was handling calculations manually. The airline added that it was working with air traffic control to "protect airport gates and tarmac areas from congestion" at ATL.

Weight-and-balance calculations are a critical part of airline operations and are typically handled by automated systems. Every flight needs a final load sheet before departure to confirm total weight and center of gravity after passengers, bags, cargo and fuel have been loaded. These figures are then used to calculate takeoff performance, including the speeds pilots need and the engine thrust required for departure.

When the automated system fails, airlines must complete the calculations manually, adding time to the process for every flight. At a hub as large as Atlanta, slower departures mean aircraft remain at their gates longer, leaving fewer spaces available for arriving flights. Holding inbound Delta flights at their departure airports helps prevent aircraft from arriving in Atlanta without an available gate.

This can help limit congestion on the ground, but it also means delays can build up at airports where Delta aircraft are waiting to depart for Atlanta. Only last week, United Airlines suffered a similar weight-and-balance system outage that forced it to hold departures across its network. The disruption lasted more than 90 minutes and demonstrated how heavily airlines rely on automated systems to keep flights moving.

While there are still some delays and cancelations due to aircraft and crews being out of place, the figures are far better than over the weekend.

The disruption occurred during the opening weekend of the travel rush before Thanksgiving, which falls on Thursday, November 26. US carriers are projected to fly nearly 29 million passengers between November 20 and November 30, representing around 2.7 million per day. Airlines are also stepping up their Thanksgiving capacity year-on-year, offering 38,000 more daily seats than during the same period in 2025.

Like all US carriers, Delta was significantly impacted by the recent nationwide ground stop on November 13 that lasted more than four hours. The stoppage followed a telecommunications failure within the FAA's system and affected flights nationwide. Delta struggled to recover its operations over the following days after aircraft and crews became displaced across its network, resulting in further delays and cancellations.

HBO's 18-episode tragicomedy only takes 18 hours to watch, and it's White Lotus meets Severance. We are missing 30-minute TV shows. It seems like every series now has to have 50-65 minute episodes, or it's not going to get made. 30 minutes is plenty of time to get whatever story you need to tell, told.

Enlightened understands this. HBO's 18-episode series from Mike White is a quick binge, but there is so much humor and drama packed into it that you'll feel like you've been watching the show for much longer. White wrote the series with the star Laura Dern, who plays Amy Jellicoe, a 40-year-old woman who has a public breakdown at work.

Once a high-powered VP, Amy takes a sabbatical and returns to work at her old company, Abaddonn, with a new outlook that does not convince her old coworkers, lovers, and family that she's actually changed. Now at the bottom rung of a company where she used to have a seat at the top, Amy has to start over in more ways than one.

The brightly-lit basement of Abaddonn, filled with odd, quirky, unambitious employees, feels just like the sterile offices of Severance, and Amy's forced smiles at work feel like the out-of-body personalities of the people in the Apple TV hit. However, Mike White's empathetic, though acid-laced writing makes for a more moving, though more harshly relatable story that fans of The White Lotus will recognize.

Enlightened is the third TV show that White created, after Pasadena in 2001 and Cracking Up in 2004. Enlightened is the first series where he has a writing credit on every episode and multiple directing credits, and it's clear this is where the White-style of filmmaking is getting honed.

Most people will have seen The White Lotus before Enlightened, and when returning to this series, you'll immediately see the similarities between the two shows. The filmmaking is strikingly similar. There are the same lens flares that The White Lotus is known for, and the panning camera shots showing people moving through gorgeous sets and vistas.

There's a similar sense of humor, with people's foibles and failures providing tough but funny jokes. White's empathy shines through as well. There are a lot of unlikable people in Enlightened, just as there are in The White Lotus, but White clearly feels for these people, and his dialogue and story encourage you to pity the worst of them.

Unfortunately, Enlightened never got the ending it deserved. Despite rave reviews from critics, including an 87% on Rotten Tomatoes and a Golden Globe win for Dern, along with a Golden Globe nomination for Best Musical or Comedy, Enlightened was canceled after its second season.

The cancelation came right after the season 2 finale, and sadly the series comes to a sudden, unfulfilling stop. Perhaps this is why White has made The White Lotus an anthology that can absorb a sudden cancelation. Despite its unfinished nature, Enlightened is absolutely worth watching; you'll just be left wanting more.

A secluded retreat tucked beneath one of California’s most photographed landmarks in Big Sur has come on the market for $3.65 million.

The home at 39320 Coast Road sits in Bixby Canyon and is one of only seven residences behind a private gate.

“Millions recognize Bixby Bridge, yet very few will ever experience the landscape beneath it,” says listing agent Michael Hornig of the Strock Team at eXp Realty.

The canyon also comes with a literary history: In 1960, Jack Kerouac retreated to a cabin here owned by Lawrence Ferlinghetti, the poet and co-founder of City Lights Bookstore. His time in the canyon formed the basis for his 1962 novel, “Big Sur.” While this is not the original Kerouac cabin, it shares the same setting.

According to Hornig, the canyon has also been home to the film director Sam Peckinpah and the Disney illustrator Ron Dias.

“There’s an extraordinary feeling about this canyon,” Hornig says. “You’re surrounded by a landscape that inspired some of America’s most influential writers, and remarkably, much of that atmosphere of solitude and wilderness remains intact today.”

Built in 1984, the single-level home has three bedrooms, two full bathrooms, and 1,489 square feet of living space. Vaulted, open-beam ceilings, hardwood floors, and three wood-burning stoves characterize the interiors, as do large windows that frame the surrounding redwoods. A wraparound porch overlooks the forest.

“Luxury doesn’t always have to be expressed through scale or elaborate architecture,” Hornig says. “Here, the luxury is the relationship between the home and its surroundings.”

The property covers two parcels: a 0.79-acre residential lot and an adjacent 0.33-acre creekside lot. It is move-in ready, with a private well, septic system, and electricity. There is also a fenced backyard, storage shed, pasture space and parking for 10 vehicles, including EV charging.

Owners share access to a privately held canyon enclave of more than 140 acres. There, a creekside trail winds through redwoods and ferns to a secluded beach beneath the bridge.

“You can walk from your front door through a redwood forest, follow the creek toward the Pacific, and emerge beneath one of the most recognizable bridges in the world,” Hornig says.

Next to the property, Brazil Ranch adds about 1,200 acres of U.S. Forest Service land for hiking, hunting, and recreation. The historic Old Coast Road offers miles of biking, horseback riding, and backcountry exploration. Highway 1 is about five minutes away. Carmel, Monterey, and Big Sur’s restaurants and state parks are about 20 minutes away.

“To me, the greatest luxury here is the privilege of belonging to a landscape that most people will only ever admire from a distance,” Hornig says. "It's this contrast between one of California’s most recognizable landmarks and the hidden residential community beneath it."

The most recognizable spam email type is way older than the internet

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From a Spanish prisoner to a Nigerian princes, this familiar email scam has a surprisingly long paper trail.

Your email's spam folder has most likely seen a Nigerian prince or two pay a visit. These types of emails often claim to come from people with a tremendous fortune, and conveniently enough, all of them have issues accessing their wealth. If you're kind enough to cover the fees first, they generously offer to share the wealth with you. To nobody's surprise, the promised millions never reach your bank account, but the requests for more money probably will.

Infamously known as the Nigerian prince or 419 scam (a nod to Nigeria's criminal code), it's a typical advance-fee fraud that has become an internet meme of sorts. The perpetrator might pose as royalty trying to claim their inheritance. Other times, it's some wealthy banker or a government official who wants to move funds out of the country. Although the details change, the art of the steal doesn't: pay upfront for a fortune that isn't there.

The interesting part is that this scam predates email. Different versions of the same con were out in the wild as physical letters long before computers made mass messaging easier (and cheaper). In fact, the scheme had already been around for decades when the first widely recognized spam message was sent over ARPANET in 1978 to advertise computers.

Long before the Nigerian prince scam showed up, one of its earliest documented relatives was the Spanish Prisoner scheme. The script was similar: someone stuck in a foreign prison needing financial help to recover their "hidden fortune." The unsuspecting recipient was asked to reply via telegram. For being a good Samaritan, they'd supposedly get rewarded with a pretty handsome cut of that nonexistent fortune.

Surprisingly, one such letter showed up all the way back in 1897 in an Australian Police Gazette, offering £25,000 for helping recover £75,000 in bank checks. Apparently, all you needed was a convincing story and the hope that an impressionable victim would fall for it.

The "royal" approach was already in play by 1949. According to a National Archives blog, a 16-year-old Nigerian calling himself Prince Richard asked Americans to send clothes and money in exchange for jewelry and other goods. Your Highness reportedly went silent once the requested items arrived, and unsurprisingly, investigators found no connection to Nigerian royalty.

This trick wasn't exactly unique either. A 14-year-old Nigerian going by the name Prince Bil Morrison promised ivory and jewels in exchange for cash or clothes, per the National Archives. According to investigators, he adopted the fancy title "just for fun's sake." US postal officials issued fraud orders against both teens, but records don't say what became of the youth.

By the 1980s, Nigerian advance-fee letters were gaining traction internationally, and as email spread in the 1990s, scammers could cut the cost of envelopes, postage and international delivery. While the story barely changed, it was a lot easier to reach people without using a single stamp.

These days, the prince has plenty of competition, with supposed lawyers handling unclaimed inheritances or bankers who really need your account details to release funds. If a stranger is promising you a truckload of money and asking for upfront fees or personal details, ignore the message and any links attached. It's best to report the message as spam or phishing, and then send it to the trash can. While blocking the sender does help with that specific email address, the next attempt could simply come from somewhere else.

Even though email is a lot cheaper and easier, scammers haven't given up on physical mail. As of July 2026, the FTC was still warning consumers about letters supposedly sent by law firms offering shares of unclaimed life insurance policies worth millions. You guessed it: The letters promised recipients a large share of the funds, but the supposed lawyers were really after financial information or cash.

A GTA 6 parody within The Binding of Isaac: Repentance+ was sullied by the use of AI-generated music

The Binding of Isaac: Repentance Plus was recently at the center of gaming's latest generative AI controversy. Creator Edmund McMillen claimed music made with generative AI was used without his knowledge for a new daily run riffing on Grand Theft Auto 6. Repentance Plus was released in full on Nov. 19 alongside its new console ports.

A Nov. 19 update for The Binding of Isaac: Repentance Plus on PC let players access a daily run inspired by GTA 6. It has unique music, lets players drive around in a cart like it's a car, and even implements GTA's star rating and cop chases the more that players kill enemies.

While this unique run was meant as a silly parody, playing on the fact that the console ports were going up against GTA, reception to the update quickly turned sour. Reddit user u/ShweetsStuff discovered that the run's special music was made with the generative AI tool Suno, since the songs still have Suno metadata in the game's code.

This discovery led to backlash and review bombing of The Binding of Isaac: Repentance Plus on Steam. After that, McMillen, the game's creator, finally responded on X, saying he "obviously" didn't know AI-generated music had been used for the daily run's music. 10 HBO Crime Shows That Are Perfect Weekend Binges

Crime shows have been a TV staple for decades, and the reason is fairly simple. The truth is that everyone loves a good mystery. The problem, however, is that the genre is so crowded that most crime shows have started to blur together. Too many of them lean on the same familiar formulas featuring brooding detectives with a dark past and last-minute twists that only exist for shock value. This means very few have the potential to leave a lasting impression.

HBO has always taken a different approach, though. The network uses the genre as a vehicle to explore broken families, corrupt institutions, and personal demons. In doing that, these series are never afraid to leave viewers a little uncomfortable, instead of offering them perfect closure. Speaking of which, many of them also tell a complete story in a season or two. Here is a list of short HBO crime series that make for a perfect weekend binge.

The Night Of shows just how quickly one bad decision can unravel someone's entire life. Nasir “Naz” Khan (Riz Ahmed) is a quiet Pakistani-American college student from Queens who takes his father’s taxi without asking so he can go to a party in Manhattan. Instead, he ends up spending the night with a young woman named Andrea, and when he wakes up, she has been stabbed to death. Naz is arrested within hours, and with no memory of what happened, his only hope is John Stone (John Turturro), a low-rent lawyer who usually handles petty cases.

What sets The Night Of apart from other crime dramas is how closely it follows the legal system itself. The show walks the audience through every step of Naz’s case, from the police station to Rikers Island and finally the courtroom, and it becomes painfully clear that nobody involved is really interested in the truth. Everyone on both sides is more focused on winning the case than on what actually happened that night. By the end, the question isn’t just whether Naz is guilty, but what the system has turned him into. The Night Of is slow and patient, and it remains one of the most gripping crime dramas HBO has ever made because it asks the hard questions instead of delivering a typical whodunit.

Mare of Easttown feels less like a crime show and more like an accurate portrayal of how toxic and suffocating small-town life can be. The series features Kate Winslet as Mare Sheehan, a Pennsylvania detective who is still grieving her son’s death while fighting to keep custody of her grandson. To make things worse, she is also haunted by a missing persons case she never managed to solve, which has led to the entire town growing increasingly skeptical of her. When teenage mother Erin McMenamin (Cailee Spaeny) is found dead in the woods, Mare is put in charge of the investigation, and every lead points back to people she grew up with.

Most crime dramas rush to the next clue, but Mare of Easttown takes its time to show what the job costs the person doing it. Mare can’t separate her work from her life, because in a town this small, every suspect is someone she has a connection to. The mystery is full of clever twists, but the show is really about grief and how people carry on after the worst has happened. It’s also surprisingly funny at times, especially in Mare’s prickly scenes with her mother, Helen (Jean Smart). Mare of Easttown is a tight mystery with real heart, and its final reveal is one of the most devastating in recent TV, and the fact that it manages to do all that in just seven episodes makes it all the more brilliant.

Big Little Lies is surprisingly gripping, even if it is a story about a bunch of rich, suburban moms. The series is set in the wealthy coastal town of Monterey, California, and opens with news of a death at a school fundraiser without revealing who died or who is responsible. From there, the story rewinds to follow this group of mothers whose lives become tangled together. Madeline (Reese Witherspoon) is outspoken and loves a good feud, while Celeste (Nicole Kidman) appears to have the perfect marriage. They soon befriend Jane (Shailene Woodley), a young single mom who has just moved to town with her son, and that’s when things start to take a turn.

The genius of Big Little Lies is how it hides a heavy story inside what looks like a glossy soap opera. At first, it’s easy to get caught up in the playground drama and petty rivalries between the moms. Then, the show peels back the surface to reveal the trauma and abuse some of these women are living with behind closed doors. The mystery of the fundraiser death keeps the story moving, but what keeps the viewer coming back is the messy, complex friendship that forms between these women who start as rivals. Big Little Lies is still airing, with Season 3 in production, but its first two installments work perfectly on their own for an addictive weekend binge.

Perry Mason tells the story of TV’s most famous lawyer and gets right into his gritty origin story. The series, set in 1930s Los Angeles, introduces Perry Mason (Matthew Rhys) as a broke, burned-out private investigator who is still haunted by his time in World War I. His life takes a turn when he is hired to dig into the kidnapping and murder of a baby, a case that quickly grabs the entire city’s attention. As the investigation pulls him into a web of corrupt cops and powerful church leaders, Perry starts down the path that will eventually lead him to the courtroom.

Fans of the classic Raymond Burr series might not recognize this version of Perry at first, and that’s the point. The show strips away the lawyer’s polished image and replaces it with a flawed man who has to earn his place as a lawyer, often by bending the rules along the way. The show’s version of 1930s Los Angeles is gorgeous, but the city feels rotten because here, money and power decide who is guilty. The courtroom scenes in Perry Mason are a masterclass in suspenseful storytelling, and they make the lawyer’s hard-won victories feel genuinely earned. Perry Mason ended after two seasons, but it’s a stylish, old-school crime drama that never stops feeling exhilarating.

Tokyo Vice offers both style and substance by giving the audience a rare look at Japanese organized crime through the eyes of an outsider. The series is based on Jake Adelstein’s memoir and follows Ansel Elgort as the crime reporter who lands a job at a major Tokyo newspaper in 1999. Now, Jake is eager to prove himself and starts digging into stories his editors would rather he leave alone. Naturally, it doesn’t take long before he’s tangled up with the yakuza. Along the way, he teams up with veteran detective Hiroto Katagiri (Ken Watanabe), who is fighting his own battle against the city’s criminal underworld.

Tokyo Vice treats Tokyo as more than just a backdrop. The neon-lit streets and smoky hostess clubs feel alive, and the show takes its time explaining how the city’s unwritten rules shape its citizens’ choices. Jake’s fish-out-of-water story keeps things accessible, but the show’s best moments often belong to the yakuza themselves, who are written as complicated people rather than one-dimensional villains. The series is slower than a typical crime thriller, and with two seasons, it’s a longer binge. Still, Tokyo Vice is one of the most immersive crime shows on HBO Max.

Your nemesis respects you, which sounds flattering until you work out what it means. He chose you because nobody else was worth his time.

Your nemesis has no personal interest in you whatsoever, and that is precisely what makes him unsurvivable. You are not a rival. You are a stop on the way.

The Penguin proves that a great crime story doesn’t necessarily need a superhero. The show picks up right after the events of The Batman and follows low-level gangster Oz Cobb (Colin Farrell), who sees his chance to climb to the top of Gotham’s underworld after the death of crime boss Carmine Falcone. His biggest obstacle is Carmine’s daughter, Sofia (Cristin Milioti), who has just been released from Arkham Asylum and wants revenge on the people who put her there. Along the way, Oz takes a young man named Victor Aguilar (Rhenzy Feliz) under his wing and drags him into a war he isn’t ready for.

The Penguin plays out much more like a classic mob drama than a typical comic book show, and that’s exactly why it works so well. Farrell portrays Oz as a hungry, resentful man who has spent his whole life being underestimated. That backstory makes him both sympathetic and terrifying. The show pairs his rise with Sofia’s story, and the clash between two outcasts fighting for the same throne is easily the most gripping part of the story. Victor’s relationship with Oz adds another layer of complexity to the drama, and it’s hard to watch him slowly fall under Oz’s influence. Viewers don’t need to know a thing about Batman to get hooked, and by the finale, The Penguin stands shoulder to shoulder with HBO’s greatest mob dramas.

The Undoing is a glamorous Manhattan thriller about how little people really know about the ones they love. The story follows Grace Fraser (Nicole Kidman), a successful therapist married to charming pediatric oncologist Jonathan (Hugh Grant), and their life together seems perfect. However, everything falls apart when a young mother from their son’s private school is found brutally murdered, and Jonathan suddenly disappears. As the police close in, Grace is forced to question everything she thought she knew about her husband.

The Undoing is the kind of show that makes viewers change their minds about the killer every single episode. The audience experiences the entire ordeal through Grace’s eyes, so every new piece of evidence feels like a personal betrayal. Grant plays Jonathan with an ambiguity that makes it impossible for one to tell whether he really is a devoted husband or a serial killer who is great at covering his tracks. The murder essentially bursts the privileged bubble the Frasers live in, and that is one of the most important parts of the narrative. Every episode delivers a twist that turns the entire story on its head and makes it impossible for the viewers to stop watching until they have all the answers.

The Jinx is the only documentary on this list, but it’s every bit as gripping as any scripted show. The series, directed by Andrew Jarecki, follows Robert Durst, the eccentric heir to a New York real estate empire who has been linked to several deaths over the decades. To complicate things further, his wife, Kathie, vanished in 1982 and was never found. Not just that, but years later, both his close friend Susan Berman and a neighbor in Texas turned up dead. Surprisingly, Durst agrees to sit down with Jarecki for hours of interviews, and what he says on camera changes the course of the case.

The Jinx is fascinating because Durst himself is the most compelling thing about it. He is both awkward and strangely charismatic in his interviews, and it’s impossible to tell what he is lying about as he tries to explain himself. The series builds its case slowly, and by the final episode, the tension is almost unbearable. The Jinx’s ending is one of the most shocking moments in documentary history, one that actually had real-world consequences. The Jinx Season aired in 2024 to follow what happened next, and together, the two installments make the docuseries essential viewing for any true crime fan.

We Own This City comes from The Wire creator David Simon, so it had pretty big shoes to fill. Thankfully, the series stands on its own as a look into the true story of the Baltimore Police Department’s Gun Trace Task Force, an elite unit that was meant to take illegal guns off the streets. However, under Sgt. Wayne Jenkins (Jon Bernthal), the officers robbed the very people they were supposed to protect and lied their way into huge overtime checks. When civil rights investigator Nicole Steele (Wunmi Mosaku) is brought in to look into the department, she realizes the problem runs much deeper than one rogue unit.

What’s interesting is that We Own This City never treats Jenkins as the whole problem. He gets away with his crimes for years because he brings in the arrests and seized guns his bosses want to see, and nobody bothers to ask how he managed to do it. The show also spends time with the people who pay the price for that corruption, without ever feeling preachy. The story jumps between timelines, so it takes some focus, but the payoff is well worth it. By the end, We Own This City leaves the viewers angry but informed, and that is exactly what a show like this can aim to achieve.

The Staircase revisits one of the most debated true crime cases of the 21st century, and it somehow makes it even more fascinating. In 2001, crime novelist Michael Peterson (Colin Firth) calls 911 to report that his wife, Kathleen (Toni Collette), has fallen down the stairs of their North Carolina home. The police aren’t convinced it was an accident, and Michael is soon charged with her murder. The trial tears his large blended family apart, while a French documentary crew captures every moment of the case on camera.

The Staircase absolutely refuses to tell the audience what to think, but most importantly, it turns a courtroom story into a family drama. Michael’s children grew up loving and trusting him, and the show captures how painful it is for them to see their father in a brand-new light, not knowing whether they can trust him any longer. Michael himself is a writer who knows how to tell a good story, and the show cleverly leaves the audience asking whether he’s doing the same thing with his own life. The Staircase is less about the fall itself and more about everything that came after, and it’s the perfect show to close out a weekend.

Ina Garten Makes A Pretty Convincing Case For Thanksgiving Over Christmas

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Barefoot Contessa knows exactly what she wants out of a holiday.

Christmas might get the lights, presents, and monthlong buildup, but Ina Garten has a few thoughts about which holiday really deserves the fuss. And if a certain Thanksgiving side dish comes up in conversation, the Barefoot Contessa’s face might tell you everything you need to know.

During the November 18 episode of her Happy Hour with Ina Garten podcast, the cookbook author asked guest Rose Byrne about her first Thanksgiving in the United States. The Australian actress recalled attending a Friendsgiving, with everyone contributing something to the meal, and Garten immediately wanted to know whether the whole thing had been organized in advance or “if everyone just showed up with string beans.”

Things got even funnier when Byrne mentioned candied yams topped with marshmallows, something she found “pretty weird.” Garten visibly grimaced at the mention of the dish, chuckling before describing them as “very American." Judging by that expression alone, I wouldn't expect to find the dish making an appearance at her next dinner party.

Garten's feelings about Thanksgiving itself, however, are considerably warmer, especially when compared to Christmas.

"Thanksgiving is just quiet. It's all that one day and it's all about the food," she explained, pointing out that Christmas comes with gifts, parties, and a whole lot more going on.

It makes sense coming from someone who’s built an entire career around bringing people together over a good meal. And we already know Garten’s enthusiasm for Thanksgiving extends beyond what’s on the plate. Last year, she shared her favorite wines to serve with Thanksgiving dinner, including Morgon, a lighter French red from the Beaujolais region, and white Burgundies like Meursault or Montrachet to accompany the turkey. She also likes a Sonoma Valley Pinot Noir, giving the American holiday a little hometown representation in the wine department.

At least now I know that if I ever get invited to Thanksgiving at Ina’s, I'll gladly let her handle the wine—and I know to keep the marshmallow-topped yams at home.
