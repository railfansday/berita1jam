Harry Kane: ‘My biggest fear is finishing my career thinking I could have done more’

Interview: The England captain discusses his elite mentality and the long journey from debut nerves to a historic 126th cap when he surpasses Peter Shilton’s record at Wembley on Thursday night

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Harry Kane has answered every critic and extinguished every doubt. At 33 he is a double winner with Bayern Munich, England’s greatest goalscorer, and sits on the Ballon d’Or throne. On Thursday night, Kane will reach another famous milestone when he surpasses Peter Shilton’s tally of England caps and collects his 126th against Croatia in the Nations League at Wembley.

From the academy coaches at Arsenal who released him aged eight, to the Tottenham coaches who didn’t think he could handle first-team football, to the fans who labelled him a one-season wonder and the pundits who prematurely called time on his England career, each served to motivate Kane. You might think he would finally feel satisfied, vindicated, ready to drop his guard, to take his foot off the pedal, to eat a Twix or two.

Far from it. Kane is speaking to a huddle of journalists at Tottenham’s training ground, back where his career began, and insists he still has a point to prove.

“I don’t think that will ever leave me,” he says. “It’s probably part of what makes me push myself to the limits because in football, you’ve never completed it. No matter what you’ve achieved, there is always a trophy that someone else has won or a goal record that someone else has. There’s always something. Whether it’s noise from the outside, media or the fans or whatever it may be, it just helps motivate me to realise there’s a lot more to achieve.”

Where does that come from? How can the unassuming man who stood on stage at the London Palladium last month, nervously twiddling his buttons and repeatedly sweeping away specks of nothing from his pristine tuxedo, who spent his 12-minute speech naming dozens of people who had helped his career, also be a ferociously driven world-class athlete who carries a kind of burning wrath inside him?

“I think it’s just my mindset, because I’ve had to prove people wrong my whole career. From early academy days proving coaches wrong, proving scouts wrong, it has just been instilled in me. If someone tells me I can’t do something it just motivates me more to do it. Of course, you have to combine the mentality with being able to produce it, so that’s where the self-belief and the training and all the consistent work I do has allowed me to do that.

“I’ve spoken about Tom Brady before and that unwavering mentality to be the best. Maybe with me, because I’m a calm person, you don’t see the ego of some of these great players and I’m a bit more relaxed, but it doesn’t mean that I don’t want it as much as everyone else. It doesn’t mean I don’t put in the work and drive myself and drive everyone around me to be the best.

“Maybe getting released that early and proving them wrong and then maybe proving my early coaches at Tottenham [wrong], just bit by bit, loan moves that didn’t go quite well, again, same thing – ‘I’m going to show them’ – using all the negative stuff as motivation to push me to another level.”

There is something else behind Kane’s drive, a different kind of emotion: the dread of leaving a single stone unturned.

“My biggest fear is finishing my career and thinking I could have done a bit more if I could have just done that or done that. I want to make sure that I cross every bridge so that whenever that day does come, I can say I’ve done everything possible, I have this record, I don’t have this record – but it doesn’t matter, I can sleep well at night.”

Kane can still remember arriving at his first England camp, in the spring of 2015, and admits he felt “nervous and anxious” about being the new guy. Wayne Rooney was the England captain and was the first person to talk to the 21-year-old Spurs striker. “He told me to just be myself,” Kane says.

But as soon as he got on the training pitch, Kane felt a confidence flood through his body. “I felt like this is my comfort zone, I know what I can do, I can score goals, I can even talk and shout – I felt more comfortable in that sense. I remember training really well and feeling like, ‘OK, if I can do it here I can do it anywhere’, and that gave me even more confidence. I just took that into the game.”

Kane replaced Rooney in the second half and scored 79 seconds later with his third touch in an England shirt. He would assume the captaincy from Rooney two years later, when Gareth Southgate embarked on an overhaul of the squad.

“I was talking to Michael Olise at Bayern and I was asking how old he was, and he was saying at the time he was 23 and I thought, ‘Jeez, I was [England] captain then’, and I looked at him and he looked so young! So I don’t think I realised in the moment how young I was to be named captain.

“I’ve always been quite mature, even from late teenage years, always led by example, always done the right things. It felt quite natural for me. I’ve been lucky enough to do it for a long time now, and honestly the feeling of being captain still feels as good as that first moment I wore [the armband].”

Eleven years and 125 games after his England debut, Shilton will present Kane with a special cap to commemorate the achievement. It will be a busy pre-match, with Kane also expected to find time to parade the Ballon d’Or to the Wembley crowd.

Even in Kane’s world, it has been an extraordinary, whirlwind few weeks.

“I think I struggle to take it all in to be honest,” he says. “I think it is just my natural mindset to look ahead. The fact that I had just equalled the record [last month], it was, ‘right, next camp, let’s go and break the record’. Maybe tomorrow will feel a little different, actually breaking the record and being the one that sits alone on top.

“But in this period especially when there are so many games, I don’t think I’ve taken it all in. What’s happened in the last few weeks, I don’t think I’ve accepted it all, I guess. But maybe during the winter break when I’m away somewhere, on the beach, it’ll be a moment to reflect and then really soak it all in.”

Analysis: Is Nvidia too big to fail? Its AI financing web raises the stakes

📅 2026-11-11  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The swirl of sex-and-spying allegations around Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang is raising a worrying question: Could trouble at the chipmaker spread through the tightly connected financial network Nvidia has built to support the artificial intelligence boom?

Serious problems with Nvidia could do far-reaching economic and financial damage. The company has made hundreds of billions of dollars in financial commitments to other firms to help the industry grow, earning it the nickname the "central bank of AI." The Federal Reserve and other financial institutions are closely watching the AI buildout to monitor for the possibility that an unexpected event could destabilize the global financial system.

Even back in July Fed officials were discussing the possibility of of a "broad-based repricing of assets" in the event of problems in the AI sector.

A rerun of the 2007-2008 financial crisis isn't likely, according to analysts and current and former financial officials. Nvidia isn't "too big to fail in a bank sense at all," said Sheila Bair, a former government regulator who ran the Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. Unlike a troubled bank, Nvidia isn't vulnerable to a sudden loss of confidence and would still produce a product customers want to buy even if something went wrong. And its massive equity gives it a cushion.

But regulators need to watch carefully for exposure by the banks and the wider financial system to Nvidia and other firms like it, Bair said.

"Nvidia going down would be a pretty seismic event," she said.

Investors still have high hopes for Nvidia, which reports earnings on Tuesday. Analysts expect its revenue to more than double in the current fiscal year, and then to roughly double again over the following two years, according to LSEG data. But with Huang under scrutiny by the FBI, Congress, and Nvidia's board over his relationship with a woman who The New York Times reported was suspected of spying for China, analysts see a wide range of outcomes as suddenly possible, including the potential removal of Huang, who co-founded the company in 1993.

The company didn't immediately respond to a request for comment. Nvidia's board has expressed confidence in its CEO.

Nvidia's stock fell 30% from its Oct. 6 peak through Tuesday's close, amid a broader reassessment of the AI trade prompted by Democrats' surprising victory in the Nov. 3 midterms. Its stock recovered Wednesday, rising more than 4% as of midday. Still, people who closely track the financial flows underlying AI say that even sharper losses by Nvidia likely wouldn't trigger the kind of systemic problems that regulators guard against.

And yet the risks flowing from AI are keeping some finance officials up at night. They are watching for the possibility that many of the factors that have helped the AI boom could reverse simultaneously. If the value of Nvidia's GPU chips fell, for instance, it could prompt investors to ask whether companies that borrowed money to buy them would be able to pay off their debts.

In that case, the financial assistance big tech firms have extended to others in the industry could suddenly become drags on their growth. Tech companies would have credit problems at the same time that their equity is falling. Problems among giant tech firms wouldn't stay contained to tech investors' portfolios, but could cause problems across the economy.

"I think that's a real concern because both the economy and financial markets are standing on the same leg, which means a drawdown could have reinforcing effects between the two," said Jason Wu, an assistant director at the International Monetary Fund who oversees the Global Markets Analysis unit.

Wu was the lead author of a chapter of the IMF's Global Financial Stability report published in October that warned about the potential for growing leverage among AI firms and investors to trigger broader problems. The so-called hyperscalers — the biggest tech firms — have grown from just 1% of bond issuance in the $15 trillion investment-grade debt market to 10% in just two years. Hedge funds and retail investors are making risky bets even riskier by taking on debt to buy stocks or investing in funds that have embedded leverage.

Nvidia is at the center of all of that. The company has repeatedly stepped in to help backstop smaller AI companies known as neoclouds, which build data centers using Nvidia chips and lease capacity to other firms. Among other obligations, Nvidia disclosed in July that it has committed to buy up to $36 billion in cloud services from those companies. That helps Nvidia ensure that there will be more customers for its AI chips, and it helps those companies find easier financing.

Wu and others refer to these kinds of arrangements as circular financing. When business is growing, it can be productive for bigger firms to help their customers buy their products. But if Nvidia couldn't honor its obligations, it would ripple through the firms that borrowed the money and the banks that lent to it. Relatively opaque private credit funds have also lent heavily into the AI boom, and how they would fare is a subject of debate among regulators.

Nvidia didn't reveal its specific partners, but some neoclouds such as CoreWeave and IREN have separately disclosed their relationships with the bigger firm.

Many neoclouds have been under stress in recent weeks. The cost of insuring CoreWeave debt, as measured by credit-default spreads, rose by 94% — nearly doubling — from Oct. 1 to Nov. 5, before settling, according to LSEG data. The price of an IREN convertible bond maturing in 2029 fell 23% from Nov. 3 to Nov. 5, amid a 26% decline in the company's share price.

Nvidia's other obligations include a $105 billion phased guarantee to a major data center project in Ohio planned by OpenAI and SB Energy, subject to certain conditions. Nvidia promised to backstop up to $125 billion of a $500 billion credit program by Wall Street financial firms.

These obligations come in different forms and are unlikely to come due all at once, if ever. Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh, a Columbia Business School finance professor, estimates them at $400 billion overall.

That is a huge sum, but still a small fraction of the $4 trillion market capitalization Nvidia has even after its stock decline. "The company would have to get to the brink of extinction before all these guarantees come into jeopardy."

"I don't want to say it's a safe system, but I do want to say there's a lot of equity in the system right now," Van Nieuwerburgh said.

But in the past few days, Nvidia's troubles have separated it from the pack. Most neoclouds' equity prices have recovered this week as Nvidia's share price has fallen. That suggests investors see Nvidia's problems as specific to the company, for now.

Meanwhile the basic laws of economics are driving other firms to try to move into Nvidia's lucrative chips business. AMD, Broadcom, Intel, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI all have efforts underway to build their own AI chips, Van Nieuwerburgh said.

Over time, they could replace Nvidia's products. That could cap the economic fallout of any potential troubles at the company.

And yet it's impossible to entirely write off the financial risks of the AI trade suddenly hitting a wall, added Van Nieuwerburgh, who spoke to me over Zoom from the sidelines of a bimonthly meeting of finance officials in Basel, Switzerland.

Central bankers "are clearly very, very, very anxious about this and thinking hard about these questions," he said.

Missouri-based BJC Health and St. Luke's Hospital are planning a merger.

The providers announced on Wednesday that they have signed a letter of intent to come together, bringing the community hospital into the fold with the Show Me State's largest health system. The pair expects the deal to improve access and improve local care in the St. Louis region.

St. Luke's said in the announcement that the letter "follows a deliberate and thorough evaluation process," conducted by its board of directors and leaders "to identify the best path forward for St. Luke’s and the communities it serves."

The providers said they intend to spend the next several months building a definitive agreement to merge and complete the needed regulatory approval process. In the interim, St. Luke's patients can continue to see their regular physicians.

BJC said in the announcement that it is committed to supporting local, community care while also allowing St. Luke's to tap into the resources available across its ecosystem. It intends to provide key support for the hospital's clinical technology and equipment as well as backing infrastructure and facility upgrades and provider recruitment and retention efforts.

St. Luke’s is a well-respected hospital, known for clinical quality, patient experience and the personal attention given to everyone in our care,” said Andy Bagnall, CEO of St. Luke’s, in the announcement. “At the same time, the demands of health care delivery and our communities’ needs are changing rapidly."

We believe that together with BJC, we can strengthen patient care in St. Louis in ways we could not easily do on our own," Bagnall said.

Nick Barto, CEO of BJC Health, called St. Luke's "a trusted leader in our region with a reputation for excellence."

"Together, we will build on that foundation through our aligned clinical strengths and complementary services," Barto said in the announcement. "Our shared goal is to expand access to differentiated, high-quality care while ensuring St. Luke’s can continue serving the growing communities of West St. Louis for generations to come.”

Google Fi wireless customers with Pixel phones just got a serious upgrade to their mobile and Wi-Fi connections for free.

Google has announced two new “super network” connection features for Fi Wireless: one that automatically switches between two of the largest 5G cellular networks, and another that automatically connects to premium Wi-Fi in millions of locations without you needing to log in.

The features are rolling out today for Pixel 9 phones and newer models on every Google Fi plan. If you’re not a current Fi customer, you can sign up for a free seven-day trial to test it alongside your current carrier.

Here’s how the new mobile connection feature works: if your main connection slows or becomes unstable, Fi will automatically move to a secondary connection. This not only means you’ll have the fastest option when your connection is good, but you’ll have fewer dead zones when service is limited.

The feature will run on MEP (Multiple Enabled Profiles) technology, which allows a single eSIM chip to host and run multiple SIM profiles simultaneously. Footnotes in Google’s release note that you’ll need to have “any non-Fi SIMs disabled.”

Google Fi originally offered automatic switching between two networks when the service debuted a decade ago, but it was between Sprint and T-Mobile, which merged in 2020. Google didn’t reveal what the “two of America’s best 5G cellular networks” were, but ZDNET has reached out to ask. The service usually runs on T-Mobile. Along with T-Mobile, the three biggest carriers are AT&T and Verizon, so it’s likely the second carrier is one of those.

In addition to a mobile connection upgrade, users are getting better Wi-Fi, too. Wi-Fi Auto Connect+ will automatically connect you to faster Wi-Fi in millions of locations like airports and malls (Google defines “faster” as two times better reliability and 30% improved latency compared to normal Google Fi cellular networks) without you having to log in or head to your settings. These connections will be automatically encrypted, Google said, keeping your online activity safe.

Carelon agrees to pay $10M to settle ghost network, coverage denial allegations in New York" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Carelon will pay more than $10 million across two settlements in New York over mental health networks and coverage denials.

The company, a division of Elevance Health, will pay $2.35 million in penalties, fees and costs as well as up to $8 million in restitution for members who paid out-of-pocket for out-of-network mental healthcare, to reimburse those whose claims were improperly denied or to overhaul its networks.

New York Attorney General Letitia James said in an announcement that the settlements resolve two investigations from her office that determined the company misled members by directing them to seek care from providers that were out-of-network—through so-called "ghost networks"—and improperly denied coverage for treatment for substance abuse disorder.

James noted that Carelon administers behavioral health benefits for multiple plans, including Anthem Blue Cross, EmblemHealth, Independent Health and the Empire Plan, which covers state and local government workers.

Across these insurers, Carelon manages behavioral health benefits for more than 4 million people in the state, James said.

The alleged behavior also goes against a 2015 agreement with the AG's office, where the company agreed that it would maintain accurate provider networks and "use approved criteria" when denying substance abuse treatments.

"When someone is ready to seek help for a mental health condition or addiction, every day matters," said James. “Carelon left New Yorkers calling provider after provider who could not see them and made it harder for people to get the treatment to which they were entitled."

"These settlements will help people get the care they need and put money back in New Yorkers’ pockets," she said.

Elevance Health had not responded to a request for comment at the time of publication.

Carelon was hit with a lawsuit in April over the alleged ghost networks and their impact on the Empire Plan in particular. In the case, plaintiff's attorneys conducted secret shopper calls to analyze Carelon's network, calling 300 providers. Just 17% of those contacted took insurance and were open to new patients.

These "ghost networks" have also drawn concern in other markets, such as for maternal care services in Medicaid and in Medicare Advantage. A study released last month found that commercial plans also routinely have inaccurate provider directories.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/caresource-launches-1m-grant-program-support-ai-powered-solutions" hreflang="en">CareSource launches $1M grant program to support AI-powered solutions</a>

📅 2026-11-11  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
CareSource launches $1M grant program to support AI-powered solutions" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Nonprofit insurer CareSource is launching a $1 million program that aims to back responsible AI solutions in healthcare.

The initiative, launched through the CareSource Foundation, is seeking proposals for ways to advance and identify deployments for AI that can drive better outcomes and address barriers for individuals enrolled in Medicaid or who otherwise struggle to access care.

The program will provide up to $1 million over the course of two years to ensure the tool can deliver on meaningful and scalable benefits for the communities it serves.

Erhardt Preitauer, CEO of CareSource, said in an email to Fierce Healthcare that AI offers the potential to improve not just patient care but the experience for providers too as they navigate complex conditions.

"I'm a big believer that when technology, healthcare and the people in our communities rally around a shared purpose, magic can happen," said Preitauer. "Leaving no stone unturned is the only way we can make healthcare work better for people, and AI gives us the power to explore every possibility."

The program will include two phases. First, interested applicants can submit shorter proposals, with the goal of generating interest. Applications that are selected to move on will be tasked with submitting a more fleshed out proposal, including details on implementation, AI governance and sustainability.

CareSource is aiming to identify solutions that ready for implementation and has the potential for scale. Potential focus areas include care coordination, member engagement, behavioral health and rural healthcare. Solutions should use AI to support care delivery but it should not replace human judgement or oversight, the insurer said.

To be eligible, a qualified nonprofit organization must be leading the effort, according to the announcement. Technology companies, academic researchers, healthcare organizations and community-based groups can also play a role as partners.

Applicants moving to the second phase of the program will also be required to showcase data privacy and security, solutions to address and prevent bias and ongoing performance tracking.

1 in 3 Infants Diagnosed With Peanut Allergy May Not Have the Condition

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Peanut allergies have been on the rise in recent decades.

According to statistics provided by Food Allergy Research & Education (FARE), an estimated 6.2 million children and adults in the United States are allergic to peanuts.

However, new research published in the November 2026 issue of The Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology suggests that over a third of infants thought to have this diagnosis might not actually have a peanut allergy.

In fact, having a history of reacting to peanuts and positive allergy testing on their own may not be sufficient to diagnose a peanut allergy for many infants.

The study’s authors suggest that a threshold oral food challenge — in which the child is given increasing amounts of the suspected food allergen under medical supervision to look for an allergic reaction — should be routinely considered to help confirm peanut allergy.

This additional testing could safely prevent children who are not allergic to peanuts from undergoing unnecessary treatment with oral immunotherapy.

It could also spare families from burdensome food restrictions and allergy precautions.

The researchers’ goal was to evaluate the accuracy and safety of the Allergy Development to an Accelerated Path to Tolerance (ADAPT) peanut oral immunotherapy program, which was established in 2024 at 10 public children’s hospitals across Australia.

This program provides a standardized approach to evaluating and treating infants with suspected peanut allergies.

The study included infants who had experienced a mild to moderate reaction to peanut before 12 months of age and had evidence of peanut sensitization through allergy testing.

Researchers reviewed medical records to examine how accurately the program’s approach identified peanut allergies and whether it could safely guide treatment.

A key part of the evaluation was a threshold oral food challenge. The challenge involved up to seven doses, ranging from 15 to 1,300 milligrams of peanut protein.

Medical teams monitored the infants for reactions and provided treatment when necessary.

Researchers reviewed records from April 2024 through October 2025. During that period, 1,275 infants were assessed through the program, and 778 underwent an oral food challenge.

The researchers also examined allergy test results and whether infants who reacted during the challenges could begin oral immunotherapy at an appropriate starting dose.

At the time of the oral food challenge, the infants had a median age of 13 months.

Of the 778 infants who underwent an oral food challenge, 507 (65.2%) had an allergic reaction.

However, 271 (34.8%) did not react to peanut during the supervised test, despite having a history of a previous reaction and positive allergy test results.

These findings suggest that relying on medical history and allergy tests alone may lead some infants to be labeled with a peanut allergy when they can actually tolerate the food.

Among the infants who reacted during the challenges, 61 experienced anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction that can affect breathing and blood pressure. This represented 12% of infants with a positive challenge and 7.8% of all infants who underwent testing. Medical teams safely managed these severe allergic reactions.

Researchers also found that infants who reacted to peanut generally had higher skin prick test results and Ara h 2-specific IgE levels than those who did not react.

However, these test results did not reliably indicate how much peanut would trigger a reaction or how severe that reaction would be.

Of the 394 infants who began peanut oral immunotherapy, 384 (97.5%) successfully started treatment at a dose below the amount that had triggered a reaction during their food challenge.

The researchers say these findings support considering supervised oral food challenges to confirm suspected peanut allergies before starting treatment or recommending long-term avoidance.

Erin Banta, MD, a board certified allergist and immunologist who treats both children and adults at Latitude Food Allergy Care in Great Neck, New York, said these findings are significant because they provide evidence for early use of oral food challenges in infants with peanut reactions.

“In those with mild-moderate prior reactions to peanut, early oral food challenge may allow for either delabeling of food allergy (in 1/3 of the patients in this study) or diagnosis of food allergy,” she told Healthline.

Banta, who wasn’t involved in the study, added that early delabeling takes a significant burden off the patient and their family.

“On the other hand,” she explained, “early diagnosis allows for early initiation of treatment with oral immunotherapy at a time in the patient’s life associated with the greatest success rates.”

Banta further noted that the study offers different pathways for oral immunotherapy based on the patient’s reaction threshold. This means that some patients may be able to complete treatment more quickly due to their individual threshold dose.

Lindsey Forstie, FNP, a family nurse practitioner with clinical training and experience in allergy and asthma care at Carefree Allergy in Phoenix, AZ, added that while further evaluation may be needed to confirm a peanut allergy diagnosis, it’s important for parents not to try introducing peanuts at home.

“Instead, they should speak with their pediatrician or allergist about appropriate testing and whether a supervised food challenge is safe and necessary,” she said.

According to Forstie, who was also not part of the study, the goal is to avoid unnecessary food restrictions while also keeping children safe.

“The key message is that not every positive allergy test means a child has a food allergy,” she told Healthline. “Accurate diagnosis helps protect children while allowing those who are not allergic to enjoy a more normal, less restrictive diet.”

Adobe dropped 24 CVEs in Adobe Connect on November 10. Eighteen are Critical. Six are CVSS 10.0.

Every one of the six 10.0s shares the same vector: AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:C/C:H/I:H/A:N. Network-reachable, no authentication, no user interaction, scope changed. No precondition an attacker has to work around.

Two are confirmed by their own CVE records: CVE-2026-121356 and CVE-2026-121889, both SSRF.

The other four sit across path traversal, XXE, code injection, and SQL injection — six different flaw classes landing on the same score. That's not a cluster. That's a codebase where input validation was inconsistent, and a researcher (edra, credited for 15 of the 24) found every place it wasn't enforced.

A server on 12.12 with no suffix is still vulnerable. A patch scanner that compares integers (>= 12.12) will show green. The suffix is the version.

This matters if you're writing detection logic, compliance checks, or SIEM rules against installed software versions. String comparison on the full version — including the alphabetic suffix — is the only correct check.

None of these is an exotic class. All of them are in the OWASP Top 10. The finding is that a mature, shipping product hadn't applied consistent defenses across its own surface.

Adobe states it's not aware of exploits in the wild, and nothing is in CISA KEV as of November 11. That's accurate as of today.

A 10.0 SSRF with no auth requirement is a short research project to weaponize. Seven SQL injections on an internet-facing meeting server are automatable with off-the-shelf tooling. "Not aware of exploitation" is a timestamp.

The Agentic Shift: How Autonomous AI Agents Are Transforming Human Resources

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Agentic Shift: How Autonomous AI Agents Are Transforming Human Resources

Human Resources is experiencing a profound architectural shift. While the first wave of enterprise artificial intelligence focused on generative text and passive chatbots—answering standard FAQ questions or drafting job descriptions—the modern workplace is embracing agentic AI. Unlike traditional automation that follows rigid if-then rules, AI agents possess contextual reasoning, autonomy, and multi-step execution capabilities. In HR, autonomous agents are evolving from administrative assistants into active operational partners that streamline recruitment, personalize employee experiences, and proactively support talent retention.

Recruiting has historically suffered from manual screening bottlenecks and fragmented applicant communication. Agentic workflows fundamentally redesign this funnel:

Dynamic Candidate Sourcing: Instead of waiting for applicants or running static boolean keyword searches, agentic sourcing systems continuously analyze industry repositories, professional networks, and open-source contributions. They evaluate technical competencies, trajectory, and cultural alignment against real-time workforce planning data.

Autonomous Pre-Screening and Scheduling: Sourcing agents interact with candidates conversationally across messaging channels, verifying prerequisite qualifications, handling mutual calendar coordination, and answering candidate questions about compensation benchmarks and team culture 24/7.

Objective Candidate Shortlisting: By anonymizing candidate profiles during early assessment rounds and focusing strictly on verifiable project outcomes and technical assessments, AI agents reduce unconscious bias in initial talent screening.

Traditional onboarding often overwhelms new hires with compliance checklists, disparate portal logins, and static policy documents. An onboarding agent acts as a dedicated 90-day digital concierge:

Workflow Orchestration: The agent coordinates across IT, facilities, and department heads to provision hardware, assign software licenses, and schedule introductory peer check-ins before Day One.

Contextual Knowledge Retrieval: Rather than forcing employees to search hundreds of pages in an intranet wiki, the agent answers nuanced inquiries—such as healthcare tier eligibility, 401(k) vesting timelines, or travel reimbursement policies—tailored specifically to the employee's jurisdiction and job level.

Check-In Cadence: The agent proactively prompts new hires at days 7, 30, and 60 to gauge onboarding satisfaction, identify learning friction, and notify team leads when extra support is needed.

Annual performance reviews are notoriously backward-looking and time-consuming. Agentic HR tools shift organizations toward continuous coaching and internal mobility:

Real-Time Feedback Synthesis: Agents analyze structured feedback loops across project management tools, peer acknowledgments, and goal milestones to provide managers with objective performance summaries ahead of 1-on-1 coaching sessions.

Dynamic Skills Gap Analysis: As market requirements evolve, talent agents cross-reference employee project histories with company strategic priorities, autonomously recommending targeted learning paths and internal gig opportunities.

Proactive Career Pathing: Employees receive personalized recommendations for lateral transfers, certifications, and mentorship pairings that match their long-term professional aspirations.

Employee turnover often catches leadership by surprise. Autonomous agents provide early-warning signals while respecting personal data boundaries:

Anonymized Engagement Pulse: Instead of relying solely on annual engagement surveys, agents analyze aggregated sentiment across anonymized pulse surveys and workplace communications to detect team-level burnout or cultural friction points.

Predictive Flight-Risk Triggers: By correlating macro indicators—such as stagnant tenure in grade, market compensation divergence, and project fatigue—agents alert HR business partners to intervene proactively with tailored retention or role-refresh conversations.

With autonomous agency comes the vital responsibility of ethical stewardship. Organizations deploying HR agents must maintain strict governance guardrails:

Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) for High-Stakes Decisions: AI agents should recommend, summarize, and execute administrative workflows, but all consequential personnel decisions—such as hiring, termination, disciplinary action, and compensation adjustments—must remain strictly in the hands of human leaders.

Algorithmic Transparency & Auditability: Every decision tree, ranking score, and recommendation made by an agent must be auditable, explainable, and compliant with emerging employment regulations (such as NYC Local Law 144 and EU AI Act employment standards).

Data Privacy and Segregation: Employee personal data, medical leaves, and performance notes must be strictly partitioned with enterprise-grade encryption and role-based access control, ensuring external language models are never trained on proprietary organizational data.

The emergence of agentic AI does not eliminate the human element in Human Resources; rather, it liberates HR professionals from routine operational overhead. By deploying autonomous agents to handle repetitive coordination, continuous data aggregation, and real-time support, HR leaders can reclaim their essential role as strategic advisors, culture builders, and empathetic mentors. Organizations that balance agentic efficiency with rigorous ethical oversight will build the most agile, resilient, and human-centric workforces of the future.
