Major League Baseball's free agency period opened earlier this week and teams are now clear to pursue any roster upgrades they deem necessary. That includes players coming over from Japan's Nippon Professional Baseball league, South Korea's Korea Baseball Organization, and Taiwan's Chinese Professional Baseball League. Those are high-level leagues that have sent many impact players MLB's way over the years.

Look no farther than the Dodgers to see how important international talent can be to a club. The Dodgers employed four different former NPB and KBO players this past season, including two-way star Shohei Ohtani, ace Yoshinobu Yamamoto, and starter Roki Sasaki. Ohtani was signed as an MLB free agent, but the others went to the Dodgers through the posting system.

Per the posting agreement with MLB, posted NPB and CPBL players get a 45-day window to negotiate and sign with an MLB team. KBO players get a 30-day window. Players who do not sign during that posting window return to their previous team in NPB, CPBL, or KBO. The MLB team that signs a posted player pays a fee to the player's former NPB, CPBL, or KBO team.

$5M plus 17.5% of contract value over $25M

$9.375M plus 15% of contract value over $50M

For example, the White Sox paid Munetaka Murakami's former team, the Yakult Swallows, a $6.575 million posting fee on his $34 million contract last offseason. That's $5 million on the first $25 million plus 17.5% of the remaining $9 million, which is $1.575 million. The $5 million plus $1.575 million equals the $6.575 million posting fee paid to the Swallows.

Unlike last lockout, MLB will not be pausing the signing window during the expected lockout, according to The Athletic. In other words, if owners lock players out on Dec. 1 as expected, that date will essentially serve as the same deadline for already posted NPB players unless the lockout ends before their 45-day window expires.

There are no superstar-caliber players like Ohtani or Yamamoto available in this winter's international free agent crop. There are, however, several interesting players who can improve an MLB roster in 2027. Here are the six best NPB, CPBL, and KBO players to know this offseason.

Add This Spicy Ingredient To Ground Beef For A Burger That's 10x Juicier

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

If you're looking for a burger patty that not only has a bit of kick, but also plenty of juice, you need 'nduja, a legendary Italian sausage. It is not only extremely flavorful, but also quite high in fat and moisture, making it a perfect addition no matter how you like to cook your burgers.

'Nduja is technically a type of salami, except it's far more decadent and incorporates a hefty dose of Calabrian chili peppers for next-level taste. Its high fat-to-lean meat ratio makes it perfect for introducing more moisture, even in small amounts. Even if you only have exceptionally lean meat on hand, one part 'nduja to four parts beef keeps your patties tender and juicy, and even helps develop a better crust.

The idea is similar to many meatball recipes from around the world that incorporate both pork and beef. Whereas beef provides body and a hearty texture, pork can provide extra fat, flavor, and moisture, working together to cook up something truly delicious. The peppers also create a slightly savory and spicy flavor profile that, while immediately noticeable, isn't so overwhelming that you might need to change up your preferred toppings. Each bite will drip with flavorful juices that mix with your condiments for the most moisture-rich burger possible.

Incorporating anything into a burger is a bit of a tightrope walk. You want to disperse your ingredients evenly but not work the muscle fibers so much that your patties end up dense or tough. 'Nduja's texture makes this quite easy, but you'll want to be careful about what type of meat you add it to.

Since it has a spreadable, pâté-like texture, working 'nduja directly into the meat is quite easy. Whether you mix it in thoroughly or squeeze pinches into already formed patties for pockets of flavor, you get plenty of fatty juices. Still, if you're worried about overworking your patties or want to add the salty meat at the very last minute, 'nduja is typically safe to eat right out of the package. Treat it like cheese and add a dollop after flipping your patties, letting it melt over the whole thing for a juicy entree.

If you want a custom burger blend, you have to be mindful of the proper fat-to-meat ratio. 'Nduja's exceptional fat content means it's best to add it to already lean blends so you don't end up with anything too greasy. The leaner your beef, the more 'nduja you can incorporate, so it's a balance between how much of its flavor you want included. Something that's at least 85% lean is ideal, letting you incorporate enough to taste without adding so much that you can't appreciate the beef's natural flavor.

Modern life is changing the DNA of bacteria in our guts

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Gut bacteria can acquire genes from neighboring microbes, adding to the genetic differences linked to how people live.

Diet, antibiotics, and indoor plumbing help shape which bacteria settle into a person’s gut. That much has been clear for years.

What researchers couldn’t say is whether the bacteria themselves have been evolving in response.

A study of nearly 6,000 gut bacterial genomes finds that they have.

Across the species examined, strains from industrialized communities carried more genes than those from non-industrialized communities.

Most of the extra genes arrived recently. The bacteria didn’t inherit them from a parent cell. They took them from other microbes living alongside them.

So modern life hasn’t only changed which bacteria live in the gut. The same species now carries a different set of genes depending on how its host lives.

Researchers at Kiel University’s Institute of Clinical Molecular Biology and MIT’s Department of Biological Engineering ran the work, with the evolutionary modeling done in Japan.

Everything here was measured in bacterial genomes, so none of it shows that a bigger bacterial genome makes a person healthier or sicker.

All of the genomes come from the Global Microbiome Conservancy, a non-profit effort to bank living strains from communities that microbiome research has mostly passed over.

For this study, 20 volunteers in Cameroon, Ghana, Malaysia, Nigeria and Rwanda gave fresh stool samples. None had taken antibiotics recently.

Scientists processed each sample on site within three hours. The samples were then frozen in liquid nitrogen at minus 321°F (minus 196°C).

In the lab, the material went onto plates inside an oxygen-free chamber. Colonies grew at 99°F (37°C) for one to two weeks. Single colonies were then picked and sequenced.

The cultures yielded 1,841 new bacterial genomes. Added to 4,140 strains already banked, the collection reached 5,981 genomes. They came from 56 donors in nine countries.

Researchers classified donors as industrialized or non-industrialized using a development score for where they lived, compared with their country’s median.

From that collection, the team picked ten species sampled well on both sides of the divide, with at least eight strains from each side. Most are Bacteroides and close relatives, the workhorses of the lower gut.

E. coli was left out on purpose. Its genetic diversity has already been mapped in detail.

In all ten species, strains from industrialized guts carried more protein-coding genes. The difference was statistically significant in eight of the ten species.

Core genes, the ones every strain of a species has, showed no difference. The extra material was all optional genes, the kind one strain carries and the next one doesn’t.

The optional sets also varied more from strain to strain in industrialized hosts, again in all ten species. The team then widened the comparison to 34 species and saw the same pattern.

A bigger genome could mean two different things. Either these strains picked up genes lately, or strains that already had big genomes happened to do well in modern guts.

Separating the two fell to a group in Okinawa, Japan. They compared the family tree of a single gene with the family tree of the species carrying it.

A mismatch between the two marks the spot where a gene was gained, lost or handed over.

Gene gains clustered near the tips of the evolutionary trees, indicating that they occurred relatively recently.

They also piled up on the branches belonging to industrialized hosts.

Between 80% and 90% of those gains were transfers from other microbes. Only a small share came down from a parent cell.

Many arrived packaged inside plasmids and viruses that infect bacteria.

Both deliver several genes at once. Swapping of this kind goes on well beyond the gut, in soil, seawater and thawing permafrost.

Gergely Szöllősi heads the Model-Based Evolutionary Genomics Unit at the Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology.

His group built these tools for a much older problem, and Lénárd Szánthó, a senior bioinformatician in the unit, adapted them to a far shorter stretch of time.

“These methods were built to look back hundreds of millions and billions of years, so it was not obvious that they would tell us anything about something this recent,” said Szöllősi.

“Lénárd made them work for this problem, and the picture that comes out is that bacteria in industrialized hosts have been acquiring genes from their neighbors recently, and this is what makes their genomes larger.”

About 5% of the gene families in a typical species collection tracked with the host’s way of life.

The team checked that against bacterial DNA read straight from the stool of 1,015 people, and 83% of the signals pointed the same way.

A few gene families turned up in more than one species, which is harder to put down to chance.

Two of them help build the outer coat of the cell, the part that other microbes and the immune system meet.

Another repeat hit was an enzyme that breaks down penicillin-type drugs. Genes like that ride the same mobile DNA now spreading antibiotic resistance through cities and farms.

The team also counted genes where protein-changing mutations had built up faster than mutations that did not change the protein. That pattern is the usual sign of a change being favored.

About 1.4% of the genes examined qualified. One of them helps a cell hold its outer envelope together under stress, and it carried the signature in three separate species.

In the five species with the most genomes, the researchers tracked single-letter DNA differences in core genes.

The variants tied to industrialized hosts kept landing on the same few jobs: building the cell wall, handling stress and making vitamin B12.

A companion study from the same group found more vitamin B12 production in the gut communities of people more exposed to industrialized environments.

Another cluster of changes hit the genes that build a molecule on the bacterial surface. The human immune system reads that molecule and reacts to it.

Researchers have not tested whether any of this changes how a person’s immune system behaves. The authors wrote that it is a possibility worth following up.

“I hope our results will inspire further studies to use this knowledge in supporting us to live a healthier life in our changing environment,” Szánthó said.

The analysis can’t say which part of industrialized living is responsible. Diet, medicine, sanitation and exposure to outdoor microbes all travel together, and this data can’t pull them apart.

Bigger genomes may not be an adaptation at all.

Extra genes build up on their own when the pressure to discard useless DNA eases, and the authors couldn’t rule that out.

Ten species is a thin slice of a gut that holds hundreds, and most of the ten belong to one bacterial group.

Species that have never been grown in a lab are missing here, which matters because industrialized guts hold fewer bacterial species to begin with.

The study itself doesn’t test what those extra genes actually do inside a person. Getting that answer will take experiments rather than more genomes.

The authors call for controlled experiments with bacteria, laboratory studies of evolution, and more detailed measurements of people’s living conditions.

The full study was published in the journal Cell.

Claude Personal Finance vs. ChatGPT Finances: What sets them apart?

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Both AI tools offer to delve deep into your bank accounts, but they differ in privacy settings.

Ahead of its plans to go public with a potentially record-breaking valuation, Anthropic recently announced a new feature for Claude called Personal Finance. It's an obvious rival to OpenAI's Finances feature within ChatGPT, which debuted in May. Both options from these AI giants are meant to provide users with a better understanding of their finances and are not designed to offer expert financial advice, but there are slight variations in how they do it that might make you lean towards one or the other. To help you decide which AI chatbot to go with for more clarity on your finances, we've laid out the major similarities and differences of Claude's Personal Finance and ChatGPT's Finances features.

Covering a broad spectrum of personal finance topics, there's a lot of crossover in what both OpenAI and Anthropic's offerings can do. Both of their AI chatbots can address general questions about financial goals, like buying a house, planning an expensive family vacation or creating a savings plan. Claude and ChatGPT will also generate dashboards to offer users better visuals at things like monthly expenses, investments and spending habits.

To activate either Claude's Personal Finance tool or ChatGPT's Finances feature, you have to link your bank account. Both AI companies partnered with Plaid to make this happen, allowing connections with the most popular financial institutions, like Bank of America, Capital One or Chase. Once set up, both Claude and ChatGPT only have "read-only" access to your financial accounts, meaning they can only view the info provided and would not be able to make any changes.

Anthropic specified that Claude's Personal Finance feature can only see your bank name, account name, account type, last four account digits, balances, credit limits and up to two years of transactions. Similarly, OpenAI said that its Finances tool can see balances, transactions, investments and liabilities.

While the core function of Anthropic's Personal Finance and OpenAI's Finances are very similar, the major difference between the two has to do with privacy settings. By default, Anthropic said, "when you chat with Claude in the Personal Finance hub, those conversations aren't used to train our models." Anthropic added that Claude will no longer see any data that you previously provided to its Personal Finance feature if you unlink your bank account.

On the other hand, OpenAI said that "your conversations with connected financial accounts follow the same model training settings you choose across ChatGPT." In this case, you have to opt out of your data being used by navigating to Data controls in ChatGPT's settings and turning off the option called Improve the model for everyone, which is on by default. When you disconnect your bank accounts from OpenAI, the company said that "your synced account data will be deleted from OpenAI's systems within 30 days."

To differentiate itself from OpenAI's alternative, Anthropic said that its Personal Finance feature "has no ads or sponsored recommendations, and it doesn't offer credit cards, loans or savings accounts of its own." While OpenAI doesn't explicitly mention ads with ChatGPT Finances, the company has been testing out ads for its cheaper tiers as of January.

OpenAI said it's looking to bring more features to ChatGPT's Finances by partnering with Intuit to cover more bases like credit card applications or tax questions. As for Anthropic's next steps with Personal Finance, the company wants to hear from banks, wealth managers and financial services companies to help improve the AI tool.
