Tesla’s Cybercab, with no steering wheel, starts carrying riders in Dallas

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Dallas is the second city for Tesla's two-seat robotaxi, after Austin in September.

A Tesla Cybercab on display at the Auto Zürich show in Zurich in November 2026.

Tesla has started offering rides in its Cybercab in Dallas, the second city after Austin for the two-seater with no steering wheel or pedals. Tesla already runs Model Y robotaxis in Dallas and plans to start delivering Cybercabs to private buyers in Texas in December.

Tesla’s Cybercab, its two-seat robotaxi with no steering wheel or pedals, is now giving rides in Dallas, InsideEVs reported. “Cybercab has arrived in your city,” Tesla’s Robotaxi account posted on X on Thursday.

Dallas is the second city to get the Cybercab, after it began giving rides in Austin in September. Tesla already runs driverless Model Y robotaxis in Dallas. Riders book both in the Tesla Robotaxi app.

Tesla’s robotaxis also run in Houston, Fort Worth, San Antonio, Tampa, Orlando and Miami, mostly with the Model Y. It has focused on Texas and Florida, where the rules are looser than in states like California.

The US road safety agency, NHTSA, is looking into how Tesla decided a car with no wheel or pedals meets US safety rules. Unlike Amazon’s Zoox, Tesla did not ask for an exemption. NHTSA is also reviewing claims that Tesla robotaxis, including Cybercabs, sped in school zones.

Tesla is also starting to sell the Cybercab to some customers for $29,990. Owners must pay a $299 monthly fee, or the car will not run. The first cars should reach buyers in Texas in December.

To build more of them, Tesla plans to wind down Cybertruck output at its Texas plant around mid-2027.

Casablanca Pre-Fall 2027

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“The area where I grew up, Belleville, is really different from the Hamptons,” said Casablanca’s Charaf Tajer. “Growing up between Paris and Morocco, it seemed a very different landscape: it sounded very exotic to me.” Globalization might have shrunk the world—and flattened it too—but distance still creates room for the imagination to generate glamour. For this resort season Tajer projected his Casablanca formula on the Hamptons.

His starting point was a survey of Jackie Kennedy and the milieu of American East Coast society during the middle of the last century. The seaside estates, sailing boats, country clubs, and sprawling lawns of this privileged playground offered a new destination for a brand whose MO has always been rooted in a romanticized dislocation. “It’s kind of near the cities,” Tajer said of the Hamptons, “but it has its own bubble.” Peering into that bubble through documentaries and archival imagery was an exercise in escapism, and Tajer intended this collection to be an escape route too. “It’s not that I don’t care: I’m really into the news. But I also feel like we need to care less, because the news can take us into a mental spiral.”

Tajer presented the collection as the wardrobe of a dynastic clan on vacation, with each member bringing their own interpretation of American sportswear through a Casablanca-fied lens. Although shot in the UK, closer to Hertfordshire than the Hamptons, the images were cleverly set and staged. A scion crunched the gravel with a chunky labrador in a nicely homaged embroidered Skyliner jacket, wearing wide-leg chinos in one shot and skate shorts in another. Another lounged on a plump couch in seasonally-printed tennis gear, or lurked on the stairs in a camp collar shirt and embroidered wide-leg pants.

The women wore striped knitted separates, scarf-print silks, and tennis dresses, as well as a long black knitted dress that offered a more universal option for evening. Throughout, the preppy and streetwear references were blended and sometimes distorted through color, proportion, and the prints and embroidered illustrations created by the brand’s three in-house artists.

The chinoiserie pattern adapted from a stately temple jar in this synthetic Camelot backdrop was prominently transposed into prints and patches. For Tajer, it served both as a hint towards a future project and as a reminder of how the decorative vernacular of one culture can become the status symbols of another.

Tajer said his ambition is to create a Casablanca wardrobe that can accommodate almost any occasion. The silk shirts that established the label have been joined by much more: this season’s additions included a leather tracksuit and new hybrid driving/boat shoe. Tajer cited Ralph Lauren as an inspiration, saying: “This is always what I wanted to create with Casablanca: a full, cohesive world.”

Mortgage applications for new-home purchases fell 13.6% year over year in October as rising mortgage rates and affordability pressures weighed on buyer demand, according to the Mortgage Bankers Association’s (MBA) Builder Application Survey.

Applications also declined 7% from September. The figures are not adjusted for seasonal patterns.

The 30-year fixed mortgage rate climbed about 60 basis points over the two months leading up to October, reaching 7.5% last month, said Joel Kan, MBA’s vice president and deputy chief economist.

“As a result, applications for the purchase of newly built homes declined year-over-year for the fourth consecutive month,” Kan said.

MBA’s estimate of new-home sales increased slightly in October, but the annualized sales pace for the past two months remained at its weakest level since July 2022. Affordability pressures and uncertainty surrounding mortgage rates have prompted prospective buyers to delay purchases, even as builders continue offering incentives, Kan said.

MBA estimated that new single-family home sales ran at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 592,000 units in October, up 1.4% from September’s pace of 584,000 units.

On an unadjusted basis, MBA estimated 44,000 new-home sales in October, down 6.4% from 47,000 in September.

Conventional loans accounted for 47.9% of new-home mortgage applications in October, while Federal Housing Administration (FHA) loans made up 36.9%. U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) loans accounted for 14.1%, and  U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) loans represented 1%.

The average loan size for new homes declined to $368,934 in October from $374,724 in September.

MBA’s Builder Application Survey tracks mortgage application volume from homebuilder-affiliated mortgage companies nationwide. The association uses the data, along with other market information and assumptions, to estimate new-home sales at the national, state and metropolitan levels.

The survey provides an early indication of new-home sales because mortgage applications typically coincide with contract signings, when the Census Bureau records new-home sales in its monthly report.

Tiny egg-eating snake discovered in Ethiopia

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 20, 2026 12:01 PM EST

Biologists have discovered multiple new species of egg-eating snakes slithering through the forests near southeastern Ethiopia’s Bale Mountains. Although researchers only described the white-throated egg-eater (Dasypeltis albigularis) in their study recently published in the journal Herpetozoa, they promised that information on additional finds is on the way.

“Three other species in this genus are still waiting in line,” Arthur Tiutenko, a study co-author and herpetologist at Germany’s Friedrich-Alexander University of Erlangen–Nuremberg (FAU), said in a statement.

Many snakes will snack on an egg if the opportunity presents itself. But for a handful of species, it’s all eggs, all the time. These nonvenomous snakes solely eat shelled embryos, and largely exist in Africa and some portions of India. To devour their meals, egg-eating snakes have evolved a set of very clever biological tricks. First, their back bones extend all the way into their esophagus and function like a saw to slice open shells. From there, the snake’s neck muscles contract and funnel the egg contents down its throat. Once finished, the reptile simply spits out the cracked shells.

However, Tiutenko’s team almost missed D. albigularis entirely. At first glance, the snake looks extremely similar to a species called the montane egg-eater (Dasypeltis atra). A closer inspection revealed some key differences—a patch of white scales extending down its stomach earned its “white-throated” moniker, while the skin between each scale has a distinctly blue shimmer. Other egg-eating snakes can rub their scales to create a rustling noise that startles foes, but only the D. albigularis females do the same—and they do it silently.

Don’t envision python-sized snakes chowing down on chicken eggs though. D. albigularis is less than eight inches long, weighs only 0.05 ounces, and is about as thick as a knitting needle. This means the snakes only have eyes for particularly tiny eggs from various diminutive birds. Luckily, their regional home is full of options.

“That may also be the reason why egg-eating snakes only live in Africa, and in particular in a small area in the southwest corner of the Arabian peninsula,” added Tiutenko.

On Friday, Google’s Gemini team announced the results of a new partnership with the wildfire-tracking app Watch Duty, after Gemini engineers spent six months embedded with Watch Duty’s first responders. The Gemini AI improvements got a real-world test during Washington state’s Railroad Fire in July 2026, when Watch Duty issued what it calls its first-ever nighttime Critical Alert. The alert stemmed from a nearly unintelligible, static-filled 911 call that the Gemini tool transcribed in under 30 seconds. From the data collected, Gemini engineers and Watch Duty identified several measures of success.

The Gemini tool continues to analyze approximately 200 hours of radio speech traffic from 400 feeds per day. The tool was made to handle more than 10,000 radio feeds if necessary.

A controversial bill aimed at speeding up infrastructure projects on public lands passed the U.S. Senate Thursday night and now heads to the House.

The Senate declined to vote on a flurry of last-minute amendments to the nearly 500-page text of the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act — including one that would have banned data centers on public land, before voting for the third time late Thursday night. Support in the Senate eroded over the series of votes, from 73-25 on the first vote Nov. 9 to a final vote of 62-33. A bill requires a simple majority of 51 votes in the Senate to pass.

BAAJA is co-sponsored by Sen. Martin Heinrich, a Democrat from New Mexico often considered a conservation champion, and Sen. Heinrich says BAAJA cuts red tape and will speed up renewable energy projects to help fight climate change, increase jobs, and bring down energy costs for average Americans.

Conservation advocates, however, fear it will also lock in projects many sportsmen and women have spent years fighting, like a copper-sulfide mine by the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness or the Pebble Mine in Alaska’s Bristol Bay. It also may well open the door for massive data centers on public land, something most of the conservation and sporting community resoundingly oppose.

Many of the nation’s tribes have forcefully spoken out against the bill saying it severely curtails tribal input.

Once these projects are approved, they would be difficult to unwind,” according to the Western Environmental Law Center. “BAAJA would cause grave, generational harm to the West’s public lands, waters and wildlife, and communities.”

Even conservation groups who support portions of the bill question how fast such a massive piece of legislation is moving through Congress. This is the first major permitting reform bill in decades and was introduced Sept. 30.

Researchers Publish Security Analysis of Bitcoin’s Post-Quantum SHRINCS Proposal

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Several researchers have published a 131-page security analysis of SHRINCS, a proposed post-quantum signature scheme for Bitcoin, and are asking the wider community to look for flaws in it. Researcher Mikhail Kudinov announced the first version Thursday in a thread on X, saying the paper reduces SHRINCS’ security to the properties of its underlying hash function.

The analysis, dated Nov. 18, estimates classical security of 121.4 to 122.7 bits for SHRINCS, though those figures rest on assumptions that include three unproven conjectures. Kudinov said the work is not finished. The team is still working on a new analysis of a property called SM-DSPR, whose bound has so far been conjectured, and on tightening a reprogramming step and improving a bound against quantum attackers.

SHRINCS is a hash-based scheme, meaning its security rests on hash functions rather than the elliptic curve math behind today’s Bitcoin signatures. It places two designs under one key: a stateful part built on FXMSS with WOTS+C, and a stateless fallback built on SLH-DSA. Because both use the same hash function, Kudinov said, the proof has to cover both.

Signature size is the main cost. Under the draft proposal, stateful SHRINCS signatures run from 548 to 4,619 bytes, while the stateless fallback is 5,777 bytes. Schnorr signatures on Bitcoin today are 64 bytes, and ECDSA signatures typically come in around 71. Signatures take up scarce block space, so larger ones mean fewer transactions per block.

The compact stateful path carries a danger. It relies on one-time keys, and if a wallet accidentally reuses one, an attacker can forge a signature for that address and steal the funds. Wallet software therefore has to track the state of every key. The stateless path avoids the problem, but at a much larger size.

Jonas Nick, Blockstream’s director of research, told Isabel Foxen Duke, head of the MARA Foundation, on MARA Foundation TV that wallets must automatically fall back to the stateless path whenever a state counter could be compromised. “It needs to be impossible for the user to behave in a way that this catastrophic event happens,” he said. “Wallet developers need to deal with this stateful component correctly.”

Nick also warned that heavy use of the larger signatures would weigh on the network. “We don’t know what the fee market will look like once we have these very big signatures,” he said. “And it’s not just the fee market, it’s also just fewer transactions fit into a block, meaning fewer people can use Bitcoin. And I think this changes the value proposition of Bitcoin.”

The authors are clear that the analysis needs scrutiny. Kudinov said it has not been sufficiently peer reviewed, and the project’s GitHub repository warns that the documents may contain errors and should not be the sole basis for deploying SHRINCS. “We tried to be careful, but a proof of this size can have mistakes,” he wrote, asking readers to open an issue if they find one.

The team also produced two results that are not specific to SHRINCS: a classical bound for a property called ITSR without reprogramming, and a quadratic quantum bound for undetectability. Kudinov said they also apply to SPHINCS+ and SLH-DSA, the hash-based schemes SHRINCS borrows from, and that they were published as separate papers.

The SHRINCS work lands as quantum risk climbs the agenda. A global securities regulators’ group this week warned that moving blockchains to quantum-resistant cryptography may be especially hard, and researchers recently built a post-quantum defense for the Lightning Network after Bitcoin’s first quantum-safe transaction was shown without a fork.

The draft Bitcoin Improvement Proposal for SHRINCS is also open for comment. “We need all the possible feedback — engineers, cryptographers, protocol developers,” Kudinov, a post-quantum cryptography researcher at Blockstream, said of it. “There are a lot of things to check, test, complain about, or praise.”

Two-Thirds of Dementia Content On TikTok Is Misleading, Researchers Say

A new survey suggests that about two-thirds of dementia advice videos on TikTok are misleading

Of the videos examined, the analysis shows that nearly half contained inaccurate and potentially damaging information.

The researchers say that approximately 44% of TikTok creators who share dementia content lack credibility and appropriate qualifications.

Although misleading dementia content may appear on other social media platforms, this research examined only TikTok content, warranting further investigation.

The survey was commissioned by Homewatch Caregivers and has not been published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal.

A spokesperson for TikTok told Healthline that if health-related content is potentially harmful, it is flagged for removal. The spokesperson added that 99.6% of misinformation deemed violative was proactively removed from the platform in 2026.

The spokesperson added that TikTok relies on fact-checking and guidance from public health authorities to help assess the accuracy of content. The platform may also apply warning labels to content that cannot be verified as accurate.

Looking to social media for health information is not uncommon. A recent poll reports that 31% of adults in the United States use social media at least once a month to look for health information and advice.

Experts advise seeking information about dementia from trusted sources, such as a primary care doctor and organizations like the National Institute on Aging (NIA) and the Alzheimer’s Association, rather than social media, where content creators who share advice often lack expertise.

“It’s normal to want answers quickly when you’re caring for someone living with dementia, and a short video from someone who seems to know what they’re talking about can be really convincing,” said lead researcher Jennifer Winarski, senior manager of care operations and quality for Homewatch CareGivers.

“Despite there being some genuinely helpful information out there, you don’t always know whether it’s backed by medical evidence, so we wanted to bring our own expertise to uncover whether popular TikToks can be trusted,” she told Healthline. “We hope our report will encourage people to be a bit more careful about what they act on.”

For the survey, researchers selected a sample of 500 of the most-watched dementia-related videos on TikTok with hashtags such as:

Researchers then transcribed the video data to assess its accuracy.

The researchers found that two-thirds (66.4%) of the popular TikTok videos contained misleading information. Of those videos, about half (47.6%) contained potentially damaging misleading dementia information.

“It’s clear that many of the creators mean well, but much of what we saw was personal opinion rather than proven information, and in a few cases, general good health tips were presented as if they were specifically helpful for individuals living with dementia,” Winarski said.

“If families act on that, they could end up spending time and money on things that won’t help — and in the worst of cases, it could delay getting the support their loved one really needs,” she added.

Dung Trinh, MD, an internist for MemorialCare Medical Group and chief medical officer of the Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, said it’s important to be aware of the volume of misleading dementia information on social media. Trinh was not involved in this survey.

Some people may be making decisions about their health based on incomplete, misleading, or incorrect information, he said.

“For example, someone might see a video claiming that a particular supplement can reverse Alzheimer’s disease or that certain memory problems automatically mean someone has dementia,” Trinh said.

“These messages can lead to unnecessary fear, false reassurance, wasted money, or delays in getting a proper diagnosis.”

“We are entering an exciting era in Alzheimer’s care, with advances in blood-based biomarkers, earlier detection, and treatments that may slow disease progression in appropriately selected patients,” Trinh continued.

“Getting an accurate diagnosis and receiving timely medical guidance are becoming increasingly important.”

The researchers examined the content creators behind the TikTok videos included in their analysis. They found that 44% of video creators behind the misleading videos had no relevant qualifications.

Rehan Aziz, MD, vice chair of education in the Department of Psychiatry at Hackensack Meridian Jersey Shore University Medical Center and associate professor of psychiatry and neurology at the Hackensack Meridian School of Medicine in New Jersey, said that content creators offering dementia advice should have relevant qualifications. Aziz wasn’t involved in the survey.

“Start by looking at the creator’s profile for a full name and a specific credential, such as MD, DO, PhD, or RN, along with a specialty,” Aziz told Healthline.

“For dementia, the most relevant experts include neurologists, geriatric psychiatrists, geriatricians, neuropsychologists, and nurses, including advanced practice nurses, or social workers who specialize in dementia care.”

“You can confirm a doctor’s license through your state’s licensing board website and check board certification at certificationmatters.org,” Aziz said.

As of 2020, more than 55 million people worldwide were living with dementia — a neurological condition that negatively affects the brain. Researchers predict this number will hit 78 million by 2030.

The increase in the number of people seeking dementia content online is a reflection of this trend.

When looking for accurate dementia information and guidance, Nestor Beltre, MD, a neurologist with Baptist Health Kenneth C. Griffin Neuroscience Institute, part of Baptist Health South Florida, said a great first step is to speak with their primary care physician. Beltre wasn’t involved in the survey.

“Memory changes can occur for many different reasons, and some causes, including vitamin deficiencies, depression, and certain medical conditions, may be treatable,” he told Healthline.

“Early evaluation can help identify what may be contributing to these changes and determine the most appropriate next steps. Depending on the individual’s symptoms, a neurologist, psychiatrist, or neuropsychologist can provide further evaluation and guidance,” Belter continued.

Beltre advised turning to trusted resources such as the Alzheimer’s Association and the National Institute on Aging, which can increase public understanding of memory-related conditions and available treatment options.

“The most important message is that patients and families have resources available to help them better understand and manage cognitive health,” he said.
