Earth's churning mantle may have created the Great Unconformity

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Explaining the Great Unconformity, where Cambrian deposits sit on top of older rocks.

In the Grand Canyon, there is a line in the rock where time seems to have gone missing. Ancient crystalline rocks mark its bottom, while sandstones laid down as life started to diversify during the Cambrian explosion mark the top. “It’s called the Great Unconformity, and it’s a gap in the geologic record,” says Nicolas Flament, a geoscientist at the University of Wollongong, Australia. “This gap can be anywhere between 200 million years, which is not that impressive, to more than a billion years.”

In a recent Nature study, Flament and his colleague Santosh Dhungana argue that this gap was carved with help from deep within the planet. According to their models, slow currents of hot rock in Earth’s mantle pushed entire continents up for hundreds of millions of years, letting glaciers grind them down and then let them sink, allowing fresh sediments to bury the scar. The largest hole in the rock record might be a fingerprint of processes happening deep in the planet’s interior.

The oldest sediments resting on the Great Unconformity in North America are between 635 and 482 million years old. Geologists have traced it across most of North America and parts of northern Europe and northern China. “Something seems to have happened, in particular in North America,” Flament says.

The leading explanation was glaciation. Between roughly 720 and 635 million years ago, glaciers reached the tropics, and some scientists think the entire planet froze over, a scenario called Snowball Earth. “Ice sheets are very good at grinding, scouring, scraping the surface,” Flament explains. He said that glaciers are about 10 times more effective than rivers at eroding rock away. But if the whole planet froze, the Great Unconformity should be found everywhere. And it’s not.

Other explanations included rifting, pulling and stretching of Earth’s crust, and a global drop in sea levels. But rifting acts mostly on the edges of continents, and a sea level change should have affected all continents alike. “There’s no doubt that there was extensive glaciation. There’s no doubt that there have been sea-level changes throughout time. The question is, was that enough to put the Great Unconformity puzzle together?” Flament says.

A fourth idea held that the mantle itself was moving continents up and down, a process called dynamic topography. “It had been proposed, but it hadn’t been quantitatively tested,” Flament says. So, his team set out to do so.

From roughly 100 to 200 kilometers beneath the surface down to nearly 3,000 kilometers, Earth’s mantle slowly convects. “An analogy would be a lava lamp, where some stuff comes up and some stuff comes down,” Flament explains. “Cold rock sinks and hot rock rises.”

The mantle is solid, yet a piece of rock can slowly travel from the surface to the deep Earth over some 200 million years. That motion tugs on the surface. Where cold slabs of ocean floor sink into the mantle at subduction zones, the ground gets pulled down; where hot mantle rises, it gets pushed up. The amplitude of this up and down motion is still debated, though. “The short of it is several hundreds of meters, and it’s still to be worked out exactly what that [precise value] is,” Flament says.

Dhungana and Flament ran six simulations of mantle convection going back 1 billion years, driven by reconstructions of how tectonic plates moved over that time. Then they grouped regions where the ground rose and fell in similar ways to find where the unconformity should have formed, and where it should have survived to the present. Flament, however, says the starting configuration of these simulations remains uncertain.

Magnetic records in ancient rocks reveal how far continents were from the equator, but not their longitude. “It’s possible that some continents may be in the wrong hemisphere,” Flament admits. The ancient ocean floor, long since recycled into the mantle, had to be invented entirely from scratch in the models. Still, the simulations produced a consistent story.

Laurentia, the ancient core of North America, had little or no subduction along its margins between about 930 and 510 million years ago, much like Africa today, so it sat above some rising mantle and was pushed up. Five of the six models showed this pattern of Laurentia seeing long uplift followed by sinking in the Cambrian. For northern Europe and North China, this pattern showed up in four.

But the uplift alone, Flament’s team concluded, was too gentle to strip away enough rock to create the Great Unconformity.

“The uplift could create a tilt for the whole continent, but it’s not great at creating a steep slope,” Flament says. The missing piece of the puzzle, he argues, were glaciers that ground through rock even on low ground and gentle slopes. The models show the uplift was still building when Snowball Earth hit. “This gentle uplift that’s continuous, and then that glacial erosion that comes on top, work together,” Flament explains. “They’re not coupled. They just happen to coincide in time.” When the continents sank in the Cambrian, seas flooded in and buried the eroded surface.

The models made by Flament’s team also explain why the gap is so obvious in North America but hard to find elsewhere. Compared with records of sediment preserved on each continent, North America and Eurasia show one long rise followed by one long fall, which the researchers interpret as the ideal recipe for the Great Unconformity.

South America and Australia, on the other hand, went through two cycles. “This would form potentially two unconformities, and then the Great Unconformity would be more difficult to see,” Flament says. Africa was a different case altogether—hemmed in by mountain belts at the time, its erosion and sediment were shaped by the mountains rather than by the mantle beneath.

But if the mantle goes through such up and down cycles on a regular basis, the remaining question was why we can see only one Great Unconformity, and not many.

The team says the Great Unconformity is unique in the geological record because of a combination of a rise-and-fall cycle lasting over 900 million years, a near-global glaciation that happened at the right moment, and rock records that get harder to read farther back in time.

Flament suspects there may be an older candidate, tied to a glaciation 2.4 billion years ago, but rocks that old lack the fossils that make younger sediments easier to date. “Having life in sediments makes it a lot easier to have a sense of time,” he says. His work, however, may help explain what happens right after the Great Unconformity formed.

The intense erosion that carved the Great Unconformity washed rock material into the oceans, possibly fertilizing them just before the Cambrian explosion, when calcium became abundant in seawater. “That’s when life learns to mobilize calcium and create skeletons and shells,” Flament says. He admits, though, that mantle dynamics likely wasn’t the only process that enabled the Cambrian explosion. “I think it would be misleading to say the mantle set the pace here. But I think it was one of the forces that worked together.”

What Does an Attention Deficit Really Mean?

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Posted November 6, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

Most of us have experienced what it’s like to struggle to pay attention to something. I’ll offer a recent example of my own.

My mind wandered further and further from the focus of the long academic meeting I was attending. I looked around at the others in the room. They seemed to be listening—or at least staring at the screen.

We were in a plush venue just off campus, the sort we often hire for these long meetings. Rain pounded on the glass roof. Beyond it, the grounds were lush and green.

A young waiter, about the same age as my son, clinked some glasses, replenished the water, juice and coffee, and left a tray of small chocolate brownies and fresh fruit at the back of the room.

I remember how I wanted one of the brownies and some plump green grapes, still shining with moisture. But walking to the back and helping myself felt rude. Everyone else seemed to be managing to pay attention. Weren’t they?

It felt as though I had lost all ability to focus.

But my attention hadn’t disappeared. I could focus on the rain, the trees, the grapes and the brownies. I could attend to them effortlessly. But I couldn’t keep my mind on the sixth presentation of the day.

I tried to care about what was being said. I really wanted to. But I simply couldn’t.

Something inside me had stopped cooperating. I’d reached a point of attentional saturation. It was a little like realising you’re full and that each extra mouthful feels forced.

I longed to get up, walk outside, or breathe fresh air. I wanted to think about something other than the presentation. I felt as though I was dying of boredom.

I felt resentful that I was expected to keep listening, but also guilty that I couldn’t find the attention the speaker seemed to deserve. What was wrong with me? Why was I so inconsiderate?

Perhaps you can remember similar experiences. Encountering situations in which our capacity to ‘pay attention’ is stretched is so often part of modern life.

I wondered what it reflected about me. Was I bored? Had the subject failed to interest me or feel important enough? Was it about motivation? Was I tired? Had I simply reached the limit of what I could sustain that day? Or was I reacting to an expectation that I should or must keep listening?

The idea that something can be ‘wrong’ with our ‘attention’ is now woven into the cultures and institutions in which we live. But psychologists don’t really use the term attention to describe one clearly defined ability.

In a 2019 paper, psychologists Bernhard Hommel and colleagues describe the common idea of attention as the mechanisms that help us make efficient use of our limited capacity to process, store and retrieve information. That sounds straightforward.

But the word is used as a proxy for a wide range of abilities and experiences:

These are all capacities that might be (and often are) referred to when someone uses the word ‘attention.’ However, Hommel and colleagues argue that it is highly unlikely that the same set of functional or neural mechanisms would be involved in and responsible for each of these. They also caution that dividing attention into neat, separate functions can obscure what those functions have in common too.

They’re all examples of "attention," but they might not share the same underpinning anatomical or neurological roots. This matters more than you might think. Their argument has implications beyond the lab.

For example, if attention describes different things, what are we saying when we tell someone to “pay attention”? Furthermore, what are we referring to when we say that someone has an “attention deficit”?

What’s more, when someone struggles to stay with a task, the difficulty may indeed involve attention. But it may also involve motivation, intention or emotion. Perhaps the task feels pointless, the person doesn’t want to do it, or they are bored. The label 'attention' alone doesn’t tell us which of these is at play in a situation.

Psychological labels deserve care and scrutiny, no matter how deeply entrenched they might be. A label can help us recognise a pattern. But it can frequently give us the impression that we understand more than we do.

The authors of the paper above ultimately concluded that we’d be better to drop the term 'attention' as a label for a specific functional or neural system, because, they argue, it doesn’t actually identify a single functional or neural system. This is significant.

If attention were no longer treated as the label for a single mental or neural system, what would we make of things like Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)? The name itself seems to point to a deficit in a particular capacity we call 'attention.' But if there is no single system called attention, then what, precisely, is deficient in something like ADHD?

This doesn’t necessarily make ADHD imaginary, or the difficulties people experience less real. But it does make the label seem less like an explanation than it first appears. Perhaps it names a recognisable pattern of difficulty, while leaving the processes, circumstances and histories that give rise to it in any one person wide open.

Post-Trail Blues: Learning to Live Off the Trail

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

I miss the trail. It went by too fast. Where did the time go? Sometimes, I feel like I must have blacked out somewhere in the middle because suddenly, I was in New England. It’s hard to wrap my head around the fact that I did something so incredible, so challenging, and so far beyond what I ever thought I was capable of.

I would do anything to be back out there.

Smiling somewhere in a fire tower in New Jersey.

I never understood the people who thru-hike the Appalachian Trail multiple times. But the other morning, the thought crossed my mind, and for the first time, I understood the desire. No, I don’t think I would ever actually hike the entire trail again. But to quiet the mental battles I’m facing right now? To find that peace again? I would do it in a heartbeat.

I miss my friends. I miss who I was out there. I miss the simplicity of it all, the freedom of not constantly stressing about the future or wondering how I’m going to remain happy, fulfilled, and inspired. Out there, I didn’t have to have everything figured out. I just had to keep moving forward, one day and one mile at a time.

Coming back to the real world has been harder than I expected. People don’t look at me the same way, or maybe I’m just seeing things differently now. I don’t know if it’s envy, indifference, or if people are simply caught up in their own battles. Some people have no idea what I just accomplished, and others can’t seem to wrap their heads around it.

I’d be lying if I said I wasn’t disappointed. I wish more people asked me about my hike. I wish more people were curious about what I experienced, what I learned, and what it took to get through it. I wish more of the people who said they would join me had shown up along the way.

Words of encouragment outside of Hanover, NH

I think a part of me expected to come home and have people want to hear every little detail, to understand just how much this meant to me.

But I also know that life doesn’t revolve around me, my experiences, or my wants and needs. Everyone has their own lives to live and their own battles to fight. Just because this experience changed me doesn’t mean everyone else will know how to understand it.

I talked about these feelings with other thru-hikers who were experiencing something similar.  A few mentioned that this was one of the reasons they blogged. Writing gave them an outlet for sharing everything they’d experienced, a place to tell their stories and get all those thoughts out of their heads. Hopefully, those stories find an audience who cares, but even if they don’t, there’s something valuable about having a place to put it all.

That really resonated with me and inspired me to start writing for The Trek again. Maybe this is another way to stay connected to the trail, revisit those memories, and share a little piece of the experience with others.

And now, here I am, trying to figure out what comes next.

What am I going to do with my life? How do I hold on to the person I was out there? How do I find that same sense of purpose, peace, and inspiration when I’m back in a world that feels so much louder and more complicated?

Looking for moose on Ethan Pond in New Hampshire

And perhaps the question that keeps creeping into my mind: How soon can I hike another long-distance trail?

I knew coming home would be hard, but I don’t think I fully understood just how much I would miss the trail until I was no longer on it. I thought finishing would feel like an ending worth celebrating. Instead, it feels like I left a part of myself out there somewhere, and I’m still trying to figure out how to bring it home.

Ueno Bank scandal plunges Paraguay into political crisis

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Suspected financial fraud, public money at risk and millions of customers affected: Paraguay is in serious crisis following the sudden collapse of Ueno Bank. President Santiago Pena is facing impeachment.

The Ueno Bank collapse is turning into one of the biggest financial and political scandals in the history of Paraguay. At issue are suspected banking irregularities, hundreds of millions of dollars in public funds, and business ties between the bank and the country's president, Santiago Pena.

The crisis was triggered on November 3, when the Paraguayan central bank (BCP) initiated resolution procedures against Ueno after determining that it had breached minimum solvency requirements. The public prosecutor has since announced three investigations into suspected financial crimes. 27 individuals have already been formally charged.

Pressure on President Pena intensified. On November 5, he tendered his resignation, but the Paraguayan Congress did not accept it. Instead, the Chamber of Deputies voted unanimously to impeach him. A joint session of both legislative chambers was called to consider the resignation, but the session was not quorate, so no decision could be taken. Pena remains in office for now, and the Senate has opened impeachment proceedings.

Ueno Bank is a Paraguayan digital bank belonging to the Grupo Vazquez, a big Paraguayan conglomerate. Launched in 2021, it expanded remarkably fast: It recently had around three million customers, almost half the population of Paraguay. At the same time, there were warnings that the bank might have extended excessive loans to companies associated with its owners.

On November 3, 2026, the Paraguayan Central Bank (BCP) intervened, having determined that the bank had breached minimum solvency requirements: It no longer had sufficient capital to provide security for risks it had incurred. Business operations were abruptly suspended, leaving millions of customers unable to access their accounts.

Investigations are primarily focused on three potential problems.

First, Ueno Bank is believed to have extended credit to other companies from the same group, and these may have exceeded legal limits.

Some business dealings may also have served to conceal the lack of capital, and make the bank's financial situation appear better than it actually was.

There is also the question of why the Central Bank didn't intervene sooner, despite indications that there were problems.

Ueno Bank held huge deposits of public money. Its most important investors include the Instituto de Prevision Social (IPS), the Paraguayan social security agency responsible for pensions and social welfare payments. According to media reports, Ueno Bank administers public funds totalling as much as 800 to 850 million US dollars.

Not only is the size of the amount problematic, so is the concentration of public funds in a single financial institution — one that has had to be placed under state control. Other state institutions are also said to have continued to deposit funds with the bank.

This raises questions about how state institutions make their investment decisions, and the risks to which they have exposed public funds.

There is Pena's business relationship with Grupo Vazquez, to which the Ueno Bank belongs. The Paraguayan president maintained business connections with the conglomerate until March 2025, long after his election as head of state. However, he denies having had any direct involvement with the bank.

On the other hand, Pena's appointment of Carlos Carvallo as president of the Central Bank in 2023 has come under scrutiny. The decision is controversial because until then Carvallo was the chair of the finance company that became Ueno Bank, Financiero Ueno. It raises questions about possible conflict of interest, and the independence of banking supervision.

Carvallo resigned on November 3, at Pena's request.

On November 4, the public prosecutor's office formally charged 27 people: eight Ueno Bank executives, and 19 others with connections to companies associated with the bank.

The investigations are looking into suspected breach of trust, attempted fraud, breach of duty in crisis situations, and violation of the legal obligation to keep records.

On November 5, a judge remanded several former bank executives in custody, including Federico Miguel Vazquez Villasanti, the president of Grupo Vazquez. Two other accused were placed under house arrest.

The Central Bank has initiated winding-up proceedings against Ueno Bank. Deposits of sums up to the equivalent of 37,600 US dollars per customer are protected by law. A law has also been passed that will enable around 20,000 US dollars per saver to be paid out early. However, it is still not clear when the affected customers will actually get their money back, or what will happen to deposits greater than the legally protected maximum.

The political situation is more complicated. Santiago Pena tendered his resignation, but remains in office. A joint session of the two chambers of Congress could not decide whether or not to accept his resignation as the meeting was not quorate. The Senate has now initiated impeachment proceedings on charges of serious breaches of duty. Pena has announced that he will face the proceedings and exercise his right to defend himself.

What is at stake here is political immunity. If Pena leaves office in a regular way – by resigning — he will automatically become a senator for life, and this will give him parliamentary immunity from prosecution. If he is impeached, he loses this status, and can be prosecuted.

Should Pena leave office, the constitution decrees that his vice-president, Pedro Alliana, would take over as president. Congress would then have to elect a new vice-president. The current term of office runs until August 2028.

Democrats just won control of Georgia Power’s regulator for the first time in 30 years — and its OpenAI data center contract was entirely redacted

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

“This story was originally published by Grist. Sign up for Grist’s weekly newsletter here.”

Beginning in January, three of the five officials on the Georgia Public Service Commission will be Democrats. It will be the first time the party has controlled the powerful energy regulation board in 30 years. The commission, which regulates utilities including the state’s largest electric provider, Georgia Power, has come under more scrutiny in recent years due to rising power bills and the high energy demands of data centers.

Now, Democrats plan to focus on affordability, clean energy, and closer scrutiny of Georgia Power.

“There’s a long laundry list of things that need to be looked into,” said Democratic commissioner Peter Hubbard, who won his reelection bid this week. “And so we’ll have to triage, in fact, and decide how best to move forward.”

One of his first priorities will be launching an affordability docket, he said — a series of hearings that would delve into the reasons for high power bills and ways to save customers money. Hubbard said he and his fellow Democrats also plan to bring close scrutiny to upcoming Georgia Power requests around making more energy and adjusting customer rates. Shelia Edwards and Alicia Johnson are the other two Democrats on the PSC.

But any changes, especially to power bills, will likely take time.

“We may not be able to expect immediate effects on our wallets,” said Emory University associate professor Kristin Phillips, who studies energy burden and the PSC. “But I think we can expect immediate change in the types of decisions that are made.”

Affordability and high energy bills were driving issues in the campaigns both this year and last year, the two elections that together resulted in the new Democratic majority on the commission.

“We knew that we had to connect the dots between energy and affordability,” said Brionté McCorkle of Georgia Conservation Voters, a group that campaigned for the Democratic candidates and worked to draw attention to the PSC elections. “It’s not just this obscure regulatory body like it used to be treated. Its decisions directly affect household budgets, communities, health, and our energy future.”

Chris Womack, the CEO of Southern Company, Georgia Power’s parent company, also acknowledged the central importance of affordability during the company’s third-quarter earnings call on Thursday. But Womack said he’s confident the utility can address those concerns with the new commissioners.

“If you look at what we have been doing with rate freezes, with benefits, with how we are providing benefits from growth to customers — existing customers — as well as to the communities, we think that’s a strong platform to stand on,” he said.

Since the data center boom in Georgia began, Georgia Power has maintained that the increased revenue would more than pay for the new infrastructure to serve them, which the utility argues would keep rates lower for residential and small business customers. The utility calls this “downward pressure.” It doesn’t mean rates will go down, but rather that any increases would be offset by the influx of revenue from data centers.

The utility recently doubled down on that promise as part of its deal to serve a massive new OpenAI data center. The deal, Georgia Power said, would bring $15 of not savings but downward pressure per month for what Georgia Power calls the “typical” residential customer.

Separately, Georgia Power also credited the increased revenue from data centers for the temporary rate freeze approved last year. But the rate freeze received criticism from some advocacy groups. Instead of first holding a PSC hearing to give advocates a chance to verify the utility’s financial claims, Georgia Power announced the temporary rate freeze. Advocates feel that the backward process left them unable to confirm whether the company was delivering on its promises and fully shielding ordinary customers from data center costs.

It’s part of a broader criticism often leveled at the PSC and Georgia Power: lack of transparency. The OpenAI contract was entirely redacted, and only a summary provided to the public after it was approved. Specific details involved in service contracts, deals to buy energy, and construction costs are often redacted from the utility’s public filings under the auspices of trade secrecy.

“I absolutely think transparency is potentially one of the biggest impacts here,” said Phillips of the election results. “It has been often on advocates to push on the way that the PSC communicates about the decisions that it’s making, and I’m hoping that there will be more clarity and transparency.”

The Democrats on the commission have promised to bring increased scrutiny to the next rate-setting process, due in 2028, along with Georgia Power’s long-term plans to make and deliver energy, its forecasts about rising demand, and other issues. Hubbard has also proposed conducting a rate case for the state’s natural gas utilities, which he said hasn’t been done in over a decade. And he said they’ll push for other changes going forward.

“We’ll continue with an agenda next year and the year after that to take advantage of this four-year period when we have the majority and can push for long-lasting reforms and shifting the direction of the state in terms of energy,” Hubbard said.

‘Everyone knows why we lost,’ JD Vance points to the Iran war — which has already cost at least $38 billion and 19 American lives

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

While votes were still being counted in the U.S. midterm elections, administration figures in Washington were already inviting speculation over how the results might be connected to the conflict with Iran.

On the morning after the election, Vice President JD Vance wrote on X that the major reason for the dramatic loss for Republicans was the American public’s opposition to the war. “Everyone knows why we lost. And any honest conversation about where we go from here must start there,” he wrote, in reference to the ongoing conflict that began with U.S.-Israeli airstrikes against Iran in late February 2026.

While it is rare for a vice president to make such a critical proclamation about their own administration’s policy, President Donald Trump later stated that he did not disagree with Vance’s appraisal of the role that frustration with the Iran war played in voters’ minds.

But whether this changes Trump’s view on next steps in the ongoing conflict remains unclear. Reporting over the past few weeks has indicated that the U.S. military has been actively preparing options and increasing capabilities in the Persian Gulf region in the event the president directs a resumption of hostilities.

As an expert on Middle East affairs and a former senior official at the National Security Council during the first Trump administration, I’m aware that months of conflict have failed to bring Iran to its knees, force regime change or militarily defeat a perceived threat to U.S. assets and allies. Escalating now is unlikely to change those realities – and it carries the additional risk of further damaging Republicans in the eyes of Americans voters as they turn toward the 2028 presidential race.

Even if Trump were to ratchet up the conflict, moreover, it’s unclear how much wiggle room he’ll have come January facing a Democratic Congress almost uniformly opposed to the war.

Part of the issue facing Iran war hawks in Washington now is that the conflict has caused serious setbacks for the regime in Tehran without satisfying the officially stated strategic reasons for going to war.

They argue that pulling back now – as welcome as that might be for Americans struggling with the associated rising cost of fuel – may allow Iran to rebuild before the conflict is “won.” Moreover, it would render the military and reputational costs already sunk by Washington as wasted.

Among the achievements of this war touted by the Trump administration has been the death of former Supreme Leader Ali Khameini, along with the killing of scores of senior leaders within the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps – including individuals like the former head of Iran’s National Security Council Ali Larijani. Significant elements of Iran’s air defense and radar network, ballistic missile and drone production, and storage and launch sites have also been destroyed. And Iran’s already outdated navy and air force has been further degraded.

Meanwhile, the U.S.-imposed naval blockade against Iranian-linked shipping in the Persian Gulf cut off a crucial lifeline for Iran’s economy. And additional sanctions and economic penalties imposed by Washington have served to further devalue Iran’s currency and deprive Iran of additional financial links to the international banking system.

Yet for all the U.S. successes in degrading the Iranian military by limitless aerial bombardment and the significant financial pressure against the Iranian economy, an obvious endgame remains elusive.

Despite weakening the Iranian government – now led by Ali Khameinei’s son, Mojtaba, and a new echelon of senior IRGC commanders – the Islamic Republic remains intact. Moreover, it still possesses enough ballistic missiles and drones to hold the region at risk, while continuing to impose significant control over its population.

Meanwhile, U.S. military spending continues to rise. In September, the U.S. Congressional Budget Office assessed that the military costs of the Iran War had already totaled US$38 billion. Since then, that number has only increased. And that is to say nothing of the associated global economic costs that have resulted from Iran’s de facto closure of the Strait of Hormuz – a key shipping route for oil and gas.

Should Trump decide to change course on Iran, he – and others – may think that the money, effort and lives of 19 American servicemen killed in the conflict would have been spent with no obvious objective achieved. Yet continued military expenditure with no strategic endgame could only magnify those same costs.

To Iran, the midterm results have not changed its public position. Mojtaba Khameini has issued statements directing the IRGC to widen the conflict in the region and perhaps beyond should the U.S. and Israel attack again. At the same time, a statement attributed to him indicates that hostilities between the United States and Iran will persist regardless of the balance of party political power in Washington.

This raises questions about the prospects for diplomacy. During the summer, the U.S. and Iran agreed to an initial 14-point memorandum of understanding that, if finalized, could have brought a permanent end to the hostilities. But since the 60-day clock on that agreement lapsed, no parallel diplomatic agreement has emerged.

Even with the major shift in the U.S. political landscape, it remains unclear whether Trump will resume operations against Iran before the new Congress is sworn in. The messy election result in Israel – which has left the pro-war, and potentially outgoing, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyanu with potentially more reason to double-down in Iran – only complicates matters.

The U.S. military certainly appears to be ready to engage in a new round of attacks against Iran if so directed, and Iran is ready to retaliate. But given the shortcomings in the U.S. inventory – with munitions and missile and air defense capabilities depleted by eight months of war – escalation is something Trump and his inner circle will have to consider with caution.

Iran might force the issue by engaging in asymmetric attacks in the region or beyond that they have not attempted already. Or it could launch a coordinated offensive with its partners in the region – notably the Houthis in Yemen, who have become more active in their own conflict with Saudi Arabia in recent months.

But save an event that the Trump administration perceives is giving it cover to act, there are no clear indications emerging from Washington as to which way it will move.

Vance diagnosed a widely perceived political problem for Republicans: Voters don’t want more war. But what he noticeably didn’t mention was a practical solution. The Middle East conflict – and the United States’ role in it – are, as such, locked in a stalemate.

America and Iran have both borne significant political, military and economic costs. A decision not to pursue an endgame would leave those sunk; doing so may only increase them.

Javed Ali, Associate Professor of Practice of Public Policy, University of Michigan

Stylist Soki Mak on 10 Things That Inspired Kim Kardashian’s <i><em>All’s Fair</em></i> Season Two Wardrobe

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

From 1990s Helmut Newton shoots to classic Bond girls to unexpected Comme des Garçons finds

In fashion, anything from niche archival research to a favorite film to an outfit you saw on your morning commute can make for inspiration. In our new series 10 Things, we ask industry professionals and tastemakers to divulge the (you guessed it) 10 things that had the most impact on their aesthetic choices.

The second season of Ryan Murphy’s legal drama, All’s Fair, opened with reality star-turned-actor Kim Kardashian in a patchwork Alexander McQueen suit. The rare ensemble, composed of a peplum jacket and fluted skirt, came from Lee McQueen’s theatrical Spring 2004 collection, titled Deliverance, in which models danced down the runway in shimmering gowns, dramatic feathered skirts, and tulle galore. “There were comments about Kim’s clothing being inappropriate in the workplace, but that’s what it should be,” Soki Mak, the visionary behind Kardashian’s Allura Grant character, told Harper’s Bazaar. “If I’m going to watch a show, I want to be like, ‘What the hell is that?’”

When Mak first joined the All’s Fair team—handpicked by Kardashian, via Instagram, to dress her onscreen as a high-powered lawyer—she had never ventured into costume design. The stylist, who has worked with everyone from Rosalía to Pamela Anderson, spent the first season developing the visual identity of Allura, merging her client’s own flair for shock value and fantasy with the limited character descriptions she received. We saw hyper-cinched waists and archival Jean Paul Gaultier; Hermès Birkins and suits with exposed thongs. It was Kardashian, with a twist.

But for Season Two, Mak focused on separating Kardashian from her character, tracking Allura’s emotional journey through darker color palettes and less expected silhouettes. She dug deeper into designer archives, emerging with moody gems from some of the most beloved collections of the ’90s and early-aughts. Rare vintage pieces from Maison Margiela, Comme des Garçons, Yohji Yamamoto, and Alaïa each made their cameos.

Ahead, the stylist dives into 10 influences that shaped Allura’s costume design, from Kim Kardashian herself to classic Bond girls to a baker-boy cap she had to fight for.

[For Season One,] no one gave us any sort of visual references. I’d never been a costume designer before, but usually, when I’ve worked on character-based stuff—I used to work a lot with Nadia Lee Cohen—we would develop the character first, so when I go into styling, it’s quite easy to visualize that person. There was no time for that, and there was no visual reference for Allura. So, when I came in, I was just thinking, “Okay, what do I see her as?” I looked at Kim personally, and she’s this fantasy. There is no one like her. So I’m like, “Okay, if you have all of the money in the world and all of the access in the world, all the power in the world, what would you look like?”

I’ve always thought of Kim as someone that creates and shocks and is the first to do something, whether people like it or not. And that’s the energy I wanted to go into this with. She challenges the norm. I’ve also always been a big believer in trying to be a trendsetter rather than a follower. In some of these moments, I would find it difficult to make a mood board because the references weren’t there. That was where I would say to Kim, “Trust me.”

For Season One, we started reaching out to brands, and Jean Paul Gaultier was [the first to say], “Let’s do it.” The minute we had them, the whole narrative of the show changed. They went deep into the archives and started sending these looks that hadn’t been worn in 20 years. It was amazing. We were sourcing vintage and archival pieces on the side, but we really focused on Jean Paul Gaultier because everything else compared to that just looked sad.

When the images started circulating from the first season, a critic that I respect wrote that the fashion was “kind of obvious.” Although I disagreed, I understood where he was coming from. Maybe Kim wearing a Mugler suit that accentuated her body and her curves was something we had seen her wear before. This season, I wanted to do something really different. I wanted to go less out-there with the colors and a little less predictable with the shapes and the styling. I picked designers and pieces that weren’t obvious choices, and I think it showed growth from last season. We took it somewhere else completely.

For hair and makeup references, I would go on Pinterest and pull old Helmut Newton shoots because I wanted it to look very timeless. I think that was the key. Everything looked … not minimal, there was definitely no minimal… but very ’90s-based and gorgeous.

Kim loves Madonna. There were a lot of old Madonna images for her platinum hair and makeup, a lot of which was done by Pat McGrath.

I think I’d made a point this year that I wanted to take it down a dark route. I almost wanted Allura to be like, “I just had a shit experience in the first season My man’s left me. I’m pissed off. I’ve gone away, and I’ve come back, and I’m serious now.” So everything’s much more covered up. There are darker layers. She’s experimenting. When I was going through the [recent] runway shows, everything was very pink and quite eclectic, so we mainly went with archival pieces. I used a lot of really rare Yohji Yamamoto, Comme des Garçons, and Lee McQueen.

There was a Margiela skirt suit [from the Spring 1994 collection, Retrospective] and it was just very kitsch. It was a long skirt with a suit jacket and was paired with a black leather baker boy hat from Hermès. We were in a bidding war with a collector in Japan. When we were filming the scene, someone in production or maybe Ryan [Murphy] had mentioned they wanted to tone down the hats for this season. But I got that memo after styling the look, and I was adamant that the look needed the hat. I begged. And then she sat at the table wearing a hat and I could see everyone was like, “How is she getting to wear a hat?” I just felt like the look needed it. It took [Kardashian] out of her normal visual stance and how she usually portrays herself.

Comme des Garçons is very destructive and not really tailored to fit a woman’s body in the way [Kardashian] usually likes. And we got her in [a gray jacket and long lilac-gray column skirt] and worked it around her body. She never once made any comments. She would just look at herself for a second and be like, “Cool.” And then go on set.

I have to let Kris Jenner take credit for [Elizabeth Taylor’s 1969 Scorpio cape, worn by Kardashian in Episode 1], because that’s her cape. She would come to set all the time and once had a conversation with Ryan about the fact that she has this cape. He was like, “That’s it, we need it.” From there, we shipped in a $10 million diamond that was most similar to what Elizabeth Taylor was wearing [in original imagery]. Here's How the Blue Tsunami Will Turn the Healthcare Tide

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

— The Democrats have an opportunity to advance public health, but getting things done won't be easy

Hubris played such a central role in the 2026 midterm election that the ancient Greeks would surely have loved it.

The midterms featured a mortal who promised to avoid war, lower prices, expel immigrants who had committed crimes, and bring prosperity to his nation. He proudly insisted that he -- and only he -- knew how to achieve these Herculean goals. He bragged about the cleverness of his methods and his many (supposed) successes.

All the while, he sowed chaos and distrust, ignored human needs, blamed others for his failures, used excessive force, alienated friends, embraced dictators, and hurt the economy. A survey showed that more than six in 10 of his countrymen said he prioritized his own interests above those of his country.

On November 3, 2026, tens of millions of people rose up to punish his extreme pride, dangerous overconfidence, and arrogance that have, in one way or another, affected them negatively. Healthcare issues were certainly among the grievances.

Now that the blue tsunami has changed the playing field, Democrats must deliver. They need to achieve something concrete over the next 2 years, and they must prioritize the issues, such as healthcare, that were salient to voters in this election cycle. Otherwise, they risk being punished for their own ineptitude and hubris.

Democrats will quickly learn that getting something done will be harder than they imagine. Here are some of the health policy challenges Democrats, Republicans, and President Trump will face in 2027 and 2028.

Between the election and the swearing-in of a new Congress on January 3, 2027, the old Congress will be in a lame duck session. These periods tend to promise a lot and then deliver extraordinarily little. Beyond appropriations, defense reauthorization, and perhaps some renewed efforts to end the war in Iran, I expect little to nothing else, including on healthcare.

During a lame-duck session, nominations and legislation go nowhere unless they have broad bipartisan support and members of Congress feel some urgency. Even then, agreed-upon compromises may not survive procedural objections, time constraints, or general indifference from departing Congress members who want to go home. Simply put, those who have retired or lost their races are deeply conscious of their lame-duck status.

In prior MedPage Today editorials, I pointed out that cabinet secretaries often leave after 2 years, but that Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. would likely stay. After all, Trump has continued to support Kennedy, and Kennedy has continued to have the opportunity of a lifetime to advance his Make America Healthy Again agenda and anti-vaccine stance.

Post-election, the job will quickly become a nightmare for Kennedy, as he will have to respond to hostile investigative hearings led by Democrats on the health committees. And Kennedy is unlikely to get White House help: Trump will have bigger fights to address and less political capital to spend.

Also, if impeachment hearings were to start, Kennedy would be behind only Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth as the object of Congressional attention. This situation will evolve, but I suspect Kennedy will leave (resign) rather than face congressional challenges to his own hubris.

HHS Leadership and Senate Confirmation Hearings

Controversial nominations rarely advance during a lame-duck session. Once the new Congress starts, all past nominations expire, and the White House must start anew. They are not obligated to submit the same names.

Given the White House affiliation with Heidi Overton, MD, PhD, and her weaker credentials compared to her predecessors, I see no way senators would confirm her as FDA commissioner in the lame-duck session. Obviously, her odds would be even worse next year with a Democratic-led Senate. Kennedy, or perhaps his successor, will need to look elsewhere.

Hypothetically, Overton could be renominated solely to trigger provisions in law that would allow Kyle Diamantas, JD, to continue as acting commissioner in 2027. But more likely, another nominee will emerge.

Investigations, Vetoes, Riders, and Compromises

Investigations are the classic response to leading one or both houses of Congress when the President is of the other party. There will be multiple hearings on the administration's opposition to vaccines; the first one will probably focus on measles.

Of course, the new Democratic majority has other options to advance its policy agenda:

As an example of advancing policy via riders, let's look at one of those must-pass bills: FDA user fee legislation. The bill renewal comes with a tradition of serving as a vehicle for non-user fee issues. If it isn't enacted sooner, I would expect efforts like the Clinical Trial Modernization Act to be added to the reauthorization bill. The FDA user fee legislation must become law by September 30, but ideally should be completed by June or July. Perfect timing.

Of course, it is too soon for anyone to know the Democratic strategy because it hasn't been decided. My guess: it will turn out to be a combination of approaches.

Despite the threat of Trump vetoes, Democrats will have the opportunity to advance two of their healthcare priorities in 2027: undoing Medicaid program cuts and restoring Obamacare insurance premium subsidies.

The easy part is creating a reconciliation bill, so that only 51 votes will be needed for Senate passage.

The harder part is finding $1.2 trillion over 10 years to pay for the program restorations. In the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), these cuts already offset the cost of the OBBBA tax cuts.

Democrats will have to find that money through program savings, tax increases, or by admitting they are adding to the deficit. Despite Democratic enthusiasm for action early next year, I don't see how they can do that quickly or easily. They will need to build a consensus on the offsets first.

Many of the newly elected House Democrats from red, pink, and purple states will not be reelected in 2028 if Democrats focus on a dramatic, expensive new federal program instead of solving the issues at the heart of the midterm elections. Those Democrats outnumber the enlarged and noisy new influx of progressives pushing Medicare for All.

Polls suggest two-thirds of voters support "Medicare for All." However, support is cut in half when the issue of tax increases is raised. In my opinion, advocates would best serve their cause by focusing first on a smaller Medicare expansion, such as a buy-in option for individuals between 55 and 65.

My larger concern is that Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.), likely the next chairman of the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor & Pensions and a passionate supporter of Medicare for All, will put a spotlight on this issue, and give lesser attention and support to other health programs that have a more realistic chance of moving forward over the next 2 years.

Not long ago, a broad swath of health issues was bipartisan and rarely controversial or challenged: vaccines, an effective CDC, NIH funding, the value of research universities, and a strong FDA. While some resistance may linger, I believe these will be bipartisan again by the next election cycle, if not sooner. That is a huge win for public health and for all Americans.

Justin Sun Warns Quantum Threat Could Hit Crypto Within 2 Years as TRON Pursues Resistance

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Quantum computing is advancing faster than many expect, TRON CEO Justin Sun says. While most people believe quantum computing is still far away, Justin Sun today said crypto attacks are looming.

In an interview organized by FINTECH.TV, Justin Sun said the dangers posed by quantum computers are real and could affect cryptocurrencies within the next year or two. The TRON CEO anticipates that breakthroughs in quantum computing infrastructure are making machines more powerful. This means computers’ ability to break cryptographic systems is becoming more imminent.

The executive recognized potential dangers to public-key cryptography, the backbone of blockchain networks. Crypto networks such as Bitcoin, Ethereum, and many others rely on cryptographic signatures like ECDSA, which are more vulnerable. According to Sun, advancing, powerful, fault-tolerant quantum computers could soon use Shor’s algorithm to break cryptographic signatures more easily than people think.

Currently, most crypto assets are kept in addresses with exposed public keys, putting them at risk of quantum computer attacks. Sun warns the imminent threat is likely within two years, as attackers can collect encrypted data now and decrypt it as quantum computing technology advances.

Today’s quantum computers can’t break cryptographic networks. However, the threat is growing as the technology becomes more powerful. This explains why Justin Sun said TRON is working on post-quantum cryptography.

Last month, the network announced that post-quantum cryptography is available on its testnet. The development marked a significant step toward preparing its infrastructure for potential advances in quantum computing.

This commitment matters because experts project that quantum computing could eventually overpower some of the cryptographic methods currently used to safeguard crypto assets. Post-quantum cryptography aims to build security controls created to resist attacks from powerful quantum computers.
