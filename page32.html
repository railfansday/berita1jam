Q: “My Spouse Complains About My ADHD Constantly!”

“Start with an honest conversation that acknowledges the effects of your ADHD on your partner and explains how it thwarts your efforts to be the best possible spouse .”

Q: “How can I help my husband understand my ADHD so he’s not just complaining all the time about how hard this is for him?”

First, begin by acknowledging the truth of what he is saying: Your ADHD does, indeed, have tangible effects on him. It also has emotional effects; he likely burns much more mental energy than you can see worrying about whether you completed agreed-upon tasks.

Let down your guard for an honest conversation about the impact of your actions (or inaction) on him. Show him that he matters to you and that you empathize with his experience. You may need to spend significant time here; don’t move on until you’re positive that he really feels that you get it.

This sets the stage for the second part of the conversation — how ADHD thwarts your efforts to be the best possible partner. In other words, you have good intentions, even if they aren’t obvious, and you can feel equally frustrated and disappointed by what makes him upset. Also, your ADHD makes your life harder, not just his. While his complaints might be accurate, his too-frequent criticisms are not helping.

Finally, discuss how you can work together to address your hotspots. Ask: What can each of us do, or at least try to do, differently to smooth out some of our pain points? For example, do you want reminders? If so, how and when? Also, does he want to give those reminders?

It’s also helpful to discuss protocols when things don’t go according to plan. How and when can he bring up his concerns in a way that you can hear without getting defensive? How can you tell him that his proposed solution is not working for you?

Encourage your partner to focus on their experience rather than dissecting your actions and making declarations about why you did something.

For example, it’s more effective to say, “It stresses me out when you don’t follow through on commitments because then I feel like I have to take over, and I really don’t want to keep doing that,” versus saying, “You don’t worry about doing things because you know I’ll just do it if you don’t.”

Toxic secrets are hiding in Arctic ice

A century-old pollution trail leads scientists back to Montana mining

At the turn of the twentieth century, the Anaconda Smelter in Montana spewed arsenic into the air — those heavy metal particles that are now trapped in Arctic ice cores.

Ice cores record Earth’s secrets, even the dirty ones.

Some of the cylindrical sections of ice extracted from the Arctic contain elevated levels of arsenic, lead and other heavy metals that circulated in our planet’s atmosphere in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Those pollutants, which have been tied to declines in IQ and higher rates of certain cancers, were probably emitted by a mining and smelting company in Montana whose grounds are now designated as multiple Superfund sites, the researchers report November 3 in Nature Sustainability.

The ice cores contain tiny particles that were once airborne — including pollutants, volcanic ash and salt from ocean spray — and fell to our planet’s surface within a few days or weeks. In polar regions, that fallout combines with snow and is preserved as ice.

Joseph McConnell, an environmental scientist at the Desert Research Institute, and his collaborators recently analyzed 10 ice cores collected across Canada, Greenland and Russia. The cores contained material that had fallen to Earth as early as 210 BCE, but the interval spanning the Industrial Revolution in the United States — from roughly 1870 to 1914 — was what caught the team’s collective eye.

In the ice layers from that period, arsenic concentrations in some of the ice cores were up to 15 times higher than pre-Industrial Revolution levels. Because the heavy metal exposure has been tied to upticks in the rates of certain cancers, McConnell and his team set out to determine the most likely source of all that pollution.

The largest increases in arsenic existed in the ice cores from Greenland. That finding was a big hint, McConnell says, because atmospheric circulation patterns tend to move material from west to east. “The only way you can explain that pattern of deposition is if the emissions are coming from North America,” McConnell says. “Had it been from Europe, we would have seen much larger increases in the Russian Arctic.”

The most likely source, the team concluded, was a massive copper mining and smelting operation in southwestern Montana operated by the Anaconda Copper Mining Company. It was one of the largest in North America during the Industrial Revolution and, unlike other copper mining operations in the area, it worked with copper ore that contained heavy metals like arsenic and lead. It was no secret that those toxic metals were entering the atmosphere, says Fred Quivik, a historian and the author of Smoke and Tailings, a book about the environmental history of copper smelting in Montana. “Ranchers and farmers started seeing their livestock die from arsenic poisoning.”

Based on the concentrations of arsenic in the ice cores and models of how material disperses in the atmosphere, McConnell and his colleagues estimated that the Anaconda Copper Mining Company was emitting roughly 30 tons of arsenic per day into the atmosphere in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. By comparison, Chile, currently the world’s largest producer of copper and home to more than 20 mining operations, collectively emits roughly one quarter as much arsenic today.

Beginning in 1919, the Anaconda Copper Mining Company installed devices near its smokestacks and other facilities to capture arsenic and prevent its release into the atmosphere. Arsenic emissions dropped by about 60 percent as a result, the ice core data showed, but by then hundreds of thousands of nearby residents had already been exposed for decades. That exposure could have been lessened, McConnell says, if the Anaconda Copper Mining Company hadn’t waited so long to install the pollution-abatement technology. “They delayed installation for about a decade.”

These new findings should be combined with census records to trace the effects of this pollution on human health in the 19th and 20th centuries, McConnell and his team suggest. “We hope that epidemiologists will take this information and really investigate potential impacts on human populations.”

Announcing Long-Context Training on TPUs with MaxText Context Parallelism

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Long-context training — including pretraining and post-training, has become a foundation of frontier AI models. Longer native sequence lengths give large language models (LLMs) the working memory to read a whole code repository or ingest hours of video, and reason through multi-step agentic tasks. In three years, context windows have grown from 128K–200K tokens to 1M–2M, and models trained toward 10M tokens are arriving. Training at those lengths, not just serving them, is where the system limits show up.

In particular, efficient training at multi-million-token sequence lengths runs into the following bottlenecks:

Today, we are excited to announce long-context training support in MaxText, Google Cloud's open-source JAX training framework. MaxText enables this by supporting four context-parallel strategies on Cloud TPU: All-gather, A2A (DeepSpeed Ulysses), P2P (Ring Attention), and the A2A + P2P hybrid (Unified Sequence Parallelism, or USP), combined with load balancing and sequence packing. All four use the Tokamax Splash Attention kernel. Combined with the 3D inter-chip interconnect (ICI) ( supported on TPUs such as TPU 5p or TPU 7 (Ironwood) ), this lets one sequence span an entire slice while communication overlaps compute and memory per chip stays flat. In our benchmarks, Llama 3.1 8B (25 head dim, 16 query heads) demonstrates exceptional long-context efficiency, achieving 70% Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) on TPU 5p for sequence lengths up to 4 million tokens. Across both TPU 5p and TPU 7, context can be seamlessly extended to 14 million tokens—with TPU 7 consistently maintaining 50% MFU across all sequence scales below 6 million tokens.

This functionality is available today in MaxText v0.2.4(doc).

Context parallelism splits a long sequence across chips, so no chip holds the activations of the whole sequence. Context parallelism can be combined with data and tensor parallelism, and the smallest context-parallel size that fits your sequence in HBM is usually the right one. This also holds for sparser attention variants which cut attention compute but not the activations of every other layer.

Attention is the one global operation where every token needs the keys and values of every preceding token, so each context parallel strategy has to bring those keys and values back together. Each makes a different tradeoff between memory (how much KV a chip holds), communication (how much moves per layer, and whether it overlaps compute), and model shape (how many attention heads to split).

All-gather: Each chip keeps its chunk of the sequence and, at every layer, gathers the keys and values from every other chip. It is the simplest option. However, because causal masking causes later tokens to attend to larger prefixes, All-gather requires balancing compute load across devices.

All-to-All (Ulysses): Gathering keys and values makes every chip hold the whole sequence, so attention memory never shrinks no matter how many chips you add. All-to-All changes what each chip owns instead. Attention is independent across heads but not across tokens, so the head axis is free to shard and the token axis is not. An All-to-All before attention rotates the sharding from one onto the other: each chip gives up the heads it is not responsible for and receives the tokens it was missing for the heads it keeps, ending up with the full sequence for a few heads instead of a few tokens for all heads. This is also why Ulysses does not need token load balancing. Attention then runs as ordinary single-chip attention with standard kernels, and a second All-to-All rotates the output back. KV per chip shrinks by the context-parallel factor, but you cannot split across more chips than the model has heads — and tensor parallelism shards that same dimension, so TP × CP is capped by the head count (under GQA, by the KV heads: eight for Llama 3 8B). The all-to-all itself, though, does not overlap with compute — attention cannot start on a partial sequence, so every chip waits for data from every other chip before any of them begins.

P2P (ring attention): Ulysses' ceiling is the head count; ring attention removes it by never leaving the token axis. Keys and values never materialize in full. KV chunks travel around a ring of chips one hop at a time, neighbor to neighbor. Each chip attends its queries to the chunk it holds while the next one is in flight, and merges the partial results as it goes. A chip holds its own chunk and one visiting chunk, never the full sequence, and the communication overlaps compute. However, dividing attention computation into smaller chunks can reduce local kernel efficiency, and unlike All-to-All Ulysses, ring attention requires explicit load balancing.

A2A + P2P (USP): A2A + P2P (USP): Neither strategy scales on its own — All-to-All stops at the head count, and a long ring accumulates hops while thinning the overlap that makes it work. Unified sequence parallelism composes them on two axes of the chip mesh, so the context-parallel degree becomes the product of the two: All-to-All along one axis, sized at or below the KV-head count, and a ring along the other to extend past it. Because each All-to-All group has already pooled its tokens, a ring step now carries a chunk larger by the All-to-All factor, and the ring is correspondingly shorter — more compute per hop to hide the transfer behind, and fewer hops to accumulate latency and causal imbalance. On Cloud TPU the two map onto two axes of the slice's chip mesh.

These strategies cover the attention variants in today's major models, from multi-head and grouped-query attention to DeepSeek-style multi-head latent attention.

Splitting the sequence only pays off if the attention compute on each chip stays efficient. All four strategies run on Splash Attention, the block-sparse flash attention kernel from Tokamax, Google's open-source library of TPU kernels. It skips fully masked blocks and handles packed sequences without padding. P2P calls it once per ring step, on the local queries and the KV chunk that has just arrived, while the transfer for the next step runs underneath, in the forward and backward pass alike. Each chip does the same fused, block-level work it would do without context parallelism, with the communication hidden behind it.

P2P (Ring Attention) is the primary workhorse for very long-context training, whereas All-Gather is the standard baseline for most other cases where the KV can fit. These are the primary CP strategies to use in practice. We compare how far each can push context length on a fixed 64-chip TPU 5p slice with Llama 3.1 8B (256 head dim, 16 query heads) , and measure their MFUs. In ideal settings where both strategies can have large enough chunks (S/CP) and local batch size - the two major variables driving MFU on TPU 5p, we observe that P2P can outperform All-Gather almost every context length from 64K to 14M tokens, where communication becomes mostly hidden with the Ring circular transport schedule.

TPU v7x (Ironwood) takes the same recipe further. With twice the HBM per chip, P2P training of 14M tokens on 64 TPU 7 chips reached 48.7% Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) compared to 37.1% on TPU 5p. We observed that ring attention performs better than other context parallelism strategies -- e.g. Ulysses and USP –- on TPU 7 chips.

For all CP strategies, when you run low on memory: Offload before you recompute. MaxText can move saved activations, optimizer states, and parameters to host memory tensor by tensor. At long context lengths, these transfers are hidden behind compute, making offloading effectively free. Offload attention outputs first, followed by optimizer states, layer inputs, parameters, and finally remat as a last resort. MFU stays flat through the offload steps while all-gather falls off and eventually stops fitting. For more details, see the remat tuning guide.

Looking ahead, we see two natural extensions. The first is broader model coverage. Models that mix sliding-window and global layers, such as Gemma, would run the window layers with a short exchange between neighboring chips while the global layers use the ring. We also plan to support linear attention mechanisms such as Gated Delta Net (GDN) and Kimi Delta Attention (KDA). The second is performance, with further tuning of the Tokamax kernels and the P2P backward pass to narrow the remaining gap to all-gather at short context, and recipes by model and context length for TPU 5p and 7 chips.

What is Pathways? Single-controller training for large models

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Today, we announce the General Availability of Pathways on Google Cloud Platform. In this post, you will learn about Pathways, the distributed system Google built to train its own large models, including Gemini. We discuss how Pathways addresses the coordination challenge by introducing one client that describes the whole computation as a graph, and how the distributed runtime places it, moves the tensors, and keeps the accelerators busy.

Most large-model training runs as Single Program, Multiple Data (SPMD) programs. Every accelerator runs the same program in lockstep and coordinates through collectives like AllReduce.

It works well when your job is the same program running on every device. It struggles when the job is no longer uniform, like a pipeline whose stages wait on each other or an RL loop where a sampler, a reward model, and a trainer are different programs that have to overlap.

Large runs also have to survive hardware failures, which interrupted Gemini 2.5 training multiple times per hour. In SPMD, one lost machine stops every process and sends the run back to its last checkpoint. In each case, the challenge is coordination. You have to decide what runs where and in what order, how tensors move between devices, and how the job keeps going when a machine drops out.

In this post of the series “Pathways on Google Cloud”, you will learn about Pathways, the distributed system Google built to train its own large models, including Gemini. The post discusses how Pathways addresses the coordination challenge by introducing one client that describes the whole computation as a graph, and how the distributed runtime places it, moves the tensors, and keeps the accelerators busy.

By the end, you'll know where SPMD is suboptimal, what single-controller execution changes, and how Pathways coordinates a whole training job across a cluster.

If you train PyTorch models, you almost certainly use Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) or Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP). DDP runs a copy of your script on every accelerator and averages gradients with a collective operation such as AllReduce. FSDP shards parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across workers so that the model fits at all.

DDP and FSDP both follow the Single Program, Multiple Data (SPMD) execution model. Every process runs the same copy of your script, owns its accelerators for the whole run, and moves in lockstep with the others, because the only way they coordinate is through collectives like AllReduce. Inside one tightly connected group of accelerators those collectives run over a fast interconnect and are cheap. Scale the job across groups and the same collectives cross the datacenter network, which is far slower. On a TPU v5p, for example, each chip gets 1,200 GB/s of bidirectional chip-to-chip bandwidth inside its group, but only 50 Gb/s (gigabits, about 6 GB/s) to the datacenter network which is roughly two orders of magnitude less.

And because every step blocks on its collectives, that communication lands directly in step time. The more groups you span, the larger the share of each step spent moving data, so throughput grows slower than the number of accelerators you add.

As long as your training job is one program that fills the hardware you give it, this execution model fits well. You write your model as if there were one device, and the collectives do the rest. But fitting a model into memory is only part of the problem. Keeping every accelerator busy is the harder part, and this is where the SPMD model starts to show its limits.

Take pipeline parallelism as an example. You split a model into blocks across devices, and each device now runs different code over smaller micro-batches. Activations flow forward, activation gradients flow back, and while device 0 works on the first micro-batch, devices 1 and 2 wait. Apart from the idle accelerators you keep paying for, this drops utilization and throughput, even though every device has work in the steady state.

Another example is RL post-training for an LLM. You have a sampler that generates rollouts, a reward model that scores them, and a trainer that updates the policy and sends the new weights back to the sampler. These are three different programs that should overlap; otherwise the sampler is idle while the trainer runs, and the trainer is idle while the sampler generates.

In both scenarios, the job is now several different programs running on different devices, and their dependencies can no longer be managed through a single collective. You now have to coordinate which devices share which tensors, which processes run while others are still finishing, and how early to enqueue the next buffer so an accelerator never waits for input. Because each worker only has visibility into its local devices and data, no individual process can manage all of these dependencies itself. That has several consequences.

First, because each process owns its accelerators for the whole run and every collective blocks until all devices finish, one failed machine prevents the collective from completing and stops the entire job, forcing a restart from the last checkpoint. You cannot add devices mid-run, and you cannot give devices back.

Second, because each process holds its accelerators from start to finish, capacity can't move to where it's needed. Within one job, a stage that needs fewer chips than another still keeps the extra ones. They sit idle, you keep paying for them, and no other part of the job can use them until the run ends. Across a team, the same happens at cluster scale. Every job owns its accelerators for as long as it runs, so sharing a cluster means splitting it into per-job allocations, and each new job brings up its own processes on its own hosts.

Finally, the development process itself gets harder. On one host you iterate fast because you run the script, drop into a Python shell, set a breakpoint, and debug. This is not true once the job spans many hosts. You need a launcher to push your script to every host, you hit coordination bugs that never showed up on one machine, and there's no single process to break into. When something hangs, each worker is stuck at a collective waiting for the others, with no one place that shows the whole job.

So the common challenge here is the coordination itself. As soon as the job stops being one program in lockstep, you have to decide what runs where, in what order, and how tensors get from one device to the next.

One way to describe this is as a graph, where each block is a node, each tensor that moves between blocks is an edge, and the devices are where the nodes are placed. You can wire that graph up by hand in SPMD, one process at a time. But what if a single client declared that graph, including what each block does, what it needs, and where it runs, and a distributed runtime handled the transfers, dependencies, and ordering along its edges?

That is the idea behind Pathways, the distributed system Google built to train its own largest models, including Gemini. Pathways runs JAX programs on TPUs and it is available on Google Cloud.

With Pathways, you define the end-to-end computation from a single Python client, without writing custom coordination code, and a distributed runtime executes it. That client is decoupled from the hardware. It runs on an ordinary CPU machine and you write your program against virtual devices; a resource manager maps them onto real chips, and a worker on each accelerator host runs the compiled programs it receives.

Because your code refers to virtual devices rather than physical ones, the runtime is flexible to allow users to change the devices used for a given computation. This allows you to stay in control while the runtime handles the mapping to hardware. In an RL loop, for example, the trainer and the samplers rarely want the same number of chips, so you can give the sampler stage more chips by asking for more virtual devices.

Also, the same capability helps when a host fails or the hardware isn't ready yet. If a host dies, the resource manager remaps the work onto healthy chips and the run keeps going instead of relaunching the whole job, which is the basis for elastic training. And because the program targets virtual devices, compilation can start before the physical chips are provisioned, so setup overlaps with provisioning instead of waiting on it.

The resource manager owning the chips also means one Pathways cluster can serve several clients, so researchers on a team can share its capacity. With the Shared Pathways Service, the cluster stays up as a long running service, which lets you develop interactively on TPUs hosted on GKE. Each client connects on demand from a laptop or VM, gets its own slices (groups of tightly connected TPU chips) from the shared pool, and hands them back when it disconnects. When every chip is busy, the next workload waits until capacity frees up.

Take the three-block model from Figure 2. Block A runs on one group of devices, block B on a second, block C on a third. You declare what each block computes, its target devices, and where its outputs go. Pathways expresses those dependencies as a distributed dataflow graph, in which each block is a compiled SPMD program and each edge is a tensor the runtime has to deliver.

In a single SPMD program, the framework handles the collectives that keep the devices in sync within a group, with or without Pathways, and your code doesn't change. The difference shows up once the job runs several programs on different groups (Multiple Programs, Multiple Data, MPMD), like these three blocks or the sampler, reward model, and trainer of an RL loop. Then no single process sees both sides (the same limit from before, where each worker only knows its own devices), so getting a tensor from block A to block B is a hand-off you have to coordinate yourself.

Pathways closes that gap. You declare the dependency once, and the runtime moves and reshards the tensor for you, over the fast interconnect within a group and over the datacenter network between groups.

To keep the accelerators busy, Pathways prepares launches ahead of time with futures and parallel asynchronous dispatch. While block A is still computing, block B can't run yet, but its input shapes and dtypes are already known, so the runtime allocates B's buffers and schedules the transfer before A's output even exists. Host-side setup for every block happens up front, and each block launches when its inputs arrive.

This is also what keeps the single client from becoming a bottleneck. Because dispatch is asynchronous, the coordination work overlaps with the compute already running on the chips, so the client stays off the critical path instead of stalling the accelerators between steps.

In Pathways, the runtime decides execution order when a group has more than one operation ready. In pipelined training, B's devices often hold both the forward pass for an upcoming micro-batch and the backward pass for an earlier one. If each device picked on its own, their collectives would conflict and the group would stall.

Pathways has a central scheduler that puts every program's operations into one consistent order, so operations that must communicate start together instead of deadlocking.

And because it's still one client, the development loop we mentioned before comes back. It's the same single Python process whether you drive one host or thousands of hosts, so you can drop into a shell, set a breakpoint, and inspect the run as it goes. When something stalls, there's one place that knows what's queued on every device and which dependency hasn't been met, instead of a hang spread across separate processes.

Put together, Pathways separates model building from execution. You choose how the model partitions across hardware using the client. And the runtime handles the transfers, ordering, and scheduling that keep accelerators busy, across the whole cluster rather than just your slice of it.

Described this way, Pathways can start to sound like an orchestrator you already know. If scheduling work, moving tensors, and keeping a whole cluster busy brings Ray to mind, it should. But the two work at different layers.

Ray is an open-source framework for orchestrating Python-native AI workloads across large clusters. It provisions the cluster, schedules your Python code as tasks and actors, and launches your workload across hosts, holding data loading, training, serving, and tuning under one control plane. To Ray, an accelerator is a resource to schedule.

What Ray doesn't change is how your training runs on the chips. The job inside each worker still runs as SPMD. The framework inserts the collectives, and each process sees only its own devices. Ray places and starts that job but it doesn't turn it into a single program that drives the whole cluster.

That is the layer Pathways works at. It changes the execution model underneath, so one client coordinates every chip. Because they sit at different layers, the two are complementary. In principle, Ray can orchestrate the cluster and the pipeline while Pathways handles how the training step executes.

So concretely, Ray's job is about orchestration while Pathways' job is about execution. With Ray, you tie your data, training, serving, and tuning together and spread heterogeneous work across accelerators and clouds. With Pathways, you run one model across many groups of chips, express patterns beyond SPMD, and recover from a failed host instead of relaunching the whole job.

When a training job stops being one uniform program, the challenge becomes coordinating the dependencies between stages, the tensors that cross the network, and the ordering that keeps every accelerator fed.

SPMD requires you to address these dependencies. With Pathways, you describe the computation once, from a single client, as a graph of blocks and the tensors between them. NCIS has been bringing in new agents for quite some time, yet it still hasn't filled the gap left by a certain character. Cote de Pablo’s contribution as Ziva David dates back to 2013, but her departure has affected the characters around her. Even now, with Michael Weatherly back as Tony DiNozzo in Season 24, the series is finding reasons to bring her up, which is a pretty remarkable achievement for a character who hasn't been a regular cast member in more than a decade.

Ziva has had an unusual introduction to team NCIS. She arrived in the Season 3 premiere, "Kill Ari (Part 1)," after Kate Todd's (Sasha Alexander) death, and she happened to be the half-sister of the man responsible. Ari Haswari (Rudolf Martin) had murdered Kate, and Ziva was the one who ultimately shot him to save Gibbs (Mark Harmon). Understandably, nobody was thrilled to have her around at first. She had to convince an office full of suspicious colleagues that she belonged there, and the show gave her plenty of room to do exactly that.

Plenty of memorable agents have come after Ziva, and they don't need to be carbon copies of her to be worthwhile, but her departure left a specific gap for a reason. Her story connected the procedural's action to its family drama, while her relationships with Gibbs and Tony gave the series two very different emotional threads to follow.

When Ziva joined NCIS, she had already experienced more than most of her fellow employees could imagine. Her father, Eli David (Michael Nouri), headed the Mossad, and she grew up where discipline and responsibility took priority over everything else. Ziva served in the Israeli army before being recruited into the Mossad, lost her youngest sister, Tali, to an explosion, and learned how to survive in a world that demanded she be cruel.

From the start, Ziva clearly had the skills to succeed. She spoke several languages, was skilled at hand-to-hand combat, and performed well even in the most difficult situations, but what made her interesting was that she spent so many years learning to accept violence as a solution to everything that she had to learn how to live without it.

The change became especially important after her capture and torture in Somalia. Gibbs, Tony, and McGee (Sean Murray) helped bring her home in the Season 7 premiere, "Truth or Consequences," but getting her back was only part of the story. Ziva eventually resigned from Mossad and pursued a position at NCIS, choosing to build a life with people who had become her family.

She also had a personality that was entirely her own. Her difficulty with American idioms became a recurring source of comedy, particularly when Tony was around to make things worse. Ziva could be intimidating one minute and completely baffled by an expression the next. De Pablo understood that the humor made her feel like someone you actually wanted to spend time with.

The relationship between Gibbs and Ziva is one of the best examples of how much her character changed after joining NCIS. Their introduction wasn't exactly warm; Gibbs had every reason to question her loyalties, and Ziva wasn't particularly accustomed to explaining herself to anyone. Still, the circumstances surrounding Ari's death gave them a connection that neither could easily dismiss.

One of their earliest defining moments comes in "Kill Ari (Part 2)." After Ziva saves Gibbs by killing her own brother, she visits him in the hospital. When he struggles to remember what happened, she takes his hand and slaps herself with it, prompting him to remember that she saved his life. The moment ends with Ziva breaking down in his arms. It's a rare glimpse of how much the experience has affected her, especially since she's spent so much time trying to appear unaffected.

Their relationship continues to deepen in Season 7's "Good Cop, Bad Cop." After Ziva is cleared in an investigation involving the deaths aboard the Damocles, Gibbs kisses her on the forehead. It is a small gesture, but it matters to anyone with issues around her father. Eventually, Gibbs becomes the person Ziva is comfortable enough to show her vulnerability around, which is something she lacked growing up – unconditional acceptance and belonging.

This will make Season 17 especially significant. Ziva is understandably hurt that Gibbs never searched for her after she was presumed dead, and the two have to confront what that absence meant to them. They have to work through the resentment, which makes their eventual reconciliation feel earned.

When it came to their relationship, Tony and Ziva had chemistry from the moment they began working together, although neither was particularly eager to admit it. Their early dynamic was full of teasing and competitive banter, but Season 3's "Under Covers" gave viewers a memorable glimpse of what lay beneath. The pair go undercover as a couple, complete with a romantic rendezvous, and the episode helped establish the attraction that would become one of the show's longest-running storylines.

What made Tony and Ziva work wasn't constant flirting. They were colleagues who understood each other's instincts, challenged one another, and developed a loyalty that extended well beyond the job. Their relationship took years to evolve, and even when they weren't saying what they felt, the audience could usually tell.

Their eventual daughter, Tali, brought that story into a new chapter. After Ziva was presumed dead following an attack on her farmhouse, Tony left NCIS to raise their daughter. Ziva's return in Season 17 revealed she was alive, allowing the series to revisit a relationship left unresolved for years. The pair eventually reunited in Paris, and their story continued in the Paramount+ spinoff NCIS: Tony & Ziva.

The spinoff's cancellation after one season left Tony and Ziva's story in an uncertain place, but the flagship series hasn't exactly forgotten about them. Tony's return in Season 24 has brought Ziva back into the conversation, even though she isn't appearing on screen. Tony tells McGee and Nick Torres (Wilmer Valderrama) that she's in the Boston area, working at a language institute while keeping an eye on Tali, who has started boarding school nearby. He even suggests she could be available if the team needs her.

Showrunner Steven D. Binder has said Ziva won't appear in Season 24, so that update isn't confirmation of an imminent reunion. Still, it's telling that the show takes the time to establish where she is and what she's doing. Her life remains relevant to Tony's, and Tony's life remains relevant to the show.

Bishop (Emily Wickersham), Knight (Katrina Law), and the agents who followed Ziva have brought their own strengths to NCIS. The series doesn't need another Mossad operative to make the team interesting, and trying to recreate Ziva would probably be a mistake. What it hasn't found is another character whose personal history could influence so many corners of the show at once. Ziva's story was about more than becoming a better agent. It was about a woman trained to serve other people's expectations finally deciding what she wanted for herself. She found a family in the people who once questioned whether she belonged, and built a future that looked nothing like the one her father had planned. NCIS can fill another desk, but it can't manufacture that history overnight, and judging by how often the show still brings Ziva into the conversation, it hasn't stopped trying to find a way to bring her back.

Over the next five years, I would watch three things closely: whether Search maintains its competitive position, whether Google Cloud continues growing profitably, and whether AI investments translate into stronger free cash flow.

In The Ogre’s Mouth — John Muir Trail Day 12

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

I woke up in my little tent spot nice and early, around 5:30 a.m., and then it took me two hours to get on the trail.  During that time the sun appeared to light the mountain on the opposite side of the valley.

Early morning view from my camp site. I needed Geologist Sam to explain the stripes in the rock…

This time the biggest delay was that the bottom of my tent was quite damp, so I spread it out to dry before packing it and heading on my way.

While I was doing all that, along came Sally who greeted me warmly and then walked on by.  Here’s a little more information about Sally that I learned the previous day:  she was born in Canton, and she works in medical research.  The rest I already reported.

By 7:30 a.m. I started off and immediately started down the infamous Golden Staircase.  I could see how it got its name.  Oh my gosh, it was just a straight down drop of short switchbacks back and forth, and back and forth, and back and forth, seemingly endlessly.  It was enough to make your ears pop from the altitude change.  Going down was bad enough, but I really felt sorry for the poor people trying to ascend it!  I could imagine how it might be demoralizing and exhausting as well.

It’s a little hard to see, but there is a trail going down that near-vertical descent!

Finally, I got to the bottom and eventually I even caught up with Sally.  We stopped for a water break together and then I went past her onto what was the nicest, cruisiest stretch since I started the John Muir Trail.

After the river’s steep drop coming down, the landscape leveled out and the trail mostly followed the gentle contour of the river heading due west.  As a smooth quality trail, it meant I could make very good time.  In the lower elevation the lizards reappeared, and I finally got some pictures of them.

As a child in Mexico City I bred lizards, so I have a special place in my heart for them!

I also  took a picture of a deer, some very large pine cones, as well as shots of several waterfalls.

It feels like somehow they know I am not a predator…

Lower elevations also brought much bigger pine cones.

Mostly, though, I kept a fast pace for about seven miles down the Palisade Creek valley to the river juncture with the Middle Fork Kings River, where the trail turned sharply north and started climbing again.

I reached that juncture at about 10:30 where I rested and had a snack.  Along with the food I took my last dose of antibiotics from the hospital.  I had to admit the medication had been highly effective, but not perfectly so.  I slept through the night now but still coughed occasionally.  I was easily 95% better though and grateful for it!

This was also the spot for errands including filtering water, filling my storage containers, and washing my socks and bandanas.  It was a little challenging to do this as the river was flowing swiftly, but I got the errands done.

My pack resting against a log. (I was resting too…)

As I took stock afterwards, however, I came up one sock liner short and assumed it had washed down the river.  Rather than accept its loss, I went back and looked for it.  To my pleasant surprise, I found I had unwittingly dropped it on the trail before washing.  So, I returned to wash it, then brought it back to its lonely mate.

Looking at those sock liners, I was disappointed.  In the past I considered them indestructible, but now I saw holes forming around the toes and elsewhere, apparently from chafing.  They were still worth using, but I realized they would not endure beyond the end of the hike at best.

Sally arrived at the juncture shortly before I departed.  Today it seemed we were tag-teaming more than we were leapfrogging, but I enjoyed a snack with her and directed her to the water collection site before hoisting my pack.

As I started going upriver, I realized I was at 8,000 feet, my lowest point so far on the JMT northbound route.  The next pass, John Muir Pass, would be almost 4,000 feet higher.  I resolved to try to get more than half of that elevation gain on this day, before making camp.

I was pretty sure I could identify the 10,000-foot contour.  In the Sierras, open fires are not allowed above 10,000 feet.  For this reason, it seemed every time we crossed that elevation there would be a sign flagging the change in fire status.  Most hikers would thereby know whether they were above or below 10,000 feet.

This shot was actually taken on a different day, but it captures what I was talking about, so I inserted it anyway.

As I ascended slowly, I eventually reached the turnoff to Bishop Pass, an indicator that I had gone ten miles already.  I was feeling pretty strong.   Without resting, I drank some water to prevent dehydration and pressed on.  It was a boost of confidence to reach the point I intended to stop, and to realize I still had energy for several miles more.

At a bridge over the stream I looked down and saw three little fishes swimming in an eddy of the stream.  I began to sing “Three little fishies in an itty bitty pool…”  You know that one?  First recorded in 1939, so maybe not.  At that moment a hiking couple arrived and no doubt thought I was completely insane singing to the fishes!

Two of the three little fishies… the third was camera-shy.  I am fairly confident they are Golden Trout!

I continued climbing and climbing through woods that changed as I went higher up.  For a while a number of thorny bushes lined the trail.  In my shorts this was uncomfortable; though they didn’t actually break the skin, they left many surface scratches.

I stopped to fill up my water bottle again at an ideal camp site.  While tempted to stay there, I decided it was still too early to pitch my tent.  Just as I was leaving, a guy came and asked if I’d take his picture on his phone.  I looked across the river and saw a stunning mountain.  “How cool,”  I thought, “I’ll crouch really low, aiming upward, and it will make a great picture with this giant crag looming behind him!”

That was not his intention at all.  It turned out I had not realized it, but I stopped at an iconic trail location.   The man moved toward a giant boulder, and then I saw it.  It was a face!  A face naturally created  by the geology of the place, but with a huge gaping ogre’s mouth.  In that yawning maw, people had placed a row of small triangular stones to represent teeth.  They added a couple on top of his head as silly little ears.

The man entered the “mouth” and lay down between the “teeth.”  He said his girlfriend back in France had taken her picture there several years before, so now I took his picture to send back to her.   Of course, having seen “The Thing” I had to ask him to reciprocate with a picture of me!  How funny that I almost left the site without even noticing it…

The forest was reduced to occasional scraggly trees as I hiked higher, passing more waterfalls as the slope became steeper.

Waterfall catching the sunlight, with the wildflowers in front? Lovely!

Shortly after passing the symbolic 10,000-foot sign (“No Fires!”) I came to a lush meadow.  It looked like a perfect camping area, and I found what seemed like an ideal spot off the trail.

I dropped my pack and turned off my AllTrails app and prepared to settle in.  Then I realized the meadow was flat and green and there were no logs or rocks for me to sit on.  I thought, “Let me wander around here a little nearby.  I’m sure I can find a nice big stone to carry over by my tent to sit on.”  Indeed, I found just such a stone.  But when I picked it up, I found under the stone was… toilet paper!  Ugh!

I put it back down in the same spot quickly and reassessed my decision.  Suddenly that place felt like bad karma, so I turned AllTrails back on again, shouldered my pack, and continued looking for a place to spend the night.

It was late afternoon already, and I worried that the camp sites higher up might all be full.  I decided to poll the descending southbound hikers passing me to find out what I might expect.  My question was simple and uniform:  “How many people did you encounter higher up who had already made camp there?”

The first person who came by answered, “One” and I was relieved.  But the second person who came along answered, “Six.”  I grew concerned.  Finally, the third downhill hikers were two women; they looked like mother and daughter.  To my question, the mother answered, “Fifteen, I think.”   Fifteen!  Then, to my relief, the daughter chimed in, “Oh no, I don’t think so, maybe five?”

This was a quasi-scientific experiment proving how totally unreliable we humans are at reporting anything.  Yet it left me with the same dilemma.  Then the daughter continued, “there’s a pretty site just up ahead and there still seemed to be room there.”

I looked on the AllTrails app, and it did show a spot nearby with four tent sites.   I figured, “Well, even if there are a couple of people there, I don’t mind sharing  and it will give me a little extra elevation and a little extra distance that I won’t have to take on tomorrow.”

Upward I trudged.  Soon, there was the spot with three tents already pitched, but space for a fourth about twenty yards apart from them.  The owners of the tents seemed to be friends, or maybe a family, as they had camp chairs and were all playing cards together.

Late afternoon arrival, with the sky still a deep blue!

I took my spot and pitched my tent, feeling very tired.  As I took off my trail runners and socks, I realized my feet were well beyond filthy.  I wanted a bed and a shower; not necessarily right away, but soon.

That was when I broke down and texted my wife on my Garmin.  It was something like:  “If you can get me a cabin or a room at the John Muir Ranch for day-after-tomorrow, I will be very grateful.  It is probably all booked up, but just in case, please give it a try.”

It was deep twilight by the time I soaked my dinner and dug my cathole.  I wolfed down my repast and called it a night.  It felt like it might prove to be a cold one.
