Dogecoin co-creator Billy Markus, who goes by Shibetoshi Nakamoto on X, has weighed in on Bitcoin's potential role as an alternative to traditional retirement savings, describing Bitcoin as having the "best reason to exist."

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement savings plan in the United States that typically allows workers to invest through a selection of financial products, often including mutual funds and exchange-traded funds.

The discussion began on X as market participants reacted to the ongoing market crash, which has seen millions of dollars in liquidations in the last 24 hours. Leveraged traders took the brunt, with $1.92 billion of liquidations over 24 hours, according to CoinGlass data. Longs accounted for the majority of the damage at $1.59 billion, while short liquidations totaled $341.26 million.

Bitcoin fell to a three-month low below $63,000; Dogecoin fell to a low of $0.055, last seen in June 2023. At the time of writing, Dogecoin was down 4.63% over the last 24 hours to $0.059.

An X user commented, apparently in light of the market drop, "Cash out or crash out."

@mikethree bitcoin has the best reason to exist as the og, ethereum is a massive failure of its own goals but has positive brand recognition, they’re all dumb tokens for degenerates

Dogecoin cofounder Billy Markus responded, saying, "I just sell immediately and hold very little since the market is just a delusion indicator and that’s too random to track."

Another X user joined the discussion, saying Bitcoin remains "a good 401k replacement imo but depends on risk profile obviously." Markus replied to this, saying, "Bitcoin has the best reason to exist as the OG."

As cryptocurrency adoption increases, more traditionally-minded investors have begun exploring digital assets as part of their retirement plans.

Asset manager Fidelity allows Bitcoin in 401(k) plans, depending on employer approval. Fidelity is primarily known for its huge portfolio of retirement accounts and is among the largest 401(k) providers in the U.S. Fidelity also has a standard account option, announced in April 2022, that allows employees with 401(k) accounts to add Bitcoin, in addition to its more traditional accounts.

Fidelity Investments announced a new retirement plan in 2025 allowing U.S. adults to directly invest in cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, Ethereum and Litecoin.

Clippers rookie Keaton Wagler draws praise from Shai Gilgeous-Alexander

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Keaton Wagler continues to find his footing in his first season with the Los Angeles Clippers.

The fifth overall pick in the 2026 draft has found a way to contribute to coach Tyronn Lue's team, averaging 12.4 points, 3.4 rebounds and 3.2 assists through 11 games played this season.

The rookie has not made his first official start but has averaged 22.7 minutes per game off the bench.

Wagler modeled part of his game after Shai Gilgeous-Alexander, who spent his rookie season with the Clippers in 2018 before he was later traded to the Oklahoma City Thunder.

Being in the NBA has afforded Wagler opportunities early in the season to play against some of his idols with the Clippers having played the Thunder twice this season.

"It was a cool experience," Wagler told reporters about being able to play against Gilgeous-Alexander. "Growing up I really liked Shai, his game and being able to go up against him."

Wagler has drawn praise from Gilgeous-Alexander after those two meetings.

"He has good feel. Can really shoot," Gilgeous-Alexander said about the rookie after the Clippers' 113-112 victory against the Thunder on Friday, Nov. 13. "Usually when you have those things, the game will slow down for you eventually.

"He's in a good situation where he can play a lot. There are good coaches over there, so he'll learn the game the right way. As long as he works hard, he should have a long runway to be whoever he wants to be."

The Clippers have struggled to start the season, winning just three of their first 11 games, including Friday's victory against the Thunder.

Earlier this month, Lue apologized to the rookie for not getting him more playing time.

"That's just what I felt like," Lue said about Wagler's minutes after a 117-116 overtime loss to the Miami Heat on Nov. 4.

Wagler played a season-low 16 minutes against Miami. He finished with 11 points and a team-high +15 while on the court.

Lue told reporters on Nov. 8 that he had apologized to Wagler after having rewatched the film from the game, telling him he earned more playing time than he received.

Wagler averaged 19.6 minutes per game over the last three games since Lue's apology.

Scientists Just Challenged a 50-Year-Old Theory About How Evolution Works

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

In bacteria and yeast, a gene’s importance did not predict its mutation rate, challenging the idea that evolution gives vital DNA preferential treatment.

Not all genes are equally important. Some are essential for survival, controlling basic functions such as copying DNA and keeping cells alive. Others can be lost with little or no effect. But does that mean evolution has found a way to shield its most vital genes from potentially harmful mutations?

For decades, scientists have debated whether essential genes are naturally less likely to mutate. Now, researchers at the University of Bath have put that idea to the test, with results that challenge a long-standing evolutionary hypothesis.

Dr. Sofia Radrizzani and Professor Laurence D. Hurst, from the university’s Centre for Evolution, examined spontaneous mutations in the bacterium Escherichia coli (E. coli) and baker’s yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae). Their study, published November 12 in PLOS Biology, found no evidence that essential genes receive extra protection and offers a new explanation for why earlier research may have suggested otherwise.

“We’d expect natural selection to rapidly remove mutations when they occur in more important genes, because these mutations are more likely to be harmful to the organism. This can create the illusion that important genes have fewer mutations when, in reality, some mutations have simply disappeared before we have a chance to analyze them – what you can think of as ‘ghost mutations’,” Hurst said.

To reduce that problem, the team used data from “mutation accumulation” experiments designed to minimize the effects of natural selection. Three separate tests found no evidence that selection had substantially skewed the results.

“This looks like the largest and cleanest sample of mutations assembled to date,” Radrizzani said.

The researchers compared mutation rates with experimental evidence showing what happens when individual genes are removed. Deleting an essential gene kills the cell, while losing other genes has little or no effect. This gave the team a way to test whether genes a cell depends on most were less likely to mutate.

“Analyze the data any which way and we found nothing. In neither species did these key genes have a lower mutation rate,” Hurst said.

The team also assessed importance through the amount of a gene’s protein the cell produces and the strength of natural selection acting on the gene over evolutionary time.

“We found that genes producing more abundant proteins don’t have lower mutation rates. Nor does the way genes evolve over thousands of years predict how frequently they mutate,” Radrizzani said.

These findings address a debate that began in 1972 and resurfaced as recently as 2023. At stake is the relationship between the two steps in the conventional account of evolution. Mutations first generate genetic variation. Natural selection and other forces then act on that variation, favoring some traits over others.

Darwin’s finches, which Charles Darwin encountered on the Galápagos Islands in 1835, offer a familiar example. Random mutations contribute to differences in beak size, allowing birds to use different food sources. When conditions favor particular foods, birds with beaks better suited to eating them are more likely to survive and reproduce. Their traits consequently become more common over generations.

The disputed proposal suggests that a gene’s importance also influences how often mutations arise in it. Vital genes would receive greater protection, while genes where change could help adaptation would mutate more frequently. Examples of the latter include genes in which mutations give bacteria antibiotic resistance or alter birds’ beak dimensions.

There is a theoretical difficulty with that proposal. Gene mutation rates are already so low that reducing them further for individual genes would offer very little benefit, making it difficult for natural selection to favor such a protective system.

“The claim that mutation is slower for important genes is a triumph of hype over substance,” said Radrizzani, who co-wrote the paper as part of her PhD. “It’s a really old suggestion and is reinvented in various forms routinely.”

Genes copied later during DNA replication did tend to mutate more frequently in the team’s analysis, showing that some features of the genome can help explain differences in mutation rates.

“While we see known causes of variation in mutation rates, we found no evidence for the effects that have been proposed as a challenge to the conventional model. None predicted which genes would have high or low mutation rates, and there are certainly no grounds for a shift in paradigm,” Hurst said.

Reference: “The importance of genes does not predict their mutation rate” by Sofia Radrizzani and Laurence D. Hurst, 12 November 2026, PLOS Biology. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pbio.3004089

What's Your Most Unforgettable Close Call?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

People are capable of doing some supremely stupid things in cars. We've seen the warnings from informational videos over the decades, and our personal blunders and experiences have demonstrated this point, too. We can, of course, be attentive behind the wheel, but that doesn't account for the others who aren't while on the road. It's partly why dash cams and their resulting videos have gained so much traction in recent years. While someone's dumb decision may end up in disaster, others might end in close calls — moments where destruction was inches away.

Close calls happen more often than you might expect, especially when we live in an era where vehicles are equipped with every warning along with literal bells and whistles to tell drivers they might hit something. This week, we don't want to know about the little near-misses — we want to know about the big ones that haunt you. Tell us about the misses that had they been hits, would've made for calamity.

Years ago, while in the city for the New York International Auto Show, I was lucky enough to be driving a well-equipped 2021 Maserati Levante GTS. I had taken it on a scenic drive along the Belt Parkway to Amityville, and on my return to Brooklyn, I hit a bit of traffic. About 10 minutes into my drive, in a 35 mph residential zone, an Audi Q5 came speeding up behind me. I reactively slowed down to let it fly past.

I watched as this Q5 blazed through a red light and hit another car, a fairly new Subaru, before the Q5 went spinning into the other side of the intersection. That was near miss number one. Two people got out of the smashed Q5 and bolted. Instinctively, I wanted to stop and help, but I was in a part of New York I was unfamiliar with in a six-figure vehicle that wasn't mine. Instead, I opted to get around the scene.

Seconds later, a Ford Taurus came up on my bumper, then quickly maneuvered ahead to cut me off before making a turn into a parking lot where it then stopped, and a cop hopped out and proceeded to stop a kid who had run from the crash scene. The Taurus was never put in park, though, and it started rolling towards the Maserati. I panicked, trying to figure out how to reverse it with its weird shifter.

The cop saw what was happening and was able to catch the car about an inch away from the Maserati. Near miss number two. Meanwhile, another cop had arrested the kid. After a deep breath and a phone call to my husband, still quite shaken, I made my way back to Brooklyn.

It might not have been the greatest near-miss, but the feelings I had nearly five years ago remain strong to this day. With that chaos in mind, what was your most unforgettable close call? Tell us all about it and why it holds critical space in your memory bank in the comments below.

GTA 6 Streaming Guidelines In China Prohibit Car Theft, Murder, And Attacking Cops

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Chinese streaming regulations are notoriously strict, so much so that GTA was banned from being shown on platforms like Douyin, Huya, Bilibili, and PandaTV. GTA 6 may be the exception, but with a laundry list of caveats that essentially turn Grand Theft Auto into a sightseeing simulator.

In China, livestreaming games that haven't received the necessary government approval is prohibited under national regulations introduced in 2022.

According to local media outlets such as IT Home (via Yahoo), a document titled 'Guidelines for Compliance During Livestreams of GTA 6' was reportedly leaked by a Douyin creator, and its URL originates from ByteDance's corporate domain, lending legitimacy to their claim.

Access to the document has been blocked, but not before its contents were shared to the internet. The guidelines stipulate that streamers must avoid revealing clothing or certain emojis, and must black out the screen during gory scenes, or adjust blood effects to appear gray or green (something that many games in China do already, such as PUBG and Counter-Strike: Global Offensive). Streamers must also avoid attacking bystanders, assaulting police officers, or, bizarrely, stealing cars.

However, local outlets report that "the online rumor about 'securing streaming rights' has no basis in fact." The document is simply a reminder of platform rules.

GTA 6’s Extended Look is so censored on Bilibili that a few scenes are almost unwatchable.

It's unclear right now if GTA 6 will even launch in China, as it does not yet have approval, and streaming unlicensed foreign games is explicitly prohibited. However, Rockstar did launch a verified Douyin account on November 12 to promote the game. That doesn't necessarily mean the studio has obtained government approval, but it's the strongest evidence yet that GTA 6 will be permitted. Until we have official confirmation, of course, there's no guarantee that platforms like Douyin will be allowed to host GTA 6 videos. Gen Z’s job crisis has reached the ultrarich. Wealth advisors say billionaire parents are now planning for kids who may struggle to land and keep jobs

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Even the wealthiest parents in America are worried about their kids’ financial futures. The unemployment rate for recent college graduates has held at about 5.6%, and 42.5% of recent grads were underemployed at the end of 2025, the highest share since 2020. A college degree no longer guarantees a steady paycheck, and economists warn AI could upend the entry-level work that recent grads have historically done.

In a world where seemingly no one is immune to the impacts of AI on the workforce and an increasingly stiff job market, ultra-high net worth people are starting to have the same concerns as middle-class and low-income families: Will my kid be able to get a job and support themself?

“Billionaires have the financial resources to support their children, but they sometimes struggle to determine what else is needed for their children to succeed,” Tom Thiegs, managing director of leadership and legacy at Ascent Private Capital Management with U.S. Bank, told Fortune earlier this year.

Millionaires and billionaires “are recognizing this is not the same game they had to play,” wealth manager Patrick Dwyer told CNBC earlier this year. “Families have to rethink … what it means to support their children. And we’re not talking about spoiling your kids. We’re talking about: What if your kid needs retraining at 33?” Dwyer is managing director at Aligned by NewEdge Wealth, a boutique wealth management firm based in Miami. He works with clients with net worths between roughly $100 million and more than $1 billion.

Dwyer said his clients are concerned their children, typically between age 22 and 35, are struggling to secure and hang on to jobs that are historically associated with security and status, including technology, law, and health care. That means the wealthy will have to plan for a future in which they’re passing on more of their fortune to their kids.

“[They’re] realizing that if they don’t pass on more meaningful wealth to their children, or their children are not able to accumulate wealth … their kids could have [less] agency over their lives than they did,” he told CNBC.

While this may sound like an irrational fear from America’s wealthiest, it’s a reality more wealth managers recognize.

“This is a very real concern I’m hearing from ultra-affluent families right now,” Thiegs said. “On the surface it can sound irrational: ‘Why would a billionaire worry about their child getting a job?’ But realistically, no matter how much money you have, parents still want their children to succeed and lead fulfilled lives.”

The job market jitters Gen Zers face directly impacts how their parents can help them plan for their financial futures.

But the crux of the problem isn’t that parents fear they won’t financially be able to help their children, it’s more that they’re worried they won’t have the same career outcomes and sense of fulfillment as past generations.

“They’re not usually worried about the financial security of their children; rather they worry that the job market will impact their child’s sense of purpose, identity, and confidence,” Thiegs said. “They also worry that significant wealth will dampen their drive or desire to work.”

But that doesn’t mean Thiegs encourages his clients just to bankroll their kids for the foreseeable future. Instead, estate planning, investing, and other long-term financial planning is a must.

“When parents are worried about their children’s job security, we recommend creating a system that provides opportunities for growth and development rather than just a financial safety net,” he said.

It’s more important to implement plans that support a child’s self worth than just their net worth, he added.

Trent Von Ahsen, a certified financial planner and managing partner at Cedar Point Capital Partners, also says his ultra-high net worth families view the risks of today’s job market as less of a financial stability concern, but more about whether they’re setting their kids up to be indefinitely dependent on them.

“This cohort of parents seem more concerned about over-supporting their children, than under-supporting them,” Von Ahsen told Fortune earlier this year.

The shift away from historically high-paying, white-collar jobs is already evident in the choices Gen Z is making. Facing mass layoffs in white‑collar sectors and anxious about AI, many young workers are peeling away from traditional corporate routes in favor of jobs they think might offer more control or faster cash—from becoming content creators to opting for blue-collar jobs in manufacturing, electrical work, and other technical work.

In some cases, college‑educated Gen Zers are even competing for six‑figure nanny and tutor roles in elite households, chasing financial “freedom” outside of a traditional office career. A 2026 Deloitte global survey found just 6% of Gen Z and millennial respondents said reaching a leadership position is their primary career goal. Instead, many say they favor stability, skill-building, and well-being over climbing the ladder quickly.

This all means, though, billionaires and other high-net worth individuals have to financially plan differently from how they might have in the past. They have to design financial plans that “encourage growth and responsibility” instead of only making large inheritances all at once, Von Ahsen said.

“We see more emphasis on education funding flexibility, mentorship, and phased wealth transfers,” he said. It’s “really an attitude moving toward providing opportunity without removing initiative.”
