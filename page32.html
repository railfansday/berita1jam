The outage began shortly before 1 p.m. and impacted Microsoft 365 services, Outlook and Teams. At the time, about 130,000 users reported issues with Outlook.

Amazon Web Services and Google users also reported outages on the site.

"We are aware and investigating reports of users experiencing issues with Microsoft 365 services, primarily issues with Exchange Online," a Microsoft spokesperson wrote in a statement to CNBC. "We appreciate our customers' patience while we work to restore services as soon as possible."

Microsoft shared updates on the outage from its Microsoft 365 Status account on social media platform X.

Microsoft initially said infrastructure "elevated load across multiple regions" was responsible for the connectivity problems.

The company later determined that the elevated load was caused by a "number of servers being unexpectedly unavailable; straining the remaining infrastructure" and that some servers were failing automated health checks.

"We've taken steps to prevent additional servers from being impacted while we work to return the affected servers to service," Microsoft wrote.

Tesari, which uses artificial intelligence to collect evidence and specializes in national security investigations around the world, provides intelligence research to private companies and the U.S. government.

This previously unreported entanglement may raise questions about Alice Huang's intent and conduct in her relationship with the CEO and whether Jensen Huang knew his romantic partner was closely connected with his competition. Jensen Huang has acknowledged having an affair, though the precise timing of his relationship with Alice Huang is unknown.

The mysterious saga of Alice Huang involves a tangle of interlocking corporations and transactions that show her connection to entities that ultimately provided computing power to Huawei in China.

Huawei is known as a Chinese government "state champion" in the technology and telecommunications sectors, meaning it receives enormous financial support from the government in Beijing so it can lower prices and undercut global competitors. It is considered a key part of the Chinese government's push for global technical dominance and is closely linked to the Chinese Communist Party.

Businesses connected to Alice Huang were linked with supplying Nvidia computing power to Huawei, according to documents reviewed by CNBC, around the same time she and Jensen Huang were first publicly seen together, in December 2023.

In 2024, Nvidia said Huawei was a top competitor, specifically in artificial intelligence chips including graphics processing units, central processing units and networking chips.

"This activity, which surfaced through our analysis of Alice's network, warrants a national security and [technology] counterproliferation investigation," said Brian Andersen, Tesari's vice president for national security applications and a former supervisory special agent with Homeland Security Investigations.

If the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry Security and Homeland Security Investigations found evidence that anyone transferred "controlled equipment" to Huawei without authorization, potential charges might include export-control violations, conspiracy and smuggling, Andersen said.

The connection between Alice Huang and Huawei is so convoluted that analysts at Tesari told CNBC that based on their previous investigative experience, they suspect it was designed to deliberately obscure the relationship.

Nvidia and Huawei did not respond to CNBC's requests for comment. Jensen Huang did not respond to a voicemail left on a mobile phone. Alice Huang could not be reached.

The key customer of that data center was Huawei Cloud, the Shenzhen, China-based subsidiary of Huawei Technologies.

In addition, Alice Huang's firm Hexi guaranteed the loans required to finance the Shenzhen data center, which did business with Huawei.

Hexi did not immediately respond to a message via its LinkedIn page sent outside of normal business hours.

Over the past year, UnitedHealth Group has conducted a series of independent reviews of its practices as part of a broader turnaround effort at the company.

The healthcare giant released the last of these analyses on Thursday, digging into the processes it uses to establish medical policies. The reviews were conducted by FTI Consulting and Rubrum Advising, a provider-led firm with expertise in medical coverage policies.

While the auditors dove into the processes the insurer uses to develop its medical policies, the report doesn't examine the application of those polices and any impact on coverage denials, an area where UnitedHealthcare has drawn significant public scrutiny.

The report concluded the company's methods "were strong overall and that the medical policy development process reflected numerous best practices."

For instance, UnitedHealthcare's Medical Policy team uses a structured and standardized process to establish medical policies, rooted in evidence-based and stakeholder-informed approaches. Clinical evidence comes from nationally recognized authorities, and epidemiologists play a key role in the review process, per the report.

In addition, the analysts concluded that the team is well-staffed with qualified workers and operates under a strong governance model. Medical policies are built "using systematic evidence review, evidence-based tools and industry best practices," and UnitedHealthcare is transparent about posting these policies and making updates.

All policies are reviewed at least once per year, the report found.

CEO Stephen Hemsley said in a letter to investors that the company will continue to conduct such analyses.

"We will continue to subject key practices across UnitedHealth Group to independent review, share results publicly and make changes where needed," Hemsley wrote. "This work reflects our broader commitment to transparency, accountability and continuous improvement."

While the report concluded that overall processes in place at UnitedHealth were up to snuff, the consultants did offer multiple areas of improvement for the company, too. For instance, the analysts said that the team could improve its documentation practices, including how it details criteria for policy development.

Plus, the report suggests that UnitedHealthcare review how it documents vetting criteria for internal and external subject matter experts. The company could also do more to formally document training and education for members of its governance committee.

Medical policy documents can be lengthy and complex, so the consultants recommended the insurer consider developing a template that standardizes the written format for each policy, which would make it easier to understand. For example, the template could make it easier to connect the evidence summary to the coverage rationale.

"While these gaps do not diminish the overall strength of the program, addressing them would further enhance transparency and consistency and provide opportunities for continued refinement," the analysts wrote.

The request covers existing and future records and includes a Section 232 report that, according to the letter, Lutnick allegedly sent Trump on December 22, 2025.

The technique begins with weights, the numerical parameters that training adjusts. Before training, the PyTorch/cuRAND setup examined uses the Philox generator to produce repeatable values from a seed. Changing the SM count therefore changes where the same generated numbers land.

Weights reserved for words or symbols absent from training can preserve their initial values almost exactly, while trained weights may retain a weaker statistical relationship to those values. The investigators select a seed and layout on one set of weights, then test that unchanged combination on a separate set excluded from the search.

Under its statistical model, the report estimates that roughly 6 trillion seed-and-layout combinations would produce an expected strongest chance match of about 8 sigma, a statistical measure of distance from random variation. CMS said that the initial moratorium window has allowed the agency "to conduct targeted investigations and deploy advanced data analytics" to identify potential cases of fraud. An extension was deemed necessary to continue that effort, as the "circumstances warranting the imposition of the moratoria have not yet abated."

The agency said that hospice and home health have long been considered high risk areas for fraud.

“We’ve made real progress over the past six months, but fraud in hospice and home health persists and our work isn’t finished,” said CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., in the announcement. “Medicare beneficiaries deserve protection from these bad actors, and American taxpayers need to know the programs they rely on aren’t being bled dry by fraudsters.”

Like the original moratorium, the extension applies nationwide. However, the extension will allow currently enrolled hospice providers or home health agencies to add new practice locations to their existing enrollment in Medicare. Providers currently enrolled in Medicare will not be impacted and can continue to provide services to beneficiaries.

The Trump administration has issued a six-month moratorium on hospice and home health agencies enrolling in Medicare as part of its efforts to combat fraud.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services said in an announcement on Wednesday morning that the "data-driven" decision targets a key source of fraud activity. It follows a similar announcement made earlier this year of a moratorium on durable medical equipment, prosthetics, orthotics and supplies companies.

During the six-month window, CMS said it will focus on identifying and investigating bad actors and deploying advanced analytics that help accelerate the removal of hospice and home health providers that are likely perpetrators of fraud.

CMS added that recent action under the broader White House fraud push has suspended payments to 773 hospice providers and 23 home health agencies in the Los Angeles area, totaling about $70 million in funds.

“We’ve seen systemic and deeply troubling fraud in the hospice and home health space, with bad actors exploiting some of our most vulnerable Medicare patients and stealing money from the American taxpayer,” CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., said in the agency's press release. “Today we’re shutting the door on fraud—preventing new bad actors from entering Medicare while we aggressively identify, investigate and remove those already exploiting them."

CMS said that the moratorium will not affect providers currently enrolled in Medicare, and they can continue to offer key services to members. The moratoria will also prevent suspected fraudsters from evading punishment by moving across state lines, CMS said.

Texas is dropping a part of its lawsuit against Epic Systems, specifically the claims about how the company's software handles parental access to a child's electronic health records.

In December, Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton filed a lawsuit against Epic, alleging that the health tech company monopolizes the electronic health record market and cements its dominance in the market through an "anticompetitive playbook."

The lawsuit, filed in Tarrant County District Court, also accused the company of engaging in deceptive practices that restrict parental access to their minor children’s medical records. On Tuesday, the Texas AG's office filed a notice of partial non-suit indicating it was dropping the deceptive practices claims against Epic.

The notice of partial non-suit states that the decision was made "in light of Epic’s recent changes to its software."

In previous statements, an Epic spokesperson said the company "does not determine parental access to children’s medical records."

"Decisions about parental access to children’s medical records are made by doctors and health systems, not by Epic," the spokesperson said.

The state is still moving forward with its antitrust claims under the Texas Free Enterprise and Antitrust Act. The Texas Attorney General's office alleges Epic "uses a wide range of exclusionary tactics to prevent potential competition from its partners, customers and even its own employees." According to the suit, these anticompetitive practices harm Texas hospitals and Texas patients by raising costs and blocking innovative technologies.

In the lawsuit, Paxton alleged that Epic "exploits its control" over patient medical records to automatically hide children’s medication lists, treatment notes and provider messages from parents when their child reaches 12 years old. Texas alleged that Epic’s default proxy-access configurations impede parents’ ability to see their children’s health information.

"These deceptive practices undermine the fundamental right of parents to direct their children’s healthcare" and violate Texas law, which grants parents complete and unrestricted access to their children’s medical records, the attorney general's office said in the lawsuit.

In a statement issued in December, Paxton referred to Epic as a "woke corporation" and stated that Epic's default proxy-access configurations "undermine the sacred rights of parents to protect and oversee their kids’ medical well-being."

In a statement provided to Fierce Healthcare on Wednesday, an Epic spokesperson said the Texas lawsuit includes "significant inaccuracies, including claims about MyChart's customer proxy settings."

"The state filed a voluntary dismissal of those claims on Tuesday. Ken Paxton's office requested that we change our customers' system settings. We said no because those decisions should be made by doctors and health systems and not by Epic. The lawsuit followed. The State should dismiss the remaining claims," the Epic spokesperson said.

In a Jan. 20 filing in Tarrant County District Court, the electronic health record giant said it makes "highly configurable software so that its customers, healthcare organizations across all fifty states, can determine what workflows and functionalities are needed to achieve their own compliance obligations—including with respect to parental access rights to medical records."

"What might be compliant in Texas might not be compliant in another state and vice versa, and those laws may change from time to time, as the law just did roughly four months ago in Texas. Configurability helps customers bridge that gap," the company wrote in its filing.

Epic said it would not "unilaterally go into each of its customers’ systems to change their individualized settings."

Epic also argued, in its court filing, that the state failed to produce facts or legitimate legal bases to support any of its claims. "When Epic asked the State to identify any Epic customers it claimed were not in compliance with the new law, the State did not identify a single Epic customer. What appears to be going on here is that the State has decided to target Epic over a politicized issue regarding proxy access that Epic does not belong in the middle of. The State confirms as much in its press release, calling Epic a 'woke corporation' that supposedly 'undermine[s]' parents’ rights," Epic wrote in its answer to the lawsuit.

In its Answer and Affirmative Defenses, Epic said that the state's antitrust claims against it are pre-textual and baseless. Epic denied Paxton's allegations of anticompetitive behavior, citing a lack of evidence and factual flaws in the lawsuit.

The case will carry over into a changing political landscape in Texas. Paxton, who launched the lawsuit, is leaving the Texas attorney general's office after more than a decade, after his unsuccessful bid for the U.S. Senate. Democrat Nathan Johnson defeated Republican Mayes Middleton in the Nov. 3 midterm election, becoming the first Democrat elected Texas attorney general since 1994.

The leadership change could bring a shift in the office's litigation priorities. Paxton's tenure was marked by high-profile legal battles over social and cultural issues, including transgender healthcare and parental rights.

Last year, the Texas Office of the Attorney General secured a settlement with Austin Diagnostic Clinic that required full restoration of parental proxy access for children aged 12 to 17. Paxton has also issued civil investigative demands to other EHR software providers over parental access to minor children's medical records.

The case will also have a new judge. Democrat Katherine Kim defeated Republican incumbent H. Dustin "Dusty" Fillmore III in the race for Tarrant County's 236th Judicial District Court.
