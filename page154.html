Scaloni apre le porte del suo staff a Messi: "Se vuole venire, non ci sono problemi"

Lionel Messi ha chiuso il capitolo Nazionale, ma il legame con l'Argentina potrebbe non interrompersi mai. A 39 anni, dopo 208 presenze e 126 gol con la maglia dell'Albiceleste, il fuoriclasse dell'Inter Miami ha deciso di non vestire più la maglia della propria Nazionale. Ha già detto no alla Nazionale da calciatore, ma spero che continui a giocare il più a lungo possibile", ha spiegato il CT. Poi l'apertura verso un possibile ruolo tecnico: "Se vuole venire qui, non c'è nessun problema. Ora che Walter se ne va... che venga pure!".

Messi è legato all'Inter Miami da un contratto fino al dicembre 2028 e, nonostante abbia salutato la Nazionale, non sembra intenzionato a lasciare subito il calcio giocato. Scaloni, però, immagina già un futuro diverso per il suo capitano, protagonista assoluto della rinascita argentina culminata con la vittoria del Mondiale in Qatar.

2 Great Space Stocks Team Up on a $1.3 Billion Deal to Beat SpaceX at Satellite Internet

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AST SpaceMobile will pay Firefly $1.3 billion for some new rocket engines -- but how much of that cash will Firefly actually ever receive?

Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX -1.26%) dominates America's space sector. Starting off as a rocket launcher for other companies, SpaceX has evolved into a company that makes most of its money by using its own satellites to provide communications services on Earth -- a task made easier by having its own fleet of reusable rockets.

Other space companies aren't so fortunate.

AST SpaceMobile (ASTS -12.33%), for example, invented and was first to prove the concept of using satellites to connect calls between two ordinary, off-the-shelf cellphones (not specialized satellite phones). Lacking its own rockets, however, AST has to date been dependent upon other companies to provide the launch services it needs to get its satellites into orbit.

Which launch providers in particular? So far, AST has flown its satellites with India's ISRO, Blue Origin, and SpaceX itself. That last one is obviously suboptimal, though -- it's not a great corporate strategy to make yourself dependent on your biggest competitor for launch services!

And there are other options. The list of private companies that have successfully flown a rocket to orbit may not be a long one, but it's getting longer:

A couple of other names might deserve to be on this list, having flown once or twice but not continually. But for the most part, this is it. And one of these launch names jumps out especially: Firefly.

Last week, you see, Firefly offered to help AST out of its jam ... and collect a big payday for the service. For $1.3 billion, Firefly agreed to sell AST SpaceMobile 36 rocket engines, which is enough to outfit three full-size heavy lift rockets -- which AST intends to build itself. To this end, Firefly will provide AST engineering support over three years as AST builds a fleet of 20-ton "Blacklift" rockets (which it expects to have ready to fly by late 2028).

Firefly will also grant AST a license to use Firefly designs, enabling AST to build additional engines for additional rockets on its own.

For Firefly, the advantages of this deal seem obvious. Firefly gets $1.3 billion to build and deliver 36 engines (so about $36 million per engine). Firefly can then reinvest this money in itself "to rapidly expand our rocket production."

Viewed from the perspective of a Firefly investor, the only quibble I have with this deal is the form of payment. Firefly says AST will pay the $1.3 billion in "cash and shares of AST Class A Common Stock." The cash part is fine. What worries me is that Firefly might accept too much of its payment in the form of ASTS stock.

I'm not at all convinced that AST SpaceMobile can build and fly a rocket successfully by 2028.

As veteran space journalist Eric Berger at Ars Technica has pointed out, this timeline isn't just optimistic; it's "wildly, fantastically, and stupidly optimistic." After all, the fastest record for taking a rocket from design to orbital flight (ever) was set by SpaceX with the Falcon 9 -- and that took 4.7 years, more than twice the time AST is budgeting for.

While you can't rule out the possibility that AST will accomplish the feat, I wouldn't bet on it.

Nor would I invest in AST stock based on its promise to do so. Nor -- and this part is the most important for Firefly investors -- would I allow AST to pay me for my engines in too much ASTS stock.

So how much ASTS stock is Firefly accepting in payment for its engines?

Well, of the $1.3 billion headline number, only the first $400 million will be paid up front in cash, with an additional $400 million paid in ASTS stock. A tranche of $250 million will be paid upon delivery of the engines, with $250 million owed for engineering services. When all's said and done, as much as $900 million of the $1.3 billion may be paid in hard currency. But at least $400 million -- and potentially more -- could end up paid in ASTS stock, which could turn out to be worthless if (read "when") it fails to launch Blacklift on schedule.

TL;DR: This deal may not be as good news for Firefly investors as it appears on the surface, and Firefly may end up with a whole lot less cash than investors expect.

james bridle and the power of solar, zooplankton, and non-human entities

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

ancient symbols and microscopic organisms meet contemporary solar technology in an artistic reflection on energy, ecology, and coexistence.

annalise kamegawa November 17, 2026 7 min

Back in 2022, James Bridle—artist, technologist, and author of the book New Dark Age (2018)—created a work called Solar Panels (Radiolaria Series). It was a series of five functional 100W photovoltaic generators, the faces of which were etched with tiny, microscopic zooplankton called Radiolaria. Looking at images of their silica skeletons, enlarged hundreds of times so they are visible to the human eye, reveals a universe of spiky arms, swirling bodies, intricate lattices, and geometric patterns. Juxtaposed with each other, and containing their own advanced modes of capturing energy, Bridle creates a compelling parallel between non-human and human intelligence, and what that could mean for the future of the earth.

Across their writing, installations, and lectures, James Bridle has become known for thinking about the ways that technology, the natural world, and humanity influence each other, with a strong emphasis on understanding ‘intelligence’. They look beyond the popular, mainstream ideas associated with the word, often linked to IQ, large language models, and other forms of reconstructing human thought patterns. In their piece, AI was built on human intelligence. Nature has more to teach, they express the urgency of going beyond this definition, and to learn from the other creatures we share the Earth with.

It is this line of thinking that has underwritten their works with solar panels, water collection systems, and vernacular architecture. Looking back to their 2019 exhibition, Through Other Eyes, which they curated with NeMe Art Centre in Cyprus, an emphasis on more-than-human forms of thinking and creating is evident. Here, they invoked the 1922 writing of Dziga Vertov, in which he describes his fascination with documentation through the lens of a camera. Bridle parallels this with the technologies of the twenty-first century – all the mania, fascination, and consequences – saying that, if we apply these new or different ‘eyes’, those of machines and animals, ‘They might also, if we are open to them, offer us visions of unexpected insight, and startling beauty.’ It is within this vision – trans-species and trans-machine – that Bridle’s work takes form.

Things Said, Things Seen, and Things Done, by James Bridle at Mystery 19 Visual Arts Initiator III – But Don’t Tell AnyoneThings Said, Things Seen, and Things Done, by James Bridle at Mystery 19 Visual Arts Initiator III – But Don’t Tell Anyone

Putting an image of radiolaria on a solar panel conjures ideas about the ability to sustain life and other modalities of using energy generated by natural processes. It calls into question the human need to consume power. Radiolaria, microscopic yet possessing highly advanced skeletal structures, can exist symbiotically with algae that provide them with energy. There is an ancient logic to their existence that can be traced back to the Cambrian. Bridle, in their description of the project, highlights how they were ‘famously illustrated, by the German naturalist Ernst Haeckel, who coined the term “ecology” in 1866.’ The complex structures of these organisms continue to inspire wonder, demonstrating how something so small can be so powerful and complex.

Expanding this logic to the scale of solar panels, a technologically intensive mode of capturing and converting energy from the sun in a way that can power human inventions calls into question the effectiveness of human ‘technology’ when compared to that of the non-human. Here is an incredible invention that can create sustainable energy, a promising alternative to fossil fuels, yet it takes advanced electronics and logistics to achieve what a single-celled organism has been doing for thousands of years. Here, Bridle once again asks us to consider what innovation means, not when we compare it to the span of human history.

In 2023, Bridle expanded on the concept in Things Said, Things Seen, and Things Done, an installation on the archaeological site of Elefsina in Greece, Mystery 19 Visual Arts Initiator III – But Don’t Tell Anyone, just north of Athens. Instead of radiolaria, the images engraved on three 100W solar photovoltaic panels are ‘the wheatsheaf, the poppy, and the calathus,’ which ‘honor the ancient cult of Demeter, and the veneration of the living Earth which she represents.’ Here, the comparison of contemporary power generation with beliefs in ancient deities asks us to question the ways in which energy is created, used, and distributed. Bridle used solar panels to power other works in the installation and the site itself, going on to remark that ‘The technology to do these things, is available to us, and the knowledge of how to do them is not a mystery, nor a secret, but it might require new rituals, and new initiations, to distribute it more widely. This is one of those rituals.’

the poppy that would be inscribed on Bridle’s solar panelssketch of Things Said, Things Seen, and Things Done, an installation on the archaeological site of Elefsina in Greece, Mystery 19 Visual Arts Initiator III – But Don’t Tell Anyone by James Bridle

Ultimately, James Bridle’s solar panel creations reflect a broader vision of posthuman utopias that runs throughout their artistic practice: one in which technology, humanity, and the natural world exist in a more reciprocal relationship. By bringing together ancient natural intelligence and contemporary technologies, Bridle challenges human exceptionalism and conventional ideas of progress. Their work encourages us to look beyond ourselves for models of innovation, recognizing that the natural world already holds sophisticated forms of knowledge. In doing so, they invite us to imagine futures founded on ecological interdependence, collaboration, and coexistence.

Cassava reportedly seeks up to $1bn for Nvidia chips for African AI

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Strive Masiyiwa’s Cassava is in talks with Middle Eastern and US investors to fund GPUs for its African data centres.

Strive Masiyiwa speaks alongside then Secretary of State Antony Blinken and then Assistant Secretary for African Affairs Molly Phee at the U.S.-Africa Leaders Summit foreign ministers’ dinner in Washington, 14 December 2022.

Cassava Technologies is seeking to raise as much as $1bn to buy Nvidia chips for its African AI data centres, Loni Prinsloo reported for Bloomberg. The report cites people familiar with the matter who asked not to be named.

Cassava declined to comment to Bloomberg, and TNW has not independently verified the report. The talks are ongoing and may not lead to a deal, the people said.

The company, founded by Zimbabwean billionaire Strive Masiyiwa, is talking to investors in the Middle East and the US, according to Bloomberg. Most of the money would buy graphics processing units (GPUs), the chips that train and run AI models.

Some would also prepare Cassava’s fibre and data centre networks for AI workloads, the people said. Its Cape Town facility, built to Nvidia’s specifications, is already running at full capacity, Bloomberg reported.

Cassava is expanding through partners. In Egypt, it is working with Vodafone Business and Elsewedy Electric on a $1bn data centre project that includes the country’s first sovereign AI data centre. Vodafone Egypt is part of Vodacom Group.

Rivals are moving too. MTN, Africa’s largest mobile operator, has set up a data centre venture with a UAE-based investor. It plans AI-ready sites in South Africa and Nigeria.

Africa has less than 1% of the world’s AI data centres, according to Bloomberg. Gulf investors may also see Africa as a way to spread risk, after data centres in the Gulf became targets during the conflict involving the US, Israel and Iran.

A successful raise would help Cassava compete with Microsoft, Amazon and Google, which are all investing in Africa.

Chelsea are continuing to monitor Real Betis winger José Antonio Morante after making an approach for the highly-rated teenager during the summer transfer window.

The 19-year-old has rapidly emerged as one of Betis’ most exciting academy products, while his performances for Spain at youth level have also attracted attention from some of Europe’s biggest clubs.

Chelsea have tracked him for several years and appear determined to keep him on their radar despite his decision to remain in Seville.

According to ABC, as relayed by Mundo Deportivo, Chelsea contacted Morante’s representatives during the summer after his impressive performances at the Under-19 European Championship.

Morante helped Spain win the competition, finishing as joint-top scorer with four goals and earning a place in the tournament’s best XI.

Chelsea’s approach did not develop into a formal bid because Morante preferred to stay with Betis and fight for first-team opportunities. The Spanish club subsequently rewarded him with a contract until 2030 containing a €30million release clause.

That decision has already produced encouraging signs. Morante scored shortly after coming on for his La Liga debut against Osasuna and has also featured in the Champions League.

His immediate concern is fitness, with an adductor issue making him doubtful for Betis’ derby against Sevilla on 21 November.

There is little surprise that Chelsea like Morante.

He is young, technically gifted, comfortable on either wing and available for a relatively achievable fee if his development continues. At €30m, his release clause could eventually look extremely attractive.

The bigger question is whether Chelsea actually need another young winger.

Xabi Alonso already has significant competition in wide areas, although that picture could change in January. CaughtOffside reports that Chelsea could allow Jamie Gittens to leave temporarily after his struggle for regular first-team football.

That possibility could eventually create more space for another developing attacker, but Chelsea should resist collecting talent without offering a clear pathway.

Morante appears happy at Betis and has only just begun establishing himself at senior level. Leaving now could actually slow his progress.

For Chelsea, the sensible approach is to continue scouting him rather than rush into activating the clause. If Morante develops into a regular La Liga performer, €30m may become difficult to ignore but only if the Blues can offer him meaningful minutes.

Mauricio Pochettino says he has a point to prove at Manchester United, after revealing his conversation with Pep Guardiola prior to sealing a return to club management.

The Argentine was appointed new Man United head coach last week, succeeding Michael Carrick who was sacked as a result of a disappointing start to the season with the side languishing in the Premier League relegation zone.

This will be the 54-year-old’s first job in club management since leaving Chelsea at the end of the 2023-24 season and fourth overall in England’s top-flight following spells at Southampton and Tottenham.

Despite the announcement, Pochettino does not officially start work at Man Utd until Wednesday as he has been granted permission to finish up with the United States Men’s National Team [USMNT] before reporting to Carrington.

6-0 up from the first-leg of the CONCACAF Nations League quarter-final against Haiti, it is expected to be a routine exercise for USA in the early hours of Wednesday morning [UK time] for what is Pochettino’s farewell game.

“I am ready [to return],” he said. “This is what I miss a little bit. I miss to be, every day, with something here.

Microsoft's SideWinder Strategic Commander was a failed attempt to reimagine RTS controls. 23 years later, why are some players still using it?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

How an unconventional peripheral became an equally unexpected survivor

PC players have been conditioned for decades to accept the keyboard-and-mouse setup for RTS games. One hand has to handle movement and commands through a keyboard, while the other points, drags, and selects with a mouse. Peripheral makers have, usually with the addition of more buttons, made many minor variations to this time-honored arrangement, but there's only ever been one real attempt to rethink it entirely for the PC: the Microsoft SideWinder Strategic Commander.

Unveiled on March 1st 2000 at Microsoft's Gamestock event, the Strategic Commander was one of three additions to their SideWinder line, with a scheduled release that October at a pricey $64.95. At E3 later that May, Microsoft demonstrated the Strategic Commander for the first time with SimCity 3000, while promoting its potential for other such games as Diablo II, Warcraft III, and Baldur's Gate II. This was all taking place during the so-called 'Golden Age of Real-Time Strategy Games,' which spanned roughly from the mid-1990s to the mid-2000s. It was when the RTS genre was at the peak of its mainstream popularity, and we fans were devoted to mastering its intricacies in any way we could to have a competitive edge.

Microsoft, all the while, were taking notes. "The team conceived the Strategic Commander in the late 1990s," Carl Ledbetter - then Industrial Design Manager for Microsoft's PC Hardware Group, now Head of Design for Xbox Devices - recalls to me. "As RTS games grew in popularity, we saw an interesting opportunity to design a new physical interface around a new interaction model. The idea was for it to be used left-handed in concert with right-hand PC desktop mouse control. We wanted to enable players to perform faster with more fluidity, comfort, optimised control placement for multiple hand sizes, simplicity of set-up, and ease of programming."

For Ledbetter and co. to realise this ambition, the Strategic Commander was designed as a unique combination of mouse, joystick, and gamepad. To manage the increasing complexities of the RTS games of the day, it came equipped with six programmable buttons, three shift buttons, and a three-position profile switch. Back then, these atypical features provided an unprecedented 72 possible key combinations across various configurations, all at the tip of our stubby little fingers. Better yet, it allowed us to toggle between three pre-configured profiles at a time, so that we could switch up our configurations on the fly depending on which stage of an RTS match we were in.

On release, contemporary reviewers acknowledged its originality, with critics like ActiveWin's Julien Jay describing it as "probably the most innovative (with the GameVoice) and revolutionary" peripheral designed specifically for strategy players. Others, like Neoseeker's Arnel Lim, suggested that it might’ve gone overboard with its customisation options, saying "you won't use anywhere close to all of them," in reference to the controller's programmable key combinations. Either way, the Strategic Commander failed to supplant conventional peripherals in the hands of most RTS players, and it was discontinued in 2003. As was the rest of the original SideWinder line, owing to declining sales and the industry-wide shift toward standard M+K setups for all PC games and genres.

However, unlike so many of its Microsoft-made siblings, this was not the end for the Strategic Commander. Niche as it was, it went on to enjoy a surprisingly long post-shelf life, one that was stretched out by a dedicated cult following - and that continues to this day. But why? Why, after all these years, is this forgotten peripheral from a discontinued line maintaining such a enthusiastic fanbase, years after the industry around it had written it off as a failure?

The answer lies in software and OS compatibility, as much as the hardware's own appeal. Andreas Weis was the developer of one of the first unofficial patches for the Strategic Commander, allowing players to keep on using it even after the original software stopped being compatible with newer versions the Windows. By that point, the device was a decade old, and well into 'official' obsolescence, yet Weis still answered the call of the Strategic Commander's righteously frustrated - if numerically few - fans. "There was a lot of interest back in the early 2010s in software patches to keep the Strategic Commander functional when the original software for the Strategic Commander stopped working," he explains. "Users were asking around on internet forums about how to get it to work on Windows 7 and Vista machines. Since I was studying computer science at the time, I was quite interested in reverse-engineering, and I hacked together a simple tool that would cover my use cases for the device."

In the years that followed, other patches were released to keep the Strategic Commander operational, the most notable of which was Philip Merwarth’s Strategic Engine software in 2013. Unfortunately, they weren’t open-source. Worse was that the protocol for programming the Strategic Commander wasn’t documented anywhere online in a way that was accessible for users. This is why Weis realized that he could make a unique contribution.

"I polished my old prototype code for the Strategic Commander software patch and released it as a library, so that other people could use it to write their own applications for programming the tool," he says. "As for me, I released one application that made use of the library publicly, the vJoy feeder application, which allows the device to be used with games that support joystick inputs." Curiously, Weis, by this time and like many others, had found other applications for a device with such a specific selling point, and even continues to use the Strategic Commander sparingly in 2026.

"The production quality of Microsoft hardware at the time was just top-notch, and I love the design of the old SideWinder devices, which is why I did not just throw the thing out when the software stopped working," he tells me. "It was worth keeping around to find new uses for it over the years, and I eventually realized that it makes quite a nice addition to a HOTAS (Hands-On Throttle And Stick) setup, where I could put actions that aren’t time-critical and don't fit well on the throttle on the Strategic Commander instead. For example, when I’m playing Elite Dangerous in VR, I’d put actions like deploying the landing gear on the Strategic Commander to free up buttons on the throttle for actions that were more relevant for combat."

When looking back at the history of Microsoft’s SideWinder line, it’s easy to call the Strategic Commander a failed experiment, given the fact that most of us are still using the same standard mouse-and-keyboard setup to play RTS games. However, the central premise behind its design philosophy was that the interface between a player and a game could be redesigned to cater to specific genre quirks, and decades following its release, it’s still maintained a coalition of loyal users: some RTS players who believed the Strategic Commander really pulled it off, and those found other uses for the device in other flavours of PC game. And with the issue of modern compatibility, a roadblock for so much retro hardware, bypassed by homemade patches, both camps can continue twisting and clicking their Strategic Commanders unimpeded.

All the same, I wonder if it might have basked in more conventional success, had Microsoft made more forward-thinking decisions. The Strategic Commander has proven over the years that its unprecedented customisation had applications far beyond what Ledbetter’s team had envisioned, and once the popularity of RTS games settled down, other, similar click-heavy subgenres like MOBAs and Real-Time Tactics got big as well. Would a more officially maintained, potentially refined Strategic Commander model have served those crowds? I think it might. The narrow focus on RTS games may have also hurt the device's since-demonstrated broader appeal; if Microsoft had fully capitalised on this customisation selling point by communicating its benefits to everyone, not just those playing the single most popular PC genre at the time, it could have provided the blueprint for a proper, long-lived alternative to controllers, mice, and keyboards.

Alas, gaming history is littered with revolutionary peripherals that failed to actually revolutionise the industry surrounding them. The Strategic Commander is no different, but for the fact that it’s had this surprisingly long post-shelf life - and, as a result, the opportunity for insight into what could have been done to salvage the its failures. Still, that unexpected longevity can provide come comfort to the Strategic Commander's creators, as well as its lingering users.

"I think the design made sense for its time, especially with the rising popularity of RTS games," Ledbetter reflects. "Looking back, I would have liked more time to test and refine the Strategic Commander. I’m proud of what we brought to market, and I’m glad to know that it remains beloved by many players to this day."
