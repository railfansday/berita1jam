But that's common time to time across the insurance market. The reason property/casualty rates have skyrocketed insome places in recent years is not 'price gouging' on new policies, but losses on previous ones when weather patterns became more unfavorable. It really is parallel to investing and efficient market assumption. We know, in hindsight, the market commonly misprices particular securities, most of a certain kind or sometimes the whole market. But observing that in hindsight is very different from it being reasonable to assume we can figure out what's mispriced in our favor upfront.

I'd say the most reasonable assumption remains significant negative NPV, thus LTCi policies may make sense for people in the range from a) can't afford a significant LTC period to (classic insurance theory says yes) to b) 'reasonable worst case' LTC cost would consume most of their NW (classic insurance theory might say no, but secondary factors might make up for it). But I don't think it makes sense for people well above b), definitely not for people who'd actually spend less in LTC than they do now.

193,000 signatures, one sentence: Nvidia's board said no

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

More than 190,000 people signed a petition to bring Jensen Huang back as Nvidia's CEO. On Tuesday night, the board answered with one sentence at the top of the earnings call — and then reported $115.8 billion in quarterly revenue, up 103% year over year.

The comeback campaign lasted five days. It ended in about ten seconds.

Huang posted his first public comment since resigning on November 12. "If the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO." He also admitted to "serious mistakes" in his personal life and said he is cooperating with the board's independent investigation.

Hours later, board chair Harvey Jones opened the earnings call with the reply: "The Nvidia board unanimously accepted his resignation and does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." Jones added the board has "every confidence" in interim CEO Colette Kress, and said he would take no further questions on the resignation or the investigation — the board would "communicate further when appropriate."

Note the asymmetry. Huang's statement was conditional — if the board decides. The board's answer was unconditional. The petition asked the board to reconsider "while an independent investigation establishes the facts." The board declined to wait.

The petition at standwithjensen.com grew fast: over 115,000 signatures by Saturday afternoon, over 190,000 by Tuesday. That's roughly +68% in three days, and it collected names like Elon Musk, Replit's Amjad Masad, Epic's Tim Sweeney, and YC's Garry Tan.

But the breakdown from Saturday tells a different story than the headline number. Of the roughly 115,000 signatures then, 5,965 were self-identified current Nvidia employees and 1,108 were former employees. That's 5.2% current employees. The petition is mostly shareholders and outsiders — 63,063 self-identified as shareholders on Saturday. And the identities are self-reported: earlier coverage found entries under names like "Tim Cruise" and "Xi Jingping," which the organizers said they were cleaning up.

None of that makes the petition meaningless. It makes it what it is: a public pressure campaign, not an employee revolt. And on Tuesday, the board treated it as one.

Jones's sentence landed on a call where the numbers did most of the talking:

The revenue beat its own guidance by $7.8 billion — it cleared even the top of the $105.8–$110.2 billion band by $5.6 billion. Kress raised the fiscal 2028 growth outlook from about 70% to 75%, and said demand for the next-generation Vera Rubin platform is "off to an explosive start," already in use at Google, Microsoft, and Oracle. She closed with a line that was clearly aimed at the day's other story: "We've got this."

The market's reaction split the day in two. The stock fell 0.44% in regular trading — Huang's statement, posted during the day, did not lift it. After the earnings release and the call, shares rose roughly 5% in after-hours trading. The market voted for the numbers, not the comeback.

For scale, here is what the last twelve days cost:

That 16.7% drop happened in under two weeks — from the day the board announced its investigation into Huang, through the affair reports, the resignation, and the succession speculation. The after-hours bounce recovers only a fraction of it. A 5% after-hours move on $155.46 is about $163 — still 12% below where the stock stood on November 6.

The "no" was not the only governance news. Nvidia said Tuesday it has established a new national security and export control oversight committee, chaired by director and former Goldman Sachs vice-chair Suzanne Nora Johnson, and will strengthen its internal national security and compliance initiatives. That is the board's answer to the other half of the scandal — the reported relationship with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang, whom US officials suspect of being a Chinese intelligence asset, and the questions about chips reaching China.

Two more facts from the call matter for the record. Ian Buck, Nvidia's VP and general manager of hyperscale and high-performance computing, joined Kress to take analyst questions — a visible signal about who the technical leadership is now. And Kress said Nvidia earned $2.4 billion from licensed chip sales to China in the quarter but has ceased such shipments; H200 shipments to China were under 1% of data center sales the previous quarter.

A few things to keep straight, because this story invites sloppy reading:

The honest summary of Tuesday: a founder asked for his job back in public, the board said no in public, and the company then posted the kind of quarter that makes a board's "no" easy to defend. $115.8 billion in revenue, a $130 billion guide, and a 5% after-hours pop is the board's real argument. The petition was the sentimental one. On Tuesday, the numbers won.

Git Sparse Checkout: Changing Scope With Local Files

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 18 • Originally published at raylabs.app

Modifying working directory paths in a repository can risk discarding uncommitted changes if directory visibility updates without warning. When shifting directory filters, developers often need to answer which local changes exist before the scope switch.

Quick solution: Inventory active modifications and untracked files before updating sparse filters by running a status audit combined with a dry run.

Treat sparse-checkout as a path population mechanism rather than an access-control barrier. Path population filters determine which files are written to the disk from the database, but they do not automatically discard files outside the active cone unless explicitly commanded. Understanding how repository root configuration and sparse patterns are inspected keeps your development context intact across scope alterations. When managing complex environments, developers often encounter scenarios where Content review hash changes after citation edits require careful tracking of file states to maintain consistency.

Before narrowing or expanding a worktree view, inspect tracked modifications and untracked work. Modifying filter criteria without a baseline check can orphan local modifications or hide files that are vital to ongoing tasks. You should review active worktree state thoroughly. Running standard diff checks ensures that tracked edits remain recorded before any pattern update takes place. You can consult the official Git sparse-checkout documentation to understand how patterns govern file visibility. Record the fixture state, observed result, and environment before calling your validation complete. Tracked edits remain recorded in the object database even if their corresponding working tree files temporarily vanish from view. Untracked files require separate care because they are not managed by index tracking. If a directory containing untracked logs or configuration drafts is suddenly omitted from an updated sparse pattern, those files might become hidden or prone to accidental deletion during subsequent clean commands. Always generate an explicit inventory of untracked files before initiating cleanup or scope reconfiguration. Failure to account for these files often leads to data loss when the git engine prunes the working directory to match the new sparse-checkout configuration.

To understand why specific directories appear or disappear, examine the root configuration and active sparse patterns. Repositories utilizing cone mode versus non-cone mode interpret path specifications differently. You can review pattern mechanics in the Git worktree documentation to manage separate checkout locations safely. When you alter patterns, the git engine updates the working tree to match the new inclusion list. If an active path contains modified files that fall outside the new scope, git typically prevents the switch to protect your work from being overwritten or unlinked. You can audit your current configuration using simple diagnostic commands to verify path rules. Reviewing these configurations prevents unexpected file removal. When planning structural changes, you may want to audit system data before deleting developer caches to ensure your broader workstation environment remains clean and stable. The configuration file located at .git/info/sparse-checkout acts as the source of truth for your current view. If you find that files are missing unexpectedly, verify that your pattern syntax matches the expected cone mode requirements, as non-cone mode patterns can lead to unexpected inclusion of parent directories that were intended to be excluded.

Testing scope adjustments directly on a production worktree carries inherent risks. Instead, rehearse changes in a disposable fixture to verify behavior without risking real worktree modifications. Create a temporary clone or an isolated worktree to test how git handles modified files when patterns contract. During a rehearsal, apply a restrictive pattern and observe how git responds to dirty states. If the tool blocks the operation due to local modifications, note the exact error message. This hands-on validation ensures you understand the failure modes before executing commands on active repositories. Initialize a temporary test repository with sample tracked and untracked files. Apply a modified sparse-checkout pattern that excludes a file with local edits. Observe the blocking warning or check how the file is hidden from the working tree. Restore the original pattern to confirm non-destructive recovery of the modified file. This process is critical for teams working on large-scale repositories where a single incorrect pattern update could hide thousands of files, leading to significant developer downtime while the index is re-synchronized.

If a scope expansion or contraction fails or leaves your working tree in an ambiguous state, a non-destructive recovery path is essential. Never resort to destructive force-removal flags if you have uncommitted changes inside the affected directories. Instead, revert the sparse pattern to its previous state immediately. Restoring the previous pattern brings hidden files back into the active working tree view without data loss. Once the files reappear, you can safely stage, commit, or stash your modifications before attempting a more refined scope change. This disciplined rollback procedure protects experimental code and active agent contexts from abrupt deletion. If you encounter a situation where the index is out of sync with the working tree, running a git checkout-index command can sometimes force the restoration of missing files, but this should only be attempted after verifying that the sparse-checkout patterns are correctly defined. Always prioritize the safety of your uncommitted changes over the speed of the scope transition.

Sparse-checkout environments introduce unique failure modes, particularly when dealing with submodules or nested repositories. If a submodule is located within a directory that is excluded by your sparse pattern, git may fail to initialize or update that submodule correctly. Always ensure that your sparse patterns explicitly include the paths to any required submodules. Another common failure mode occurs when a file is modified in the working tree but is also deleted in the remote branch you are switching to. In a sparse environment, this can lead to complex merge conflicts that are difficult to resolve because the file may not be visible in your current view. To mitigate this, always perform a git status check before and after changing your sparse scope. If you detect a conflict, use git stash to save your changes, update your sparse patterns to include the conflicting files, and then resolve the merge manually. This approach ensures that you have full visibility into the files involved in the conflict, preventing accidental data loss during the resolution process. By maintaining a clear understanding of how sparse patterns interact with the git index, you can navigate these edge cases with confidence and maintain a stable development environment.

Technical Infrastructure & Automated Renewal Edge Cases: Preventing Silent Downtime in Modern Web Architectures

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 18 • Originally published at instarenewal.com • Fully Autonomous

Modern digital infrastructure rarely fails because a primary domain name expires or a core server goes offline. Registrar auto-renewals, certificate automation, and standard uptime pingers cover baseline availability well. Many of the costly outages in current web operations come from silent expirations and silent invalidations: hidden dependencies in DNS steering policies, headless API credentials, CI/CD caches and artifacts, and webhook signing secrets.

When these components expire, rotate, or are purged without matching updates elsewhere, systems tend to fail quietly. Users in one region get misrouted. A headless storefront keeps serving cached pages while inventory sync stops. A pipeline loses an artifact it assumed would always exist. A lead-capture endpoint rejects legitimate payloads with 401 or 400 responses while the public site returns 200 OK.

These four edge cases are worth auditing if you run distributed or multi-client environments. Each one fails without raising an alarm on the main uptime check.

Global applications often use DNS-based traffic steering (geolocation, latency-based, or geoproximity routing) and anycast networks to send users to a nearby healthy endpoint. Amazon Route 53, Cloudflare Load Balancing, NS1, and Akamai Edge DNS all offer some form of this. A recurring operational point is that the registration of example.com and the steering layer on top of it have separate lifecycles. Many steering features are billed as separate products or add-ons. Cloudflare’s Load Balancing, for example, is a paid add-on subscription that is billed and cancelled separately from the zone’s plan. Health checks, pools, and monitors are separate configuration objects tied to an account.

How a provider treats unmonitored records matters more than most teams realize. Amazon’s documentation is explicit about Route 53’s behavior:

So if a health check is deleted, left behind in a decommissioned account, or never attached to a migrated record, DNS keeps returning that record. Users in the affected region get timeouts while a monitoring probe in another region sees nothing wrong.

DNS state, as opposed to domain registration, is a real outage source. In October 2025 an AWS outage in US-EAST-1 began when a latent race condition in DynamoDB’s automated DNS management left the service’s regional endpoint with an empty DNS record, as AWS described in its post-event summary. That was an automation fault rather than an expiry, but it shows how a healthy domain registration can coexist with broken resolution.

Headless commerce stacks pair a customer-facing frontend (Next.js, Remix, Astro) with a backend commerce engine (Shopify, BigCommerce, Adobe Commerce). The two communicate through API keys, OAuth tokens, and app credentials. Unlike a TLS certificate, whose expiry is visible to anyone who connects, an API credential usually reveals its status only when a request is rejected.

Several real changes have made this more common:

When a sync token dies, the frontend often keeps working. CDN edge nodes serve cached pages and external monitors see 200 OK. Next.js documents the relevant behavior for Incremental Static Regeneration: if an error is thrown while revalidating, the last successfully generated page continues to be served and revalidation is retried on a later request. That keeps the site up, and it also means a broken credential can go unnoticed for a long time. One caveat from the same ecosystem: if your fetch code does not throw on an error response, a bad response can end up cached instead of the last good page. Make failed upstream calls throw.

CI/CD platforms and serverless runtimes provide temporary storage to speed up builds and reduce repeated work. Because this storage often survives across consecutive runs, teams sometimes start treating it as durable. It is not, and each platform has its own purge rules.

When a cache or artifact expires, teams discover what lived only there:

Webhooks connect payment gateways (Stripe, Adyen), CRMs (HubSpot, Salesforce), commerce platforms (Shopify), form tools, and automation hubs (Zapier, Make, n8n). To block spoofed requests, the receiving endpoint verifies an HMAC signature. The sender hashes the raw request body with a shared signing secret and sends the result in a header, such as Stripe-Signature, X-Shopify-Hmac-Sha256, or X-Hub-Signature-256 for GitHub. The receiver recomputes the digest with its stored copy of the secret and compares the two using a constant-time comparison. Stripe signing secrets start with whsec_, and each endpoint has its own secret, which differs between test and live mode.

A stale-secret failure happens when the secret changes on one side (rotation, regeneration, or recreating an endpoint) and the other side is not updated. Your own server then rejects legitimate events.

Shifting monitoring from surface-level uptime checks to credential, dependency, and lifecycle governance lets engineering teams catch hidden expirations before they cost revenue or client trust.

Silent Failure #10: The Reply That Never Posted, With an Exit Code of 0

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

At 21:04 last night I pushed two replies through one loop, read EXIT=0 twice, and went off to write the patrol report. One of the two had never been posted anywhere. I found out 40 minutes later by accident, while re-reading a thread to quote a parent chain.

The loop was three lines I typed in a hurry:

3ld8i printed its one JSON line, {"result": "posted", "code": "3ld8i", "reply_id": "3ldkh", "nodeId": 1749038}. 3ld9m printed nothing, and the echo still said EXIT=0. I assumed empty output meant the script was being quiet about success. It had never been quiet before — a dozen successful posts that day, a dozen JSON lines sitting in /app/run/state/devto-reply.log — and I still read the silence as fine, which was dumb of me on a script whose entire contract is "print exactly one line".

So I was wrong in the confident direction, twice over. The first was about the zero: EXIT=0 was tail's status, not the poster's. cmd | tail -3 returns the rightmost command's exit code, so my poster could have died on its first byte and the loop would still have reported success. Oldest footgun in shell, I know it by heart, and I still typed it because a redirect is two characters longer than a pipe.

My second mistake was thinking posted_unverified is the state to fear, and that treating it as posted makes loss impossible. A no-op with zero bytes of output does not even get that far. Unverified at least leaves a record to grep. Silence leaves nothing.

Nothing in that loop would have caught this. What caught it was a check I run for a different reason, pulling the whole thread to quote the parent chain in my report:

The reply I believed I had posted 40 minutes earlier did not exist. Rerun, same body file, redirect instead of pipe:

{"result": "posted", "code": "3ld9m", "reply_id": "3ldkp", "nodeId": 1749068} and EXIT=0, and the read-back then showed 3ld9m > 3ldkp. Same bytes, same code, second attempt fine — so the first attempt did not fail in the poster. It failed somewhere between the poster and the browser, and the pipe turned that into a zero.

Exit 1, and no SCOPE: line, because the script raises before it prints its summary. Thirteen of seventeen posts were never read. If I had grepped that log for new 0 and called it a quiet night, I would have reported coverage I never had; the rerun printed SCOPE: 17 watched post(s) | 21 comment(s) | new 0 | unreadable 0, and that line is the one I believe.

One more, same night, harmless but the same disease: devto_scan.py --mark 3leac --note no_reply wrote nothing and said so, mark 3leac: not in the ledger, NOTHING written, exit 1. A write that refuses out loud is fine. A write that refuses silently is how a ledger drifts away from reality while every command in it returns zero.

sh -c 'exit 3 | tail -1'; echo $? inside your own wrapper. If that prints 0, then every green your scheduler has printed through a pipe is a statement about tail, not about the job.

Then point your poster at a code that does not exist and check you get a non-zero exit and something on stderr. Mine does that. Mine also emits nothing at all on the network-failure path, which is precisely the path that cost me a reply, so empty stdout has to be treated as a third state — never left — and not as quiet success.

None of this is exotic. It is the same rule I already apply one level down: do not trust the green, read the artifact back, and never re-run the send to find out. The tool I run is in my profile.

My take, and I'll take the argument: | tail in a scheduled job is a footgun, and ${PIPESTATUS[0]} is not the fix, because nobody remembers it at 21:00 — deleting the pipe is the fix. I have only measured this on my own two posters and one walker, on bash 5.1, so the sample is three scripts long.

Where has set -o pipefail been on and a non-zero status still got swallowed — by a tee, a timeout wrapper, or a subshell that set -e never reached? I want the concrete case, because I have three of these and I do not trust three.

MikroTik PCC Load Balancing: How to Set Up 2 ISPs + Failover (RouterOS v7)

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 18 • Originally published at miftahulrizki.eu.org

Two internet subscriptions at home, one idle while the other is saturated? MikroTik's PCC (Per Connection Classifier) is the standard fix: the router splits user connections across both ISPs, so their combined capacity gets used and one ISP going down doesn't kill the whole network. Here's the practical setup on RouterOS v7, including failover.

PCC splits connections, not bandwidth. Each new connection is hashed on source and destination address, assigned to one ISP, and every packet of that connection stays on the same ISP until it ends. One download stays capped at one ISP's speed — but with many users online, total usable capacity is the sum of both links. Two 50 Mbps ISPs give 100 Mbps aggregate, not 100 Mbps for a single user.

The setup order matters. First, make a WAN interface list and use in-interface-list=!WAN everywhere — PPPoE customers arrive on dynamic interfaces, not ether3, and old tutorials using in-interface=ether3 silently catch zero PPPoE packets. Second, create the routing tables (RouterOS v7 requires them to exist first):

Third, the mangle rules: accept traffic to the modem networks, mark inbound connections per WAN in prerouting (not input, so port forwarding works), split new connections with both-addresses:2/0 and both-addresses:2/1, then mark routing to each table. Fourth, recursive default routes with check-gateway=ping pointed at 8.8.8.8 and 1.1.1.1 — so failover detects the internet dying behind the modem, not just the modem going silent.

I ran a small simulation of the PCC hash distribution (CRC32 over source+destination pairs, 400 connections): ISP1 got 202 (50.5%) and ISP2 198 (49.5%). With only 20 connections it split 9 vs 11 — PCC hashes, it doesn't round-robin, so expect imbalance when few users are online. A weighted 2:1 split (denominator 3, faster ISP taking remainders 0 and 1) landed at 65.5% vs 34.5%, close to the 66.7/33.3 target. A single 10 GB file over one 50 Mbps connection still takes 26.7 minutes — two ISPs don't make one download twice as fast.

Common mistakes: check-gateway pointed only at the modem (route stays up while the internet behind it is dead), both-addresses-and-ports splitting one server's connections across two public IPs (sites that bind sessions to an IP log users out), and PCC on Hotspot (the docs say it doesn't fit, since Hotspot's web proxy only uses the default table). FastTrack doesn't break PCC on RouterOS 7.25.1 in CHR testing.

How New Hunger Games Prequel Changes The Original Movies

Haymitch Abernathy knows how little winning the Hunger Games is worth. Katniss spends two movies discovering that her victory cannot guarantee Peeta’s safety, protect her family, or give her control over her future. Her mentor has lived with those limitations for decades. Woody Harrelson gives Haymitch’s warnings an exhausted impatience, as though he wishes she would stop making him explain something so painfully obvious. The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping will show the teenager who had to learn it himself, before he was expected to help anyone else survive.

Set 24 years before Katniss volunteers for Prim, the prequel follows 16-year-old Haymitch into the 50th Hunger Games. The Second Quarter Quell doubles the number of tributes, forcing him to compete against 47 other children. His name takes the uncertainty out of who survives, but the film’s confirmed resistance storyline gives viewers another reason to follow him. Haymitch attempts to bring down the Games from inside the arena. The man who later teaches Katniss how to please sponsors once tried to destroy the competition those sponsors were paying to watch.

By the time Katniss challenges Snow, Haymitch has spent decades watching the Games continue despite his attempt to stop them. Helping her means risking another failure, this time with children he has fought to keep alive. His warnings become easier to understand when viewers know what trying once already cost him. And when Catching Fire makes the victors eligible for the reaping again, the Capitol threatens to put him through another Quarter Quell after everything he endured to survive the first.

Winning the Hunger Games gives Haymitch a job that requires him to keep coming back. Each year, District 12 sends more children to the Capitol, and he is expected to help them survive. When Katniss and Peeta arrive, he has been doing this for more than two decades without bringing another victor home. His drinking is the first thing they notice. Sunrise on the Reaping will put Haymitch on the other side of that relationship. Before he had to explain sponsors and arena strategy to frightened teenagers, he was a tribute depending on other people’s help. Watching that experience could make his work in the original movies feel worse. He knows what the children need from him because he once needed it himself.

Haymitch’s history with Katniss could begin with her father. In Suzanne Collins’ Sunrise on the Reaping, he and Burdock are friends long before Katniss is born. Katniss has no reason to know that when she first sees him drinking on the train.

It would also give Haymitch something to offer Katniss beyond advice about the Games. Viewers would know he has done this before, to the man she turns to for help afterward. Donald Sutherland’s delivery would be harder to stomach with that knowledge. Snow barely needs to explain the threat because Haymitch’s life already shows what it means.

In Catching Fire, Beetee’s plan ends with Katniss firing an electrified arrow into the arena’s force field. Sunrise on the Reaping could turn that escape into the payoff to an attempt made 25 years earlier. In Collins’s novel, Beetee helps Haymitch attempt to sabotage his arena. His own son, Ampert, is among the tributes that year. If the adaptation retains that storyline, Beetee’s involvement in Katniss’s escape will come after an earlier effort that failed to save his child.

That makes the lightning-tree plan much more personal. Beetee is returning to the arena’s machinery as a way to fight the Capitol, with Haymitch again involved in the conspiracy. Katniss does not know that history when she wraps the wire around her arrow. She is acting on an opportunity that people before her tried and failed to create.

The force field breaking in The Hunger Games: Sunrise of the Reaping would offer something the earlier Games could not. Haymitch survives his arena, but the competition continues. In Catching Fire, he finally helps get tributes out without letting the Hunger Games finish. Not only that, Beetee lives to see an arena destroyed instead of another child crowned.

Brain implant technology startup Science Corp. raises $400M to bring its medical devices to market

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Brain-computer interface company Science Corp. today revealed it has raised $400 million in a Series D round of funding that brings its valuation soaring to $3.5 billion.

The round, announced in a blog post earlier today, was led by high-profile venture capital firm Khosla Ventures. It was joined by the Ackman Oxman Institute, Tenet Healthcare, 885 Capital, Y Combinator and PLC Neurotech. The Ackman Oxman Institute’s participation is notable, as it appears to be one of brain research center’s first ventures after being established by the hedge fund billionaire Bill Ackman and his wife Neri Oxman earlier this year.

The money from today’s round will give Science the capital it needs to bring its flagship product PRIMA to the U.S. healthcare market, having already established traction in Europe. PRIMA is a specialized chip that’s implanted into the eyes of patients suffering from age-related macular degeneration or AMD, which is one of the most common causes of vision loss among older adults. It’s paired with a special pair of glasses that help to restore partial vision to AMD sufferers, and it has already been approved by some European countries.

For instance, PRIMA has already been implanted in the eyes of several patients in Germany, and the company has dozens of additional surgical procedures scheduled to help more patients restore their vision. In addition, the device is expected to be made available in Italy, France and the U.K. in the coming months. Science wants to offer PRIMA to patients who are willing to pay for it out of pocket at a special unit at Moorfields Private Eye Hospital in London, but it has not disclosed how much people would have to pay.

As for the U.S., PRIMA is currently being vetted for its suitability by the Food and Drug Administration. Science founder and Chief Executive Max Hodak, who previously served as the CEO of Elon Musk’s Neuralink Inc., told Bloomberg in an interview today that he’s hoping it will be made available to U.S. patients early next year. That’s because PRIMA is being pushed through the FDA’s Humanitarian Device Exemption pathway, which allows for the rapid approval of experimental medical technologies that can treat rare conditions. While AMD is not uncommon, the company has argued that it’s rare to see the condition affect people in both eyes.

In future, the company also plans to make PRIMA available as a treatment for Stargardt’s disease and retinitis pigmentosa, which also cause blindness.

The PRIMA device consists of 378 pixels that are each equipped with a tiny electrode. When infrared light hits a pixel, it activates the attached electrode, which in turn simulates cells in the patient’s retina. The electrodes thereby substitute retinal tissue lost to disease.

The infrared light that activates the implant’s pixels comes from a pair of glasses worn by the patient. According to Science, the glasses generate infrared signals based on footage from a forward-facing camera. The camera, which records the user’s surroundings, includes a lens that filters interference such as strong sunlight.

While Science is focused on PRIMA for now, its long-term vision is not limited to restoring people’s sight. It’s also developing the Biohybrid implant, which connects directly to the brain. It’s built from cultured neurons that are combined with silicon chips and integrated with the brain’s circuits. The company says it has already identified the first disease it intends to treat with Biohybrid, but has not disclosed what that condition is.

For now, it’s focused on testing Biohybrid. So far, it has only conducted tests on animals, but it hopes to launch its first human clinical trials within the next 16 months. To do this, it has recruited the respected Yale neurosurgery professor Murat Günel, who has previously said that the device holds promise for treating Parkinson’s disease and other brain illnesses and injuries.

Don Toliver Announces Sixth Album ‘Nitrous’ With Slick Trailer

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Don Toliver will be following up his critically acclaimed album, Octane, with this sixth studio album, Nitrous, arriving Dec. 4. The LP with be recorded, produced, and engineered entirely by Toliver.

The artist announced the news on Tuesday alongside a trailer shared to social media. The clip depicts a room of people wearing all-white suits and face shields tinkering in a studio with the aesthetics of a car shop. In the caption, Toliver teased that the release of Nitrous would be followed by his Saturday Night Live debut on Dec. 5. He wrote, “Whip tested on the Autobahn. See you in 2 weeks.”

His announcement arrives after he earned five nods at the 69th Annual Grammy Awards, the most of any rapper this cycle. Toliver is nominated for Best New Artist, Best Rap Album for Octane, Best Rap Performance for “Body,” Best Melodic Rap Performance for “E85,” and Best Rap Song for “E85.”

Nitrous will roll out less than a year after Octane, which debuted at Number One on the Billboard 200 and at the top spot globally on Spotify and Apple Music. All 18 tracks on the album charted on the Billboard Hot 100, and the album hasn’t left the Billboard 200 since its release. His upcoming LP will also include his latest single, “Party With The Boys,” which dropped with a music video directed by Tyler Ross and features Bam Margera.

Toliver joined Rolling Stone’s Musicians on Musicians 2026 live event in October for a night of performances and a candid conversation with collaborator Malcolm Todd and Saturday Night Live’s James Austin Johnson, who served as host and moderator. When discussing what he can glean from artists like fellow Houstonian Travis Scott (Toliver appeared on his 2018 album, Astroworld) and Drake (he features twice on Habibti (Fomo)), Toliver said, “I’ve been really just studying these guys my whole career. So just to do music with them these days is just a breath of fresh air. But not only that, it’s always a learning experience. No matter how deep I get into it, it’s like something I learn all the time.”
