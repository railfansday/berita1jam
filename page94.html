13 common sourdough mistakes and what each one really costs a loaf

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Home bakers make the same 13 sourdough slips. See which ones ruin a loaf, which only blemish it, and which signs to trust over the timer.

A home baker can follow a sourdough recipe to the letter, measuring every gram and setting every timer, and still pull a different loaf from the oven each time. The reason is that a starter is a living culture, and no two starters are quite alike. Smithsonian reports that in a study led by microbiologist Anne Madden of North Carolina State University and her colleagues, starters made with identical flour each carried a different mix of microbes. Two people with the same instructions are therefore not working with the same ingredient. Because the mix of microbes varies from jar to jar, the hours in any recipe are a rough guide rather than a promise. Most of the mistakes that follow have the same cause: the baker trusts the calendar or the clock instead of watching what the starter and the dough are actually doing.

These mistakes do not all cost the same thing, and the costliest can be hard to see. A loaf that spreads flat or a crumb that stays gummy shows the problem right away. Lost flavor is quieter. The tang of sourdough comes from lactic acid and acetic acid, according to Smithsonian. A baker can't add that taste at the end, and a loaf rushed through its stages can rise well, look right and still taste like ordinary bread. Some warnings that bakers repeat are milder than they sound, and getting them wrong costs little that anyone would notice at the table. Knowing which mistakes ruin a loaf and which ones only blemish it saves a home baker time, flour and frustration.

Allrecipes set out to identify the errors home bakers commonly make when baking sourdough. These are familiar slips from everyday kitchens, so the collection works as a practical checklist for anyone with a jar of starter on the counter. The mistakes range widely. One concerns what comes out of the tap, and another concerns how a baker slashes the loaf before it goes into the oven. A third concerns the starter most people pour down the drain. Each mistake raises two questions: which sign a baker should trust in place of the timer, and how much getting it wrong really costs. The slides that follow answer both questions for every item.

Bakers who have just stirred flour and water into a jar want bread from it right away, and giving in to that urge ranks first among common sourdough mistakes, according to Allrecipes. A fresh starter needs roughly a week of maturing before it can raise a loaf. The error is about timing rather than technique, and a baker makes it before any dough is mixed. That week is a minimum, and a starter is not guaranteed to be ready when it ends. Bakers who adopt a piece of an established starter can skip the wait, because the warning applies only to a culture grown from scratch.

Even a full week may be too early. Smithsonian reports that a starter doesn't settle into a steady state, with yeasts producing enough gas to lift dough, until somewhere between Days 10 and 14. By that later timeline, a loaf baked after a few days, or even after a week, could come from a starter that hasn't yet settled. The practical course is to start checking around the one-week mark and to expect that a second week may still be needed. While they wait, bakers can influence which microbes take hold through their choice of flour, how warm they keep the room, and their feeding schedule.

The common trap is reading activity in the jar as readiness. "Yes, your starter might be bubbly, but that doesn't make it strong," Allrecipes author Stacey Ballis said. Bubbles show that the culture is alive, but a starter is ready only when its yeasts make enough gas to raise a loaf.

Readiness depends on what the starter can do, not on how many days have passed. A baker who waits for the starter to show that strength loses a few days and avoids baking a flat loaf.

Home bakers have reason to worry about the most ordinary ingredient they use. Allrecipes ranks unfiltered tap water second among 13 common sourdough mistakes. Most people never think twice about the water they pour into a starter, so it is unsettling to hear that water from the faucet could put the jar at risk. The concern is that chlorine in untreated tap water can kill the yeast a starter depends on. The open question is how often that happens in a home kitchen.

The danger may be smaller than its ranking suggests. "Some recipes advise using bottled or filtered water because chlorine can kill yeast, but I've never had a problem with chlorinated Toronto tap water," Jessica Brooks, a digital producer and pro-trained cook and baker at CBC Life, said. Neither side has settled the matter. A cautious baker can filter the water to guard against a possible problem. Still, tap water is no sure way to lose a loaf, and nobody needs to throw out a starter because it was fed from the faucet.

The bigger risk is a wrong diagnosis, because a baker who finds a sluggish jar may blame the tap and change the wrong thing. CBC reports that a new starter normally goes through a slowdown in activity. A quiet jar during that early slowdown may have nothing to do with the water. The real test is the culture itself. If a starter bubbles and rises on tap water, the water is fine.

Switch to filtered water only if the starter stays flat after that normal early slowdown, and treat the switch as a cheap experiment rather than a rescue.

Water looks like the one ingredient in sourdough that needs no thought, a neutral liquid poured between the flour and the starter. Bakers can still get its temperature wrong at every stage, from feeding the starter to mixing the dough, according to Allrecipes. A baker who pours carelessly can harm the culture well before any loaf exists. The temperature of that water sets how fast the culture works. Yeast works more slowly in cold water and can die in water that is too hot, and Julie Van Rosendaal, who writes recipes for CBC News, says a starter grows faster when it is warm and slower when it is cold. A baker who misses the mark needs to know less about one perfect number than about which side of it the water landed on.

A starter recovers from cold water, even though the warning sounds severe. Stacey Ballis, an author and recipe developer, warned that water that is too cold will "shock the yeast." Bakers also chill starters on purpose to slow them down. Van Rosendaal keeps her starter in the refrigerator and feeds it once a week, according to CBC News, and she revives a dormant starter by feeding it at room temperature for one to two days. A chilled culture loses days, not its life. The price of too-cold water is a slower start, and the remedy is to wait for the starter to warm up and grow active again rather than judge it by the clock.

Too-hot water is the riskier error, because it can kill the yeast rather than only slow it. Heat harms yeast in other settings too, and drying a starter in even a low oven kills the yeast, according to CBC News. When unsure, pour the water cooler rather than warmer, and judge the result by how lively the starter becomes rather than by how warm the water felt.

A cooler pour costs a baker some time, and a hotter one can cost the yeast.

Rushing a sourdough loaf throws away the reason to bake one. When you want bread by dinner, the temptation is to squeeze every stage, but Allrecipes reports that sourdough gets its texture from a slow, cold fermentation, and a hurried loaf loses what sets it apart. Commercial yeast is the tool for same-day bread. The Santa Fe New Mexican reports that Andree Falls, owner of Sage Bakehouse in Santa Fe, N.M., considers yeast-raised dough too quick to become anything distinctive. A rushed sourdough therefore ends up as a weaker version of a yeast loaf. Impatience is less a technical slip than the wrong choice of bread for the time you have.

Waiting is not a luxury reserved for hobbyists with empty weekends. Sage Bakehouse uses no commercial yeast at all and raises its bread only with sourdough starter, according to The Santa Fe New Mexican. The bakery keeps that slow pace while turning out 700 to 1,000 loaves a day. A home baker with a single loaf has even less reason to cut the wait short.

Patience means judging the dough, not setting the timer longer and walking away. Serious bakers plan in hours and even days, and no single number is the right one. Stacey Ballis, who wrote the Allrecipes guide, has rested her dough 24 to 72 hours in the fridge before shaping. Sage Bakehouse averages 8 to 10 hours of fermentation after mixing, according to The Santa Fe New Mexican. The first is one baker's practice, and the second is one bakery's average. Even with an average to go by, the bakery lets the dough decide when it is ready. "We say the bread is the boss," Falls said. For a home baker, the practical change is to plan the day around the dough rather than the dough around the day.

The urge to hurry comes back during the autolyse, gluten development and proofing. The Santa Fe New Mexican reports that Falls sees good baking as dedicated care for the dough from the start of its process to the end.

A baker who learns to wait at this stage avoids several of the mistakes still ahead.

The autolyse is easy to cut because nothing seems to happen during it. Flour, water and starter sit in a bowl while the baker waits. That rest does real work, though. Resting the mix without salt hydrates the flour and improves the loaf's rise, and the step adds only about 45 minutes to the bake, according to Allrecipes. A baker who skips it moves into the rest of the bake with flour that hasn't had that time to hydrate. The step depends on holding back the salt as much as on waiting, so skipping the autolyse means adding an ingredient early, not just saving time. That makes the shortcut a poor bargain. The baker saves less than an hour and gives up some of the rise, one of the results anyone can see when the loaf comes out of the oven.

The advice comes with no exception attached. "I recommend you do not skip this step. Period," said Stacey Ballis, who wrote the Allrecipes guide. The fix asks for no special skill, tool or difficult judgment. It asks only for a pause, with the salt kept out of the bowl until the pause is over. The length of that pause is approximate, too. The goal is to give the flour time with its water before the salt goes in, not to hit an exact mark on the clock. A baker who reads the dough this way can set the timer loosely.

Of all the steps a hurried baker might cut, this one costs the least to keep.

A baker checks gluten with the hands, not with a timer. Dough without enough gluten fails the windowpane test, in which a baker stretches a small piece between the fingers, and it tears instead of stretching thin, according to Allrecipes. No set number of minutes or folds guarantees strong gluten, so a baker keeps kneading until the dough passes the stretch test. A recipe's kneading time is only an estimate based on someone else's dough. This step makes the plainest case for watching the dough instead of the clock, because the baker can see the result.

A dough that keeps tearing may reflect the mixing method rather than the baker's skill. The Gazette reports that gluten develops less in hand-mixed dough than in dough made with a mixer, and weak gluten is what makes dough tear. Abigail Lucas, lead instructor at We're Cookin' Now!, considers a stand mixer essential equipment, and she recommends one whose paddle has a silicone edge. A mixer may help a baker pass a test that keeps failing by hand, but it guarantees nothing. The windowpane still decides when the kneading is done, and a machine may only help the baker get there sooner.

The type of flour also affects how easily the gluten comes together. Stacey Ballis, who wrote the Allrecipes guide, says whole-grain flours make the job harder because they contain less natural gluten. A baker who works with whole-grain flour should therefore expect a tougher time getting the dough to pass the windowpane test. The effort at this stage also pays off at a step still to come, and Ballis said that "you will find the work at the early stages will help you tremendously when it comes to forming your eventual loaves."

Proofing is the stage when a baker sets the shaped dough aside and waits. Waiting can feel like doing nothing, so this is the step a hurried baker is most likely to cut short. Allrecipes calls underproofing an error that beginners make easily and that carries a high risk, and a baker who underproofs ends up with big tunnels or a heavy, damp crumb. A loaf like that is plainly wrong inside, not just a little bland. The trouble stays hidden under the crust, and the baker finds it only when slicing into the bread. By then the flour and the day are already spent, and nothing can fix it.

The fix is to wait longer than feels necessary, not to time the proof more precisely. When the dough proofs in the refrigerator, a full day is the starting point rather than the goal. "I use 24 hours as the bare minimum for a cold-proofed sourdough, which is super forgiving," Stacey Ballis wrote in the Allrecipes guide. A baker who holds the dough cold that long can miss the exact hour without harming the loaf. In proofing, the real danger is giving the dough too little time. The safer habit is to plan for a long, cold proof and stop treating a recipe's shortest time as the target.

A baker who leaves the dough in the fridge for a full day stops depending on exact timing and gains room for error.

Shaping can look like tidying up, a quick tuck and roll that makes the dough neat before it goes into the oven. Its real purpose is structural. When a baker shapes a loaf, the work pulls the outer layer of the dough taut, and loaves shaped without that surface tension spread flat, according to Allrecipes. Dough with nothing tight across its outside has no support to hold it upright, and it bakes wide and low instead of tall. Poor shaping therefore costs a loaf its form. That result is decided in a few minutes of handling, after the starter has matured and the dough has fermented. A baker can get every earlier stage right and still lose the loaf's height at this point.

No count of minutes or folds tells a baker when the shaping is done. The dough on the counter shows whether it worked, and the check is its posture. "Your formed dough should sit up a little proudly on the counter surface, and not slump," Stacey Ballis, who wrote the Allrecipes guide, said. A shaped round that holds its height has the tension it needs to stand in the oven. A round that sags and widens on the counter lacks that tension, and the baker can see the problem in plain view before any heat is applied. That check takes a few seconds and needs no thermometer, scale or timer.

A slumping round is a warning a baker can see and act on while the loaf is still raw, rather than discover once it comes out of the oven flat.

Before it goes into the oven, a sourdough loaf has to be slashed through its skin. In the heat the dough expands quickly and has to split open somewhere, and the baker's slash decides where. When the baker cuts too shallow or skips the cut, the loaf tears wherever its skin is weakest. A cut that is too shallow, or no cut at all, can make a loaf blow out and bake misshapen and dense, according to Allrecipes. The sign to watch is in the cut itself. Bakers should "score deeply enough to breach the tight outer surface and go a bit into the dough underneath," said Stacey Ballis, who wrote the Allrecipes guide. Half an inch is the depth to aim for, but it is a target rather than a minimum every loaf must reach. The purpose of the slash is to get through that taut surface, not to hit an exact number.

How much a weak score hurts the bread depends on the rest of the bake. A blowout or a dense loaf is not certain, though. Tasting Table reports that an unscored loaf keeps essentially the same taste and texture, as long as every other step was done right and the starter is active. A dense loaf after a botched cut may therefore point to an earlier fault, such as a sluggish starter. For a baker whose starter and proofing are sound, a missed slash mostly costs the loaf its shape and some of its height. That is a flaw in appearance, not a ruined bake. Careful scoring won't fix a loaf with other problems, and a careless cut leaves the baker no room for them.

Avoiding this mistake costs a beginner almost nothing. A beginner needs no elaborate design, and a plain X $TWTR or a square made from four slashes will serve, according to Tasting Table, while a Fenikara professional bread lame sells on Amazon $AMZN for under $7.

Get the blade through the skin with a cheap tool and a plain pattern, and save the worry for the steps that can ruin the crumb.

A baker who ends the bake too soon ruins the loaf outright, because the inside never finishes cooking. Allrecipes warns that underbaked loaves come out damp and doughy. The loaf can look ready on the outside and still be raw in the middle. A finished loaf reaches 200 degrees F inside, so the baker needs to check that temperature rather than count minutes. No set bake time stands in for that reading. The fix is to measure the loaf, not to add a few minutes to the timer and hope. A recipe's bake time only suggests when a baker should start checking. The temperature at the center of the loaf is the sign that the bread is done.

Checking the center takes a tool that many kitchens already have. With a meat thermometer, a baker can read the temperature inside the bread directly instead of guessing from the color of the crust. A baker without a thermometer can still check by hand with the thump test. Either way, no special equipment is required, and a baker who underbakes a loaf has skipped the check rather than lacked the means to make it. Once the reading or the test confirms the bread is done, the baker can take it out with confidence instead of by guesswork.

For those not familiar, O’Connor, a 36-year-old Brit who grew up in Cheltenham, has been a working actor for more than a decade, but his star has only really begun to shine in the 2020s. He won an Emmy for playing Prince Charles in The Crown, and more recently has made splashes in movies like the thrilling and sexy sports film Challengers, Rian Johnson’s third Knives Out film Wake Up Dead Man (which, ironically, he appeared in alongside previous Bond Daniel Craig), and this summer’s Steven Spielberg blockbuster Disclosure Day.

It’s not hard to understand why some people’s minds might naturally push against the idea of O’Connor as Bond. Jack Lowden has only played one of television’s most beloved spies for six seasons on Slow Horses—the fit would be seamless. Whoever Villeneuve picks (even if it’s the unnamed mystery actor) will be afforded a huge amount of goodwill.

But isn’t the fact that O’Connor elicits such a reaction—consistently in his on-screen roles and through the mere idea of him becoming James Bond—proof that he’s the right pick?

Buttermilk Drop Biscuits

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Recipe by Grace Vallo, Windham, New Hampshire

These buttermilk drop biscuits are tender, fluffy and buttery—no rolling, kneading or shaping required.

Buttermilk biscuits are, in my humble opinion, the perfect breakfast. They’re endlessly versatile—perfect for drowning in spicy sausage gravy, loading up with bacon and egg for a sandwich, or simply slathering with butter and jam. There’s really nothing a flaky biscuit can’t do. But I don’t always have the time or energy to roll and cut dough in the morning, and that’s where drop biscuits come in to save the day—or at least to stop me from opening up a can of refrigerated dough.

Making drop biscuits is almost improbably easy. You just stir together a few pantry staples to make the dough, plop spoonfuls on the pan and bake them until they’re perfectly golden brown. Whether you’re an experienced biscuit maker or looking to whip up your first-ever batch, this simple recipe delivers maximum payoff with just a couple of simple ingredients and a little bit of effort.

Preheat the oven to 425°F. As the oven warms up, combine the flour, baking soda, baking powder and salt in a large mixing bowl.

Editor’s Tip: Use a fork or a whisk to give the ingredients a good stir to make sure they’re all evenly incorporated.

Using a pastry cutter or a fork, cut the chilled butter into the dry ingredients until the mixture resembles coarse crumbs.

Editor’s Tip: You could use your hands to incorporate the butter into the flour, but use a light touch. The heat from your hands can melt the butter, and you don’t want that!

Stir the buttermilk into the crumb mixture just until it’s moistened.

Editor’s Tip: Grab your buttermilk straight from the refrigerator so it’s nice and cold. Mix just until you’ve formed a shaggy dough. It’s OK if your dough looks a little bit lumpy—you don’t want to over-mix it!

Drop the dough by 1/4-cupfuls 2 inches apart onto a greased baking sheet. Bake the biscuits until they’re golden brown on top, 11 to 13 minutes. Serve warm with butter, if desired.

Editor’s Tip: Don’t worry about making them perfectly round. A little bit of irregularity is part of the fun of drop biscuits.

Drop biscuits tend to be best right out of the oven, but you can place any leftovers inside an airtight storage container, where they’ll last on the counter for three to five days. Heat the biscuits up in a 200° oven for 5 to 10 minutes to bring them back to life.

There are several buttermilk substitutes, and the easiest one—white vinegar—is probably already in your pantry. Add 1 tablespoon of white vinegar to a 1-cup measure, then fill it the rest of the way with cold milk.

Make it a 3-ingredient recipe by swapping self-rising flour for the all-purpose flour. You won’t need the baking powder or the baking soda, as they’re already included in the flour mix. Mix the flour with the butter and buttermilk as directed to create an even easier batch of drop biscuits.

If your buttermilk drop biscuits are dense, check your baking powder. It’s possible that it’s expired, which could mean that it was too weak to help your dough rise. You may have also overworked the dough, which can result in a dense, tough biscuit.

8 Reasons a Contractor Might Give You an Outrageously High Estimate

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you just got a quote for a home repair that made your eyes water, there's probably a reason it's so high. A team of contractors weighs in with possible explanations.

If you just got a quote from a contractor that was way more than you thought it would be, join the club…it happens to the best of us. In fact, it’s a pretty common occurrence. Sometimes, though, the quote is so high that you can feel your mouth forming words you shouldn’t be saying. As someone who has been on both sides of such interactions, I can say that contractors don’t like them any more than you do. They know you can shop around, and they need your business. So what’s the deal with ridiculously high quotes?

I didn’t find any shortage of contractors willing to weigh in on this topic — one that is near and dear to their professional hearts. If a quote just made you hyperventilate, take a deep breath and check out some of the possible reasons why it’s so much higher than you expected.

“If an HVAC unit has a defective part that needs replacement, most of my clients will immediately ask how much the replacement part costs,” says HVAC technician Tamer Sayed. “I always tell the current market price and the misunderstanding often starts here. A few clients forget to take into account the labor and consultation fees. They assume they only had to pay for the replacement part.”

The same thing can happen with plumbing and electrical jobs. The part needed to get things working again may be small and inexpensive, and it may only account for a small portion of the quote. Sometimes the labor quote seems excessive, and that’s usually because the client doesn’t understand what’s involved with the repair.

Jake Vincett, a builder from the UK, makes this observation: “A lot of the time, the customer is comparing quotes for different work, because the cheaper one has included a surface repair whereas the more expensive one has quoted for fixing the underlying problem…It’s common that the customer doesn’t know or consider the amount of work going into a project. A new driveway isn’t just laying tarmac – it’s digging out the old one, laying the sub base, etc.”

Plumbing, electrical and HVAC expert Bob Cardwell adds: “It’s often not only one problem to fix but a bunch of small fixes. Even though it might be possible to fix the original problem without addressing the additional issues, an experienced and reliable technician will not ignore them, will try to fix them and will discuss them with the homeowner.”

Failing to provide a quote that addresses underlying issues and then delivering a sky-high bill at the end doesn’t just make the customer unhappy, says HVAC and plumbing expert Ryan Osterkamp. “It can make them feel like we don’t know what we’re doing.”

Osterkamp is used to hearing customers say: “I can go get that part for $X at Home Depot.” What they aren’t thinking about is the amount of training and expertise that made it possible for our technician to quickly and accurately diagnose the issue, have the supplies they need in their truck and be able to do the repair same day. Sure, you may save money by going with the guy on Facebook that works out of an old, unlabeled truck, but if his work doesn’t hold up, will he fix it without giving you the runaround? Is he insured? Does he offer a warranty?”

Tariffs, inflation and the economy in general are pushing prices up, and the dollar has lost a lot of its purchasing power for the average American. The same is true for contractors. “The fact of the matter is, we’re working harder now and making less,” says remodeling contractor Kevin Cullen. “In today’s economy, a lot of people are chasing the price to the bottom and shopping for the cheapest number. Unfortunately, they usually get what they pay for.”

A contractor who knows problems might creep up, such as a rotten subfloor that needs replacement during a toilet repair, makes an effort to warn customers as part of the quote. Potential problems aren’t always obvious. “There are times,” says Nika Tveretinova, who operates an HVAC business in Sacramento, “when the actual fix isn’t that complicated, but it’s the access that makes things tough—especially with equipment installed in an attic or cramped quarters. Sometimes we also find an old installation that needs a safety or code correction before we can put everything back together.”

In such situations, a higher-than-expected quote that you can reject is far better than a higher-than-expected bill you have to pay because the work is already completed.

Joshua Kurz founded NumeralQ, a home improvement pricing guide, and he cautions against comparing a bid you receive from a contractor to information you find online. “Owners often find an estimate online, and rather than waiting for a bid, they assume that number will be the actual cost. These estimates are helpful when determining a budget, but they are not interchangeable for actual bids. A 1,500 sq ft roof in one market will not necessarily cost the same in another market, and bids can even differ for neighboring houses.”

If you find a guy on Facebook who charges a low price to do a repair, there’s no guarantee that the repair will last or be up to code. When that person makes a mistake, a licensed contractor working on the same system at a later date might have to fix it, especially when inspectors are involved.

“Over a 40-year career,” says Cullen, “I’ve found many surprises: unsupported headers, no king or jack studs, undersized headers, poorly installed headers that aren’t flush and have gaps, termite damage, carpenter ant damage, water damage, mold. That’s not every job, but you never know when a homeowner bought a house in which a Rube Goldberg did the work on it.”

Sometimes a house is in such poor condition that a small repair, such as stopping a plumbing leak, can turn into a major project that involves replacing pipes, walls or subfloors. Rather than simply declining the repair, some contractors will offer a ridiculously high quote to avoid falling into a sinkhole of extra work. If the customer accepts the quote, the contractor won’t lose money by taking the job. A customer who rejects a bid can keep shopping with no harm done.
