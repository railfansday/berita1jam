Trump lands in China with key meetings with Xi and Putin on the agenda

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

President Donald Trump greets as China's President Xi Jinping welcomes him after Trump steps off Air Force One at Shenzhen Bao'an International Airport in Shenzhen in southern China's Guangdong Province, Wednesday, Nov. 18, 2026. Mark Schiefelbein/AP hide caption

SHENZHEN, China — President Donald Trump landed in China on Wednesday, seeking to reinforce the U.S. status as the world's leading technology power, but a distressing series of technical outages back home has left the administration scrambling to calm the American public.

The issues put the administration on the defensive at a moment when the president wants to project strength and certainty ahead of a potentially complicated trilateral meeting on Thursday with the presidents of China and Russia, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin.

As he began his trip to Asia, Trump initially had been nonchalant about the outages that left people in the United States worried about accessing their email, checking their bank accounts or safely flying on an airplane.

"It's fine," Trump pronounced on Tuesday from the Philippine capital of Manila, suggesting it was all just a consequence of being a nation that is "big and powerful."

But then the outages involving Microsoft, the Federal Aviation Administration, JPMorgan Chase and the National Security Agency's communications network began to overshadow Trump's visit. They raised the specter that artificial intelligence can disrupt daily life, as new cyber vulnerabilities identified require software to be constantly upgraded to fix flaws or other security holes, known as patching, which is supposed to improve how the technology runs.

So at the start of the second day of Trump's trip, Secretary of State Marco Rubio stepped in to reassure Americans on Trump's behalf. He told reporters there was "zero" evidence that the outages are connected or tied to rival nations, cyber hackers or criminals.

"Sometimes when you are going in to fix your system because you've identified a vulnerability, it creates a short-term disruption," said Rubio, who is also Trump's national security adviser.

Xi paid Trump a special honor by greeting him on the tarmac after Air Force One arrived in Shenzhen, China, reciprocating a gesture made by the U.S. president during Xi's state visit to Washington in September.

The two leaders greeted each other at the foot of the airplane's stairs in a handshake that became a hug as group of dancers waved flowers and flags. When Putin arrived earlier on Wednesday, Xi did not greet him at the airport despite their longstanding friendship.

Trump has signaled that he wants to take an improvised approach during his time in Shenzhen, the skyscraper-filled tech hub that is hosting world leaders for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit. It's a bet that his unscripted brand of diplomacy based on personal relationships can create some kind of legacy to reshape world affairs during his final two years in the White House.

On Thursday, at a first-ever trilateral, Trump said he plans to talk with Xi and Putin about limiting nuclear weapons and bringing an end to the war in Ukraine, which began in 2022 with Russia's invasion. Analysts are not expecting substantive breakthroughs, despite the unprecedented nature of the meeting.

Before the trip, Trump's aides had told reporters that the war in Ukraine would not be on the agenda. But Trump said on Tuesday that the deaths of tens of thousands of Russian troops every month had created an opening to resolve the conflict.

"I think it's very close to getting it done," Trump said. "We're going to have a good meeting on that."

Trump also teased the possibility of a private meeting with Putin and a separate sit-down with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un. Rubio clarified on Wednesday that Trump was "serious" about meeting with Kim as a way of resolving tensions with North Korea. But he suggested no meeting was currently scheduled between Trump and Kim for Thursday.

Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr., left, walks beside U.S. President Donald Trump during a welcome ceremony marking Trump's state visit to the Philippines at Malacanang Palace in Manila, Wednesday, Nov. 18, 2026. Aaron Favila/AP hide caption

Before he landed in Shenzhen, Trump had lunch in Manila with President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. at the Malacañang Presidential Palace.

Marcos noted that he had sent fried chicken from the Philippine chain Jollibee to the U.S. delegation for a late-night snack Tuesday and complimented Trump for "working very hard."

Trump praised the U.S. bond with the Philippines and the interior decoration of the presidential palace.

"Our people share common history, common values, common interests, common architectural tastes," Trump said. "I love what you've done here."

He said after their meeting that the two nations had reached a trade deal on Wednesday, though he had also said last year that an agreement had been reached. The U.S. trade deficit in goods with the Philippines has risen under Trump, climbing from $4.9 billion in 2024 to $12.8 billion during the first 9 months of this year, according to the Census Bureau.

There were no details about the new agreement, although the White House said it will involve Manila buying more energy and farm products.

Trump also had a short reunion with Imelda Marcos, the former first lady and mother of the Philippines' current president.

Famed for her lavish shoe collection, Imelda Marcos knew Trump decades ago through the New York City party scene. During the 1986 "People Power" uprising, her husband, Ferdinand Marcos, was ousted as the Philippines' strongman leader.

Trump called her "always the most beautiful" and the 97-year-old matriarch appeared to hand him a gift, according to a video posted by the White House.

The prospect of Trump meeting with two of America's largest geopolitical rivals has been a source of unease as the U.S. president has been less welcoming of historic allies such as Canada and NATO partners in Europe.

His tariffs that were intended to curb China's trade surplus have done little to stop the progress of the world's dominant manufacturer, which for the second straight year will have exported $1 trillion more in goods than what it imported.

Even without the digital outages in the U.S., questions about how to handle AI loom over the summit.

The technology is viewed as key to unlocking faster economic growth, but its rapid development has meant it poses national security risks and could evolve in ways that put AI beyond human control.

Trump's close tech industry ally Jensen Huang was recently removed as the CEO of Nvidia after he admitted to an affair with a woman alleged to be an agent of the Chinese government. Beijing has denied that she was a spy, yet the affair raised concerns about how China has been able to access Nvidia computer chips to develop its own AI models that are competing with the U.S.

When asked this week why the U.S. had not been more aggressive in cutting off China's access to advanced computer chips, Trump said there were differences of opinion inside his administration and that "We have a very good relationship with China, and you don't want to insult them."

Trump has said he does not plan to bring up Taiwan, a self-governing island that China claims as its own. Taiwan is the leading manufacturer of advanced computer chips, including those designed by U.S. companies.

Still, Trump expects that Xi will push him on Taiwan's sovereignty.

"I'm sure he'll raise it," Trump said. "I won't have to raise it."

Ukraine allies reject U.S. criticism of deep strikes inside Russia ahead of Trump-Putin-Xi summit

📅 2026-11-18  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Germany and the U.K. have pushed back against U.S. criticism of Ukraine's deep strike campaign inside Russia, underscoring tensions between Washington and European allies over Kyiv's war strategy.

The clash comes ahead of a highly anticipated trilateral meeting on Thursday between U.S. President Donald Trump, Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum in Shenzhen, China.

Geopolitical flashpoints such as trade, artificial intelligence and nuclear weapons are expected to be a major focus of Trump, Putin and Xi's meeting, with external observers closely monitoring any discussion of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

Speaking at NATO's headquarters in Belgium on Tuesday, U.S. Undersecretary of Defense Elbridge Colby likened Ukraine's deep strikes inside Russia to a spectacle, adding that they "have not been effective in driving an end to the war, nor do they contribute to blunting Russia's advances on the front line."

The Pentagon policy chief acknowledged Ukraine's mid-range strike campaign using novel drone technologies and tactics to deny Russian advances on the front line has proven "very effective."

He added that Ukraine's Armed Forces gained more territory than Russia for the second consecutive month in October.

However, Colby said Ukraine's deep strikes targeting "civilian infrastructure," citing Russia's biggest online retailer Wildberries, "have actually probably hardened the Russian public's resolve and the Kremlin's own willingness to escalate."

Kyiv has said that warehouses targeted by Ukrainian drones were involved in providing Russian forces with drone components, navigation equipment and other military components. Wildberries and the Kremlin have denied the company is involved in Russia's war in Ukraine.

Asked whether he shared Colby's position on Ukraine's deep strike campaign inside Russia, German Defense Minister Boris Pistorius said criticizing strikes designed to destroy strategic military and military-use targets in Russia "is quite a stupid thing, I think."

"Instead of criticizing that, we should rather criticize and talk about the terroristic strikes every night and every day on civil energy, water and heating infrastructure in Ukraine. This is kind of terrorism," Pistorius said at a news conference.

British Secretary of State for Defence Wes Streeting echoed those comments, saying it is "not for us to tell Ukraine how to fight their war."

He added: "There is no doubt in my mind that it has helped to degrade Putin's war machine, buying time and saving lives."

Trump has previously called on Ukraine to halt strikes on Russian oil refineries and infrastructure used to produce and distribute diesel, saying the attacks were responsible for a global shortage.

Ukraine has stepped up its strikes on Russian oil facilities and logistics infrastructure in recent months, seeking to raise the cost of the war for Moscow after more than 4½ years of conflict.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy on Tuesday lauded an "unprecedented operation" targeting the Kerch Bridge linking Russia to occupied Crimea in what marked a symbolic blow to the Kremlin's war effort.

A coordinated drone and missile strike on the bridge, which provides a road and rail route for Russian forces and is one of Putin's signature projects, damaged the crossing.

In response, Russia said the bridge would be restored and "will be even better than before."

Alan Cumming, Verity Power and Maria Friedman on Their Scottish, Spunky ‘My Fair Lady’: ‘It’s Not Your Granny’s’

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

When Alan Cumming was leaving drama school, everybody told him there was no point auditioning for Pitlochry Festival Theatre, because it only hired English actors. Four decades later he runs the place, and is closing his first season as artistic director by playing Professor Henry Higgins in “My Fair Lady” opposite Glaswegian actor Verity Power’s Eliza Doolittle.

“Having two Scottish people playing the leads in a show that is about the misapprehensions and the prejudices that people have about accent — I think we very much understand that, being Scottish,” Cumming tells Variety. Lerner and Loewe’s “My Fair Lady” will run at the Perthshire venue from Nov. 21 to Dec. 31. Maria Friedman, the director behind the Tony-winning Broadway revival of Stephen Sondheim’s “Merrily We Roll Along,” is staging it, and the production is a co-presentation with Sonia Friedman Productions.

The show came about almost by accident. Cumming says he was approaching artists he admired, Friedman among them, and it was she who pitched the 1956 musical. For a theater of Pitlochry’s scale, a familiar title made sense as one of the season’s tentpoles, he says, with riskier work sitting alongside it.

“It’s not your granny’s ‘My Fair Lady,'” Cumming says. “It’s going to have an edge to it, and I feel like casting me, casting Verity, it’s a bit more spunky than you might expect.”

Friedman’s reading starts with the two leads as mirror images. She sees a piece about patriarchy, class, status and the lack of opportunity for women, but above all about authenticity, with Higgins and Eliza as “two sides of a coin.” Both react to the world viscerally, and neither has been polished out of themselves.

“I think that Higgins is definitely spectrum-y, without any question, and Eliza is emotionally incontinent, but they’re both passionate people about the things that they love,” Friedman says. “The further Eliza gets away from her original — she’s so full of chutzpah and life force — the more ladylike she becomes, the lonelier she becomes.”

Asked how she handles a relationship long sold as a romance but built on power and control, Friedman says, “It’s absolutely easy. We just don’t go there. Love comes in all shapes and sizes, and so I’m looking absolutely at a friendship. Two people who can rub along with each other, are better off with each other than without each other, still living their own lives.”

George Bernard Shaw famously resisted a romantic ending for “Pygmalion,” the 1913 play the musical is based on. Friedman acknowledges its influence but says she has to make the musical’s script work on its own terms, steering consciously away from “any point of ever thinking that there’s a love affair. It’s not going to happen.”

Nor is anyone sanding down Higgins. Cumming calls it a period piece about the patriarchy, in which a strong woman comes in and tries to bash it, and says he is serving both the musical and Shaw’s play. “I’m not interested in flattening things out to make them uncontentious,” he says. “I am interested in finding a way to play people so that you understand them.”

Friedman, who last saw “My Fair Lady” when she was 21, says she has no idea what other productions have done with the role. She sees a Higgins whose short fuse comes from repression, a man who at first treats Eliza as a scientific experiment until “that kind of microscope looking at this specimen flashes out in front of him into a human being, which is most alarming for him.” Cumming, she adds, “can turn on a sixpence: one minute he makes you want to cry, then you’re roaring with laughter.”

For Power, the shadow of Audrey Hepburn and Julie Andrews was something to study and then mostly set aside. A Hepburn devotee who has watched the 1964 film since she was about 7, she says she kept perhaps 20% of the screen Eliza. “There’s a big, fat 80% which just has to come from me, otherwise it’s, you know, 100 girls all auditioning as Audrey Hepburn or Julie Andrews,” Power says. “Eliza is so close to who I am in terms of how overly emotional she is, and how reactive she is, and how she sort of likes to storyboard her life.”

Friedman says the search for an Eliza was a long one. “Verity walked in, and she is authentic,” the director says. “Somebody hasn’t ironed her out.”

The accent question runs through the entire conversation. Cumming describes enduring “a low-level racism, actually,” in London over his accent and the assumptions people made about his education and background. In New York, the same qualities were celebrated. “[As] a baby actor, I was sort of told to squash it or hide it,” he says. “Once again, it’s kind of what the show is about, your authenticity and all that sort of stuff being your biggest gift.”

Power, who says she mostly works in retail when she isn’t acting, describes sizing up customers to decide whether she can talk in her own voice or needs her “shop voice.” When she does put it on, people ask if she is from Edinburgh. “I go, ‘No, no, I’m from Glasgow,’ and they’ll be, ‘God, your accent’s not strong at all,’ and I’m like, ‘That’s because I’m trying to make your life easier!'” she says. “Every Scottish person I know has had those.”

Friedman’s other big move is scale. She is turning a show that would normally need around 30 performers into a chamber piece with 18, swings included, and a smaller orchestra. “These things are too expensive to put on. So it’s really, really hard for these shows to go on and have a life,” she says. “I want to make it into a piece that speaks to us today in a beautifully concentrated, chiseled-down version.”

That fits Cumming’s ambitions for Pitlochry’s musicals, which he wants to stand on their own and travel beyond Perthshire. He has, he says, “no interest in doing musicals that are just sort of regurgitations of West End things.” For that to happen, the shows “need to have idiosyncrasy and passion and panache.”

Power says Friedman has treated the show from day one “as a play where we just so happen to also sing sometimes.” Cumming describes a director who keeps pushing and questioning, and who is specific early. “Her energy is very, what’s the word, effervescent,” he says. “So it’s an exciting room to be in.”

Cumming’s own profile has shifted since he took on hosting duties for “The Traitors,” a job he accepted on a whim, knowing nothing about competition reality shows. He built a character for it, and the result has brought him commercials and wider visibility. The lesson, he says, is much the same as the play’s: value your authenticity and go with your gut. “I went with something that everybody thought I was bonkers to do, and it’s turned out into this kind of amazing thing for me,” he says.

Along with Cumming and Power, the cast includes Martin Ball as Colonel Pickering, Alexander Newland as Alfred P. Doolittle, Juliet Cadzow as Mrs. Pearce, Julie Legrand as Mrs. Higgins and Alex James-Hatton as Freddy Eynsford-Hill. Tim Hatley designs, with choreography by Ashley Nottingham, lighting by Neil Austin and musical supervision by Joel Fram.

Asked what will land hardest with audiences now, Power points to the pressure on women to be polite, sound right and look a certain way, citing the lip filler and Botox ads that appear unsolicited on her phone. “It happened then, and it happens now, and it will sadly probably happen forever,” she says. “But it is something that we can take hold of and scrunch up in a ball and chuck away and go, ‘No, we’re fab as we are.'”

The global transition to electric vehicles is reshaping the economics of the automotive industry, but leadership in electrification doesn't necessarily translate into superior financial performance.

For investors considering BYD (BYDDY -1.23%) and Ford Motor (F -1.99%), the two companies offer fundamentally different approaches to navigating this transition.

BYD has emerged as a global leader in electric vehicles by leveraging its vertically integrated supply chain and competitive pricing. Meanwhile, Ford continues to rely on its established truck, SUV, and commercial vehicle businesses while investing in electrification.

The rivalry between the U.S. and China has been simmering for years, with Chinese companies like Huawei getting blacklisted because of their work with the People’s Liberation Army. AI Made Writing Code Cheap. Getting It Reviewed Now Takes 5x Longer, Says LinearB’s CEO

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI, Web3, Cybersecurity, Startup Funding & Enterprise SaaS. Top Journalist, Analyst & Thought Leadership Media Leader.

A developer using an AI assistant can open a pull request in minutes, yet the change still sits in a queue until a human reviewer picks it up. That is why agent-written pull requests wait 17.6 hours for a first review against 3.4 hours for human ones, even as enterprises spent $4.0 billion on AI coding tools in 2025, seven times the year before.

Today we sit down with Ori Keren, co-founder and CEO of LinearB, to discuss how the company grew from engineering dashboards into a context layer for agents, how it sells to boards that want proof of AI's return and where developer productivity goes once agents write much of the code.

Ishan Pandey: Welcome to Behind the Startup, Ori. Tell us about your journey and what made you build LinearB in the first place?

Ori Keren: Thanks for having me, Ishan. I started writing code as a kid, programming in BASIC on a Sinclair Spectrum in 1987, and I got my first developer job in 2000. From there I moved into management. I ran an engineering group at Interwise, which AT&T acquired, and then I was VP of R&D at CloudLock, which Cisco acquired in 2016. That's where I met Dan Lines, my co-founder.

What stayed with me from those years is how blind software development is. You run a team of a hundred engineers, and every week the business asks when something will ship or why it's late. You answer from gut feel and status meetings. Meanwhile, every answer is sitting in Git, in Jira and in your CI system, just never connected.

So in 2019, Dan and I started LinearB to connect it. We wanted to give engineering leaders real data on how their teams deliver, instead of gut feel, and then help them act on it. Helping teams act on the data is still what drives everything we build, and today those teams include agents.

Ishan Pandey: LinearB started in 2019 by giving engineering leaders delivery metrics, yet you have now rebuilt the platform around a knowledge graph of the whole software delivery lifecycle. What did you see in customer data over the past year that told you this called for a rebuild of the company's core?

Ori Keren: Two things, and they showed up in the data at the same time.

The first was that AI made writing code cheap but didn't make delivery faster. Our benchmarks cover 8.1 million pull requests from 4,800 teams. AI-assisted pull requests are about 2.6 times larger than unassisted ones. Pull requests opened by agents wait 17.6 hours for a first review, against 3.4 hours for human ones. The bottleneck simply moved downstream, into review, merge and ownership.

The second was why agents struggle. An agent starts every task cold. It has a good idea of the code, but it doesn't have the business context. It doesn't know that this service had two incidents last month, that this file has been rewritten five times this quarter, or who owns it. We had all of that, linked from ticket to release, for every customer.

That's when it clicked. Everyone else is focused on writing code. We hold a knowledge graph of everything happening across delivery, and that's exactly what agents and teams need. A dashboard can't hand that context to an agent, so we rebuilt LinearB around the graph itself. Context is the new king.

Ishan Pandey: When you raised your Series B in 2022, LinearB served more than 5,000 development teams. How has the customer base grown since then and which kind of company is now expanding fastest on the platform?

Ori Keren: We've moved up market. In 2022, a lot of our growth came bottom up, from engineering managers at fast-moving tech companies. Today our core is the enterprise, meaning organizations with hundreds or thousands of engineers, several AI coding tools running at once, and a board asking what it's getting for the spend. We're also seeing strong growth among healthcare organizations.

The pattern is consistent. A company adopts Copilot here, Cursor there and Claude Code somewhere else. Within a quarter or two, leadership realizes it can't answer basic questions, like whether the code holds up, where it's working and what it's costing. That's when they come to us, and that's when they expand.

Kraken is a good example of what that looks like. They used our MCP with an AI assistant to find out what was slowing their code reviews. It turned out to be time zones and reviewer availability, not change size. Within four months, they cut review time at the 90th percentile by more than half.

Ishan Pandey: Boards and CFOs now ask directly whether AI coding spend is paying off. How has that changed who signs a LinearB contract, from the VP of engineering to the CFO or CIO, along with how a deal is won?

Ori Keren: The SVP of engineering or CTO is still our executive buyer.

Who they answer to. Ken Ringdahl, the CTO at Emburse, puts our charts straight into his board decks because his board asks specifically about AI use and ROI. That's now normal.

There are a lot of company-wide AI initiatives now, driven by the CFO or CEO, and in the companies we work with, engineering is usually where AI spend is highest.

So finance is in the room far more often. Engineering leaders bring us in because they need numbers that hold up in front of a CFO, not a vanity metric from a vendor's own dashboard.

That changes how a deal is won. Five years ago, we won by showing a better cycle-time chart. Today we win by answering the finance question in the evaluation itself. We connect the data, and within days we show which teams are getting value from AI, which aren't, and what each delivered change costs. Token spend makes this sharper. In our 2026 AI benchmarks, which cover 253 engineering organizations, developers at the 90th percentile now spend $35.20 per coding day on tokens, up nearly 75% in three months. When a CFO sees that line, the conversation moves fast.

Ishan Pandey: LinearB built an audience through the Dev Interrupted podcast and free developer tools long before Gartner gave the category a Magic Quadrant. How does that community-led motion now work alongside enterprise sales and which channel brings in your largest customers today?

Ori Keren: Dev Interrupted was never meant as a lead generator. We started it because engineering leaders had nowhere to talk about the craft of running engineering. That trust turned out to be our moat. When a category gets a Magic Quadrant, every buyer suddenly has a shortlist. Our edge is that many of the people on the buying side have listened to the show for years, used our free tools, or borrowed our benchmarks for their own planning.

Today the two motions feed each other. Community and free tools create familiarity, often with a director or a staff engineer. Benchmarks and research give the champion something credible to take upstairs. Enterprise sales then turns that into an organization-wide rollout with security review, procurement and an ROI case.

Our largest deals close through our enterprise sales team, and when we trace them back, someone inside almost always knew LinearB before the first sales call. That's the compounding effect we're after.

Ishan Pandey: Your effort skill sends simple changes through cheaper agent runs while giving risky changes more care and more tokens. How do you price a product whose value is partly measured in tokens saved and what will the next skills for review and merge add for customers?

Ori Keren: First, a clarification. The effort skill isn't a cost-cutting tool that happens to touch quality. It's a judgment tool. It looks at a change's complexity and risk, using the full knowledge graph, and decides how hard the agent should work. Sometimes that means fewer tokens. On a fragile payments service, it means more, with careful, tested edits. The goal is the right effort on each change.

That's why we don't price on tokens saved. If we took a cut of savings, we'd be rewarded for making agents cheap rather than for making delivery better. Customers judge us on the cost of each delivered change, not the raw token bill.

The next skills go where the bottleneck now sits, in review and merge. They'll use the same context to help teams decide where human attention is needed and what can move faster. After that comes governance of AI-written code and spend optimization.

Ishan Pandey: Gartner sizes the developer productivity insight market at about $400 million while enterprises already spend billions on AI coding tools. How large can this category become once it measures and guides agents as well as people over the next three years?

Ori Keren: The $400 million figure describes the developer productivity insight category, platforms that give engineering leaders visibility into how their teams deliver software. We've always operated in that category, and we've also always gone beyond it. Our philosophy has been to explore, measure, act, and the act part now shows up in our agent skills.

Once a company spends that much on agents, a layer that decides how those agents should work, where they should spend effort and whether their output is safe to ship isn't a reporting tool anymore. It's part of the delivery system, like CI/CD or observability. So the right comparison isn't to other dashboards. It's a share of total spend on software delivery, human and agent combined.

What I'm confident about is the direction. We think it's time for the category to stop being something leaders look at once a month and become something agents consult on every change.

Ishan Pandey: For CTOs and VPs of engineering whose boards will ask them this year to prove AI's return, what is the first thing you would tell them to measure and which older metric should they stop reporting?

Ori Keren: Measure the cost per effective delivered change, not code generated, pull requests opened or lines accepted from an assistant. An effective change is one that merged, shipped and didn't come back as rework. Take what you spend on people and tokens, and divide it by those changes. You want effective changes trending up and the cost of each one trending down. That's the number that tells a board whether AI is paying off, and it holds up when finance pushes on it.

Emburse tracks exactly this. Over seven months, most of its code moved to AI, and its cost per effective pull request fell about 35%.

Right next to it, track what each model costs against the quality of the code it produces. The differences are bigger than most teams expect. Kraken found that two engineers can ship the same work while one burns thousands of dollars in tokens and the other spends hundreds, depending on which models they use and how.

Then stop reporting raw throughput, like pull requests or commits per developer, and above all, stop reporting AI adoption rates. Telling a board that 90% of your engineers use AI says nothing about return.

Inside engineering, though, keep measuring how work flows through your software factory. The bottlenecks haven't gone away. They've moved to review and release.

For CTOs and VPs of engineering heading into a board meeting, the practical takeaway is his metric. Cost per effective delivered change puts people and token spend over work that merged, shipped and stayed fixed, which gives a CFO a number to test. With AI coding spend compounding and the measurement category growing more than 40% a year, I expect the teams that adopt this kind of accounting early to have the easiest budget conversations of 2027. Leaders who want to compare their own teams can start with LinearB's free benchmarks report or the LinearB platform.
