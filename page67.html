Bitcoin price eyes $74K as Fed rate-hike bets hit 96% after Waller speech

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bitcoin price action and stock futures trended lower after Fed governor Christopher Waller said that he would vote to raise interest rates by 0.25% at the December FOMC meeting.

Bitcoin (BTC) fell toward $74,000, and US stock futures dropped on Tuesday as a senior Federal Reserve official voiced support for hiking interest rates.

Data from TradingView showed BTC/USD reaching $74,080 on Bitstamp, down 1.3% on the day.

BTC/USD one-hour chart. Source: Cointelegraph/TradingView

US stock market futures leaned lower as Fed governor Christopher Waller said that he would support hiking interest rates by 25 basis points at the next meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) on Dec. 9. At the time of writing, Nasdaq 100 futures were down 0.4%.

His decision, he added, would not be swayed by lower-than-expected inflation data. The October prints of the US Consumer Price Index (CPI) is due for release at 8.30 am Eastern time on Tuesday. It is expected to show a month-on-month increase of 0.3% versus September when the increase was 0.6%, pushed higher by rising fuel costs.

“While I am hoping for an improvement over September, even if this occurs, I would still support a 25 basis point increase in the FOMC’s policy rate at our December meeting,” Waller commented in a speech at the Bank of England Watchers Conference in London.

While CPI is particularly sensitive to fuel-price volatility, Waller also flagged high readings from the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) index, known to be the Fed’s “preferred” inflation gauge and which he confirmed was the “measure targeted by the FOMC.”

“Let’s just say it. September’s inflation data were bad. There was no silver lining,” he said, referring to PCE rising 0.5% month-on-month and 3.7% year-on-year, respectively.

Following the speech, markets immediately repriced odds of a 25-basis-point hike in December from 76.3% to 96.3%, per data from CME Group’s FedWatch Tool.

Fed target-rate probability comparison for December FOMC meeting (screenshot). Source: CME Group

Financial policy tightening measures traditionally act as headwinds for crypto and risk asset prices as they imply a reduction in liquidity and associated capital inflows.

“We think the slowdown in core CPI will stem from a reversion of shelter prices, primarily owners’ equivalent rents and lodging away from home. Similarly, the stabilization in diesel prices should help to quell price pressures in energy-intensive goods,” he wrote, quoted by CNBC.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), which publishes CPI, confirmed that Tuesday’s release would include only month-on-month statistics due to the 2025 US government shutdown reducing data availability. CPI data for October 2025 was not issued for the same reason.

Huupe Mini 2 Review: More Game Than It Can Handle

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

If your mom was one of those who always said, “Don’t play ball in the house,” well, she’s going to absolutely hate the Huupe Mini 2.

Huupe manufactures smart basketball hoops and add-on kits for existing setups that track shots, light up the board when a point is registered, and include social features like global challenges and leaderboards. The Huupe Mini 2 is the pint-sized, indoor version of that idea, clipping over the top of a door so you can shoot hoops without ever having to leave the room.

I’ve been testing one out for weeks, and while it’s a fun diversion, some of its features overreach beyond its core functionality, resulting in an experience that bricks as much as it dunks.

The Huupe Mini 2 is, effectively, a smart television with a basketball hoop mounted to it. Or rather, a smart TV that comes with a basketball hoop that you have to mount yourself, courtesy of four screws and a little elbow grease. The LCD screen measures 19 inches diagonally, plus a hefty border that includes stereo speakers and a camera used to track you and your b-ball prowess. (Total size of the backboard is 25 inches diagonally, which fits comfortably within a door frame.)

A 5-inch inflatable rubber basketball is included, along with a small hand pump and needle. Also in the box are a small but otherwise traditional remote control—required to do anything with the Huupe—and a USB-C charger and lengthy cable so you can top up the battery without removing it from the door.

After a quick charge, the unit is ready to set up. I was surprised to discover there’s no app for the device; while larger Huupe hardware works with one, the Mini 2 is completely self-contained and web-based. Just connect the backboard to your Wi-Fi network through a process that you step through with the remote, just like you would on any modern television.

What’s new vs. the original Huupe Mini? According to the company, most of the upgrades are behind the scenes: a faster CPU, a larger battery (rated lifespan is now four hours), better display quality, and “significantly” better shot tracking. New games are also included, which I’ll cover in the sections to come.

Once connected to the web, the Huupe Mini 2 opens up a world of entertainment to you, and, to my great surprise, not all of it is basketball-related. At first blush, the options available are actually quite daunting. It’s all arranged into traditional tiles on the home screen, which you scroll through with the remote.

Just Huupe is the board’s most basic scoring system, tracking shots and recording your shooting percentage in an untimed session. If you want to get competitive, Huupe Live and the Daily Prize Challenge are social games where you challenge players worldwide to score the most points in a given time frame. The winner gets a nominal prize—10 bucks for the daily challenge, a “cozy sweatsuit” for a monthlong challenge. Other games are fairly self-explanatory, like Most 3 Pointers in 90 Seconds and Family Mode (basically Horse).

Many of the games require online friends to play against; if you don’t have pals with their own Huupe Minis, you’ll probably be locked out of these modes—not that they add much beyond their multiplayer features.

Things get a little kookier—and buggier—from there. For example, Huupe Flyers is designed to be played without a ball. It’s a gesture-based version of Flappy Bird, wherein you literally flap your arms around to keep a basketball in the air, tracking your movements via the camera much like an Xbox Kinect. Unfortunately, unlike the ball-based games, I was never able to play it, as it failed the calibration process every time I tried to fire it up.

The system also works as a fairly straightforward streaming platform. Included are apps for YouTube, HBO Max, ESPN, Disney+, Prime Video, Hulu, Spotify, and more. There’s one glaring omission in that list, of course, and I’m not talking about Paramount+ but rather Netflix. Without Netflix support, can you even call yourself a streaming device? It’s also worth noting that the built-in speakers are surprisingly weak. On some apps the audio is OK; on others it’s nearly inaudible.

The Huupe Mini 2 includes not just a sensor that can detect when a basket is successfully made; it also includes a camera to track how far the shooter is from the backboard. This is used to assign point values to various shots (depending on the specific game you’re playing), with more points earned the farther away you are from the hoop.

The good news is that in weeks of playing on the Huupe Mini 2, I never noticed a time when a successful basket was recorded as a miss or vice versa. The distance calculation, however, is another thing. I’ve shot from across the room and had it count as 1 point, while some shots from just a few feet away ring up as 3 points. I could never figure out much rhyme or reason for this variability, except that shots straight-on to the backboard generally registered as lower point values, even if they were distant, while shots from the wings earned more. Of course, navigating all of this is highly dependent on the geometry of your room. Unless you have the Huupe Mini 2 set up in an empty garage, you are probably, like me, going to have plenty of obstacles that get in the way of a lot of your shots—and plenty of crawling under furniture to retrieve wayward balls.

I can say with authority that it is harder to make a basket on the Huupe Mini 2 than on a regulation basketball goal, even at a very close distance. The hoop is small, and the ball tends to rebound wildly off the springy backboard. It’s quite unforgiving, and it didn’t really make me want to spend a lot of time playing, chasing balls that bounced into the corner or rolled under the bed.

The other big problem: When the included inflatable ball hits the Huupe Mini 2, the impact is extremely loud, to the point where I was promptly informed by my wife that it was not appropriate for use when anyone else was in the house. While Huupe says the device is intended solely for use with the included ball, I found a workaround by purchasing a 5-inch foam basketball from Amazon, which makes a bit less noise on impact and also softens the rebound a bit. It has had zero trouble registering baskets.

Battery life is another issue. While four hours is plenty of playing time, the battery continues to drain when the Huupe is in standby mode, which meant I would play a few games in the morning, ostensibly shut the Huupe off, then come back in the afternoon to a completely dead device. The Huupe can run with the power cord attached, but only once the battery has charged to at least about 5 percent.

Unfortunately, even turning the power off is puzzlingly difficult. The power button on my remote control did nothing; I ultimately had to turn the system on and off with the physical button on the bottom of the backboard.

At $429 (on deep discount from a hefty list price of $799), the Huupe Mini 2 sits perhaps just over the edge of what might pass for a quirky holiday gift—and I think it’s safe to say no one is buying this device for themselves. You’d have to be a huge basketball nut to get enough mileage out of the device to make it worthwhile. I expect you would also have to loathe leaving your room. With today’s kids, who knows.

How to check how many chests you’ve opened in Fortnite Treasure Hunters

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Looting is more important than ever before in Fortnite’s Treasure Hunters mini-season, as there are lots of freebies up for grabs this time around, including access to everything in the shop if you get really lucky. Because of this, you may be searching for a way to keep track of how many chests you’ve opened.

Finding and opening chests is the only way to get your hands on quite a few rare items this season, so keeping an eye on all of the work you’ve put in is a helpful tool for tracking progress. It can be tricky to find on your own, though, so here’s how to check how many chests you’ve opened in Fortnite.

You can keep track of how many chests you’ve opened in Fortnite Treasure Hunters from the Quests tab. This helpful mechanic can be easy to miss, as there are quite a lot of missions to tackle listed in this section. Here’s how to find it.

Each of the chest quests listed here tracks your progress across five stages. As you hit each of the five milestones for these bonus goals, you earn extra XP, and the listed maximum number of chests being tracked increases.

These missions only track chests up to 1,000 for each type, so once you hit this number in each of the three categories, your total opened chests will no longer be displayed. This means you can only check your opened chests for the season up until you hit this cap.

The number of chests you open does not directly affect your odds of finding the Pirate Shop Access or other special loot drop items. The loot odds and drop rates are completely unrelated to how many chests you open, as this system is completely based on RNG, which means it comes down to random luck and chance.

Although opening more chests doesn’t directly increase your odds of finding Pirate Shop Access and other loot items, the more you loot, the more shots you have at potentially finding something. This system can be a bit confusing to fully understand, but a good way to conceptualize it is to compare the loot system to a bag of marbles.

Think of all the special loot items as rare, colored marbles in a big bag of grey ones. Each time you open a chest, you’re pulling a marble out. If the marble is grey, it goes right back in. If it’s colored, it stays out.

Your odds of pulling a colored one, which is a special loot item, don’t increase over time, as the grey marbles, or standard loot items, always go back into the pool. At the same time, the more you reach into the bag by opening chests, the more shots you have at finding items.

Although you can’t boost your odds of finding rare drops by opening more chests, you can help the process along by attending special events that feature boosted odds for these rare items. Treasure Hours are the best time to go looting for these assets.

Tribeca Prizewinner Darko Lungulov Wraps Production on Serbian Coming-of-Age Drama ‘1970’: ‘It Is a Story About Today’ (EXCLUSIVE)

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tribeca prizewinner Darko Lungulov (“Here and There”) has wrapped production on “1970,” a coming-of-age drama set against the backdrop of President Nixon’s visit to the former Yugoslavia during the Vietnam War. The film will take part in the Agora Works in Progress strand at the Thessaloniki Film Festival before heading to Tallinn’s Baltic Event industry showcase later this month.

“1970” follows 13-year-old Draško, who — after being abandoned by his parents — runs away with the rebellious 16-year-old Eržika. Drifting from place to place, swept up amid a roiling climate of anti-war protests, rebellion, free love, wild rock concerts, drugs and decadent parties, his desperate journey home becomes an awakening of heartbreak, loss and freedom. Related Stories ‘Star Trek’ 60th Anniversary Celebration Hits Rome MIA Market With Iconic Franchise at 'Crossroads’ After Paramount-Warner Bros. Merger

The film is written and directed by Lungulov, who shares a producing credit with Snezana van Houwelingen. It’s produced by This and That Productions (Serbia), Papa Films (Serbia), Mirage Film (Hungary), Samsa Film (Luxembourg) and PomPom Film (Croatia), with financing and support from Film Center Serbia, the Hungarian National Film Institute, Film Fund Luxembourg, the Croatian Audiovisual Center, Film Fund Vojvodina and Creative Europe MEDIA.

Speaking to Variety in Thessaloniki, Lungulov explains that while his period drama is set more than 50 years in the past, “it is a story about today.”

“The film takes us to a time when young people believed they could change the world — something we seem to have forgotten,” the director says. Pointing to recent events in his native Serbia, where after two years of struggle, a student protest movement ousted longtime strongman Aleksandar Vučić last month, Lungulov says that “that possibility came incredibly to life again.”

The Belgrade-born director, who studied filmmaking in New York, is best known for “Here and There,” which won the Best NY Narrative Award at Tribeca in 2009 and was Serbia’s submission for the foreign-language Oscar that year. The film, which contrasted the adventures of an enterprising young Serbian in New York with the experiences of an aging New Yorker in Belgrade, was praised by Variety’s Ronnie Scheib as a “laid-back character study [that] accrues small epiphanies with patient care, its mordant Eastern European humor tempered by a gentler sense of postwar absurdity.” His 2014 follow-up, the tragicomedy “Monument to Michael Jackson,” premiered at Karlovy Vary.

Lungulov’s last film was the 2023 documentary “A Fleeting Dream,” which followed the rock band Yu Grupa — a long-running musical act that, coincidentally, was formed in 1970. His latest will also incorporate documentary elements, with “1970” utilizing a mix of “real and fake home movies, newly shot 16mm and digital footage,” he says. “It is a film based on my own memories, and I wanted to create the hero’s inner world, his memories and the atmosphere of 1970 through the film’s internal language.”

Mirroring the political climate of that time, with raucous anti-Vietnam War protests greeting Nixon’s visit to Yugoslvaia, Lungulov notes that his two young leads — Andrija Femić, who plays Draško, and Lilla Gregus, who plays Eržika — were both “very active in the student movement” that brought down Vučić. At the time of filming, Gregus was a 22-year-old student at the Academy of Arts in Novi Sad, the Serbian town whose railway station disaster in 2024 jumpstarted the protest movement.

On one occasion, Lungulov himself joined the young actor and her fellow students at a demonstration after a rehearsal for “1970.” It’s that echo of the past in the present day, he says, that makes his film so timely.

“I feel this story is about today — young people searching for their voice in a hopeless world and refusing to be broken by bullying authorities, which feels like our last hope in today’s scary times,” he says. “And the Serbian students brought that hope back to us in Serbia, and perhaps to the world.”

The Thessaloniki Film Festival runs Nov. 5 – 15.
