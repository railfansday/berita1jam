Stuck on a 'treadmill of high inflation': San Francisco Fed's Daly says more rate hikes likely needed

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The San Francisco Fed president said the labor market gives the central bank room to focus on inflation after October payrolls rose 165,000

San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly said Friday that further rate hikes will probably be necessary to rein in inflation, though when the Fed acts and by how much remains undecided, she told CNBC.

"My expectation is we're going to have to tighten policy further," Daly said, according to Bloomberg. "Exactly when and how much is still to be decided because we just have to take all the variables that are coming in."

Her remarks came as the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that nonfarm payrolls rose by 165,000 in October and the unemployment rate was 4.1%. Average hourly earnings are up 3.1% from a year earlier.

Daly described the jobs report as "good news for American workers" while warning that rising prices are putting a strain on family finances, according to CNBC. "The job market remains stable and solid, but not booming, and you see real wages falling because those wages are not really keeping up with inflation," she said. "I think that's the crux of this."

The consumer price index rose 3.7% in September from a year earlier. "This has been high for too long, almost six years, and we're really starting to feel the burden of that spread everywhere," Daly said.

Daly said officials had expected cooler prices outside of housing in the services category, but that has not happened, according to Bloomberg. She also said the solid labor market gives the Fed room to treat inflation as its top priority.

Daly said she continues to prefer a measured pace of tightening, and she gets a vote on the rate-setting committee next year, according to CNBC. She said she backed both the September hike and the decision to hold last week.

Richmond Fed President Thomas Barkin said Thursday that he too expects rates to climb but does not know when that will happen, according to CNBC.

The Fed held its benchmark rate at 3.75% to 4% last week, with two officials dissenting in favor of a quarter-point increase. Fed Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack cast the dissenting votes. The committee had raised the rate by a quarter point in September, its first increase since July 2023.

The committee said it would rather hold off until "the accumulation of more information" could guide what it does next, according to CNBC.

‘Everyone knows why we lost,’ JD Vance points to the Iran war — which has already cost at least $38 billion and 19 American lives

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

While votes were still being counted in the U.S. midterm elections, administration figures in Washington were already inviting speculation over how the results might be connected to the conflict with Iran.

On the morning after the election, Vice President JD Vance wrote on X that the major reason for the dramatic loss for Republicans was the American public’s opposition to the war. “Everyone knows why we lost. And any honest conversation about where we go from here must start there,” he wrote, in reference to the ongoing conflict that began with U.S.-Israeli airstrikes against Iran in late February 2026.

While it is rare for a vice president to make such a critical proclamation about their own administration’s policy, President Donald Trump later stated that he did not disagree with Vance’s appraisal of the role that frustration with the Iran war played in voters’ minds.

But whether this changes Trump’s view on next steps in the ongoing conflict remains unclear. Reporting over the past few weeks has indicated that the U.S. military has been actively preparing options and increasing capabilities in the Persian Gulf region in the event the president directs a resumption of hostilities.

As an expert on Middle East affairs and a former senior official at the National Security Council during the first Trump administration, I’m aware that months of conflict have failed to bring Iran to its knees, force regime change or militarily defeat a perceived threat to U.S. assets and allies. Escalating now is unlikely to change those realities – and it carries the additional risk of further damaging Republicans in the eyes of Americans voters as they turn toward the 2028 presidential race.

Even if Trump were to ratchet up the conflict, moreover, it’s unclear how much wiggle room he’ll have come January facing a Democratic Congress almost uniformly opposed to the war.

Part of the issue facing Iran war hawks in Washington now is that the conflict has caused serious setbacks for the regime in Tehran without satisfying the officially stated strategic reasons for going to war.

They argue that pulling back now – as welcome as that might be for Americans struggling with the associated rising cost of fuel – may allow Iran to rebuild before the conflict is “won.” Moreover, it would render the military and reputational costs already sunk by Washington as wasted.

Among the achievements of this war touted by the Trump administration has been the death of former Supreme Leader Ali Khameini, along with the killing of scores of senior leaders within the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps – including individuals like the former head of Iran’s National Security Council Ali Larijani. Significant elements of Iran’s air defense and radar network, ballistic missile and drone production, and storage and launch sites have also been destroyed. And Iran’s already outdated navy and air force has been further degraded.

Meanwhile, the U.S.-imposed naval blockade against Iranian-linked shipping in the Persian Gulf cut off a crucial lifeline for Iran’s economy. And additional sanctions and economic penalties imposed by Washington have served to further devalue Iran’s currency and deprive Iran of additional financial links to the international banking system.

Yet for all the U.S. successes in degrading the Iranian military by limitless aerial bombardment and the significant financial pressure against the Iranian economy, an obvious endgame remains elusive.

Despite weakening the Iranian government – now led by Ali Khameinei’s son, Mojtaba, and a new echelon of senior IRGC commanders – the Islamic Republic remains intact. Moreover, it still possesses enough ballistic missiles and drones to hold the region at risk, while continuing to impose significant control over its population.

Meanwhile, U.S. military spending continues to rise. In September, the U.S. Congressional Budget Office assessed that the military costs of the Iran War had already totaled US$38 billion. Since then, that number has only increased. And that is to say nothing of the associated global economic costs that have resulted from Iran’s de facto closure of the Strait of Hormuz – a key shipping route for oil and gas.

Should Trump decide to change course on Iran, he – and others – may think that the money, effort and lives of 19 American servicemen killed in the conflict would have been spent with no obvious objective achieved. Yet continued military expenditure with no strategic endgame could only magnify those same costs.

To Iran, the midterm results have not changed its public position. Mojtaba Khameini has issued statements directing the IRGC to widen the conflict in the region and perhaps beyond should the U.S. and Israel attack again. At the same time, a statement attributed to him indicates that hostilities between the United States and Iran will persist regardless of the balance of party political power in Washington.

This raises questions about the prospects for diplomacy. During the summer, the U.S. and Iran agreed to an initial 14-point memorandum of understanding that, if finalized, could have brought a permanent end to the hostilities. But since the 60-day clock on that agreement lapsed, no parallel diplomatic agreement has emerged.

Even with the major shift in the U.S. political landscape, it remains unclear whether Trump will resume operations against Iran before the new Congress is sworn in. The messy election result in Israel – which has left the pro-war, and potentially outgoing, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyanu with potentially more reason to double-down in Iran – only complicates matters.

The U.S. military certainly appears to be ready to engage in a new round of attacks against Iran if so directed, and Iran is ready to retaliate. But given the shortcomings in the U.S. inventory – with munitions and missile and air defense capabilities depleted by eight months of war – escalation is something Trump and his inner circle will have to consider with caution.

Iran might force the issue by engaging in asymmetric attacks in the region or beyond that they have not attempted already. Or it could launch a coordinated offensive with its partners in the region – notably the Houthis in Yemen, who have become more active in their own conflict with Saudi Arabia in recent months.

But save an event that the Trump administration perceives is giving it cover to act, there are no clear indications emerging from Washington as to which way it will move.

Vance diagnosed a widely perceived political problem for Republicans: Voters don’t want more war. But what he noticeably didn’t mention was a practical solution. The Middle East conflict – and the United States’ role in it – are, as such, locked in a stalemate.

America and Iran have both borne significant political, military and economic costs. A decision not to pursue an endgame would leave those sunk; doing so may only increase them.

Javed Ali, Associate Professor of Practice of Public Policy, University of Michigan

Stylist Soki Mak on 10 Things That Inspired Kim Kardashian’s <i><em>All’s Fair</em></i> Season Two Wardrobe

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

From 1990s Helmut Newton shoots to classic Bond girls to unexpected Comme des Garçons finds

In fashion, anything from niche archival research to a favorite film to an outfit you saw on your morning commute can make for inspiration. In our new series 10 Things, we ask industry professionals and tastemakers to divulge the (you guessed it) 10 things that had the most impact on their aesthetic choices.

The second season of Ryan Murphy’s legal drama, All’s Fair, opened with reality star-turned-actor Kim Kardashian in a patchwork Alexander McQueen suit. The rare ensemble, composed of a peplum jacket and fluted skirt, came from Lee McQueen’s theatrical Spring 2004 collection, titled Deliverance, in which models danced down the runway in shimmering gowns, dramatic feathered skirts, and tulle galore. “There were comments about Kim’s clothing being inappropriate in the workplace, but that’s what it should be,” Soki Mak, the visionary behind Kardashian’s Allura Grant character, told Harper’s Bazaar. “If I’m going to watch a show, I want to be like, ‘What the hell is that?’”

When Mak first joined the All’s Fair team—handpicked by Kardashian, via Instagram, to dress her onscreen as a high-powered lawyer—she had never ventured into costume design. The stylist, who has worked with everyone from Rosalía to Pamela Anderson, spent the first season developing the visual identity of Allura, merging her client’s own flair for shock value and fantasy with the limited character descriptions she received. We saw hyper-cinched waists and archival Jean Paul Gaultier; Hermès Birkins and suits with exposed thongs. It was Kardashian, with a twist.

But for Season Two, Mak focused on separating Kardashian from her character, tracking Allura’s emotional journey through darker color palettes and less expected silhouettes. She dug deeper into designer archives, emerging with moody gems from some of the most beloved collections of the ’90s and early-aughts. Rare vintage pieces from Maison Margiela, Comme des Garçons, Yohji Yamamoto, and Alaïa each made their cameos.

Ahead, the stylist dives into 10 influences that shaped Allura’s costume design, from Kim Kardashian herself to classic Bond girls to a baker-boy cap she had to fight for.

[For Season One,] no one gave us any sort of visual references. I’d never been a costume designer before, but usually, when I’ve worked on character-based stuff—I used to work a lot with Nadia Lee Cohen—we would develop the character first, so when I go into styling, it’s quite easy to visualize that person. There was no time for that, and there was no visual reference for Allura. So, when I came in, I was just thinking, “Okay, what do I see her as?” I looked at Kim personally, and she’s this fantasy. There is no one like her. So I’m like, “Okay, if you have all of the money in the world and all of the access in the world, all the power in the world, what would you look like?”

I’ve always thought of Kim as someone that creates and shocks and is the first to do something, whether people like it or not. And that’s the energy I wanted to go into this with. She challenges the norm. I’ve also always been a big believer in trying to be a trendsetter rather than a follower. In some of these moments, I would find it difficult to make a mood board because the references weren’t there. That was where I would say to Kim, “Trust me.”

For Season One, we started reaching out to brands, and Jean Paul Gaultier was [the first to say], “Let’s do it.” The minute we had them, the whole narrative of the show changed. They went deep into the archives and started sending these looks that hadn’t been worn in 20 years. It was amazing. We were sourcing vintage and archival pieces on the side, but we really focused on Jean Paul Gaultier because everything else compared to that just looked sad.

When the images started circulating from the first season, a critic that I respect wrote that the fashion was “kind of obvious.” Although I disagreed, I understood where he was coming from. Maybe Kim wearing a Mugler suit that accentuated her body and her curves was something we had seen her wear before. This season, I wanted to do something really different. I wanted to go less out-there with the colors and a little less predictable with the shapes and the styling. I picked designers and pieces that weren’t obvious choices, and I think it showed growth from last season. We took it somewhere else completely.

For hair and makeup references, I would go on Pinterest and pull old Helmut Newton shoots because I wanted it to look very timeless. I think that was the key. Everything looked … not minimal, there was definitely no minimal… but very ’90s-based and gorgeous.

Kim loves Madonna. There were a lot of old Madonna images for her platinum hair and makeup, a lot of which was done by Pat McGrath.

I think I’d made a point this year that I wanted to take it down a dark route. I almost wanted Allura to be like, “I just had a shit experience in the first season My man’s left me. I’m pissed off. I’ve gone away, and I’ve come back, and I’m serious now.” So everything’s much more covered up. There are darker layers. She’s experimenting. When I was going through the [recent] runway shows, everything was very pink and quite eclectic, so we mainly went with archival pieces. I used a lot of really rare Yohji Yamamoto, Comme des Garçons, and Lee McQueen.

There was a Margiela skirt suit [from the Spring 1994 collection, Retrospective] and it was just very kitsch. It was a long skirt with a suit jacket and was paired with a black leather baker boy hat from Hermès. We were in a bidding war with a collector in Japan. When we were filming the scene, someone in production or maybe Ryan [Murphy] had mentioned they wanted to tone down the hats for this season. But I got that memo after styling the look, and I was adamant that the look needed the hat. I begged. And then she sat at the table wearing a hat and I could see everyone was like, “How is she getting to wear a hat?” I just felt like the look needed it. It took [Kardashian] out of her normal visual stance and how she usually portrays herself.

Comme des Garçons is very destructive and not really tailored to fit a woman’s body in the way [Kardashian] usually likes. And we got her in [a gray jacket and long lilac-gray column skirt] and worked it around her body. She never once made any comments. She would just look at herself for a second and be like, “Cool.” And then go on set.

I have to let Kris Jenner take credit for [Elizabeth Taylor’s 1969 Scorpio cape, worn by Kardashian in Episode 1], because that’s her cape. She would come to set all the time and once had a conversation with Ryan about the fact that she has this cape. He was like, “That’s it, we need it.” From there, we shipped in a $10 million diamond that was most similar to what Elizabeth Taylor was wearing [in original imagery]. It was flown into L.A. in someone’s pocket, and we had it on set for like five days with Kim’s security. It’s a piece of history. It’s really special. Each day, it would go to Kim and then back in her car home because no one wanted to take responsibility for it.

In general, a lot of my choices in clothing and costume referenced old Bond films where the women were super sexual and curvy and had Brigitte Bardot hair. There was glamour, sex, opulence, money. The outfits were completely inappropriate and not really wearable in everyday life. But if I’m going to watch a show, that’s what I want to see. I want to be like, “What the hell? What is that?” There were comments about Kim’s clothing being inappropriate in the workplace, but that’s what it should be. It’s fun.

Here's How the Blue Tsunami Will Turn the Healthcare Tide

— The Democrats have an opportunity to advance public health, but getting things done won't be easy

Hubris played such a central role in the 2026 midterm election that the ancient Greeks would surely have loved it.

The midterms featured a mortal who promised to avoid war, lower prices, expel immigrants who had committed crimes, and bring prosperity to his nation. He proudly insisted that he -- and only he -- knew how to achieve these Herculean goals. He bragged about the cleverness of his methods and his many (supposed) successes.

All the while, he sowed chaos and distrust, ignored human needs, blamed others for his failures, used excessive force, alienated friends, embraced dictators, and hurt the economy. A survey showed that more than six in 10 of his countrymen said he prioritized his own interests above those of his country.

On November 3, 2026, tens of millions of people rose up to punish his extreme pride, dangerous overconfidence, and arrogance that have, in one way or another, affected them negatively. Healthcare issues were certainly among the grievances.

Now that the blue tsunami has changed the playing field, Democrats must deliver. They need to achieve something concrete over the next 2 years, and they must prioritize the issues, such as healthcare, that were salient to voters in this election cycle. Otherwise, they risk being punished for their own ineptitude and hubris.

Democrats will quickly learn that getting something done will be harder than they imagine. Here are some of the health policy challenges Democrats, Republicans, and President Trump will face in 2027 and 2028.

Between the election and the swearing-in of a new Congress on January 3, 2027, the old Congress will be in a lame duck session. These periods tend to promise a lot and then deliver extraordinarily little. Beyond appropriations, defense reauthorization, and perhaps some renewed efforts to end the war in Iran, I expect little to nothing else, including on healthcare.

During a lame-duck session, nominations and legislation go nowhere unless they have broad bipartisan support and members of Congress feel some urgency. Even then, agreed-upon compromises may not survive procedural objections, time constraints, or general indifference from departing Congress members who want to go home. Simply put, those who have retired or lost their races are deeply conscious of their lame-duck status.

In prior MedPage Today editorials, I pointed out that cabinet secretaries often leave after 2 years, but that Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. would likely stay. After all, Trump has continued to support Kennedy, and Kennedy has continued to have the opportunity of a lifetime to advance his Make America Healthy Again agenda and anti-vaccine stance.

Post-election, the job will quickly become a nightmare for Kennedy, as he will have to respond to hostile investigative hearings led by Democrats on the health committees. And Kennedy is unlikely to get White House help: Trump will have bigger fights to address and less political capital to spend.

Also, if impeachment hearings were to start, Kennedy would be behind only Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth as the object of Congressional attention. This situation will evolve, but I suspect Kennedy will leave (resign) rather than face congressional challenges to his own hubris.

HHS Leadership and Senate Confirmation Hearings

Controversial nominations rarely advance during a lame-duck session. Once the new Congress starts, all past nominations expire, and the White House must start anew. They are not obligated to submit the same names.

Given the White House affiliation with Heidi Overton, MD, PhD, and her weaker credentials compared to her predecessors, I see no way senators would confirm her as FDA commissioner in the lame-duck session. Obviously, her odds would be even worse next year with a Democratic-led Senate. Kennedy, or perhaps his successor, will need to look elsewhere.

Hypothetically, Overton could be renominated solely to trigger provisions in law that would allow Kyle Diamantas, JD, to continue as acting commissioner in 2027. But more likely, another nominee will emerge.

Investigations, Vetoes, Riders, and Compromises

Investigations are the classic response to leading one or both houses of Congress when the President is of the other party. There will be multiple hearings on the administration's opposition to vaccines; the first one will probably focus on measles.

Of course, the new Democratic majority has other options to advance its policy agenda:

As an example of advancing policy via riders, let's look at one of those must-pass bills: FDA user fee legislation. The bill renewal comes with a tradition of serving as a vehicle for non-user fee issues. If it isn't enacted sooner, I would expect efforts like the Clinical Trial Modernization Act to be added to the reauthorization bill. The FDA user fee legislation must become law by September 30, but ideally should be completed by June or July. Perfect timing.

Of course, it is too soon for anyone to know the Democratic strategy because it hasn't been decided. My guess: it will turn out to be a combination of approaches.

Despite the threat of Trump vetoes, Democrats will have the opportunity to advance two of their healthcare priorities in 2027: undoing Medicaid program cuts and restoring Obamacare insurance premium subsidies.

The easy part is creating a reconciliation bill, so that only 51 votes will be needed for Senate passage.

The harder part is finding $1.2 trillion over 10 years to pay for the program restorations. In the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), these cuts already offset the cost of the OBBBA tax cuts.

Democrats will have to find that money through program savings, tax increases, or by admitting they are adding to the deficit. Despite Democratic enthusiasm for action early next year, I don't see how they can do that quickly or easily. They will need to build a consensus on the offsets first.

Many of the newly elected House Democrats from red, pink, and purple states will not be reelected in 2028 if Democrats focus on a dramatic, expensive new federal program instead of solving the issues at the heart of the midterm elections. Those Democrats outnumber the enlarged and noisy new influx of progressives pushing Medicare for All.

Polls suggest two-thirds of voters support "Medicare for All." However, support is cut in half when the issue of tax increases is raised. In my opinion, advocates would best serve their cause by focusing first on a smaller Medicare expansion, such as a buy-in option for individuals between 55 and 65.

My larger concern is that Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.), likely the next chairman of the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor & Pensions and a passionate supporter of Medicare for All, will put a spotlight on this issue, and give lesser attention and support to other health programs that have a more realistic chance of moving forward over the next 2 years.

Not long ago, a broad swath of health issues was bipartisan and rarely controversial or challenged: vaccines, an effective CDC, NIH funding, the value of research universities, and a strong FDA. While some resistance may linger, I believe these will be bipartisan again by the next election cycle, if not sooner. That is a huge win for public health and for all Americans.

Justin Sun Warns Quantum Threat Could Hit Crypto Within 2 Years as TRON Pursues Resistance

Quantum computing is advancing faster than many expect, TRON CEO Justin Sun says. While most people believe quantum computing is still far away, Justin Sun today said crypto attacks are looming.

In an interview organized by FINTECH.TV, Justin Sun said the dangers posed by quantum computers are real and could affect cryptocurrencies within the next year or two. The TRON CEO anticipates that breakthroughs in quantum computing infrastructure are making machines more powerful. This means computers’ ability to break cryptographic systems is becoming more imminent.

The executive recognized potential dangers to public-key cryptography, the backbone of blockchain networks. Crypto networks such as Bitcoin, Ethereum, and many others rely on cryptographic signatures like ECDSA, which are more vulnerable. According to Sun, advancing, powerful, fault-tolerant quantum computers could soon use Shor’s algorithm to break cryptographic signatures more easily than people think.

Currently, most crypto assets are kept in addresses with exposed public keys, putting them at risk of quantum computer attacks. Sun warns the imminent threat is likely within two years, as attackers can collect encrypted data now and decrypt it as quantum computing technology advances.

Today’s quantum computers can’t break cryptographic networks. However, the threat is growing as the technology becomes more powerful. This explains why Justin Sun said TRON is working on post-quantum cryptography.

Last month, the network announced that post-quantum cryptography is available on its testnet. The development marked a significant step toward preparing its infrastructure for potential advances in quantum computing.

This commitment matters because experts project that quantum computing could eventually overpower some of the cryptographic methods currently used to safeguard crypto assets. Post-quantum cryptography aims to build security controls created to resist attacks from powerful quantum computers.

Is Drew Mestemaker really QB1? A troubling trend and lessons from Caleb Williams, Trevor Lawrence

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

Caleb Williams. Trevor Lawrence. Drew Mestemaker.

Two former No. 1 overall picks and probably the hottest name in the 2027 NFL Draft all share a troubling distinction: Over the last decade-plus, only Williams and Lawrence had higher turnover-worthy-play rates under pressure in their final college seasons than Mestemaker has this year.

But there's a catch. For Williams and Lawrence, those numbers were one-year blips after considerably better performances earlier in their college careers. For Mestemaker, the mistakes have followed him from North Texas to Oklahoma State … and they've gotten worse.

None of that means Mestemaker can't be the first quarterback selected in the 2027 NFL Draft. He's put himself squarely in the QB1 conversation, and for good reason. I have Miami's Darian Mensah just ahead of him, but there's a whole lot to like about Mestemaker's game.

NFL teams will also have to answer an important question before investing a high first-round pick in him: What happens when defenses take away the easy throws and force him to make decisions under pressure?

There's plenty to like about the Oklahoma State quarterback, who spent last season at North Texas. For starters, Mestemaker looks the part. He has a strong arm, throws with anticipation and will get through his reads. He's comfortable attacking all three levels, manipulates defenders with his eyes and has the patience to stand in the pocket, reset his feet and deliver fastballs into tight windows.

Just watched Drew Mestemaker's throws vs. UCF, and he just keeps getting better. BTW, if Arch Manning had Mestemaker's tape, he'd be the consensus QB1, probably by a wide margin. Wrote about Mestemaker after he beat Oregon and he's playing better now. https://t.co/MPhvkXpD60

He has plenty of NFL-caliber throws on tape, especially on intermediate routes where he has to throw receivers open. It's easy to see why he's become a popular QB1 choice. But there are also stretches where he tries to do too much, and that's where the evaluation gets more complicated.

Through Week 9 of 2026, Mestemaker has seven turnover-worthy plays on 54 dropbacks when he was pressured, according to PFF. That's a 13.0% rate, more than double the 5.8% median for the first-round quarterbacks in our historical comparison.

(A turnover-worthy play isn't just an interception. It can be a dangerous throw that a defender drops or another mistake that puts the football at risk. And while the number itself doesn't tell us everything, it helps identify decisions that might otherwise get lost in the box score.)

Williams and Lawrence were the only first-rounders we looked at with higher rates in their final college seasons, but both had been considerably better the previous year. Williams went from 0.9% in 2022 to 15.3% in 2023, while Lawrence jumped from 1.6% in 2019 to 13.2% in 2020.

Look at Williams and Lawrence now. Both had bumpy starts to their NFL careers, and both are now two of the NFL's best quarterbacks. It's a pretty good reminder that A.) landing in the right situation is probably the most important factor in a young QB's development, and B.) troubling college numbers don't mean you're destined to fail at the next level.

Mestemaker's struggles have followed him from North Texas to Oklahoma State. His turnover-worthy-play rate was 8.3% under pressure in 2025 before climbing to 13% so far this season. Over that span, he's put the ball in harm's way 13 times in 126 such situations. That's a 10.3% rate, and it's something NFL evaluators will have to consider.

Of course, there's recent precedent for NFL teams looking past troubling numbers. I wrote last November about Ty Simpson's historically poor deep-ball accuracy, and we later learned just how much injuries had affected his play. The Rams still took him 13th overall in April, betting on everything else he did well.

Here's what makes it even more interesting: Mestemaker has gotten much better at avoiding sacks, but he's also making more dangerous throws. Last season, he was sacked on roughly 21% of plays when pressured. That number is down to 11% this year, but while he's taking fewer sacks, he's also averaging a full yard less per pass attempt in those situations (5.6 to 4.5).

The tape provides more clues. Mestemaker generally shows good footwork and accuracy when he has time, and he's capable of working beyond his first read. But there are also moments when he forces throws into coverage instead of taking the easier completion underneath, or when his accuracy suffers because he doesn't get his feet set.

These aren't all necessarily mistakes caused by pressure, but they point to a similar concern: knowing when to challenge a defense and when to take what it's giving you.

The good news for Mestemaker is that other really talented quarterbacks have entered the NFL with concerns about their decision-making under pressure. A lot of them have gone on to be really good. Of course, not everyone develops the same way.

Let's start with Patrick Mahomes. In his final season at Texas Tech, he had an 8.5% turnover-worthy-play rate when pressured, but he also averaged 9.4 yards per attempt and generated 14 big-time throws. Mestemaker, by comparison, has averaged just 4.5 YPA this season, with three big-time throws against seven turnover-worthy plays.

Mahomes took risks, but he was also creating considerably more positive plays when things broke down. That's an important distinction because making dangerous throws is one thing; consistently making spectacular plays to offset those mistakes is another.

Jordan Love offers a different lesson. His final season at Utah State was a mess compared with his outstanding 2018 campaign. He went from 32 touchdowns and six interceptions to 20 touchdowns and 17 picks, and his turnover-worthy-play rate under pressure jumped from 3.4% to 10.9%.

Love averaged just 2.8 yards per attempt when pressured in 2019, even worse than Mestemaker this season. Green Bay still took him in the first round, betting on his physical talent and development. That patience paid off, a reminder that a concerning college season doesn't have to define a quarterback's NFL career.

Then there's Josh Allen, who happens to be Mestemaker's favorite quarterback. Allen inspired him to wear No. 17, and Mestemaker has talked about relating to Allen's unconventional football journey.

Allen's final college season wasn't nearly as productive as the one before. His passing yards fell from 3,203 to 1,812, his yards per attempt dropped from 8.6 to 6.7, and he completed just 56.3% of his passes. His turnover-worthy-play rate under pressure was 8.9%, yet Buffalo took him seventh overall because his physical tools were impossible to ignore.

Paruro, the chefs counter located inside Puna, is an East Asian experience with bites like a nori-cured tenderloin and a seabass curry. Aspirations are high for the beverage program as well. Cooper’s is a moody, mysterious cocktail space with a menu full of unexpected Guanacaste ingredients. Hims & Hers struck a deal with Eli Lilly to offer the drugmaker’s branded obesity treatments through its online health and wellness platform.

Under the agreement, Eli Lilly will give Hims & Hers pharmacies direct access to its U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved GLP-1 medications at self-pay prices. Eligible patients on the Hims & Hers platform can be prescribed and order Eli Lilly's injectable Zepbound (tirzepatide) and daily oral medication Foundayo (orforglipron) without leaving the company's platform.

Prior to this agreement, Lilly drugs were available to patients on Hims & Hers only if they left the site and went to Lilly's direct-to-consumer platform, LillyDirect.

Shares of Hims & Hers jumped 8% Friday following news of the Eli Lilly partnership.

The agreement brings together Lilly’s obesity medicines and Hims & Hers’ consumer platform to expand consumers' access to FDA-approved weight loss drugs, the companies said.

"Patients can complete a consultation, receive a prescription if their provider determines it is appropriate and have an FDA-approved Lilly medicine shipped directly to their home, all in one seamless experience," the companies said in a press release.

Self-pay pricing starts at $149 per month for Foundayo and $299 per month for Zepbound.

"At Lilly, we are always looking for new ways to help more people get the medicines they need, and that means meeting patients where they already are," said Ilya Yuffa, executive vice president and president, Lilly USA and global customer capabilities, Eli Lilly and Company, in a statement. "Millions of people turn to Hims & Hers for their care, and through this agreement, those patients now have a simple, more direct way to get FDA-approved Lilly medicine at an affordable price if prescribed."

Hims & Hers' technology platform supports provider outreach, follow-up care and treatment planning. Weight loss patients on Hims & Hers receive a membership experience that includes 24/7 access to a care team, nutrition guidance, regular clinical check-ins and access to the Hims & Hers Weight Loss community, according to the company.

The agreement with Eli Lilly mirrors Hims & Hers' partnership with Novo Nordisk, which has been selling its branded weight loss drugs Wegovy and Ozempic through Hims & Hers since March. Novo had previously sued the telehealth company for offering compounded versions of its medications. Hims & Hers' agreement with Novo marked a shift in its obesity strategy toward FDA-approved branded GLP-1 treatments and away from cheaper compounded GLP-1 drugs.

Earlier this year, Hims & Hers took heat from the pharmaceutical manufacturer and federal regulators after announcing plans to launch a knockoff of Novo's oral pill. It backed down from those plans under pressure.

Hims & Hers stated that it never offered access to compounded copies of Lilly’s GLP-1 drugs and has committed not to do so in the future.

As part of the agreement, the companies have committed to protecting independent clinical decision-making, so that providers on the Hims & Hers platform continue to prescribe the treatment they determine is right for each patient.

“Lilly is one of the most innovative pharmaceutical companies in history, and I’m incredibly proud that Hims & Hers is a part of making sure more people can access the medicines they’ve created,” said Andrew Dudum, co-founder and CEO of Hims & Hers, in a statement. “By working together, we’re not just reaching more people with obesity with seamless, personal access to FDA-approved Lilly medicine. We’re showing the industry what innovators can achieve for customers everywhere when we work together to make the world well.”

Hims & Hers has faced financial pressure as it shifts from high-margin compounded GLP-1s to lower-margin branded weight-loss drugs. The company posted a $92 million loss in Q1, including $33 million in restructuring costs, largely from write-downs of its compounded GLP-1 supply chain, which executives said faced “risk of obsolescence.”

In Q2, Hims & Hers reported an $86 million loss, compared with a $43 million profit a year earlier. The quarter included $81 million in one-time costs, including expenses tied to its exit from compounded GLP-1s.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/pharma/cms-makes-it-official-codifying-medicare-drug-price-negotiations-rolled-out-under-ira" hreflang="en">CMS makes it official, codifying Medicare drug price negotiations established under IRA</a>

📅 2026-11-06  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
CMS makes it official, codifying Medicare drug price negotiations established under IRA" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Against a backdrop of repeated industry challenges in court, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) is locking in the drug price negotiation framework established under the Biden-era Inflation Reduction Act (IRA).

In turn, the process under which CMS selects certain high-spend Medicare Part B or Part D drugs and negotiates lower prices with manufacturers will carry on into 2029 and beyond. The rule codifies and expands policies previously established through guidance for the initial 2026-28 period.

The final rule is slated to go into effect Dec. 5, CMS said in a fact sheet accompanying Thursday’s announcement. By Feb. 1 of next year, CMS will select up to 20 drugs covered under Medicare Part B or Part D for price negotiations taking effect in 2029, with the potential to also select medicines included in previous rounds for renegotiation.

That upcoming group of drugs will make up the fourth cycle of pricing negotiations, with negotiated prices taking effect in 2029. CMS asserted that the rule gives both patients and drugmakers “a clear path forward as the agency builds on the program’s results and continues negotiating prices for high-cost drugs.”

The initial rounds of price negotiations have already included a slew of prominent brands, including Novo's semaglutide franchise of Wegovy and Ozempic and cancer meds like Bristol Myers Squibb’s Pomalyst, Pfizer’s Ibrance and AstraZeneca’s Calquence.

Meanwhile, the latest tranche of drugs selected earlier this year for negotiation, whose new prices would go into effect in 2028, includes notable entrants like Gilead’s HIV treatment Biktarvy, AbbVie’s Botox and Eli Lilly’s diabetes drug Trulicity, to name just a few.

Beyond codifying the framework for price negotiations in 2029 and subsequent years, CMS has also taken steps to fine-tune several elements of the program.

Namely, the rule finalizes a policy on how CMS classifies certain fixed-combination drugs, with the aim of addressing “potential program integrity concerns” that could encourage drugmakers to evade negotiated prices by steering patients toward new product formulations.

The rule also includes a provision establishing a temporary “small biotech floor” that prevents CMS from offering or accepting a counteroffer below a specified price floor for certain eligible small biotech drugs in 2029 and 2030. It also codifies two policies related to Medicare Part D intended to ensure plan coverage of medicines with a maximum fair price in effect and limit the prices plans pay pharmacies and other dispensing entities.

The IRA and the Medicare price negotiations it empowered have been frequent targets of the biopharma industry, which has broadly alleged that the policy is unconstitutional and that the pricing talks aren’t as voluntary as advertised. But attempts by major drugmakers and powerful trade organizations like the Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America (PhRMA) to push back on Medicare price negotiations have repeatedly stalled out.

In the interim, the U.S. government has made a separate and more recent push to rein in high prescription drug costs by leaning on President Donald Trump’s “most favored nation” (MFN) strategy.

“President Trump directed us to lower drug costs for Americans using every tool at our disposal,” CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., said in a statement announcing the new rule. “This rule provides additional tools to help CMS negotiate better deals, achieve greater savings for taxpayers and beneficiaries, and improve health outcomes for seniors who rely on the Medicare Prescription Drug Program.”

MFN, for its part, has yielded at times, controversial deals with more than two dozen drugmakers, generally involving commitments to price new medicines in line with prices in high-income comparator countries and offer MFN pricing on certain existing drugs through Medicaid.

Through those deals, which have also included investment commitments in the U.S., drugmakers have been able to secure temporary exemptions from the Trump administration’s threatened pharmaceutical import tariffs.

Around this week’s midterm elections, several Big Pharma executives have signaled that they expect little immediate change to their thinking around MFN. Nevertheless, the lack of transparency surrounding the deals has already drawn scrutiny from Democratic lawmakers. With Democrats set to control both chambers of Congress beginning in January, the party may soon press harder to uncover the details of those agreements.
