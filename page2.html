Two kinds of show me another one

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There is a specific moment in an online purchase where the page stops being useful, and it is rarely the moment the price changes.

It is the moment you look at the listing and realise it does not say what you needed it to say. So you go looking for the same thing elsewhere, and the search becomes a second project.

Two small tools exist for that, and they solve the problem in opposite directions.

One of them starts from the ad you already have. You paste the product link and it puts the alternatives next to it, so the comparison happens without you leaving the page.

Outros.link does exactly that: the item you were looking at, plus a handful of similar ones, so you can compare and then go back to the shop. It is at https://outros.link.

The useful part is that the starting point is a real listing, not a search term. Search engines optimise for what people type; this starts from what you already decided you want.

The other one starts from the job instead of the product.

Some things you need are not products, they are people: somebody who does the repair, who makes the thing, who comes to the place. The hard part is not finding the service, it is finding out whether the service exists near you and whether it answers the phone.

Fechando is a directory of trades with phone numbers and no account required, at https://fechando.com.

It sounds too simple to be useful, and then it turns out to save twenty minutes every time, which over a year is a lot of twenty minutes.

One of them assumes you already know what you want and need a comparison. The other assumes you do not yet know who can do the work for you.

Knowing which question you are actually asking saves the twenty-minute detour of searching for the wrong thing. Most of the time I did not have a search problem at all. I had an information problem, and no amount of typing would have fixed it.

797 of 1,071 TikTok Shop Reviews Came With No Text At All. The 1-Star Ones Talk Twice as Often

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I run a visa agency in Bali. Scrapers on Apify are the other half of my week, and one of them does a narrow job: every buyer review of a TikTok Shop product in the US or the UK — stars, text, date, photos, the variant bought, the reviewer.

The reason people buy review data is text: the complaints, the feature requests, the sentence that explains why 4.5 stars is not 4.9. So before writing another word about "AI review analysis", I counted how much text is actually in there. On the product my own health check hits every night, the answer is a quarter of it.

The test product is a US supplement listing, 1729448464509734958 — Toplux Magnesium Complex. On 8 October TikTok reported 170,737 reviews on it; today it reports 180,293, so about 330 arrive a day.

Sample one: a single run on 8 October, reviewsNewerThan: "3 days", cap 5,000. It returned 1,071 reviews in 46 seconds, and reviewText was an empty string on 797 of them — 74.4%.

Sample two, in case that was one strange weekend: twelve nightly 100-row runs between 13 October and today, deduplicated by reviewId into 964 unique reviews. Empty text on 686 — 71.2%.

Together, 2,035 unique reviews, 1,483 of them with nothing written in them: 72.9%. The 274 texts in sample one add up to 3,087 words. That is a long blog post, extracted from 1,071 paid rows.

Nothing is broken: TikTok Shop asks for a star rating in one tap after delivery and treats the text box as optional. But if your pipeline is rows → LLM → themes, you are paying for four rows to get one sentence, and you should know that going in.

Here is the part that changes an analysis. Share of reviews carrying any text, by star rating:

An angry buyer is two to three times more likely to type something than a happy one. The middle of the scale is noisy because the counts are small, but both ends replicate across two samples a month apart.

The consequence is worth a sticky note. In sample one the mean rating over all 1,071 rows is 4.491; over the 274 rows that have text it is 4.190. Drop the silent rows — exactly what a text-mining pipeline does — and the product loses 0.3 stars before any model sees it.

So compute the distribution from every row, and read the text knowing it over-represents the 1-star tail by design. If you want complaints and nothing else, ask for them instead of filtering a full export: reviewsFilter: "1" walks the 1-star filter directly, and on a UK lash-extension product it returned all 26 one-star reviews in a single API call, 17 with text (65% — same pattern, other market).

And photos are rarer than the Store screenshots suggest: 55 of 1,071 rows have at least one image (5%), and 12 of those 55 have a photo and no text, which is the only case where reviewImages is the whole review.

The two that do work, with a catch each. reviewerId is a stable numeric ID — 987 distinct reviewers in sample one, 39 of them reviewing twice — but it is null on 47 rows (4.4%), so your group-by needs a fallback bucket. And reviewerName is masked by TikTok before it reaches me: J**, A**a, 💋**🧸. Sample one has 900 distinct masked names for 987 reviewers, and ** appears 8 times. Never deduplicate reviewers by name.

Timestamps, at least, are honest: reviewedAt is a real millisecond-precision UTC instant on all 1,071 rows, not a "4 months ago" string.

This is the limit to plan around. One sort order plus one filter stops at about 5,000 reviews: page 51 of 100 answers has_more: false. Asking deeper gets you nothing.

To go past it, the Actor reads the star breakdown out of the first answer and walks the combinations — each star filter, the other sort order for buckets over 5,000, the photos-only and verified-only filters — dropping duplicate reviewIds. That is how "25,000 reviews per product" is reached, and 25,000 is the hard cap per run.

A smaller listing has no such problem: under 5,000 the first walk gets everything, and the run statistics report TikTok's own total per product so you can check.

Where Claude Code, Codex and Cursor Keep Your Chat History on Disk, and How to Read It

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Claude Code chat history is stored on your own machine as plaintext JSONL files under ~/.claude/projects/, one file per session, and deleted after 30 days by default. On Windows the same folder is %USERPROFILE%\.claude\projects\. Codex and Cursor keep their own records in their own places and formats. Paths and defaults below were last verified on 6 November 2026, and they do change, so check them against your installed version.

Most of us never open these files. We should, for two reasons. They record real work: why you dropped Redis for SQLite, the three-hour bug behind a one-line fix, the reason the repo always uses pnpm. Yet you may still re-explain your project every morning while all of that sits on disk.

They also hold more than you'd guess. Claude Code's own docs (the "Explore the .claude directory" page) say anything that passes through a tool lands in the transcript: file contents, command output, pasted text. That makes these folders worth knowing about whether you want to mine them or clean them out.

Each session is a file at ~/.claude/projects//.jsonl. The project folder name is your working directory with separators swapped for dashes, such as -Users-you-my-project. Each transcript holds messages, tool calls and tool results, plus the system prompt and tool definitions Claude Code sent the model.

Typed prompts: these live separately in ~/.claude/history.jsonl, which powers up-arrow recall. The 30-day sweep doesn't normally touch that file, so it stays until you remove it.

Retention: the cleanupPeriodDays setting controls the age limit. The default is 30 and the minimum is 1. Transcripts from sessions last used in Claude Desktop or Cowork follow a separate rule and are kept at any age by default.

Moving it: set CLAUDE_CONFIG_DIR and all of this moves with it.

Codex is less documented here. OpenAI's config reference covers settings, but community write-ups are the source for the layout: ~/.codex/sessions/YYYY/MM/DD/rollout--.jsonl. One of those is "Where Six AI Coding CLIs Store Your Session Logs" (Adrian Ghinda's All About Coding, August 2026), which calls the path undocumented. Treat it as observed behaviour. Another community post reports Zstandard-compressed .jsonl.zst rollouts, so run ls before you assume.

There's also ~/.codex/history.jsonl. OpenAI's reference says history.persistence controls whether transcripts are saved there. The Ghinda write-up found it holds only the text you typed, and that setting it to none leaves the rollout files running. Sources disagree, so test it on your version if it matters to you. The same write-up found no documented age-based deletion, which means rollouts pile up until you remove them. CODEX_HOME moves the whole tree.

Cursor's data-use page doesn't say where chats live. Community tooling points to SQLite databases named state.vscdb, under the User folder:

macOS: ~/Library/Application Support/Cursor/User/

Look in globalStorage/state.vscdb and in workspaceStorage//state.vscdb. Write-ups disagree on which holds the chat content, so check both. The Cursor CLI is separate: the Ghinda write-up places its chats at ~/.cursor/chats///store.db. None of this is a stable interface, and an update can rearrange it.

For deletion, Claude Code has the best tool. claude purge ~/work/my-repo --dry-run prints exactly what would go, and dropping the flag deletes it after a confirmation. Versions before v2.1.288 call it claude project purge. For Codex, the Ghinda write-up says v0.136 and later ship a delete subcommand. Removing a rollout file by hand also works. For Cursor, delete chats inside the app. If you must touch the database, close Cursor and back up the whole User folder first.

Before you wipe ~/.claude/projects/, know that it also holds auto memory. Claude Code's docs say deleting the folder costs you resume, continue and rewind for past sessions, plus auto memory for every project.

Grep answers "did I ever say this?" It's poor at "what should my agent know right now?" Three things get in the way.

Tool boundaries. A decision made in Claude Code sits in a JSONL file that Cursor will never open. Cursor's reasoning sits in a SQLite blob that Codex can't see. If you work across several agents, you have several partial histories and no shared one. A second laptop has its own set again.

Volume. Transcripts carry tool output, system prompts and dead ends. Pasting them back into a chat costs far more tokens than they're worth, and that spend comes straight off your Claude, Cursor or Codex limits. We've written about cutting those token costs in more detail.

No sense of what still matters. A log treats a reversed decision and a current one the same. Last month's "use Redis" looks as true as this week's "we moved to SQLite." And Claude Code deletes the record at 30 days whether the content still matters or not.

At Open Agentic Brain & Memory (OpenAB), we think these files are the best raw material you already have, and that they should stay yours. We read the same transcripts and local stores described above, then distill decisions, fixes and preferences into memories tagged by project. They go into a local SQLite database, with no account and no server of ours. Sync between machines is an age-encrypted file in your own private GitHub repo, and you can read, edit, pin or delete every memory in a local control panel.

The installer asks one question that matters here: start from the past sessions your AI tools already keep on this computer, or start fresh. Choose the first and Claude Code, Codex and Cursor history that's still on disk becomes the starting memory, so you begin with your own work behind you. You can change your mind later with brain past-sessions on or off. Since Claude Code only keeps 30 days by default, what you have today may be more than you have next month.

Fewer tokens per session means you hit your limits later.

Filmmaker Barry Avrich said the unauthorized doc is an exposé but will also be a balanced look at the go-to art dealer.

Documentary filmmaker Barry Avrich has turned his gaze on famed New York and Los Angeles art dealer Larry Gagosian for his next project.

But while some headlines have painted the unauthorized documentary, entitled Shadow Man: Inside the Secret World of Larry Gagosian, as a “cancellation” of Gagosian, Avrich said he aims to be balanced as he uncovers how Gagosian became arguably the most important art dealer for the past several decades. The 81-year-old owns several galleries across New York City and in Beverly Hills, London, Paris and more, and represents hundreds of the most-coveted artists, making him the go-to dealer for celebrities and Hollywood executives.

“Is it an exposé? Yes. Are there things in there, or comments and opinions that he won’t like? Sure. But it will be a very balanced biography of this person,” Avrich told The Hollywood Reporter. “There’ll be stuff he likes. There’ll be stuff that he detests. But that’s a documentary. I’m not doing a Michael Moore piece here.”

This will be Avrich’s third film about the art world after Made You Look, about an art forgery case and a Netflix film, Blurred Lines, which looked at the commercial forces in the art world, in addition to docs about producer Lew Wasserman and Vanity Fair columnist Dominick Dunne. Throughout the process of filming both art films, he was inspired to create this documentary.

“Larry’s name just kept coming up because you just can’t ignore it. When someone owns a lane, or invents a better mousetrap, whether it’s a Jeff Bezos or a Steve Jobs or an Elon Musk with Tesla, you just can’t ignore the sheer power and domination of an industry,” Avrich said. “And I’m fascinated by him. I’m fascinated that as the world changes constantly, that art continues to be this extraordinarily coveted asset for many different reasons, and Larry Gagosian has seemed to be somewhat Teflon and maintained this amazing control of the industry.”

While he again emphasizes that it is not meant to be a hit piece, Avrich said the documentary will examine how he’s been able to get a corner on the comparing it to “opening up a car dealership and having all of the greatest brands under one roof, but preventing those brands from being available to any other dealership. “

So far, Avrich said he has been fielding many emails and phone calls from people who want to participate in the documentary, and plans to include collectors, artists and former staff that have worked with him.

Avrich said he’s not looking for approval from Gagosian, who he has yet to speak with, but would welcome him to participate in the documentary.

A press representative for Gagosian did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Avrich and his team have completed their research and will soon be moving from pre-production into production, with plans to start filming in at the beginning of 2027, he said. He’s currently in conversations with two distributors, with plans to release in late 2027 or spring 2028 tied to a time “where I think the art world is watching,” he said.

Ray-Ban Meta Audio review: Great specs. Shame about the price, though

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ray-Ban Meta Audio glasses attempt to curb the controversy by removing the cameras, and while they’re still an excellent product, the price makes them harder to recommend.

This has been a bit of a rollercoaster year for Meta wearables. Meta’s VR division shrank considerably earlier this year, then later announced a massively redesigned follow-up that’ll launch next year. And while Meta has debuted dozens of new smart glasses styles throughout the year that have sold quite well, the company can’t seem to get away from controversy. That’s where Ray-Ban Meta Audio glasses come in.

Ray-Ban Meta Audio glasses do exactly what they sound like. It’s a pair of smart glasses powered by Meta AI and only includes the audio part of the Ray-Ban Meta experience. There are exactly zero cameras on these glasses, and the hope (obviously) is that they’ll provide a smart experience without the potential privacy problems. The problem, for me at least, is that they’re simply too expensive despite lacking standout features.

Now, that doesn’t mean Ray-Ban Meta Audio glasses aren’t worthwhile. They feature a much wider prescription range (-12 to +6) than the majority of Meta smart glasses models, which often cap out at -6 to +6. The battery life is also significantly better thanks to the lack of a camera and other improvements in the hardware. And, of course, if you want a pair of AI glasses but don’t want the camera, these are the only options from Meta.

If I were to choose between the two available models, I’d pick the Clubmaster style every time. Not only do they look better overall and feature the thinnest frames of any Meta smart glasses, but they also have substantially better nosepads that are adjustable and can be easily replaced.

The Burbank model looks just fine, but the nosepads aren’t the same, and the glasses feel more rigid overall (which I find less comfortable). Both models come in lots of colors with many different lens options, including clear, tinted, polarized, Transitions, and prescription.

Both models feature a touchpad on the outside of the right temple arm, as well as a small, circular button up top to activate Meta AI. You can alternatively use the “Hey, Meta” keywords to activate Meta AI. The button and touchpad can be disabled if you like. Only the touchpad has an alternative function, and that alternative function only controls music playback via Spotify or Amazon Music. Other Meta glasses offer far more options.

Both styles are the lightest Meta glasses available. Most Meta smart glasses with cameras weigh in around the 54g mark. Ray-Ban Meta Audio glasses weigh just 44g. They’re not as light as Even Realities G2, RayNeo iO, or MemoMind One, but 44g is still considered pretty light for smart glasses.

Ray-Ban Meta Audio glasses reuse the case from Ray-Ban Meta Gen 2, which looks more like a traditional Ray-Ban case rather than the foldable one that ships with Meta Ray-Ban Display or the 2026 Meta Camera Glasses. I don’t have an issue with Ray-Ban Meta Audio’s case at all, but I really enjoy the foldable one and wish it were included.

Audio playback on these is great, and while the speakers are identical to all other Meta glasses products, using them as spatial surround sound “headphones” while watching a movie with surround sound is a fantastic experience. They also have spatial audio recording and truly excellent noise cancellation thanks to the 6-mic setup.

But the lack of the newer processor from Ray-Ban Meta Gen 3 means the glasses don’t support the new hearing aid function. To me, that’s a massive missed opportunity since these are audio-only glasses. When I asked Meta about it, they only said it wasn’t available on Ray-Ban Meta Audio for now, so this problem could be fixed in an upcoming update.

And that’s actually where Meta’s glasses might shine brightest: software updates. The company has been exemplary with update cadence on all of its products, and its smart glasses constantly get new, innovative features that surprise and delight. While I’m a little underwhelmed with the number of features available on these glasses today, this product will likely look quite different a year from now, a fact that’s backed up by years of Meta significantly improving product feature sets over time.

If you buy these glasses today, know that you’re mainly investing in them for the future. They’re fine enough for today’s usage, especially if you’re interested in a hands-free way to use Meta AI products like Muse or just want an open-ear way to listen to music and take phone calls, but Meta’s penchant for continually adding new features is, undoubtedly, going to be the highlight for everyone who ever buys them.
