This is what can happen if too many devices are sharing your home network

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It doesn’t take much to bring a home network to its knees.

Any home network, regardless of efficiency and performance, can get overburdened when enough devices are connected, each vying for its own slice of the pie. When that happens, it's pretty obvious, with the infamous buffering circle plaguing screens, laggy online gaming, endless download times and the apocalypse landing on your doorstep. It doesn't take much to reach this point either. A single high-end smartphone can eat up quite a bit of bandwidth all by itself.

There are two potential limitations to consider. The first is the internet connection coming into your home via your Internet Service Provider (ISP). The second is the Wi-Fi connection between your router and your devices. A faster internet plan won't fix a weak signal from a router tucked into an out-of-the-way corner. On the flip side, a faster router can't do much with a slow internet service plan.

The knee-jerk reaction is to replace your router, upgrade your internet service plan speed and place the router in a more efficient position. That may indeed help, but not everyone can run out and purchase a Wi-Fi 6 or 7 router and hassle their ISP for faster service and an accordingly higher bill. But you shouldn't have to, as long as you don't mind playing with the numbers.

There's no reliable rule that says "100 Mbps internet connection supports a specific number of devices." Instead, estimate how many devices can run simultaneously without bogging everything else down. Netflix, for example, recommends a stable connection of at least 15 Mbps for each 4K stream. Three streams represent about 45 Mbps of recommended capacity without accounting for any overhead throughout the home (even coffee pots connect to Wi-Fi nowadays).

No, this doesn't mean each stream constantly consumes exactly 15 Mbps. We're just using it as a reference number for the purposes of planning things out. If there are 10 devices in the home, each using 10 Mbps, that would equal 100 Mbps. In that case, a 100 Mbps download speed (FCC's minimum broadband requirement) would leave no room to spare if all 10 devices are up and running at the same time. In reality, of course, speeds fluctuate, but it's a helpful little math drill to run if you're trying to stay organized and keep that ridiculous buffering circle off your TV screen.

Upload speeds are important for things like video calls or security cameras uploading footage of squirrels fighting in the front yard. Upload speeds are generally much slower than download speeds, thanks to asymmetrical broadband with ISPs allocating most of the bandwidth to downloads. Once you understand the speeds and the average consumption of a single device, you can plan large downloads or other Wi-Fi-intensive tasks for less busy times.

For the vast majority of households, one router is more than enough. A good mesh system or efficiently placed access points will solve most problems. An additional router makes sense in very specific situations. For instance, if your ISP has no choice but to run your wires and install your gateway in the far corner of your home, running an Ethernet cable from the gateway to a router in the center of your home might work out great.

But your best bet is to start with what you already have, and add to it incrementally on an "as-needed" basis. Switch stationary devices (PCs, consoles, TVs, etc.) to Ethernet, if possible, removing their traffic from Wi-Fi, although their internet activity will still share your broadband connection. A router upgrade is an option if you need something that can efficiently manage more devices. Just remember that advertised device capacity is great, but it can't overcome bandwidth limitations on your ISP plan.

If you opt for a mesh system to complement an ISP gateway, follow the setup instructions closely and don't leave both devices performing routing duties without checking the configuration. If the gateway and mesh remain in their default routing modes, it could create double NAT, which might cause an issue with online gaming or port forwarding. It doesn't need to get too complicated because the end point boils down to addressing what is constraining your system. Crunch the numbers first; decide which direction to go second. You got this!

Prediction: Some Anthropic Investors Are About to Learn an Expensive Lesson

Anthropic broke records this week, but some investors may be setting themselves up for disappointment.

By Katie Brockman – Nov 21, 2026 at 7:41PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Anthropic ›

Anthropic (ANT -8.50%) made history this week with the largest stock market debut on record, securing $81 billion in funding and surpassing SpaceX's initial public offering (IPO) earlier this year.

There was no shortage of investor enthusiasm either, as the stock popped by more than 17% during its first day of trading. While nobody can say how the stock will fare in the coming days or weeks, I predict that some Anthropic investors are about to learn a hard, expensive lesson.

Dario Amodei. Image source: Getty Images.

Blockbuster IPOs are often turbulent straight out of the gate, experiencing extreme price swings in their initial weeks and months.

SpaceX, for example, has fallen by more than 20% from its initial price, as of this writing. Semiconductor breakout star SK Hynix has plunged by nearly 30% since it debuted in July, and Meta Platforms – then known as Facebook – sank by more than 50% in its first three months of trading in 2012.

In Meta's case, the stock eventually bounced back, delivering total returns of around 1,400% since its IPO. But the investors who reaped those rewards were those who stayed invested through all the ups and downs rather than expecting immediate riches.

The same thing could be said for Anthropic. Over time, it could be a lucrative investment. The company recently generated $17 billion in third-quarter revenue, significantly outpacing its $1.4 billion figure from 2025.

But Anthropic also has some major bills to keep up with. Its IPO prospectus disclosed over $900 billion in expense commitments and a $104 billion net operating loss in 2026, and time will tell whether the company's revenue is sufficient to fund the enormous computing power its technology demands.

This isn't to say that Anthropic is a bad investment. But as with any IPO, there are a lot of unknowns right now, and anything could happen in the coming weeks and months.

If you believe in the business model and are willing to hold your investment for at least a few years despite any potential short-term volatility, it could be a smart buy – assuming it's part of a well-diversified portfolio.

However, investors who are buying primarily because of the hype around the stock, hoping to make a quick buck, have the most to lose. The short term is likely to be a bumpy ride, and those who are only buying with the hope of earning a fortune overnight could end up sorely disappointed.

Fundamentals beat hype every time, and the strongest investments are those with solid underlying business fundamentals and long-term growth potential.

Star Trek Reboot Officially Teased as Crimson Tide Meets Training Day Sci-Fi Thriller

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Two Denzel Washington classics are strong influences on the new Trek movie

After more than a decade of waiting, it seems we may finally get a new theatrically released Star Trek movie. While dedicated Trekkies have heard that before, the directors of the only known Star Trek movie in development recently shared details about their project. The pair helming the new Trek film, Jonathan Goldstein and John Francis Daley, broke down the movie's influences in an interview with Inverse.

Specifically, they cited Training Day and Crimson Tide as jumping-off points for what they’re going for.

For those unfamiliar with Training Day, it stars Ethan Hawke as a newcomer LAPD narcotics officer under the tutelage of the corrupt detective Alonzo Harris, played by Denzel Washington. As for Crimson Tide, it’s a thriller about a U.S. nuclear submarine facing off against a Russian ultranationalist faction threatening to launch nukes at the U.S. and Japan (interestingly enough, it also stars Washington).

As for how the Training Day influence will show up in the film, Daley said the movie will center on some of the “bad apples” in Starfleet. “I would say it’s really kind of a look at the institution of Starfleet and any institution as a whole that has existed for a long time and what that does,” Daley said to Inverse. “I think the portrayal of Starfleet and the people that work within it have always been generally idealized versions of what the future could be. And while this [script] isn’t a cynical or nihilistic take on it, I think it does acknowledge that there are some bad apples in every institution that can have a strong voice and can really make an impact on that institution and everything it stands for.”

Past Star Trek movies have dealt with Starfleet bad apples before to varying levels of success, including Star Trek VI: The Undiscovered Country, Star Trek: Insurrection, and Star Trek Into Darkness. But Daly and Goldstein have another twist on the Star Trek formula.

“It takes place almost in real time, and it just is a whole other look at what a Star Trek movie could be without betraying the fundamentals of what it should be,” Daley said.

The pair added that the film will center on all-new Star Trek characters. “I mean, look, we don’t want to rely on fandom,” Goldstein said. “We don’t want to just hope that there’s enough fans of the original [TOS] or The Next Generation or whatever who will turn out for this. We want to just make a great movie that lives in the world that people are familiar with — some people are familiar with — and that others will discover. And then, with good fortune, we launch a new franchise into existence.”

Although the film is still only in development, there are reasons to be excited based on Daley and Goldstein’s recent work. The pair previously co-directed and co-wrote the surprisingly fun Dungeons & Dragons: Honor Among Thieves, which starred former James T. Kirk actor Chris Pine. Before that, they co-directed the action comedy Game Night, and their most recent theatrical release, Mayday, also received positive reviews.

Without Daley and Goldstein’s film, Star Trek would unfortunately be at risk of going on ice for a bit. Besides Star Trek: Strange New Worlds and Starfleet Academy, both of which will end after their next seasons, there aren’t any announced TV series in the works. Hopefully, this movie can get the franchise back in Paramount’s good graces.

Look for small business grants close to home, from city and state economic development offices, utilities, corporations, and foundations. The SBA gives no grants to start or expand a business, and federal grants for companies mostly fund research or exporting. Apply only where your business fits the grant’s purpose.

A grant is the cheapest money a business can get, since you never repay it and no one takes a share of the company.

The U.S. Small Business Administration “does not provide grants for starting and expanding a business,” according to its own grants page. The federal grants a company can win go mostly to research, through the Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) and Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR) programs, and to exporters.

So for a shop, a restaurant, or a service business, the grants worth chasing are local.

Take a bakery owner who searches the federal grant listings and finds research and export programs, nothing for a bakery. Her city’s economic development office runs a storefront program that pays back half the cost of exterior work, up to $10,000. She applies before hiring anyone, as the program requires, wins a grant, spends $16,000 on a new sign, awning, and lighting, and gets $8,000 back once the work is done.

Here’s where to look, starting close to home, with what each source pays for and who can apply.

A real government grant never charges you a fee. The Federal Trade Commission says no agency will reach out to demand that you pay for a grant, and it warns against paying any up-front fee.

It also warns against paying for a list of grants, since the complete list of federal grants on Grants.gov is free.

Check the sender, too. The SBA says it writes only from email addresses ending in @sba.gov, and that anyone claiming to be the agency from another address should be reported as fraud.

Read what the grant pays for first, since many fund a single purpose. Philadelphia’s Storefront Improvement Program covers exterior work such as signs, awnings, lighting, and paint, and STEP money goes only to export activities the SBA approves.

Then check what you’d have to put in yourself. Philadelphia’s program pays back up to half the cost of approved work, so you cover at least half and pay the whole bill until the city reimburses you. USDA’s Value-Added Producer Grants require a dollar-for-dollar match.

Many grants also want a business that’s already running. STEP is for established small businesses, and the $25,000 Wells Fargo grants the Manhattan Chamber of Commerce offered in 2026 required two years in business.

The competition is steep. Boston’s storefront program says it lacks the money to give a grant to every eligible business, and Hiring Our Heroes names five winners a round. Winning takes an application that answers the funder’s scoring criteria point by point.

Start with one call to your city’s economic development office, and ask which grants it’s funding this year, what each one pays for, and how much of the cost you’d have to cover.

'SNL' Cast Members Confirm the Brutal Reality That You Don’t See Behind the Camera

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Greer Riddell is a London-based film and television journalist writing about comedy, streaming and the evolving media industry in the UK and USA. She works at Collider, covering the latest in film, television and entertainment through interviews, features, reviews and industry coverage.

Her recent brand collaborations include HBO, Warner Bros., Netflix, Hulu, Sky, Disney+ and DC Studios. She has covered titles including Saturday Night Live UK, Rivals, Scrubs, Stuart Fails to Save the Universe, Ludwig, Supergirl and Stranger Things.

Her recent interviews include Florence Pugh, Mike Faist and Christopher Abbott for Netflix's East of Eden, Taika Waititi and Jenna Ortega for Klara and the Sun.

Saturday Night Live UK writes around 35 sketches every week, but only six make it to the live show. Roughly 83% of the comedy created for an episode never reaches television, and even a sketch that makes it through rehearsals can be cut just 90 minutes before broadcast. The cast might already be in costume, the host might have performed it in front of a live audience, and the joke might have landed, but none of that guarantees it a place in the final show. If another sketch gets bigger laughs or makes for a stronger episode, the first one is out.

That ruthless selection process is central to what makes Saturday Night Live work. The show has to produce an hour of live television every week, which means there is little time to rescue a sketch that is not working or make room for one because its writers have worked hard on it. At the Edinburgh TV Festival 2026, SNL UK cast members Emma Sidi and Jack Shep, alongside Lead Producer James Longman discussed what happens to the comedy viewers never see. The result is a format in which the audience does more than watch the show — their laughter helps decide which version of the episode ultimately makes it to air.

Every Wednesday, the SNL UK team perform around 30 to 35 sketches at the table read before selecting the six that will move forward. Longman described the process as having a certain “purity” because hearing the scripts together gives the team an immediate sense of what is funny and what is not quite working. There is no guarantee that a sketch will survive because its premise sounds promising on paper — it has to compete with everything else written that week.

For the writers, that means rejection is not an occasional setback but a routine part of the job. Co-head writer Jonno Johnson explained that with more than 30 scripts to choose from, plenty of writers will inevitably find themselves without a sketch in the show. The challenge, he said, is learning “how you handle kind of failure and rejection.” That is a particularly important skill in a format that moves so quickly. Writers have only a few days to develop material, and once the team has chosen its sketches, there is little opportunity to dwell on the ideas that did not make it so resilience is key.

Cast member Jack Shep admitted at the Edinburgh TV Festival that Wednesdays can be difficult precisely because so much of the material being discarded is funny. “You do like, you know, hours and hours of hilarious stuff, and you just have to let it go,” he said. One sketch he was sad to see get cut involved a group of friends trying to persuade someone not to leave her boyfriend because he worked at Jo Malone, and they would lose access to his staff discount! Oh, and the boyfriend was a “horrific blob.”

The UK desk isn’t chasing shock value — it’s using comedy to cut through the language everyone else tiptoes around.

At 6pm on Saturday, SNL UK stages a dress rehearsal in front of a live audience, running through a longer version of the show before the 10pm broadcast. For the first time, the team can see how the sketches work as performances in front of a crowd. Longman explained that this is when they begin to feel the energy of the show, discover which jokes get laughs, and which do not. The audience's reaction becomes part of the editing process, helping the team decide what deserves to stay.

Even then, getting a laugh is no guarantee of making it to air. Emma Sidi recalled at the Edinburgh TV Festival a sketch cut after the dress rehearsal for the second episode of Season 1, hosted by Jamie Dornan. The segment was "Loose Men", a Loose Women/The View-style talk show in which Dornan blew bubbles. It had gone well in front of the audience, but Sidi admitted, “All the other sketches were way better.”

This is where the live audience becomes more than a group of people attending to purely enjoy a comedy show. Director Liz Clare explained at the Edinburgh TV Festival that the audience in the room takes precedence over home viewers because their laughter helps fuel the performance. The cast is effectively playing to two audiences — the people watching in the room and the viewers who will see the live broadcast at home. A laugh in the room gives the team useful information about the sketch, while the final decision depends on how the material fits into the episode as a whole. The audience are therefore unknowingly helping shape what millions of viewers eventually see.

After the dress rehearsal, Longman and the team gather to cut two sketches. With around 90 minutes left before the broadcast, the running order can be rearranged, material rewritten, and entire sections removed. “Everything gets scrambled, rearranged,” Longman explained. “It keeps getting rewritten, and then the show goes out of 10.” By then, a sketch can have survived the writing process, made it into rehearsal, and been performed with the host already in costume, only to disappear before the audience at home ever gets to see it. That is the brutal reality of the SNL format!

Even those at the top are not immune. Johnson recalled writing a sketch about a planet where everyone was called Paul Selby, and its inhabitants could not understand how Earth society functioned with everyone having different names. The premise seemed perfectly reasonable while he was writing it, but watching it back left him questioning the idea and his sanity!

Johnson admitted that the writers who do best on SNL UK are those willing to play with other people's ideas and abandon their own when needs must. There is not enough time to keep refining a sketch that is not working. Sidi expressed a similar attitude, explaining that writers have to be able to recognize that an idea was good and still accept that it is dead. As Longman put it, “It's all about the show. It's not about what I think is funny.”

That is the logic behind Saturday Night Live's 17% rule. SNL UK is not trying to preserve every promising idea or reward the people who have spent the longest working on it. It is trying to make the strongest possible show from the material available, using the audience's reaction as one of the tools that helps it get there. For viewers at home, the result is a show that appears to move effortlessly from one sketch to the next. Behind that apparent ease is a process of constant comparison and rejection, with most of the comedy created each week left on the cutting-room floor. Let's hope for an extended cut one day.
