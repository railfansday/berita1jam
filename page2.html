Is Patriots TE Hunter Henry playing today?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

New England Patriots tight end Hunter Henry is officially listed as questionable to play in Sunday's game against the Green Bay Packers.

Henry suffered a foot injury that forced him to leave last week's game against the Miami Dolphins early. He missed the first two practices earlier in the week, but he returned as a limited participant at Friday's practice.

The Patriots didn't elevate another tight end to the active roster on Saturday, which could be a good sign that Henry is going to try to play through the injury. If Henry doesn't play, rookie third-round draft pick Eli Raridon would project to the top of the depth chart as the No. 1 option, with Tanner Arkin and Cameron Latu serving as the reserve options.

With top wide receiver A.J. Brown and Romeo Doubs both sidelined, Patriots quarterback Drake Maye has leaned more heavily on Henry as his safety blanket in the passing attack. Not having that option against the Packers' top-ranked passing defense would be a huge loss for the Patriots.

However, the offense could look improved in Week 9 if Henry does play. Patriots veteran wide receiver Mack Hollins is returning from injured reserve, which means Maye could have two of his most reliable receiving targets back on the field at the same time for this matchup.

..With Nuno Espirito Santo out of the job and with Scott Parker just 72 hours in, the 7,500+ travelling fans turned on their stars.

Under no illusions after the result – and the away fans’ response – yesterday: Scott Parker

Traditionalists will likely be aghast at the thought of fans undermining their own team in this manner, whilst those who participated no doubt feel it was the only way to show their contempt for the perceived lack of commitment and effort which those eleven representatives  of the Claret and Blue heritage displayed.

Not to mention the hundreds of pounds it costs each supporter, every game, merely to arrive at the turnstiles in support of our club. But is it the ‘right’ thing to do?

For me – it’s a no. I started attending games with my son when he was ten years old and however bad it got (it was pretty dire in that season of 2003-4) we never left early, never booed our own players (Luis Boa Morte excepted, I must confess to once letting my frustration with his indolence bubble over) and always, always applauded those who’d put in a shift, regardless of outcome. And I’d not even heard ‘you’re not fit to wear the shirt‘ sung by West Ham fans in my couple of decades’ worth of attending games.

I just don’t see it helping team morale or positively influencing the final outcome. Which is, after all, the point of ‘supporters’.

Giving ‘The Sun’ another gleeful headline to point out our sufferings sticks in my throat too.

But don’t get me wrong, each has absolutely the right to their own response. Just don’t see it helping is all.

Blizzard pulled out all the stops to ensure World of Warcraft: Forever survived a chaotic launch night. What was the company's secret weapon? Splurging an absurd amount of money on infrastructure made up of 3,924 virtual machines with 70,480 CPU cores and a whopping 251,136 GB, or 251 TB, of memory.

Praised by media and players as one of the smoothest launches in Blizzard's history, World of Warcraft: Forever requires significant computing firepower. 3,924 virtual machines work out to 18 cores and 64GB of memory per instance. Or, the company could be using a mixture of 16-core and 32-core nodes with slightly less memory.

To put the infrastructure's scale in consumer terms, we are looking at about 8,810 mainstream processors, or 7,848 high-end gaming systems with 32GB of memory each. In any event, the hardware is greatly improved compared to what World of Warcraft used to run on back in 2009.

*Slaps Roof of WoW: Forever* This baby is running on: 3,924 Virtual Machines 251,136 GB of Memory 70,480 CPU Cores https://t.co/P9PdRBzke7November 5, 2026

In 2009, Blizzard said it used 10 data centers worldwide to host World of Warcraft. At the time, the infrastructure included 13,250 server blades, totaling 75,000 CPU cores and 112.5 TB of memory. By comparison, World of Warcraft: Forever leverages 6% fewer CPU cores but rocks 2.2X more memory. Modern processors are leagues faster than chips from 17 years ago. By today's standards, the bottleneck is memory capacity and bandwidth, which is why the infrastructure can run on fewer cores but has more than double the memory to compensate.

Blizzard does not specify the exact hardware that World of Warcraft: Forever runs on. However, if we were to translate the core specifications to, for example, AMD's EPYC 128-core Bergamo or 96-core Genoa chips, it could be anywhere between 275 and 365 2U server nodes, depending on which processor it uses. Therefore, World of Warcraft: Forever could be running on just eight to ten 42U server racks, a noteworthy improvement over 17 years ago.

Blizzard should have no trouble building the infrastructure for World of Warcraft: Forever. A ballpark estimate for about ten 42U server racks with EPYC chips is at least $15 million, even with today's memory pricing. But then again, since Microsoft owns Blizzard now, the company could just be tapping its parent's existing Azure platform. Technically, it is not free compute, but Blizzard would benefit from cost pricing and priority allocation of Azure's capacity.

World of Warcraft: Forever shows that a high-profile launch does not have to be a nightmare when companies are willing to invest in computing infrastructure. In this case, many would agree Blizzard is putting those 251TB to good use, although Sam Altman may say otherwise.

It’s not necessarily too late to plant amaryllis bulbs for Christmas, but timing does matter. Amaryllis typically blooms six to eight weeks after planting, so early to mid-November is your last chance to get bulbs in the pot if you want striking holiday flowers in time for the big day.

If you’re growing amaryllis for holiday flowers, choose the right type. Amaryllis usually refers to Hippeastrum, a South American native, not true Amaryllis, also known as belladonna lilies from South Africa. Most Hippeastrum bulbs sold this time of year are grown in the Southern Hemisphere and will bloom in six to eight weeks. Dutch-grown bulbs take longer, so avoid these.

Flowering also varies by the plant’s growing conditions, which means you have some control. While early or mid-November is the ideal time to plant a holiday amaryllis, you can do a few things to speed up the process if you’ve just missed the window.

Getting healthy holiday blooms isn’t just luck. How you choose and plant your bulbs is important. Start by selecting the best bulbs. Look for large, firm, and healthy bulbs without soft spots, damage, or mold. If you see a bulb that has the tip of a green stem emerging, grab it. This bulb has already begun to break dormancy and should flower sooner.

While unusual varieties can be expensive, you don't need to spend a lot of money on bulbs – Walmart sells preplanted bulbs for under $6.

Next, choose a good-quality potting mix, such as Miracle-Gro Indoor Potting Mix, available at Amazon, and a container with drainage holes that is only one or two inches (2.5 to 5 cm) wider than the bulb. Position the bulb in the pot so that the pointy end is up and one-third to one-half of the bulb is above the surface of the potting mix.

Water your planted bulb thoroughly, letting it all drain through, before setting it in a warm, bright spot. Keep the potting mix just slightly damp, never soggy. Amaryllis doesn’t need much water and can rot in soggy conditions.

Wandrd’s Magnetic Camera Straps Are My New Favorite Accessory

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

More than a decade ago, Peak Design rethought the humble camera strap. This was a necessary development. When you buy a camera, manufacturers almost always toss in a chintzy, uninspired strap with their logo on it. These cheap straps have cheap mounting systems that make it hard to quickly remove the strap from the camera when you need to, like when you're attaching your camera to a tripod or gimbal and you’d rather not have a dangling strap whacking you in the face.

Peak Design solved this with its Anchor Links system, small circular nubs you leave attached to camera eyelets that make detaching or even swapping straps easy. The quick-connect mechanism is now the de facto industry standard and has inspired countless copycats.

Now, Wandrd has outdone that design using magnets.

“We're photographers ourselves, so we're constantly ideating, tinkering, thinking of ways that things can be better,” Ryan Cope, Wandrd cofounder, tells WIRED. “A camera strap like this has been at the back of our minds for a while.”

Wandrd—known for its camera bags—launched its Magnetic Camera Strap system on Thursday. It features a circular anchor similar to Peak Design, called “Torus Links.” The anchors attach to your camera’s eyelets and are rated to carry up to 198 pounds—so unless you're bench-pressing your camera, they'll do the job. If the cord does start to wear down over time, you'll first see yellow under the black fabric—a sign you should replace the Torus Link. If it's red, you should stop using the link immediately—a warning system borrowed from Peak Design. (Wandrd offers a two-year warranty.)

Unlike Peak Design's anchors, the Wandrd anchors look like donuts, with a hole in the middle. When you attach the strap, the embedded magnets guide the Torus Link into the strap's CoreLock mechanism, which pinches into the hole to secure the camera body. The magnets make connecting a strap easier, and they keep the strap fastened to the anchor, but you can’t just pull the strap off like you’re pulling a MagSafe accessory on your phone. You’ll need to use the strap’s dual-pinch mechanism to remove it from the Torus Link, a design that’s more secure.

The Torus Link is the donut-shaped object that attaches to your camera. On the right is the Magnetic CoreLock at the end of the strap. Pinch the top and bottom of this mechanism and let the magnets guide the strap into the Torus Link.

It’s a much faster way to connect and disconnect a strap, and that speed pays off in the field. This week, I had to photograph a product during a press briefing. Usually, I use Peak Design's Leash and, instead of taking off the strap, I bunch it up in my hand when it's not around my neck. It's not that it's hard to remove—it's just an extra step I have to think about. But Wandrd's magnetic system felt faster to unlock mid-shoot when I wanted to lower the camera for an awkward, low-angle shot, and reattaching the strap was practically instantaneous. I did the whole swap one-handed, with the magnets doing most of the work.

“I love magnets, man,” Cope says. “I love the simplicity they create—guiding things into place. You don't always have a place to put your camera down; I like that magnets help guide them and are a lot more secure, and they're also just fun.”

I have two Torus Links on my camera body, one on the right eyelet and another on the tripod baseplate. But my favorite configuration turns the strap into a single-point sling. The strap has a built-in “Torus Loop” on the buckle—clip one end directly into it to create a closed loop worn across your torso, leaving just a single magnetic connector to snap onto your camera. When you unclip the camera to shoot freely, the strap stays neatly in place across your body instead of sliding off your shoulder.

The nylon felt comfortable across my chest and near my neck—there's no padding, but that's intentional. Cope says Wandrd spent two years developing this custom “liquid nylon” tubular webbing that is softer along the edges, so it won't feel like the strap is cutting into your neck after a long day of wear. The lack of metal throughout the strap system also prevents the strap from scratching up your camera gear.

Best of all, you don't have to replace your strap to use Wandrd's system. If you have a strap you love—maybe a fancy leather strap someone gifted you—Wandrd is selling a DIY kit ($29) that lets you attach its Magnetic CoreLock system to the ends of your preferred strap. Otherwise, the strap kit comes in a 30-mm size ($69), a 15-mm “Lite" ($49), and a wrist strap ($39).

I don't know if Wandrd's system will see the same success as Peak Design's Anchor Links, but I don't see myself switching back anytime soon. Magnets are just too dang cool.
