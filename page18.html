Funny ha-ha or funny peculiar? Yesterday had plenty of both

Here’s a question: did last night’s Newcastle United 2 Arsenal 2 match at St James’ Park show we can be the equal of the reigning Premier League champions?

At 16 minutes past six, after a miserably one-sided first half, you would have been embarking on mission impossible to find anyone saying “yes” among the 52,000+plus in the stadium or the millions watching on TV.

By the final whistle, blown nearly 10 minutes after the 90 by Chris Kavanagh, few would have replied “no”. Such is football.

Let’s start at the beginning, or at least on the 12.30 Azuma train from King’s Cross to Newcastle. The LNER service was predictably packed with Gooners. Passengers in Carriage J were overwhelming confident of a one-nil or two-nil to the Arsenal, as they like to crow in north London.

Based on their team’s results this season, that was to be expected. They don’t score many and, apart from a blip near Brighton, they concede even fewer.

Arsenal are, in a word, efficient. I find them boring. The descriptions are not mutually exclusive. No way as the train rumbled into the magnificent Central Station about 32 minutes late was I looking forward to a feast of entertaining football.

In dire need of a drink, I walked up Pink Lane with Big Nige, who had boarded the delayed train at York after it made an unscheduled stop south of the old Viking stronghold of Jorvik. Known as Eboracum in Roman times, btw.

Pink Lane was awash with happy Londoners. There are many, many reasons to be proud of Newcastle; one is the warm welcome the natives offer strangers (excluding Mackems, obviously. There have to be limits).

Which was why the mild hostility that greeted me in the Mosaic Tap was a big surprise. A young chap called Rob walked up to our table, where Paul and Gowa had already got in the drinks, and said: “You can’t wear that scarf in here.”

His mate had apparently clocked me as a Gooner, having mistaken my half-and-half for an Arsenal favour.

A little bit of background. Said scarf was a souvenir from the Bayer Leverkusen v NUFC match. Not that I bought it on my visit to the BayArena shop a mere 11 months ago, when it would have cost £20. This one was acquired in October for a tenner in the NUFC pre-Christmas online sale, while present-buying for my daughter Jo and her family. The PIF need all the help they can get . . .

For all those who detest a half-and-half, that’s your prerogative. I view it as a reminder of a great trip, more useful than a match programme and less likely to be lost.

Rob quickly realised his error, probably because I had slipped into my best am dram persona by screaming: “Look at the scarf! Look at it! You need to go to Specsavers, mate!”

The faux outrage reduced poor Rob to a gibbering, hilariously apologetic wreck. “Sorry, sorry, I’m really sorry. It was my mate . . . What are you drinking, can I get you one, please?”

The free glass of malbec was much appreciated, thanks. Considering the prices at Mosaic, the scarf has probably paid for itself. Funny ha-ha, Rob.

We played the “who would you select” game and all four of us agreed Gonzalez was not justifying his place. Starting Steur was a no-brainer, the back five picked themselves, based on the Fulham game, with Livramento available if needed to give us a second-half lift. The midfield and attack, apart from Steur and Barnes, were as clear as a peasouper.

The big news at 4.15 was Bruno G starting for his new team. Who had expected that?

There was just time for a quick one in Bar Loco, where I bumped into the legendary Tony Mallabar, before safely negotiating Turnstile 31 at the Leazes End. My seat in row MM was in line with the edge of the six-yard box, which gave a perfect view of the fourth goal, unfortunately.

The Booing of Bruno had been a hot topic. My opinion is that every opposition player should be relentlessly jeered, booed, abused and barracked (isn’t SJP on Barrack Road for a reason?) That’s what I recall, perhaps falsely, from big games in the Seventies . . .

Anyway, the Brazilian midfielder was booed from the warm-up until his replacement by Lewis-Skelly when we were leading 2-1. The effort of singling out our former captain probably backfired, because it distracted the more vocal supporters from doing the job of cheering their favourites.

Not that the players or the play gave reasons to be buoyant in the first half. Arsenal did what Arsenal do, using their 11 players against our outfield 10. Whenever Osula or Willock or Murphy or Barnes or Steur attempted to put pressure on Raya, he found the spare man whom our would-be presser had just left unmarked. Boring and efficient by Arsenal. Half-cocked and aimless by the Mags.

In my opinion, Arsenal are no great shakes, whatever the title says. A great team have a top, top striker. They don’t. As they proved by managing only one effort on target before the interval. Hornicek didn’t have a prayer once Hall chested down a long ball and instantly lost possession near the edge of his box.

Our left-back is a wonderful footballer but, having rarely played as a defender in his youth at Chelsea, he is still learning some of the skills essential to be a watertight obstacle.

Murphy tried to catch Raya off his line with a speculative long-range effort and one other shot flew narrowly wide of Raya’s left post via a deflection off Barnes. There was almost nothing else of note in the first half as the champions biffed nearly every shot high, wide and ugly.

Burn and Thiaw stood reasonably firm, Dedic and Murphy seemed on different wavelengths, Steur harried Bruno G and Barnes showed some unlikely aggression. Miley plugged away and Osula ran gamely but United lacked any sort of perceptible gameplan. We rarely managed to find a player in space. Worrying times.

Where was our next goal coming from, wondered my mate Dave at the interval. It had been a while at SJP.

The answer came within four minutes of the restart, soon after the first of three stoppages caused by apparent injuries to Raya. More importantly, after Matthias Jaissle made a triple substitution.

Harvey Barnes smashed a shot low to the keeper’s right, ending a quick attack. Willock had pressured Odegaard into presenting the ball to Osula. An assist on the headless chicken’s credits, fitting reward for another tireless display.

This was when the match became funny peculiar. Miley man-marked Bruno, Arsenal misplaced passes, United won 50:50s, we found space to run with purpose. The gulf in monetary and player resources vanished as the Mags reverted to our best version of Howe-ball.

Only Hornicek of the summer 2026 signings was still on the pitch, Steur having been subbed.

Arsenal looked distinctly second-best. Raya went down again. A corner on our right from Hall was aimed beyond the far post, where the unstoppable Man from Blyth powered a header goalwards. In a frantic kerfuffle, a couple of blocks merely delayed the inevitable. Thiaw smashed it home and we were ahead.

Around me in row MM, the earlier, almost incessant moans and grounds became voices of encouragement. Supporters who had failed to back their team in a dire first half were suddenly full of the joys of November . . .

Ramsey, Livramento and Elanga all caused Arsenal problems. For a few minutes, we battered them.

Then Raya collapsed for a third time. The long delay gave the champions a break they desperately needed.

Whatever anyone else says, Jaissle and his men were right to keep pressing for a third. Arsenal were always going to fashion at least one good chance in the time remaining after we went ahead. To sit back with 28 minutes left, plus a lot of stoppage time, would have been professional suicide.

My suspicion is that those who now bemoan our aggressive play would have whinged even more if the team had sat back. The match became attack and counter-attack. Arsenal, through Madueke doing a decent impression of the absent Saka, kept cutting onto his left foot. Hall, sometimes having to mark two opponents, let the right winger swerve inside and slam a low shot through two defenders and past Hornicek.

The keeper got two hands to the ball but could only push it into the Leazes End net.

Havertz nearly produced what would have been a truly wonderful moment when his head diverted a late Hall corner beyond Kepa and against the crossbar. So near, yet so far.

Then Barnes capped what I reckon was his best game since Wembley 2025 by outstripping Rice on the left touchline and playing the ball perfectly to Ramsey on the edge of the 18-yard box.

In the words of John Ando Anderson, just hit the bloody thing, man. Ramsey did. Sadly, though, instead of putting his laces through it, he tried to bend the ball into the postage stamp and it flew high and wide.

What a feather in Jaissle’s cap a win would have been. One point was good, three would have been great.

The late introduction of Wissa, who replaced Willock, was a failure. He contributed almost nothing, which has been the story all too often since his expensive arrival.

I would have thrown on Fernandez-Pardo as the game opened up. No team enjoy speedy opponents running hard at them and the Belgium international could have been the joker in Jaissle’s pack.

On the balance of play a draw was reasonable but this, like the Liverpool result, felt like two points dropped.

What is inarguable is the team’s willingness to battle for their mates, their manager and the Toon. Long may that continue.

Scrambled Eggs Are 10x Tastier When You Add One Store-Bought Ingredient

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

We may receive a commission on purchases made from links.

When you're a fan of eggs, they can be pretty delicious however it is that they come off the stove or out of the oven. Even so, sometimes you're in the mood for something that doesn't require a lot of preparation yet is adaptable and tastes delicious. That's where scrambled eggs come in. They have a beautiful texture — whether you like them super soft and slightly gooey or a bit on the crispy side. There are a lot of different techniques, and some ways to cook them are better than others – like cooking them in water is probably a bad idea. Following some expert tips for scrambled eggs can get you to full-on restaurant quality; for example, fresh eggs at room temperature will give you great-tasting results that cook evenly. But when you want to keep it super simple at home, you can improve your everyday scrambled eggs by adding in a little bouillon.

Either beef or chicken bouillon will work. Beef bouillon will give your eggs a deep, unctuous taste that can be delicious, but may be a bit heavy if you're not looking for something super bold. Using a little chicken bouillon, on the other hand, will add some bright, salty notes that still have plenty of extra umami. Chicken bouillon will bring out the natural savory, even slightly sweet flavor of the eggs without overpowering them — which makes sense because they're both, well, chicken. Whichever you choose to use, bouillon makes a super easy addition to your scrambled eggs that really only takes a few extra seconds of work.

As far as the bouillon goes, really anything will work — although it is often easier to get exact smaller measurements with powdered jars or pastes, like Better Than Bouillon Premium Roasted Chicken Base. Most standard bouillon cubes are equal to 1 teaspoon of bouillon powder or paste, but it can differ between brands, so take a look at the label. You'll want about a quarter teaspoon per three eggs. You can simply whisk the bouillon into the raw eggs with whatever seasonings and dairy you'd normally use (although you may want to lay off extra sodium because it's already quite salty), then scramble them to your preference. You can even wait to add the bouillon until the eggs have begun to set, then remove them from the heat and fold in the bouillon, so it just sort of melts in.

If you want to go beyond milk or cream, you can try it with a little crème fraîche, sour cream, or cream cheese, or even a Gruyère or sharp cheddar. Pepper is a must to bring out your egg's full potential, and you might want to even try a pinch of sugar for a hit of sweetness that blends in nicely — especially with chicken bouillon. As for the veggies and herbs, this trick will go great with pretty much everything you usually like to see in your eggs; just consider the flavor profile if you're using beef versus chicken. You can even use canned veggies in your scramble; for example, canned tomatoes are subtly sweet and tangy, pairing excellently with chicken bouillon while canned mushrooms add umami and even more depth to beef.

Life in Isolation: Are We Living in a Human Zoo?

Posted November 22, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

In Stolen Focus, Johann Hari explores the distress of captive animals through veterinary behaviorist Nicholas Dodman's description of "frustrated biological objectives": innate needs that remain active even when circumstances prevent their expression. Zoochosis describes abnormal behaviors observed in captive animals, including compulsive self-directed activity.

Let’s consider if zoochosis could apply to human beings too: could some of our depression and anxiety be less about defective individuals and more about the increasingly unnatural environments we live in? We recognize something is wrong when a tiger paces endlessly in a concrete enclosure. Yet when human beings experience restlessness, alienation, depression, or anxiety, we are remarkably quick to diagnose them. “I don’t know who discovered water but I doubt it was a fish,” said Marshall McLuhan. Maybe it’s the water that surrounds us that’s toxic and not something inside of us?

Remote work, online shopping, food delivery, streaming entertainment, and virtual socializing have become increasingly normal since the pandemic. Many of these things are genuinely convenient and some have improved people's quality of life. But maybe our frictionless lives are not necessarily an upgrade to the human experience? Maybe we thrive when we have little victories like trips to the store, restaurants, gyms and other third spaces where we interact with others?

We work on one screen, order necessities through another, socialize through messages, exercise in front of a monitor, and unwind by scrolling through videos of other people living lives that appear to be more interesting and exciting than our own. We can spend days on end interacting with two-dimensional representations of the world without actually being face-to-face with other people. Ever heard of pheromones?

We accumulate hundreds of online acquaintances without experiencing the vulnerability and authenticity that we need and crave. We communicate constantly while remaining profoundly lonely.

Humans evolved to move through complex physical terrains, not sit on couches with laptops on our laps. We need touching, exploring, smelling, creating, cooperating, taking risks, and belonging to communities in three dimensions, not two.

And being constantly available is not our friend. Neither is excessive optimization nor compulsive productivity. Having no time for friends, family, or contemplation should be seen as failure. We can no longer simply take a walk; we need to track our steps, monitor our heart rates, and demonstrate that we have accomplished something in contrast to “just walking.”

So what if some of what we call individual psychopathology is really a response to collective conditions that frustrate fundamental human needs?

Psychology should examine not only what patients are thinking and feeling but also take into account the influences that are causing people to think and feel the way they do. We should ask questions such as, “Do they have true agency? Are they physically engaged with the world? Do they experience meaningful relationships, belonging, creativity, and contribution? Do they have opportunities to experience spontaneity, wonder, and delight?”

Perhaps we need to reconsider the conditions under which we expect human beings to live, work, love, and flourish. That means examining not only individual choices but also workplaces, economic incentives, social institutions, and the technologies that increasingly mediate our experience of reality.

Helping people adapt to their circumstances is an important part of psychotherapy but our adaptation to COVID-lockdown reclusiveness may be more akin to zoochosis than we have hitherto believed.

Hari, J. (2022). "Stolen Focus: Why You Can't Pay Attention—and How to Think Deeply Again." Crown.

GTA 6 “Greatest Artistic Achievement In History,” Says Cyberpunk 2 Director

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Grand Theft Auto 6 is the highest-rated game in history, only rivaled by Ocarina of Time — but no developer should try to rival its scope and ambition.

We don't know what exactly the budget was, but Rockstar head of publishing Jennifer Kolbe recently pushed back on rumors of a $2 billion price tag, clarifying it cost somewhere under $1.5 billion. Most studios don't have that kind of change to throw around, because most studios aren't printing money with a game like GTA Online.

Indeed, Cyberpunk 2 creative director Igor Sarzynski has made it clear that CD Projekt Red has no intention of rivaling GTA 6, despite the broad-stroke similarities between both series.

"GTA 6-induced-open-world-game-dev-depression is real," he mused on Bluesky. "Probably the single greatest artistic achievement in history, I mean it. Scale, density, coherence, thoughtfulness of it all, just a technological and creative miracle. Cyberpunk 2 is canceled, imma go sweep autumn leaves in my backyard now.

"Seriously though I think we're doing something of our own, with a different unique flavor," he continued. "GTA is impressive in a way all great art is, it expands what is possible, a captivating vision flawlessly executed."

It's a sentiment echoed by others in the games' industry, like Larian Studios head of publishing Michael Douse, better known by his online moniker - Very AFK.

"Maybe in a world that often feels like it's going to sh*t it is nice for everyone to enjoy a cultural moment together that brings every audience (and every generation) the same excitement, behind something that has turned out to be genuinely brilliant," he posted to Twitter. "Not every game needs to be GTA, and that both makes space for other types of experiences, and the rare moment even more special!"

Naughty Dog game designer Michael Barclay even said that GTA 6 appears to him "as a Bugatti Veyron would to a nervous Victorian child."

Considering, just three years ago, the industry warned that Baldur's Gate 3 was the game no other developer should try to replicate, given the unbelievable scope and sheer amount of interactivity, recorded dialogue, and branching paths it offered to players, that's one hell of an endorsement.

TheGamer's own Lead News Editor, George Foster, put it bluntly in his GTA 6 review, "we'll never get a game like it again," and frankly, we shouldn't.

GTA 6 has reportedly been pre-ordered almost ten millions times.

Ballooning development budgets are already putting a target on studios' backs, making every single new release a financial gamble that could mean mass layoffs or even closure, and those high-stakes are only making the industry increasingly risk-averse.

GTA 6 shouldn't set a new bar for other developers to strive for. The unimaginable amount of resources, personnel, and time it took to develop the game is simply unrealistic for other studios. Thankfully, CDPR understands that. Let's just hope the rest of the industry does, too.

The most anticipated video game of all time is a triumph on every level that exceeds years of unbelievable expectations. You're not ready for Leonida.

How Diego Forlan fought to become a Manchester United cult hero

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Even those who watched Diego Forlan closest disagree over whether he was any good at Manchester United.

Sir Alex Ferguson remembers the striker as a “tough, grand player”. But Gary Neville, in his autobiography, lumped Forlan in with Kleberson, David Bellion and Liam Miller as the epitome of the ‘Djemba-Djemba years’.

Regardless of whether or not he was rated, Forlan remains revered by those on the Stretford End all these years later.

Forlan’s song is still sung with gusto around Old Trafford and by travelling Reds, especially around meetings with Liverpool. It was at Anfield where the forward scored the two goals that cemented his place in United fans’ hearts and their place in his.

“I heard the 3,000 fans in the away end loud and clear,” he wrote in his column for The National in 2016. “‘Diego’, they sang, ‘Wooahh, Diego, wooaah. He came from Uruguay, he made the Scousers cry.’

“I allowed myself a second to enjoy the moment. These fans sang with their Manchester accents louder than anything the Liverpool fans could come up with.

“As they sang their beautiful song that they still sing to this day, I thought: ‘I am a lucky man. This is the type of moment that you dream of as a boy, the type of thing I’ve worked so hard for.'”

Forlan’s Anfield heroics came in the middle of a three-month period that was by far his most productive spell in a United shirt – a time that ensured he will always be remembered fondly at Old Trafford.

Those goals against Liverpool and splendid late winners against Southampton and Chelsea were just-reward for Forlan’s graft. Like so many cult heroes, what the forward lacked in ability and luck, he compensated for with spirit, determination and hard graft.

That’s enough for some people. “Ole Gunnar Solskjaer, my former Manchester United team-mate, once told me that I train harder than any player he’s played with,” Forlan has since said.

Even football’s harshest critic, Roy Keane, was willing to cut Forlan some slack on the basis of his application and hunger.

“If a player tried – and Diego did – we’d drag him with us; we’d try and help him,” he said. “Diego was honest, so in training you’d go, ‘Unlucky; it’ll come good tomorrow,’ not, ‘You can f***** do better than that.'”

Forlan may well be the only player to make Keane feel empathy. But the skipper could see there was a player in Forlan, and though he was rarely able to show it at United, it wasn’t for the lack of trying.

Of course, that talent did eventually shine through – almost blindingly so. And almost as soon as he was sold by United at close to a £5million loss.

In the seven seasons that followed his United stint, Forlan took Spain by storm, first with Villarreal, then during four seasons at Atletico Madrid.

The Uruguayan won the Pichichi Trophy and the European Golden Shoe twice each before peaking at age 31, when he scored twice in the Europa League final before winning the Golden Ball for the best player at the 2010 World Cup.

So why did it not work out for Forlan at Old Trafford?

The stick most often taken to Forlan’s United spell is his goalscoring record – 17 in 98 appearances.

It’s hardly unfair to judge a forward on his goals return, but in Forlan’s case, context is required. Only a little over a third of those appearances came from the start.

Furthermore, of his 61 appearances as a substitute, 43 of them involved less than 20 minutes’ work. He had less than 10 minutes to impress on 16 of those occasions.

Some players simply do not thrive coming off the bench, and Forlan was one of them. Only five of his goals for United were scored as a sub.

Conversely, played from the off, the striker’s ratio was a goal every three starts. Hardly prolific, but certainly more creditable.

Unfortunately for Forlan, with Ole Gunnar Solskjaer halfway through his sixth and most prolific season for United, and Ruud van Nistelrooy already on 24 goals by the time he joined, starting opportunities were hard to come by.

And when he was handed the chance to play alongside Van Nistelrooy, it didn’t work.

In Ferguson’s words, Forlan “ran up against the problem of Ruud’s singularity”.

“Ruud wanted to be the number one finisher, that was his nature. Diego Forlan didn’t register on his radar at all, so when you put the two of them out there, there was zero chemistry.

“Diego was better with a partner. But he scored some priceless goals. He was a good player and a terrific pro.”

Forlan’s first half-season at the club saw him make seven starts and 11 substitute appearances, but the striker finished the campaign goalless, with United trophyless.

The United fans remained supportive but more in sympathy rather than expectation as 26 goalless appearances passed – 19 of those off the bench – before the misfiring striker’s moment arrived.

United were already 4-1 up in their Champions League group opener at home to Maccabi Haifa when Forlan was dispatched from the bench. With the contest effectively over, Forlan had 35 minutes against the Israelis to scavenge for his first goal.

Thirty-four of them had passed before United were awarded a penalty and David Beckham presented the ball to his desperate team-mate, who placed it on the spot facing the Stretford End.

“Please. please. Anyone with an ounce of humanity says please on his behalf,” begged ITV commentator Peter Drury as Old Trafford watched an otherwise inconsequential penalty through its fingers.

Forlan took a five-step run-up and rammed the ball into the bottom corner, sending the goalkeeper the wrong way in a manner that belied whatever nervousness he must surely have been feeling.

Forlan’s own celebration was far more muted than those of his team-mates.

That could not be said, however, when he broke his Premier League duck one start and six substitute appearances later, with United searching for an equaliser at home to Aston Villa.

After directing home a powerful header from Mikaël Silvestre’s floated pass, Forlan’s shirt was off, with the equaliser enjoyed with far more gusto than the usual ‘grab the ball and get back to the halfway line’ routine usually seen from Ferguson’s sides.

Forlan’s chest was bare three times in a little under three months during the period that saw him write his name into United folklore.

He endeared himself to the Old Trafford faithful further still a week after his first Premier League goal, when he came off the bench to smash in a brilliant, dipping 25-yard winner with five minutes remaining against Southampton.

Just as memorable as that strike to those present on that November day was the sight of a half-naked Forlan tracking back to make a tackle with his shirt still in his hand after the Saints had restarted.

United’s new Nike jerseys that season had integrated base layers, which the players hated and took scissors to. But not Forlan, apparently, who more than once struggled to solve the puzzle of turning the garment the right way around in a timely fashion having just whipped it off in a frenzy.

The white shirt remained on his back, however, at Anfield on the day that United fans remember so gleefully – and that Jerzy Dudek still has nightmares about.

The Liverpool keeper dropped the kind of bollock you simply never expect to see from a professional goalkeeper, let alone an international.

Dudek bent a knee to scoop up Jamie Carragher’s back header, but his elbows and legs opened like the Mersey Tunnel as the ball passed through him as if he was a hologram.

Forlan accepted his gift, but the forward still had to roll the ball into the empty goal only four yards away. The angle was hardly acute, but the traveling Reds still held their breath while their No.22 took a touch, took aim and very precisely sidefoted into the far side netting.

Forlan’s surge in confidence was evident almost immediately, when Dudek was put on his back for the second time in three minutes. This time, after gathering Ryan Giggs’ toe-poked pass, the forward smashed the ball inside the Liverpool keeper’s near post.

Forland described in his column for The National in 2015 how that moment felt: “This time, I ran to the away end and the fans went crazy.

“Faces looked like they going to explode. My team-mates were delighted, for the team and for me as they knew I’d had a tough time. Stewards rushed forward to push us away from the fans.

“Ryan Giggs, who set me up for the goal, told the stewards in bright orange jackets where they should go.

“I repeated what Giggs said. Nobody was stopping us enjoying this moment. Nobody.

“I wanted to jump over that fence and get in with the fans, which is not responsible, but I was so happy and my adrenaline was pumping.”

Forlan, who has stated his desire to watch the Red Devils play at Liverpool from that Anfield Road end, had perhaps only more more major adrenaline rush in a United shirt.

That came after he lashed Juan Sebastian Veron’s exquisite split pass into the roof of Carlo Cudicini’s net in added time to give the Red Devils a precious 2-1 win over Chelsea. Cue more chest baring and shirt faffing…

Seven goals in his first full campaign at Old Trafford helped United reclaim the Premier League title back from Arsenal, but despite matching that haul the following season, it was clear Forlan was fading further out of the picture as Ferguson put his faith in a lone-striker system.

A goalless run of nine appearances was broken with four goals in four games in a 10-day period taking in three different competitions, but only two more Premier League goals followed – an injury-time brace in a 4-0 win over Aston Villa – before Ferguson signed Louis Saha from Fulham.

Forlan played only seven more times in the Premier League, finishing on the losing side on three occasions and failing to score a goal.

United finished third and saved their season only by winning the FA Cup. Forlan was not included in the squad for the final.

Forlan was flogged to Spain that summer, but he certainly doesn’t deserve to be lumped in the Djemba-Djemba group. United have had some much less successful strikers both before and since.

Forlan doesn’t look upon his time at Old Trafford as a failure, though nor would he claim it went as well as he might have realistically hoped.

But it should not be forgotten that Forlan was still a developing talent when he landed in Britain. Only 22 years old with fewer than 100 senior games behind him, he was a late bloomer, as his subsequent achievements demonstrate.

He eventually peaked in his thirties – at least a couple of years later than many professional footballers.

In 2008-09, Forlan won the Pichichi Trophy after scoring 32 goals in 33 La Liga matches, as well as the European Golden Boot.

After his 31st birthday, the Uruguay hitman won the Golden Ball, the Golden Boot and Goal of the Tournament at the 2010 World Cup.

Forlan’s personal career highlight did not come until he had turned 32, when he helped Uruguay become Copa America champions, meaning his family had become the first to have three winners of the competition from three generations.

That was 10 years after United brought Forlan to Europe. Still developing back then, the truth is, he neither over-achieved nor under-performed at United.

But supporters remember moments more readily than numbers which is why they still serenade the man who came from Uruguay and made the Scousers cry.

‘we are tiny ecosystems’: iris van herpen on learning to collaborate with nature

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

exploring the hidden intelligence of natural systems, the dutch designer reflects on living materials, experimentation, and the future of design ahead of her upcoming sculptural commission with maison perrier-jouët at design miami.

‘The extraordinary possibility is that we are not discovering intelligence, but discovering how much intelligence we have been unable to perceive,’ says marine biologist David Gruber, whom Iris van Herpen quotes during our interview. The idea resonates throughout the Dutch designer’s work, which repeatedly looks beyond the boundaries of the human body to explore the complex systems connecting people, materials, and other forms of life. Through collaborations with scientists, biodesigners, and artists, van Herpen investigates how these relationships might reshape the way we create. Her upcoming collaboration with Maison Perrier-Jouët, presented at Design Miami, continues this exploration, drawing on the vibrations and energy of microorganisms living beneath the soil of the champagne house’s vineyards to develop a new visual language (find designboom’s previous coverage here).

‘I look at the body beyond our physical boundaries, as something liquid, tiny parts of a large blue planet,’ van Herpen tells designboom. ‘We tend to perceive ourselves as individuals, but we are tiny ecosystems, existing in a complex symbiosis and entwinement with countless other forms of life, both inside us and around us.’ The designer sees technology as a way to expand human consciousness, opening up possibilities for understanding how the body relates to the natural world beyond its physical boundaries. Our senses, she points out, allow us to experience only a small part of reality, while other species communicate through systems that remain largely beyond our understanding. ‘More than 8 million species share our planet, we only understand the language of one,’ the designer highlights.

Van Herpen’s understanding of the body also shapes how she approaches couture, using the medium to explore the connections between human life and the wider natural world. ‘Couture can become an extension of our senses, inspiring us to move beyond the idea of the human being as the center of everything, towards an awareness of ourselves as part of a huge, vastly complex, living intelligence,’ the designer notes. Her work often draws on natural phenomena, but its relationship with nature goes beyond visual references. She is interested in how the processes and behaviors of living systems might influence the way garments are conceived and made.

For the Sympoiesis collection, van Herpen collaborated with biodesigner Chris Bellamy on a living look inhabited by 125 million bioluminescent algae, Pyrocystis lunula. The organisms emit light in response to the wearer’s movement, creating an interaction between the human body and another biological community. ‘Instead of shaping a material towards my ideas/vision, we had to learn about and understand the needs and rhythms of these living organisms,’ she explains. ‘It was for the first time that we were not “just” constructing a look, but instead, we had to nurture a living community.’

The algae required maintaining specific levels of light, humidity, and temperature, as well as attention to their circadian rhythms. The whole experience influenced how van Herpen thinks about collaboration and authorship. The title Sympoiesis, meaning ‘making together’, reflects how living things can work together to create something. ‘We need to stop seeing ourselves as separate from nature, embrace our interdependence (for both our physical and mental well-being), and create and act with the awareness that we are nature,’ she says. For designers, she believes, this means thinking beyond the materials they use and finding ways to make the creative process and its results contribute to nature’s cycles.

Her experiments bring her into contact with forces that cannot be entirely anticipated. For Sonic Starquakes, developed in collaboration with Dale R. Sulak, Iris van Herpen began with a relatively simple dress that was charged inside a particle accelerator and cryogenically preserved. The process left it as a metastable reservoir of energy containing billions of trapped electrons. In the nights before the show, the dress began to discharge, producing branching flashes of lightning that etched microscopic hollow channels into its three-dimensional structure. ‘The artwork you see is not controlled or created by me or anyone else; it was the pure force of lightning that created the 3-dimensional detailing,’ she tells us. The result went beyond what she had originally imagined, but this is precisely what the fashion designer seeks through experimentation. ‘That is why I invite chaos and the unknown into my everyday in the atelier.’

In contrast to the constant demand for new collections, van Herpen follows a slower, research-led approach that she describes as ‘slow fashion’. Her collections consist of one-off haute couture pieces, with a single look taking anywhere from four to ten months to complete. Research collaborations with scientists, artists, engineers, and architects can take one to two years, particularly when they involve developing new materials. She now presents one show annually to give these processes more time to develop. ‘Time is the new luxury,’ she points out. Much of this material research also focuses on sustainability, although for van Herpen the value of taking time lies equally in allowing unexpected discoveries to develop.

Living organisms have developed the ability to grow, adapt, repair themselves, and participate in cycles of matter and energy over billions of years. The challenge, in her view, is for human invention to learn from that complexity. ‘Some tend to think that technology is more advanced than nature. The opposite is true; nature remains infinitely more complex than current technology,’ she shares with deisgnboom.

Her interest in working with nature also carries into her upcoming collaboration with Maison Perrier-Jouët, which will be presented at Design Miami. The project draws on cymatics, using vibrations and energy generated by microorganisms beneath the vineyard soil as the basis for a series of visual motifs. The work continues van Herpen’s exploration of natural processes that usually remain unseen. It also connects to her wider ideas about the future of design, from materials that can grow, regenerate, and biodegrade to architecture and cities that support the ecosystems around them.

Iris Van Herpen believes designers, architects, and engineers have much to learn from the way nature works. ‘Nature has spent billions of years evolving extraordinary systems of intelligence, adaptation and symbiosis. There is so much we can learn from these processes,’ she believes. In fashion, this could mean garments that respond and adapt to their environments, grow through biological processes, and eventually return safely to an ecosystem. ‘I am convinced that the next revolution in design will cause a shift from controlling nature to collaborating with her,’ the Dutch fashion designer argues.

4 iPad Pro alternatives for those looking to ditch Apple

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apple's iPad Pro is far from the only game in town.

We may receive a commission on purchases made from links.

If you're looking for the best tablet on the market  — or at least the best with a mobile operating system  — it is undoubtedly the 2025 iPad Pro. The latest iPad Pro sports Apple's M5 chip, which powered the previous year's MacBook Pro. It's a chip so powerful that most users are unlikely to brush against its performance ceiling. The tandem OLED display is similarly overkill, and is a feature no other tablet available in North America has. Apple is so far ahead of its competitors in the high-end tablet market that it feels like Tim Cook wanted to taunt them with its existence.

But the iPad Pro is not perfect. The locked-down iPadOS experience means you're limited to the apps Apple approves of. It also means you cannot install apps without signing into an Apple account. If you don't own other Apple devices, things can get awkward as the iPad becomes siloed away from the rest of your gadgets, requiring you to find workarounds for syncing files or notifications.

Thankfully, things are getting more interesting for tablets outside of Apple's walled garden. While no Android tablets can rival the iPad Pro's performance, many offer other advantages such as ecosystem synergy with Windows PCs, gaming features, expandable storage or more included accessories. And if performance is what you're after, it's worth a gander at Windows tablets now that Windows on ARM is coming into its own.

The easiest alternative to recommend is Samsung's newest flagship tablet, the Galaxy Tab S12 Ultra. The first thing you'll notice is its size: it looks like someone tore the screen off a full-sized laptop, with a 14.6-inch Dynamic AMOLED 2x display that dwarfs even the larger 13-inch iPad Pro. If you love watching videos on your tablet, this is the only device that can give the iPad's luxurious tandem OLED display a run for its money. It also has Samsung's anti-reflective coating, which the iPad lacks.

What you won't get with the Galaxy tablet, or with any Android tablet, is the iPad Pro's raw horsepower. The Galaxy Tab S12 Ultra's MediaTek Dimensity 9500 processor is decently powerful, but it's nowhere near the desktop-class Apple M5 chip in the latest iPad Pro. That said, the Galaxy has more than enough firepower for games, video editing, and other taxing tasks, and it comes with 12GB of RAM (or 16GB on the 1TB storage version) to help get the job done.

Samsung's One UI is widely considered the best Android skin and an excellent alternative to iOS or iPadOS. It also has several features you won't find elsewhere in the Android space. If you have a Windows PC, One UI lets you use the tablet as a second PC display, for example, or share your mouse and keyboard with your computer.

Despite its reduced performance, the Galaxy Tab S12 Ultra also competes with the iPad Pro on price. It starts at $1,400 for the 256GB model with 12GB of RAM and only gets steeper from there, maxing out at $2,000 for the 1TB model with 16GB of RAM.

Until recently, Amazon made cheap, underpowered Fire tablets meant mostly for basic entertainment. The new Alexa Tablet 12 Pro bucks that trend. Alexa Tablets don't compare that favorably to the iPad Pro, but they're cheaper and make reasonable compromises. Amazon has also ditched its Fire OS Android skin in favor of a more traditional Android experience — crucially, with access to the Google Play Store.

Amazon's head of hardware, Panos Panay, told Engadget the screen was the one component of the Alexa Tablet 12 Pro the company refused to compromise on. It has a slightly higher pixel density than the iPad Pro, with an optional matte coating similar to certain Samsung devices and Apple's iPhone Duo. Our review of the Alexa Tablet 12 Pro found the MediaTek Dimensity 8400 chip inside presented no obstacles to productivity and entertainment tasks, but did stutter a bit while multitasking. You get eight and 16GB of RAM on the 128- and 256GB storage models, respectively.

If price is what turns you off from the iPad, Amazon's starting price of $500 will be very appealing. However, that's for just 128GB of storage, which will fill up quickly since it lacks a microSD card expansion slot. The Nanomatte display is an extra $50. If you want the top-spec 256GB model, expect to pay $680, with the Nanomatte display included.

If you want a powerful tablet with gaming-focused features at a more affordable price than the iPad Pro, look no further than the RedMagic Astra 2. Part of that lower price, though, is because the Astra 2 has a 9-inch screen, 2 inches smaller than the smallest iPad Pro. It makes sense for gaming  — a large tablet can be tiring to hold up  — but if you want a large screen for videos or productivity, this may not be the device for you.

The Astra 2 runs the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5, making it arguably the most powerful Android tablet on this list, but still far short of Apple's M5 chip. The biggest barrier to consistent performance on mobile chips is cooling: Most Android devices with top-end chips throttle them to prevent overheating, but RedMagic compensates with a large vapor chamber and real liquid cooling to dissipate heat. GSMArena benchmarks show that the cooling gives the Astra 2 a slight advantage over the OnePlus Pad 4, which uses the same Snapdragon chip.

RedMagic has equipped the Astra 2 with a few bells and whistles Apple doesn't match. The display, though smaller, is more pixel-dense at 313 ppi vs. the iPad Pro's 264, while its 185Hz refresh rate also beats the iPad Pro's 120 (the newly updated iPad Mini is still stuck at 60Hz). It's a bit brighter, too. All of this enables a better gaming experience, which emphasizes who this tablet is for.

Price-to-performance is another bright spot for the Astra 2. RedMagic charges $750 for the base 256GB model with 12GB of RAM, or $850 for the 512GB configuration with 16GB of RAM. That makes it the best bang-for-the-buck option on this list, assuming you approve of its gaming-focused design.

Android tablets have plenty of nifty features, but none equal the iPad Pro's desktop-class processing power. If that's what you want, look to Windows. The Microsoft Surface Pro is more complicated than any other tablet on this list, but that's because it has the full power of Windows 11 running on either a Qualcomm Snapdragon X2 Plus or X2 Elite processor.

The Snapdragon models are ARM-powered, just like the iPad. That gives them better battery life than traditional x86 Windows laptops, albeit at the cost of software support. Many apps aren't yet optimized for ARM, and you'll need to check whether the software you need is fully supported. Whether you want to deal with Windows 11 on a tablet is down to personal preference, but if you've used a Windows 11 PC in the past, the Surface Pro is no different. It's a marked departure from the touch-optimized interface of iPadOS or Android, but professionals in need of pro-grade software will be able to get more done on a Surface Pro than on an Android tablet, and arguably more than on an iPad Pro, depending on which software is required.

The Surface Pro is as expensive as it is capable. The X2 Plus-equipped 12-inch model starts at $1,140 for the 256GB model with 12GB of RAM and an LCD. The 13-inch Surface Pro starts at $1,200 for the same configuration. If you want an OLED screen that bumps the refresh rate from 90 Hz to 120, you'll also have to upgrade to the more powerful Snapdragon X2 Elite chip; that variant starts at $1,500, maxing out at $2,849 for the top configuration with 1TB of storage and 64GB of RAM.
