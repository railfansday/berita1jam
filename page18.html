EFL legend and all-time Championship top scorer Billy Sharp has warned Birmingham City that there's still "a long way to go" after Chris Davies' side reached the second-tier summit ahead of the ongoing international break.

The Blues enjoyed an exceptional run of games between the three-week break in September and October and this current one, winning six of their seven games, three by at least a three-goal margin.

They dropped points only at Cardiff City, a side that has yet to lose at home this season, and have maintained their own unbeaten home record, winning all four games to add some gloss to a St Andrew's record which was pretty draw-heavy in the infancy of the campaign.

The win against former table-toppers Swansea City saw them climb above Middlesbrough on goal difference, and they will hold that top spot for at least another week until action resumes ahead of the Christmas period.

The question in the meantime, though, is: Can they stay there?

Billy Sharp has been a regular pundit on EFL shows this season, after formally announcing his retirement from the professional game back in October. The 40-year-old was promoted out of the Championship three times in his career, so he knows what it takes to maintain top-two form.

The all-time Championship top scorer waxed lyrical over Birmingham through the opening 15 games of the campaign, and was actually surprised that their run to the top of the table hasn't been given as much attention as it arguably should have.

Pennsylvania Pebbles

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

It hasn’t rained for weeks. That changes today, just in time to join forces with the Autumn leaves and northern Pennsylvania rocks to brew up a wicked hiker test.

They can’t help themselves, these white-tailed deer. They like to visit us campers. At 3:00AM this morning a deer, which had almost certainly sensed the rain coming, perhaps curious if there was space inside for it to keep dry. I told it that to save about $50 I bought a 1 person tent, it muttered a snort before prancing off.

The next thing I remember rain was pattering onto my tent. Shucks, I wasn’t going to escape with a dry tent this morning. I had been clever enough to tuck my groundsheet behind the rain fly so that the water would fall to the ground rather than pool up. The fact that I was camping on a layer of leaves was a boon as well, I wouldn’t have to deal with mud getting on everything. I fell back asleep but emerged from my tent shortly before sunrise to an eerie scene – the forest was enveloped in a thick mist. My tent looked sorry, the water weighing down the rain fly, the stakes no doubt shifting from their precarious posts during the night. Usually this is the time where I come up with some excuse to stay in my tent for half the morning, but not this time. The rain had brought about 10 degrees of warmth along with it, it was in the mid fifties. It wasn’t currently raining, so I certainly didn’t want to dilly dally only for it to start raining, making packing up a mess. I wolfed down my breakfast and set about packing. By 7:00AM I was making my way northbound.

I had made it a grand total on 1 mile north of the Leroy A. Smith shelter yesterday, leaving a little over 18 miles back to my van at the Delaware Water Gap. I knew rain could arrive at any time, there was a decent chance of it throughout the day. I was liking the foggy trail conditions, though it came at the expense of the valley views I had yesterday.

On the north side of Wind Gap a rocky yet mercifully short climb awaited me. Once the trail leveled out, I found that the area was still consumed with fog. As I continued my meandering, a small flock of birds, perhaps starlings, took off from the ground near the trail and disappeared into the white mist. Several chipmunks scampered into their burrows, revealing little more than a flash of brown fur to me. My thoughts ran to the interaction between these two very different species of animals. Where are the birds searching for food, potentially at the expense of the chipmunks? Had I stumbled upon nature’s version of capture the flag?

The scene on the trail early this morning.

I wouldn’t watch a scary movie before wandering into this part of the forest.

For a while the rain held off. I enjoyed more of the trail surrounded by fog from all sides. It creates some fascinating scenes for sure. I didn’t miss the valley views, I had something completely different to experience. Nature was about to throw something different to experience at me. Right after I turned onto the Wolf Rocks bypass trail, the mist that had been on and off all day turned into a light rain. I tossed on my poncho for the first time since Vermont. I had made excellent progress, most of the bad rocky sections were behind me. I trudged on through the fog and rain determined to stay on track for an early finish.

Suddenly I wasn’t alone, ahead of me a figure was descending the trail. On a day like this, it could only be a long distance hiker. A large yellow pack and sky blue sun hoodie confirmed as much. Then I realized that I knew those items and the face of the hiker well, Pebbles! I called out to her, yet again struck by the encounter as I was sure that this time she would have been far down the trail. In fact, she had spent Halloween trick-or-treating back in Unionville, NY. That explained why I only saw her once last week, jumping ahead on the trail should have put us on track to meet again the next day. She reported the town was very friendly to her and her thru-hiker costume. She may be a genius, I kicked myself for not thinking of the idea. Maybe I’ll buy some candy for myself at the post-halloween discount.

Pebbles and See You Tomorrow catch up on the trail.

I didn’t envy Pebbles, who was heading straight into the rocky sections that tested me yesterday. I, on the other hand, reached the gravel road near Mount Minsi. I could really make time on the wide open path. After a brief rocky descent on the AT I switched back onto the gravel road. I figured it would be a much smoother descent, and it also led straight back to the AT just before the trailhead where my van was parked. It was not perfect, there were plenty of rocks on the primitive road, but there were none of the step downs that I would have had on the AT. My ankle has taken a lot of abuse this year, but it feels up to finishing off the hike and I want to keep it that way. Just before the trailhead, I noticed and abrupt change in the color of the gravel on the road. It turned into a mix of turquoise and gray, a combination I felt was worth admiring for a few moments before finishing out my hike.

I was back at my van around 1:30PM and quickly got myself ready for town. The local chain cafe was open until 10:00PM giving me time to get all 4 blog posts from this week ready for publishing. With luck I could have another light Sunday coming up, just what I need as I enter the closing stages of my year long hike!

Yes, I caught myself admiring the rocks in northern Pennsylvania of all places!

The Audacious Southern Chef Teaching Angelenos to Shut Up and Listen

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Vinyl bars like West Hollywood’s Darling, from star chef Sean Brock, will help you to appreciate music, and food, and life, all over again.

In our increasingly soulless world of algorithmic playlists and AI-generated art, one of life’s great analog pleasures is listening to records with friends. A dusty old LP pre-breaks the ice for new connections, unlocks stories you haven’t heard from old ones, gives you the chance to get collectively lost in the music.

Vinyl night is such a vital human need, it’s a surprise there aren’t more third spaces built around satisfying it. Characteristically, Japan is miles ahead of America on this front. In the country’s countless kissa bars, bartenders mix drinks and spin records, and—I swear this is true—patrons keep their voices down and listen. My partner and I saw and heard it for ourselves on a trip to Japan last year: every major city has a few joints where the speakers are big, the tables have signs that say NO LOUD LAUGHING, and the customers obey. At a packed and reverently silent bar in Kyoto called PM Sounds, a few whiskeys deep and well into the AM, side one of Jeff Buckley’s Grace hit just right. “Did we really fly halfway around the world to sit and listen to an album we have at home,” I asked Ben on the walk back to the hotel. “Yes,” he replied, and we both agreed it was worth the trip.

Americans are famously not a quiet people, nor are we particularly good with being told what to do. Could kissa culture happen here? A few places in Los Angeles are rising to the challenge: The Gold Line in Highland Park has a hip crowd and shelves swelling with the massive record collection of owner Peanut Butter Wolf. Hollywood’s The Living Room is massive and meticulously acoustically treated, and it’s currently one of the tightest doors in town. But in West Hollywood, across the street from the metastasizing gay club The Abbey and steps away from the log cabin where the hip AA meetings happen, Southern super-chef Sean Brock just might have hit the sweet spot: his first California restaurant, Darling, has an eclectic menu, outstanding cocktails, and speakers that will make you feel like you’re right there in the recording studio. “Music brings people together, just like food,” Brock says. “I've been obsessed with listening bars for a while, and I've been trying to get people into them since 2023. The thrill of discovery is amazing, and I think the more AI the world gets, the more we're going to reach for this stuff to balance it out.”

Brock looks every inch both the 21st-century Generation X chef and DJ: horn-rimmed glasses, sleeve tattoos, ADHD energy. He’s the James Beard Award-winning chef behind a string of beloved Nashville restaurants past and present, including modern Southern standby Husk and two-in-one restaurants Audrey and June. He’s respected by foodies and the hoi polloi alike; ask anyone who did time in Nashville this century, and they’ll tell you a snack at the bar of a Sean Brock joint is the reward for a hard day’s work. When we first met, just before Darling opened, we bonded over his massive collection of 45s in the DJ booth that looks over the restaurant’s bar area. As a secular song from New Orleans preacher Bobby Powell faded out, he wanted to play me something. “Have you heard this?” It was a cover of the Mamas and the Papas’ “California Dreamin’” by a Jackson, Tennessee band called The Jacksonians. “This is all they ever did! They teased us with this and then never did another thing!” As it played, we paused, we nodded our heads. “This is the one. When people hear this, they stop talking.”

The DJ booth and its speaker cabinets were made custom for the space. “The key is the wood,” he said. “13-ply Baltic birch, which is impossible to get now.” Within the cabinets, massive Italian speakers, with Altec Lansing drivers upcycled from their first life in movie theaters.

When Brock is at Darling—generally on the weekdays if he’s not traveling—he plays records as the restaurant opens, takes over in the kitchen as the dinner rush starts, then hops back onto the 1s and 2s after the last dish has been served, going as long into the night as the city will let him. “Getting to play music for people after working in the kitchen all night is my reward now. For the longest time, it was getting a beer after work. Now it's just watching music bring people happiness.”

As a record collector and a chef, Brock is always thinking about the evolution of local flavor. “What I’m chasing is the knowledge of the sound and the rhythm of a city and a time,” he told me. Take Memphis in the early ‘70s, for example. “There was Willie Mitchell at Royal Studios with the Hi label doing Al Green and Anne Peebles, and you had Stax. And then there was this anger building because of the Vietnam War, people are upset, and you feel that in the music.” He grabbed a 7-inch from the pile, the gateway 45 that got him into record collecting: Blackrock’s “Yeah Yeah.” And I did feel it, because the band is a murderers’ row of ‘70s Memphis session musicians, but mostly because of those speakers. At the right volume and in the right seat, the T. Rex from Jurassic Park has nothing on Blackrock.

At its outset, Brock intended Darling to give him a new identity away from the Southern cuisine that made him famous. “I didn't want to come here and cook a bunch of stuff I'd already cooked,” he said. “And we'd been told LA wasn't interested in Southern cooking, that it would be too heavy or whatever. So we didn't cook any Southern dishes when we opened.” The next time I stopped in, once Darling had been up and running for a few months, that whole idea had been 86’d. “About three weeks in, everybody was like: Where are the grits, Sean?”

“Now this has turned into a place where people come to taste the flavors of the South that I've been working on for 20 years — all the seed saving, all the research on traditions and recipes and history. I had gotten a little bored with it honestly, but all of a sudden I have a brand new audience for whom it's completely new, and I’m completely reinvigorated.”

He was describing, almost exactly, the experience Ben and I had hearing Jeff Buckley in Japan. Whether it’s food or music, there’s something revelatory about paying close attention, even to something you think you know.

So that’s the food, and I can tell you from experience that the steak burger is worth the trip (and make that trip early; he only makes 24 a night). As for the music, Brock believes in the power of the listening bar with the zeal of the recent kissa convert. “I had a kind of life-changing moment in a listening bar around 2022,” he says. “I was sitting in the sweet spot — the tip of the triangle, equidistant from two gigantic Tannoys, and the sound became 3D. It floated. I stopped drinking nine years ago, but I thought they'd accidentally given me alcohol.” He got on Nashville Craigslist that very night, searched “Tannoy,” and sent a message to a seller, who wrote back “I ate in your restaurant last night.” It’s good to be the king.

Brock has seats in the bar area of Darling situated at the perfect distance and angle from the speakers to give customers an out of body experience if they want it. But like a legendary Southern chef distancing himself from grits, a listening bar in Los Angeles is a risky concept. In Japan, the locals and tourists know to keep quiet and focus on the music, but this is West Hollywood, where everybody wants to be the thing that is focused on. Where if people have a song selector they trust, it’s usually their Peloton instructor. As an Angeleno, I have to worry whether anyone will pipe down long enough to pay attention. “There's a difference between a night where you come to eat and drink and socialize while music is playing, and a night where we're saying: what we're playing is special and rare. I want to test that theory soon,” Brock told me. He’s been promoting more and more listening sessions: tributes to jazz record labels Strata-East and International Anthem, a vinyl party hosted by Sylvan Esso, Dolly Parton’s first twelve albums, in order, on those THX-tested speakers.

Even the untrained ear can hear it coming together. I swing through again to hear local legend and NTS radio host DJ Morse Code spin a set of ‘70s and ‘80s jazz fusion and R&B. The restaurant is busy and buzzing, there’s a nice crowd up front in the good speaker seats, at the top of the triangle. Some of the folks up front are quieting down to hear the music, even as more people streamed in, even as the new arrivals were striking their poses, even as we realize that Anderson.Paak is at the bar, that Malibu cult singer-songwriter Ned Doheny is at a table in front of the DJ booth, that Kate Pierson is in the restaurant.

Darling is heating up, and the flavors, the sound, and the rhythm are continuing to evolve. The chef and the city are both learning to listen.

Want to Turn Back the Clock? These 10 Diets May Help You Age Better

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

You’ve got way more options than just the Mediterranean diet.

When healthy aging is the goal, it makes sense to try everything you can to make it happen. That means doing your best to exercise regularly, keep up with doctor's appointments, and eat a well-balanced diet. You have lots of options when it comes to the last one—but it can be hard to know which of the many popular eating plans is right for you. Thankfully, new research has helped narrow the choices.

The study, which was published in Nature Communications, analyzed several different diets to see how they impacted biological aging. (Biological aging is the age of your cells, while your chronological age is how old you are.) Popular eating plans like the Mediterranean diet and the DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) diet made the cut, of course, but several lesser-known diets did too. Here’s what to know.

Meet the experts: Jessica Cording, RD, author of The Little Book of Game-Changers; Keri Gans, RD, author of The Small Change Diet; and Sonya Angelone, RD, PhD, a former spokeswoman for the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics and a San Francisco-based dietitian.

For the study, researchers analyzed data from 6,470 participants who were 30 and older. Researchers looked at some of the participants' biomarkers that correlated with biological age and compared that with information about their diets.

After analyzing the data, researchers found that healthier eating in general was linked with slower biological aging, no matter which diet people chose. Each specific eating plan offered an edge with certain perks, though. Case in point: The DASH diet (which is specifically meant to stop hypertension) had more cardiovascular benefits for biological aging, while the MIND diet (which combines the Mediterranean and DASH diets for brain health) offered cognitive perks. But researchers discovered that there were a lot of shared biological aging benefits between the different types of diets.

All of these diets are slightly different, but there are a few common threads. "They generally emphasize fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and other nutrient-dense foods," says Keri Gans, RD, author of The Small Change Diet.

The diets also stress the importance of minimally processed foods, while limiting refined carbs, added sugars, and processed meats, according to Sonya Angelone, RD, PhD, a former spokeswoman for the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics and a San Francisco-based dietitian. "The recommended foods provide fiber, antioxidants, polyphenols, vitamins, minerals, and healthy fats that support metabolic health and help protect cells from oxidative stress and chronic inflammation," she says. Oxidative stress and chronic inflammation are both linked to a higher risk of disease, Angelone points out.

These diets also support the heart, brain, and digestive systems, says Jessica Cording, RD, author of The Little Book of Game-Changers. "All of these things factor into the aging process and somebody's quality of life as they age," she says. "When you're eating to support these different organ systems, you support your longevity and lower your risk of disease."

There are a few things to take away from the findings, according to dietitians. One is that there isn't a single eating plan that's superior to others when it comes to longevity. "It's the overall quality of what we eat consistently that matters most, rather than following a particular diet or avoiding individual foods," Gans says.

While there are plenty of diet plans out there that claim they can help you live longer, Angelone stresses this: "Healthy aging doesn’t have to include expensive foods, supplements, or restrictive diets."

Finally, the study underscores the power of nutrition in keeping you healthy. "Our genes are not our destiny," Angelone says. "We can change the expression of our genes—genomics—with nutrition and lifestyle habits."

Supporting healthy aging with the way you eat means giving your body the fuel it requires to function well, Angelone says. "Healthy aging isn’t about finding a perfect diet or miracle food or supplement," she says. "It is about consistently providing foods that supply nutrients needed by our bodies to function optimally, repair cellular damage, and protect themselves. This will impact how we age."

How to Lose Weight Naturally, According to Experts

Here are 10 easy-to-follow tips to speed up the process—and make it more enjoyable.

Starting a weight-loss journey is no easy feat. There’s a lot to think about, between getting into a regular workout routine, changing up what you eat, and maybe even considering a GLP-1 medication.

But at its core, weight loss is about developing a healthy, sustainable relationship with food and movement. You don’t have to make a dozen changes all at once—the goal is long-term success, even if it’s a slow process. That’s why you should aim to lose one to two pounds a week, says Danielle Crumble Smith, RDN, a dietitian at Eat Well, Live Well. Working out at a manageable pace, eating balanced meals, and making maintainable changes can help you feel your best.

Meet the experts: Danielle Crumble Smith, RDN, is a nutritionist at Eat Well, Live Well. Kami Blease, CPT, is a personal trainer at Fyt Personal Training. Tatyana Johnston, CPT, is a former Nike master trainer and event specialist at Lululemon. Jordan Hill, RD, is a nutritionist with Top Nutrition Coaching.

Ahead, experts share their 10 best tips to make your weight-loss journey successful.

First, be sure to consult your doctor—they’ll help you set mindful, attainable goals that will keep your body healthy and strong. Then, try implementing more of these habits into your lifestyle:

Take the stairs every day at work or hold a quick plank first thing in the morning. "All these things add up over time," says Kami Blease, CPT, a personal trainer at Fyt Personal Training. "I recommend finding things you could do almost daily.”

Additionally, try adding a brisk 30-minute walk into your day (even if it’s broken up into three 10-minute walks) to increase calorie burn and push you closer to your weight-loss goals, per the Mayo Clinic. "It's low impact and not a super strenuous form of movement, which increases the chances that someone will stick with it,” says Tatyana Johnston, CPT, a former Nike master trainer and event specialist at Lululemon. "Just remember to recover with hydration, fuel, and stretching.”

Strength training “helps with balance and stability, improves metabolism, and even lowers your risk of certain diseases," says Blease. Resistance training has also been found to help with weight loss and body recomposition, according to a 2026 study in Frontiers in Endocrinology that had 304 adults follow a calorie-restricted diet and either resistance train, do aerobic exercise, or no exercise for 1 to 18 months.

To get comfortable with form, try practicing bodyweight exercises first, Blease recommends, then add weights after you’ve mastered the move. This can help you focus on progressive overload—which means gradually increasing the weight, frequency, or number of reps over time, Blease says. Eventually, you should aim to work all major muscle groups at least twice a week, per the new ACSM resistance training guidelines.

Cardio can also help with weight loss—the CDC recommends 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity (such as brisk walking) per week, or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity (like running) each week. However, this number can vary based on someone’s individual health, and the intensity of the cardio. "Consistency is key. If you can establish and stick to a cardio schedule, you have a better chance at losing weight and keeping it off," Johnston says. Playing sports, working out with friends, or listening to a podcast during cardio can help boost your motivation too.

Also, if you enjoy high-impact exercises and want to lose weight fast, HIIT workouts can be a great way to expend a lot of energy in a short period of time. "You can ramp up the intensity by trying to get in more reps during a certain amount of time or using heavier weights while maintaining the same time goal,” says Blease. “Both will increase your intensity and continue to challenge your body."

If the gym or workout classes aren’t really your speed, choose a form of movement that you love so you can stay consistent with it. “Hiking, swimming, skiing, surfing, or biking are all forms of movement that might feel more sustainable,” says Blease.

"Start smaller than you might think," Blease says. "Often, people overcommit because they are excited. However, it's then easy to get burnt out." After a few weeks, add in a second day, and when that feels seamless, try a third, and over time, you'll find a cadence that works for you, she adds.

Just make sure you always have at least one rest day. "People can get so fixated on weight loss that they end up [overdoing exercise and] hurting themselves," Blease says. "Take time to stretch, rest, and recover as needed."

"Having a trainer create a program that is specific to your needs ensures that you are more likely to get the results you are looking for—and keep your body safe in the process," says Blease. They can help you lose fat while building muscle, which can better your overall metabolic health and insulin sensitivity, and regulate blood sugar to support a healthier body recomposition.

A trainer will also make sure you maintain proper form and that you stay consistent. Decide on a number of days per week that fits your goal and budget, and ask your trainer to help hold you accountable, Blease says.

Research suggests that working with a personal trainer may have more profound effects on weight loss: A 2024 study in Heliyon had 66 adults train individually, with a partner, or a personal trainer over 12 weeks, and they monitored their improvements in bench presses, squats, skeletal muscle mass, and fat mass. The group that worked with a personal trainer improved their leg training and reduced fat mass compared to the other groups.

Protein can help you stay full throughout the day, stabilize your blood sugar levels, and synthesize muscle mass, Smith says, helping with weight loss. While individual dietary needs vary, you can generally aim for 30 to 35 grams of protein per meal to help you stay full throughout the day. Some good sources include eggs, poultry, seafood, red meat, beans, peas, lentils, legumes, nuts, seeds, and soy, per the current Dietary Guidelines for Americans.

Additionally, "fiber is more slowly digested and helps keep you feeling full for a longer period,” Smith adds, recommending vegetables. Increased vegetable intake is also linked to lower blood sugar levels, lower triglycerides, and can help promote liver function. If you don't love veggies, you can also puree them and add them to a sauce or weight-loss-friendly smoothie.

Lastly, don’t forget about healthy (unsaturated) fats—which can help provide energy, per the Mayo Clinic. Try avocados, extra-virgin olive oil, nuts, seeds, soybeans, and seafood.

"Drinking enough water can help you to feel full and prevent overeating," says dietitian Jordan Hill, RD. Aim to drink about half your body weight in ounces of hydrating fluids per day.

Also, avoid sugary beverages. "Juices, sodas, and fancy coffees are often the calorie-equivalent of a meal, yet they will still leave you hungry," says Smith. "The sugar content is often more than a candy bar, which will cause blood sugar levels to rise." Instead, replace your beverages with fruit-infused water, herbal tea, or coffee with cinnamon or zero-calorie sweeteners.

Avoid foods that are high in added sugars, unhealthy fats, and empty calories, which can contribute to weight gain, says Hill: "Instead, focus on eating whole, nutrient-dense foods that will provide your body with the nutrients it needs."

Listening to your internal cues of hunger and fullness can help you control portion sizes, which can also aid weight loss. "Honoring fullness can help prevent overeating and ensure that you're not consuming more calories than your body needs," says Hill. Keeping a food journal can also help you become more mindful of what you're eating, when, and how you're feeling throughout the process of losing weight.

“When you skip meals or go too long in between meals or snacks, you get overly hungry. This makes it much more difficult to make healthy choices, and it also makes it harder to not overeat later," says Smith. Skipping meals can also result in a slower metabolism and leave you feeling fatigued, which can negatively impact your workouts both in effort and recovery.

If you struggle to eat consistent meals, Smith recommends munching on small snacks throughout the day. “Set yourself up for success by bringing healthy snacks with you when you’re away from home, so you’re not caught in a situation where you are really hungry but have no access to healthy food,” she says.

If you need extra assistance, a dietitian can analyze your diet and recommend personalized tips for how you can eat to lose weight, which may be more helpful than doing it on your own. A 2025 review of articles on weight-loss interventions provided by a dietitian for overweight and obese adults in the Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics looked at how working with a dietitian improved their health markers, as opposed to not working with one (the control group). The group that worked with a dietitian had improved weight loss, waist circumference, blood pressure, and quality of life compared to the control group.

When you encounter a setback (like when you skip a few days of workouts), it can be discouraging and all-consuming, and can cause you to fall off track. "One of the most crucial components of successfully reaching a goal is to not let setbacks ruin our progress,” says Johnston. “The setback is often not as severe as we think, and will not impact our end goal very much at all.”

Just take a breath and realize we’re all human and mistakes are normal; then move forward with your health plan in mind.

And don’t forget to be kind to yourself. Practicing self-compassion and returning to your plan will help reinforce your goal and remind you that this weight-loss journey requires patience and perseverance. "Practice giving yourself grace when [you] slip up,” Johnston says. “As long as it doesn’t become a habit, you will continue moving forward towards your goal.”

It’s part of a broader effort that includes push-button videos, AI-written article summaries, and even whole articles. That sort of automation echoes moves across the media industry.

NewsGuild members at USA Today papers have demanded an end to the Palantir deal, citing reader trust and a conflict of interest for reporters who cover ICE. The union has also pushed back on some of the company’s artificial intelligence initiatives amid continuing layoffs. Last month, after the rollout of a new reader-facing chatbot across USA Today websites, guild journalists at two papers in Ohio pulled their bylines; among its hallucinations, they said, the bot misidentified the reporter covering a murder case as the suspect.

In an interview in September, Mike Reed, the USA Today CEO, told Fast Company that the company won’t replace journalists with AI. But unlike Ziff Davis, IGN, The New Republic, Time, and CalMatters, it hasn’t put that promise in a contract—something that NewsGuild journalists are now demanding in ongoing negotiations.

The layoffs keep coming, too, even in cases where contracts include AI protections. (The Local Journalist Index estimates that the U.S. has lost about 80% of its local journalists since the turn of the century, from about 40 per 100,000 residents in 2002 to just 7.8 today.)

But the upheaval is also fueling an organizing surge, one Schleuss says the union hasn’t seen since its founding in 1933 as the American Newspaper Guild, during the Great Depression. In the time that he has been president, the guild has added more than 10,000 workers from 246 workplaces—not just journalists but people in the publishing, tech, and nonprofit worlds. (Reporters at Fast Company and Inc. unionized with the Writers Guild of America East in 2018.) “You see CEOs that make millions of dollars and don’t increase your pay and want to lay you off and speak in this weird, AI-written code-speak,” Schleuss says. “It tends to tick people off when the price of groceries is just through the roof.”

Schleuss spoke with Fast Company about the industry’s embrace of AI, the growing labor movement in journalism, and what it will take to protect reporters—and the work they do. The conversation has been edited for length and clarity.

Generative AI is being rolled out all over the place, from healthcare to hiring to scammy content creating. Why is the discussion about AI in journalism so critical?

The news industry is the only industry mentioned in the Constitution—we can start there. A free press is essential to having a democracy, and so if you replace the workers who provide that free press with bots, you get misinformation. 

Our currency is trust. And you can’t do that if you’ve got corporations that are just spewing out word calculators to replace those working journalists who have ethical responsibilities: seeking truth and reporting it, minimizing harm, acting accountable and independent, and being transparent. Those are all our key ethical concerns, and if we stray from them too far, then we lose faith, we lose trust, we lose our free press, we lose our democracy. So I’d say it’s all a pretty straight through line: Without journalists, we don’t have democracies, and replacing journalists with AI is a very quick way to get rid of the democracy. Which might be a goal.

Let’s start with USA Today Co., formerly Gannett, the biggest newspaper chain. How are journalists there seeing AI get rolled out in its newsrooms?

They’re pushing AI on people. One tool, Espresso, will take a press release and then try to spit out a story. But I don’t know, it seems really dangerous if the Nazis are emailing you a press release and you just make a quote-unquote story about it. They’re also trying to do that instead of hiring humans to write a story. So it becomes this one-sided slop that journalists are then forced to edit in as quick an amount of time as possible.

That’s not exactly journalism.

You have to approach AI with journalistic ethics, which I think is Gannett’s real problem. And that’s been the challenge all along: How do you make sure that we are doing the things necessary as journalists to be ethical when we’re using these systems? And that requires seeking the actual truth and reporting it, and not just relying on the output from a single-sourced community news announcement that was emailed to you. It requires actually fact-checking things. That requires going through any data that’s being analyzed to verify that these things that you’re saying are true.

Fact-checking is already a heavy lift. And more AI often means more checking. With fewer journalists and more AI, don’t we risk automating away the very things that we need to make the AI work? To make facts and society work. It’s like, remember why we are doing this to begin with. Why we are reading things.

Do you remember when the company Tronc was created? That was my life. I lived and worked for the L.A. Times, which was owned by Tronc. And its chairman at the time, Michael Ferro, was like, “We’re just going to put the content in different funnels.” It’s basically, I think, what [USA Today CEO] Mike Reed’s strategy is today. And it was a failed strategy. It’s like he doesn’t think about the work that journalists do as telling stories. He thinks of it as content. This is the thing that is just so broken to so many media executives. They think of content; they don’t think of stories. It’s absolutely tone deaf and 10 years old.

The thing that infuriates me: You go to any of the USA Today websites now, and the websites are broken. I wish that he would spend more time working on actual user interfaces. Try not to have so many ads that are autoplaying, because if you’re a reader and you’re trying to read the news, you are holding onto a bucking bronco trying to follow the words on the screen. Everything’s moving around so fast. 

To me, the crux of the issue is: This is all about trust with readers. And if you align yourself with a very shady and dark company that has actively supported an agency that has hurt journalists, your readers are not going to trust you as much. And the latest polling shows that 52% of Americans are more concerned about AI than excited about it. It just seems such a bad move, when trust in journalism’s already at such a record low, for a journalism organization to then lean into AI. 

What else do you think USA Today’s leaders are getting wrong? And how is the union responding?

There’s the part of his interview with you where Mike Reed says AI won’t replace journalists: “I think the Guild plants this, as well as outside cynics, that we’re going to replace journalists with AI. That couldn’t be further from the truth.” But the reality is, we have 95 contracts [across the union] that have AI language in them, and the best-case-scenario ones say the employer will not lay off workers because of AI. But Gannett has refused to agree to any of that language. 

What’s the status of those conversations now? 

We’ve got 46 different contracts at Gannett, so it depends on where each of them are. They’re still bargaining for a first contract at The Columbus Dispatch—they just unionized this year. But everyone’s been fighting for no layoffs because of AI. Mike Reed’s refused to agree to that, but then they’ve also actually laid people off. 

What’s some of the most interesting AI-related language you’ve seen in other newsroom contracts?

What’s one of the funnest ones that’s super simple? The New Republic in December of ’24, says: “Generative AI may be used by bargaining unit employees as a complementary tool in editorial work, but it may not be used as a primary tool for creation of such. The use of AI shall not result in the layoff of a bargaining unit employee, be used to avoid filling any vacant bargaining unit position, or result in an employee’s compensation decreasing.” 

There was also a lefty magazine, The American Independent, and its contract said, simply: “Employees shall not use AI to produce any published material, with the exception of quotes derived from interview transcripts . . . or shall not utilize or approve the use of AI to produce editorial content for publication.” It was aggressive. 

What is the guild aiming for generally in its contracts?

The five major points that we fight for in contracts are: 

Number one is no layoffs because of AI. 

Number two is no salary reductions because of AI—another thing that Gannett has refused to agree to. 

Number three is usually the formation of a labor-management committee so that issues can be worked out in real time. This has been, frankly, really successful and could be successful at Gannett, but journalists there have kept away from it.

Number four is just making sure that you don’t do any impersonation of journalists. Don’t train off of their work or impersonate them without their permission. 

And the fifth and final one is really important for our readers: If you’re going to use AI to generate something, you need to label it.

When we spoke, Reed made it sound like that was a policy, that they would be clear if they were using AI.

The policy is adorable, but it’s not legally binding. It’s the AI language you actually want in a collective bargaining agreement—because if you don’t have a union, then you get no say. 

What they typically push for from the management side is saying something like, “AI may be used to generate news content that is supplementary to local news reporting and is not a replacement for it.” And then they’ll say, “The use of AI by all parties shall be consistent with the ethical guidelines and policies for Gannett journalists regarding AI-generated or assisted content.” But there’s nothing, at least in these contracts that I’m looking at, that says anything about labeling. So it might be in that guideline that they put out, but again, if they can just change that, they will. 

There’s a really good bill that’s sitting on [Governor] Kathy Hochul’s desk in New York called the New York FAIR News Act. It would require labeling for anything that was wholly generated using AI on a news website. Publishers are freaking out about it. They’re like, “Well, this is a violation of the 1st Amendment.” In 1933, whenever you didn’t want journalists to organize a union, you claimed the 1st Amendment protected you. But you have to put terms of service on [your website]. You have to have a privacy policy on your website. Labeling AI content on your news website shouldn’t be bad. It seems basic. 

What happens when AI gets better at simulating journalism, and the disclaimers disappear?

It’s already gotten better at writing, but the problem is, I think, people have also gotten better at seeing it. And even if it’s like, “Is this Coke or is this Coke Zero?”—people then are just assuming it’s all shit. So that’s also a problem. Actually, it would be so much more productive to say: We are not in support of AI. That would actually resonate with people, because it’d be news without AI. I think that you could actually wholly market a news organization off of that.

I hate this, because I’m a big believer in the open internet, but I actually think that news organizations right now need to create siloed universes where people will build reputational trust with an organization, because it’s not AI slop. Within five years, the majority of things we see on the internet will just be AI slop. It’s already there. So why not create a website that people can actually trust, written and curated by humans?

And Reed was gesturing at that. At the idea of being this oasis of trust in a sea of slop.

Then why put out AI slop?

Let’s talk about their deal with Palantir. USA Today’s execs told me Palantir works only with “highly controlled” data and has no access to newsroom systems or reporters’ notes, and accused the union of spreading fear and falsehoods about the deal. What did you make of that? 

My general reaction was just one of disappointment that both [president of USA Today Media] Kristin Roberts and Mike Reed have been so far removed from working in a newsroom that they forgot what it was like to have an organization that’s powered by journalists. They came off as extremely defensive of the relationship with Palantir, which has actively been working for ICE and DHS [Department of Homeland Security], which is not only a major news topic right now, but also is an organization that has actively assaulted journalists in city streets in the United States. We’ve had to file three separate lawsuits on it because it’s been so bad. So it’s really disappointing that they can’t see how this is an ethical issue, that this is a safety issue, that there’s a security issue here. 

Palantir has worked with or is working with a number of media organizations, including Axel Springer and Fox News. What specifically are union members concerned about?

What are you actually doing with readers’ data here? What are you losing control over? And then they came out swinging in defense of what Palantir has access to in terms of their journalists’ data. They’ve only now offered some clarity in your interview, when they have refused to do it until this point. But there’s much that’s still unclear. 

I also wonder about how one goal of the partnership—targeting readers using “anonymized” data—impacts the relationship with readers. Of course, many media organizations target their audiences with ads.

Reporters in the group chat say they’ve heard from readers who are really concerned about this relationship, what it means about their data. So that’s been an open question. 

Instead of using Palantir, they could find a good data journalist who could look at Google Analytics and help you figure out that this actually does better here and that does better there. And this webpage is taking three minutes to load: Maybe we should fix that?

I think some of the best things that work for us—this is so basic that it sounds stupid—is literally creating decently written newsletters that bring readers in and center their concerns. Here’s this newsletter from a local reporter; here’s a human being who wrote this. And it’s not just headlines and links. It’s literally, this is what happened in your neighborhood in New York City over the last day. That’s what people are hungry for, and it’s not rocket science. You just create and curate communities where people actually want to congregate and learn.

What else have journalists been pushing for specifically when it comes to AI in the newsroom?

So there’s notification—this is what we won the Politico arbitration over. Politico didn’t notify its workers and journalists that they were rolling out new tools. One was a report builder tool, and it was supposed to be based off of the archive. And our members asked it, “Is abortion legal in the United States of America?” And it was like, “Yes, ever since Roe v. Wade.” And it just totally missed the story, broken by its own journalists, that showed that the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade. So we filed arbitration for that one, and they shut down the tool—they had violated the contract, which required 60 calendar days’ notice of any new tool.

And how about compensating journalists if their work has been used in AI training and inference? 

The compensation piece has been a real challenge. There’s not been a company, a news company, that’s agreed to compensate journalists. There’s a prohibition on training off of work by a journalist if they don’t get their consent. So you need consent if you’re going to have anything trained. 

The New York Times [Company], for instance, has a deal with Amazon for AI and their content, and they have refused to provide compensation to New York Times journalists. Which is wild, because if a newspaper in Brazil picks up a story from the Times, the journalists get compensated for it.

It seems like the media companies are still trying to figure out the compensation schemes themselves. Isn’t some kind of compensation essential to the future of the business?

So many of these news companies are entering these dumb deals with AI companies, though they don’t know how much the Atlantic deal is worth versus the New York Times deal versus the McClatchy or the Gannett deal. They don’t know the cost. You’d think that they’d know how to bargain better, but they are bargaining in the dark and then accepting peanuts for something that these AI companies are just hungry for, which is original content. And that’s being stolen from all these journalists.

How do you think AI tools could be valuable for journalism?

I think that there could be some that were valuable if they were for a specific purpose. You look at The New York Times’s Manosphere Report, which basically looks at and transcribes hundreds of hours of podcasts from right-wing podcasters and then turns that into an internal email report that goes to Times journalists. That’s really helpful, because it saves you the time of having to listen to a lot of podcasts. And then it gives you a sense of, well, is there a particular trend that is happening in this particular area? Analyzing millions of records from Jeffrey Epstein being released by the government—that’s helpful. Transcribing interviews—that’s helpful. 

But it all needs to be checked. Every single one of these things has to be checked. You can’t just publish it and just say it’s the truth because it came from the machine. 

But back to Gannett. There’s another AI tool called Streamer, which essentially pulls from the archive and puts together short-form videos with AI-generated text and voiceover. The main problem, journalists say, is that it sucks. It’s not good. And there’s a 10-year-old internal tool that’s better than the AI tool.

Have you personally found a way to use AI that’s helpful, that’s reasonable?

In my union work, I have been unimpressed with its lack of accuracy. I could see ways, maybe, at some point, where it could be helpful to try and do comparisons of contracts, but I’ve just seen it fail.

Back when I was working at the L.A. Times, one of my coworkers was building a machine learning system to build a big database of recipes, because recipes are structured data. Then you can point the machine learning algorithm at the newspaper from a hundred years ago, at a PDF, and say: Figure out where the list of ingredients is, the ordered list of operations. That was a really helpful tool. And that was more than 10 years ago. 

I think organizing large sets of data is really interesting. Like the Epstein stuff. Or if you’ve got a large cache of, say, the archive of the Guild newspaper, and you want to find letters about constitutional questions. We just published our archive on our website, this printed newspaper that goes back to 1933. It’s 1,300 PDFs—huge. That’s an area where, if you’ve got a ton of documents, it’s actually helpful. Not to provide verified truths from the AI model, but to be able to say, “Well, how many times was automation mentioned in the 1930s?” And then have it directly cite, say, page 4 of this issue. And then you’d have to go back as a human and check it.

Kristin Roberts mentioned an internal chatbot at USA Today that helps reporters with FOIA [Freedom of Information Act] requests. Are there other ways that AI might be useful in newsrooms, or things that you’d like to see management do with it?

In my old job, I would scrape websites, like transit agencies and police departments. And I’d be creating a spreadsheet, so I’d have to write these scripts in Python. One thing that’s nice is you could actually speed that up a bit using AI. What would take me weeks and weeks to work on scraping is something that I could do much faster, because I’m able to use a system that has been trained on a ton of Stack Overflow pages and can say, here’s the programming language. It’s still important to know what the language that’s coming out says, so that you know it’s not just hacking the website or making things up. You’ve got to double-check it. 

And there’s definitely an argument, too, that we should just have more staff to be able to do it, because Mike Reed’s ultimate argument here is, this is going to free us up to do more things. But you’ve already cut the staff so much. You need to instead just hire staff to do the actual journalism, not try to find tools to fix a problem that you’ve created by cutting the staff so much. 

Beyond jobs, what are you hearing about AI’s impact on the process of reporting and writing itself?

Besides the jobs, I think an overarching issue is that a lot of the executives at news companies don’t actually understand what AI does, and they’re just trying to push it out. So we see that, whether it’s Axel Springer or McClatchy or Gannett, they’re all just these guys at the top who think that this is a good idea because they were with one of their millionaire friends who said this is a good thing, and they just believed them, and they don’t actually know what the systems are doing. 

I think the other thing in this moment of AI is, it actually makes it harder for journalists, because there’s so much AI slop and misinformation. And so it’s making it harder for journalists to know what’s true. 

Are there certain areas of coverage that newsrooms need to be paying more attention to now?

I think that having accountability reporting is really, really key right now, because when we lose journalists at the local level, that costs us money. When we don’t have someone who’s watching city council, watching our school boards, that means that the elected officials then can just do whatever the hell they want. So accountability is the big thing, and they’ve been shifting away from that. McClatchy has also done a major shift away from that, which has been really horrible. They laid off 30% of our represented membership in September. They got rid of a lot of accountability.

So how about, don’t lean into the very unpopular thing that people say is bad for society, and instead focus on humans that are telling stories and holding power to account. Because that’s been a working formula for thousands of years. Ever since we started grunting around as Neanderthals, we were telling stories.

InfoQ Homepage News Netflix Turns Operational Data Into a Knowledge Graph for AI-Driven Observability

Nov 13, 2026 3 min read

Netflix is developing an ontology-driven knowledge graph to accelerate production investigations and provide AI agents with a shared understanding of its operational environment. The platform connects information about applications, incidents, deployments, alerts, and infrastructure dependencies, addressing a problem that can leave engineers spending hours piecing together information scattered across different systems.

The company claims it handles more than 2 billion requests daily and upwards of 38 million real-time logging events every second. During the November 2024 Jake Paul-Mike Tyson fight, its infrastructure supported approximately 65 million concurrent streams. Yet investigating a typical application regression can take around four hours, with much of that time spent reconstructing relationships between systems rather than analysing the underlying data.

Netflix's solution is to make those relationships explicit through an operational ontology and knowledge graph, creating a foundation for automated root-cause analysis and, eventually, self-healing infrastructure.

Netflix already collects metrics, events, logs, and traces (MELT) across its applications and infrastructure. However, these signals are spread across systems such as alerting, incident management, deployment tooling, and engineering communication platforms.

An application regression might originate from a client release, an experiment, infrastructure load shedding, or a service several dependencies downstream. Although each system contains useful information, engineers must often connect the evidence manually.

Netflix identified the missing capability as a consistent way to describe not just individual components, but how they relate to one another.

Its Observability Ontology provides that common model, defining operational entities, their properties, and the relationships between them. It uses established standards including RDF, OWL, SHACL, and SPARQL, while the resulting knowledge graph is stored in QuipuDB, Netflix's distributed RDF database built on Rust's redb storage engine.

The graph is populated through a pipeline of components Netflix calls enrichers. These consume information from operational systems and transform it into structured relationships, represented as subject-predicate-object triples.

Some enrichment is deterministic, while other stages use LLMs and probabilistic techniques to interpret less structured information. Netflix constrains these outputs through strict validation rules, including consistent entity types, URI naming conventions, and checks against its software catalogue.

Netflix also captures information from incident discussions, including hypotheses, rejected explanations, and mitigation decisions. This allows human reasoning to become part of the operational knowledge base rather than disappearing when an incident conversation ends.

Its correlation engine examines 13 types of signals, including log errors, metric anomalies, traces, alerts, incidents, deployments, configuration changes, experiments, and code changes. It searches for potential causal relationships, evaluating evidence such as temporal proximity and severity to produce ranked hypotheses.

Netflix describes the process as a knowledge flywheel: Observe, Enrich, Infer, and Adapt. As investigations produce new insights, those relationships can inform future detection and analysis. The knowledge graph also underpins AutoRCA, Netflix's internal agentic AI system for root-cause analysis.

Rather than having specialist agents exchange loosely structured findings, they use the ontology as a common interface. Agents analysing traces, logs or deployments can contribute information about the same operational entities without needing to understand each other's internal implementations.

Netflix intends to use this foundation to produce ranked, evidence-backed explanations for incidents, with humans retaining approval authority over proposed changes and remediation.

Platforms such as Datadog, Dynatrace, and Splunk already correlate observability signals and increasingly incorporate AI into incident investigation. Netflix's approach emphasizes the semantic model behind those signals, making operational relationships persistent and directly accessible to both engineers and machines.

The company ultimately wants to extend this capability from automated root-cause analysis into automated remediation and self-healing infrastructure. It also plans to explore integration with its Unified Data Architecture, connecting operational information to broader business and technology domains
