Thirty-one killed in massive fire as fuel tanker crashes on Cairo highway

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Huge fire erupts near Cairo killing at least 30 people

Thirty-one people have been killed and 21 others injured in a large fire on a busy highway in Cairo after a fuel tanker crashed, Egypt's health ministry says.

Hospitals nearby treated patients suffering burns and smoke inhalation after the incident, which occurred on a section of a ring road in the Warraq neighbourhood, to the north of the old city centre, on Wednesday evening.

Unverified footage circulating on social media showed billowing flames and black smoke towering high into the night sky, with multiple nearby vehicles torched.

Officials have not given an account of how the fire broke out, but state-owned media reported the tanker had overturned, dousing nearby vehicles with petrol.

The regional public prosecutor has launched an investigation into the incident and asked that DNA samples be taken from bodies of the dead.

The tanker, which belonged to a private contractor, had been travelling along the highway when a high-voltage cable fell in front of it, prompting the driver to lose control, Al-Ahram reports, citing sources with knowledge of the investigation.

It then crossed the central reservation, overturned and caught fire, the state-owned newspaper says.

Egypt has suffered several deadly fires in recent years, with informal residential areas and often run-down infrastructure making incidents more common, the response to which is often complicated by slow emergency response times and locally improvised rescue efforts, even in the sprawling Egyptian capital.

Images from the scene show a large crowd gathered on the highway after the fire had been extinguished, as emergency services blocked off entry routes.

The health ministry said 39 ambulances had been dispatched to respond to the fire, while it posted images of Egypt's health minister overseeing the treatment of the injured at one of Cairo's hospitals.

Road collisions are also common in Egypt, often attributed to lax maintenance and the widespread flouting of traffic laws.

Nearly 6,000 people died on Egypt's roads last year, official figures show, a rise of several hundred on the previous year's total.

Emergency responders have since cleared the highway and allowed it to reopen, state broadcaster NMA reports.

A bulldozer could earlier be seen at the scene clearing the burnt-out wrecks of cars.

Posted November 25, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

I was running out to grab a cup of coffee and asked my co-worker if I could grab one for her. No biggie, just a common courtesy. I literally didn’t even go out of my way. But when I returned, she couldn't have been more grateful. Sometimes when we do small, simple things, we realize later how much they meant to others.

It could be something like a thoughtful message or a quick check-in where we take a few minutes to listen.

The thing is, we often underestimate the impact of these simple gestures. We are all so busy these days, caught up in daily responsibilities and checking all the boxes to complete everything on our list, that we tend to forget how much we can effortlessly offer to others.

Of course, in this fast-paced world, we are always striving for more from life. And, at the same time, it's important to pause and recognize what we have and how it can be shared with those around us.

Just take a moment to think about some of the people who make a difference in your life. Perhaps it was that friend who checked in on you during a difficult time or a supervisor who encouraged you. They may not have given their generosity much thought, but I bet you remember how it made you feel, right?

Let me share with you a research study that supports this idea. A 2023 study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology found that people frequently underestimate how positive acts of kindness impact others. In one study, participants who gave strangers hot chocolate thought the recipients would be happy, but in reality, the recipients were incredibly grateful for the gesture.

Generativity, a concept developed by psychologist Erik Erikson, describes how a person wants to contribute to the lives of others and make a difference for future generations. This is evidenced in the things that we do, like mentoring others, supporting a friend or family member, or sharing lessons.

It’s important to mention that the research demonstrates a strong connection between helping people and well-being. In 2020, Psychological Bulletin published a meta-analysis of more than 200 studies and found a consistent relationship between helping people and positive well-being.

It’s important to mention that giving to others doesn't mean we are neglecting our needs. It just means that we are also helping one another build relationships. And the research also shows that a sense of connection really matters.

Sadly, we are often so busy managing all that we have to do that we don't think about how we can support others.

You will both benefit if you reach out, offer a little encouragement, listen, or just let someone know they are on your mind. The point is that it doesn't have to be this big grand gesture. Small acts of kindness go a long way.

Life's most meaningful moments happen with people who took the time to listen, remember, or encourage, or helped us feel less alone. Don’t wait for more time or money, or until you've figured everything out, before you try to make a difference. Just start with where you are.

Take a moment and think about one small act of kindness that was bestowed upon you. How can you do the same for others?

Study: Meta settlement needs YouTube, TikTok to succeed

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Meta settled a massive social media addiction lawsuit brought by multiple state attorneys general in August, it invited YouTube and TikTok to join the terms.

Meta, which agreed to implement new restrictions on teen use, argued that the safeguards amounted to a new industry standard. They included a two-hour daily time limit, removing nighttime access to Meta apps by default, and no notifications during school hours.

"These protections will only be truly effective if we work with our peers — TikTok and YouTube — to put the same measures in place," Meta said in a statement at the time. Neither social media platform has joined the settlement since.

Now, a new study published in JAMA suggests Meta was right about the potential effect of YouTube and TikTok agreeing to the restrictions.

The authors analyzed existing data from ongoing research known as the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, which tracks teen screen time and social media habits. They looked at data from 1,803 participants over a five-year period between Nov. 2020 and Aug. 2025.

They found the Meta apps Instagram and Facebook accounted for only 20 percent of total daily social media use amongst those participants. Snapchat, TikTok, and YouTube comprised the remaining apps named in the litigation.

Additionally, the authors found that significantly more teens would benefit from the time limit, nighttime access, and notification restrictions if the other companies implemented them.

"I think many people hailed that settlement with a huge sigh of relief, but unless the other companies join suit, these data suggest it's not going to make a huge difference," Dr. Dimitri Christakis, principal investigator at the Center for Child Health, Behavior and Development at Seattle Children's, said in a statement. (Christakis has served as an expert witness in social media litigation and has received consulting fees from the attorneys general of California, New Mexico, Virginia, and Washington.) Dr. Jason Nagata, an associate professor of pediatrics at the University of California, San Francisco who was not involved in the study, said the real-world phone-use data allowed the authors to examine how the settlement's proposed protections might come into play.

The daily time limit, for example, would affect just 7.7 percent of teens in the ABCD study. If implemented by TikTok, YouTube, and Snapchat, that would rise to 63 percent.

Blocking Meta access from midnight to 6 a.m. would affect more than three-quarters of Meta users. By contrast, the same rule applied to TikTok, YouTube, and Snapchat would cover nearly 90 percent of users in the ABCD study.

There's a similar disparity for overnight notifications. Shutting them off between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. would affect almost two-thirds of Meta users, but 86 percent across TikTok, YouTube, Snapchat, Facebook, and Instagram.

"The study shows how difficult it can be to address social media use one company at a time when teens regularly move across many different platforms," said Nagata, who previously used ABCD data to study smartphone use at night and during school.

Nagata noted that the JAMA study doesn't indicate how well the proposed restrictions will work in the real world. Teens might change their behavior, shift to other platforms, or develop workarounds. Nagata believes future research must evaluate which restrictions reduce problematic use and improve teen health and well-being.

The authors acknowledged other important limitations in their research. The ABCD study sample included only Android users, not iPhone users, so it may not reflect the experiences of all U.S. teens. The passive phone tracking may miss use on other devices like tablets or laptops. The study didn't capture the content viewed by users, which may affect teens differently. The estimated reach of the restrictions is also hypothetical.

Still, the researchers argue their estimates provide a baseline for understanding the restrictions' potential impact.

"The settlement will affect some teenagers but not enough,” Christakis said.

Major developers, legal experts, and 32 states back Epic in Apple’s SCOTUS appeal

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In a significant development in Apple’s Supreme Court appeal of a civil contempt ruling, Epic Games has received backing from 32 states and the District of Columbia, along with major developers, legal experts, former federal judges, and more. Here are the details.

Apple’s ever-sprawling fight against Epic Games over its App Store policies is currently being fought on two fronts: in the Supreme Court, where Apple is seeking to overturn a civil contempt ruling, and in the district court, where the companies are disputing what commission, if any, Apple can charge on purchases made through external payment systems.

The civil contempt ruling came after Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers found that Apple had violated an earlier injunction issued in its App Store dispute with Epic.

In a nutshell, after she ordered Apple to allow developers to direct users to alternative payment methods outside the App Store, the company responded by allowing external payment links, but imposed a commission of up to 27% on purchases made through them.

Since then, Apple has been trying to overturn the civil contempt ruling, basically arguing that the original injunction did not explicitly prohibit the commissions and other restrictions it imposed on external purchases.

After the Ninth Circuit upheld the contempt finding and denied its request for rehearing, Apple took the case to the Supreme Court, which agreed to hear its appeal.

Importantly, the Supreme Court will only consider the legal standard used to uphold Apple’s civil contempt ruling, not the separate question of what commission, if any, the company can charge on purchases made through external payment methods.

That will still be determined in the district court, where proceedings have continued despite Apple’s attempts to put them on hold pending the Supreme Court’s decision.

With oral arguments scheduled for January 11, 2027, the case has recently seen a flurry of activity, including Apple’s opening brief in September and Epic’s response earlier this month.

Apple also received support from several organizations in September, including the Washington Legal Foundation, the International Center for Law and Economics, the Computer & Communications Industry Association, and the U.S. Chamber of Commerce. The US government also submitted a brief supporting neither party, arguing that the contempt finding was unjustified over Apple’s 27% commission, but warranted over its restrictions on button designs.

Which brings us to the latest developments: a series of amicus briefs filed in support of Epic Games.

Last week, a total of 9 amicus briefs were filed in support of Epic Games, including one led by Utah and joined by 31 other states and the District of Columbia.

This particular brief notes that “the district court correctly held Apple in contempt for continuing to engage in anticompetitive conduct barred by a prior injunction,” adding:

“The court found that Apple engaged in unlawful anti-steering restrictions and required Apple to lift those restrictions. Apple then lifted them in name only, imposed new (and substantially similar) strictures on developers that mirrored the old ones and produced the same result. Worse still, Apple did all this in bad faith: Apple knew its behavior was wrong and actively hid that fact from the court, including by providing false testimony. Under any reasonable reading of the district court’s injunction, Apple’s conduct is worthy of civil contempt.”

The states also argue that “Injunctions in competition cases cannot afford complete relief without addressing the ongoing effects of anticompetitive behavior,” and that “[c]onstruing competition injunctions narrowly defeats their purpose and prevents meaningful redress.”

Similar arguments appear throughout the other briefs, which include submissions from Spotify, Match Group, and Microsoft, as well as nonprofit organizations such as the American Antitrust Institute (AAI), Public Knowledge, and the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF).

In their joint brief, Spotify, Match Group, and Microsoft argue that they “have first-hand experience with the effects of Apple’s conduct and evasion efforts,” adding that “Apple’s new restrictions were no different from the unlawful rules they replaced.”

As for the brief filed by former federal judges, they warned that “[i]n cases like these, where enjoined parties seek to circumvent an injunction through reliance on its literal text, courts must have the power of contempt to deter and remedy evasion. Otherwise, courts would be forced into a game of whack-a-mole, trying to anticipate every possible means of evading an injunction […]”

Arguments by the Little Tech Association, AAI, Public Knowledge, and the EFF all follow a similar line, with some also addressing how Apple’s restrictions on external payment links could affect consumer choice, pricing, and competition.

Comment | A missed opportunity for a full accounting of cultural cleansing

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An installation view of Heritage in Resistance: Timbuktu to Odessa, at the Cité de l’Architecture et du Patrimoine in Paris, until 3 January 2027 Photo: © Didier Plowy

It is always gratifying to see one’s work referenced—especially 20 years after the fact. The exhibition Heritage in Resistance: Timbuktu to Odessa, at the Cité de l’Architecture et du Patrimoine in Paris (until 3 January 2027), traces attacks on material and intangible culture in conflicts beginning with the dynamiting of the Bamiyan Buddhas in 2001 and ending in Ukraine, Sudan and Gaza.

The vast exhibition spans art, digital re-creations, photography and filmed interviews. It is conceived of as an investigative report organised into sections on erasure, resistance and repair.

A wall text references my 2006 book The Destruction of Memory: Architecture at War, which first set out systematically why such targeting happens. The exhibition credits me with coining the term “cultural cleansing” in respect of the Bosnian War to explain the pattern of heritage erasure that can accompany, or presage, ethnic cleansing and the persecution of populations. It notes that Unesco adopted the term from 2012 to describe “the destruction of World Heritage Sites by terrorist groups”.

Except that book says much more than that. It talked about this same process in other places such as the Israel-occupied West Bank. It examined cultural genocide (like cultural cleansing, this still has no definition in international law) witnessed in instances ranging from the Ottoman Empire and Native American populations to the Holocaust and Tibet. I would now add the experiences of the Uyghurs in China, the Rohingya in Myanmar, the Yazidis in Iraq and the Palestinians in Gaza to that dreadful list. I argue that patterns of heritage destruction can evidence genocidal intent. Other genocides, such as that in Rwanda, did not have a pronounced material culture component.

These are matters that the Cité de l’Architecture et du Patrimoine exhibition unacceptably skirts. As in Germany and the UK (where a talk at London’s Building Centre including Architects for Gaza was cancelled following an intervention by Lawyers for Israel this summer), the French state and its institutions are censoring pro-Palestinian voices.The crucial work of Forensic Architecture in documenting destruction, for example, is not present after a dispute over the group’s use of “the G word”. How can you explain what has happened to Gaza’s cultural heritage unless you are at least prepared to discuss possible intent and patterns of destruction?

As an investigative report, the show obfuscates as much as it reveals. Institutions and governments shield themselves behind the argument that it is up to the courts to determine genocide. They forget the Genocide Convention’s duties of prevention—as litigated in the 2007 International Court of Justice (ICJ) case, Bosnia and Herzegovina v. Serbia and Montenegro, or South Africa’s inclusion of attacks on culture when considering Palestinian “conditions of life” at the ICJ.

An installation view of Heritage in Resistance: Timbuktu to Odessa, at the Cité de l’Architecture et du Patrimoine in Paris, until 3 January 2027 Photo: © Didier Plowy

There is an impression that heritage destruction in conflicts has got worse over recent decades and that the propaganda impact of destruction is actually exploited by some groups. The jury is out on this. As reflected in the show, however, the international heritage community has had an almost singular focus on actions by terrorist groups, whereas over the past decades (Islamic State and its affiliates aside) it has been the firepower of United Nations State Parties that has been overwhelmingly responsible for the devastation to material history—including but not limited to the United States, Russia, China, Israel and Saudi Arabia. Isis iconoclastically wrecked religious sites in Mosul, but it was the liberating forces that comprehensively flattened the historic city.

Looting, too, is problematically covered in the Paris exhibition. This is a serious issue in war zones where security vanishes. But the myth repeated here that the sale of looted artefacts is a meaningful source of terrorist funding “on a par” with resources such as oil is simply unevidenced. (Recovered Isis documents show something quite different.)

The exhibition is sponsored in part by the French office of Unesco. No surprise then that the organisation’s manifest failure to protect heritage in conflicts is absent from the walls. Unesco is celebrated—as it should be in some cases—for its work in Timbuktu, where the International Criminal Court brought the first (and, to date, only) successful prosecution for the targeting of monuments as a war crime. Less convincingly, the show argues that this episode was a turning point after which Unesco formulated a more grassroots approach to preserving culture by working with local people on local priorities.

It was Unesco that disgracefully praised Putin for liberating Palmyra despite the terrible damage Russian forces allied with Assad inflicted on Syria’s people and heritage. Unesco has been just as partial since: while it “strongly condemns” Russian attacks on heritage in Ukraine and Israel’s on Lebanon, it does not offer the same degree of pressure in its statements on heritage destruction in Gaza or Iran. Even then, unlike other UN agencies, Unesco never names Israel. Unesco has not invoked the 1954 Hague Convention in relation to Gaza, despite citing it in virtually all similar instances. Unesco did not seek urgent action by the UN Security Council or the General Assembly to protect cultural sites there as it did in respect of Isis.

The Paris show is more subtle in its understanding of the ways that post-war heritage reconstruction can be problematic and not necessarily healing, but is just as silent on Unesco’s move away from the centrality of authenticity to reconstruction work. Whereas the notion of rebuilding the Bamiyan Buddhas was deemed unacceptable, Unesco promoted building a brand-new Mostar Bridge in Bosnia and Herzegovina as peacebuilding—despite community relations actually deteriorating in the city. And then the agency had the temerity to declare the facsimile bridge a World Heritage Site, undermining its own scientific tenets to bolster a failed symbolic gesture. All this bad-faith is set out in my follow-up book, Monumental Lies (2022).

One is left to read between the lines for hints at the full curatorial mindset. Once through the exhibition doors, the first large-scale works displayed are photographs by the Palestinian artist Taysir Batniji of Gazans’ retrieved housekeys, with their heartbreakingly quotidian fobs—a friendship bracelet, a cheap carabiner clip, a Minnie Mouse figure. The unknown fate of their owners is pencilled in below.

In its silences and voids, Heritage in Resistance at least accurately mirrors failures of international institutions and laws and the partiality and misinformation that surrounds responses to contemporary heritage destruction. Ultimately, the show offers false hope about successful resistance via digital surveying, reconstruction and Unesco work. What we should be feeling is despair.

Anthropic Stock Is Down 33% From Its All-Time High. Here's What a $5,000 Investment Could Be Worth in 1 Year.

Anthropic's post-IPO sell-off has been brutal, and history suggests an investment could produce dramatically different outcomes over the next year.

By Adam Spatacco – Nov 25, 2026 at 5:15PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Anthropic ›

The artificial intelligence (AI) revolution has made 2026 a historic year for initial public offerings (IPOs). In May, chip designer Cerebras Systems (CBRS +1.01%) raised $6.4 billion through its IPO, followed by Elon Musk's Space Exploration Technologies' (SPCX +1.30%) record-setting $75 billion offering in June.

However, AI developer Anthropic (ANT +1.08%) subsequently eclipsed SpaceX's record when it made its Nasdaq debut on Nov. 19. Despite the initial enthusiasm surrounding the maker of Claude, Anthropic stock has already plunged 33% from its all-time high.

Let's explore Anthropic's growth prospects, assess its competitive landscape, and examine what history suggests a $5,000 investment could be worth around the company's first-year IPO anniversary.

Including the full exercise of the underwriters' option to purchase additional shares, Anthropic's IPO generated $93.5 billion in gross proceeds. Anthropic also completed a concurrent $26 billion private placement involving Nvidia, Microsoft, Alphabet, Advanced Micro Devices, Broadcom, Micron Technology, and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing. Combined gross proceeds across the two transactions reached $119.5 billion.

When Anthropic began trading, shares opened around $111.50. The company's valuation reached approximately $2 trillion, instantly propelling Anthropic to one of the world's most valuable companies. Shares subsequently climbed to $115.10, although investors have since grown more cautious about the company over the last week.

Anthropic's revenue growth is nothing short of extraordinary. In 2023, the company generated just $41 million in sales. By 2025, the company's top-line had increased by more than 100x -- reaching $4.6 billion.

During the third quarter alone, Anthropic grew revenue by approximately 1,127% year over year to $17 billion. Furthermore, the company exited October with an annualized revenue run rate of $81 billion.

Despite Claude's historic adoption rate, Anthropic's infrastructure requirements and competitive landscape complicate the company's investment thesis.

Anthropic faces competition from two categories. The first stems from other dedicated frontier AI developers such as OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT. The second source of competition includes diversified technology companies such as Alphabet and SpaceX, which develop Gemini and Grok, respectively.

Big tech conglomerates have alternative revenue streams that can help finance their AI ambitions. Alphabet operates high-margin advertising and cloud computing businesses, while SpaceX generates revenue from satellite connectivity and rocket launches.

These financial resources afford Anthropic's rivals greater flexibility when pricing their AI products. As large language models (LLMs) become increasingly sophisticated and open-weight alternatives mature, Anthropic may face pressure to reduce prices in order to retain customers.

Meanwhile, Anthropic has accumulated $901 billion in infrastructure commitments stretching across the next several years. During the third quarter alone, the company incurred $9.9 billion in computing and infrastructure expenses.

If rising competition drives AI pricing lower while capacity expenses remain elevated, Anthropic could struggle to translate its revenue growth into sustainable profits. This outcome could cause investors to re-rate the company with a lower price-to-sales (P/S) multiple, even as its revenue continues expanding.

To understand how Anthropic stock might perform over the next year, let's consider how several major technology IPOs performed during their first year as public companies.

Amazon and Alphabet generated first-year returns of 281% and 179%, respectively, while Tesla stock appreciated by 18%. By contrast, Meta Platforms, Uber, and Rivian experienced first-year declines of 31%, 21%, and 67%, respectively.

Although the average first-year return across these six companies washed out to about 60%, the results were heavily skewed by Amazon and Alphabet. The median return was actually negative, while the average and median maximum drawdowns during the first year were approximately 49% and 46%, respectively.

Perhaps more relevant to Anthropic is a separate analysis of the 10 largest U.S. IPOs between 2000 and 2024. Across this cohort, the average first-year return was -26%, while the median return was -34%. Furthermore, the average and median maximum drawdowns were 54% and 53%, respectively.

Using Anthropic's recent share price of $80, a decline consistent with the average or median first-year return among these mega-IPOs would imply a stock price between $53 and $59. Under these scenarios, a $5,000 investment would shrink to roughly $3,300 and $3,700.

Conversely, if Anthropic were to match the average first-year return of Alphabet or Amazon, shares could reach $223. This would transform a $5,000 investment into nearly $14,000.

Ultimately, history illustrates just how unpredictable investing in IPO stocks can be. While the performance of previous mega-IPOs suggests Anthropic could trade lower around its first anniversary, the limited number of successful offerings demonstrates that meaningful upside is also possible.

Given the range of possible outcomes, investors considering buying Anthropic stock might be better served by gradually building a position over the long term as Claude’s competitive strengths and the company’s long-term profitability become clearer.

Andy Taylor, Duran Duran Guitarist, Dies at 65

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

He served as the band's guitarist during their commercial height and later became a founding member of the supergroup The Power Station.

Andy Taylor, Duran Duran‘s guitarist during the band’s commercial peak in the 1980s as well as a founding member of supergroup The Power Station, has died. He was 65

Taylor died following a battle with prostate cancer, his family and Duran Duran announced Wednesday on social media.

“To the world, Andy was a brilliant guitarist, but to us, he was the most wonderful husband, a loving dad and the proudest grandfather,” his wife, Tracey Taylor, said in a statement. “He was the rock of our family. For all the incredible experiences music brought him, family always came first. Nothing made him happier than having us all together, and no matter how far music took him, his heart was always at home.” Related Stories Lifestyle Veruschka, Mesmerizing Supermodel of the 1960s and '70s, Dies at 87 TV Steven Hack, Original 'Cats' Castmember and Busy Onscreen Character Actor, Dies at 68

As the band said in a statement of its own: “Andy was there at the very beginning of our story. From the moment he arrived in Birmingham from the North East with a guitar and an unshakeable belief in what we could become, he brought an indelible fire and swagger to our music. His playing drove the songs that took us around the world, and with a dry wit that was matched only by his wonderfully unsentimental, no-nonsense outlook on life, he kept us grounded and gave the band its edge through the extraordinary years that followed.”

The Englishman wasn’t technically a founding member of Duran Duran, but he joined the group in 1980 before they recorded their eponymous 1981 debut album, and he served as the guitarist through the band’s commercial zenith on their next two albums Rio and Seven and the Ragged Tiger.

He left amid the recording of the group’s fourth album, Notorious, but rejoined Duran Duran from 2001 to 2006 and appeared on the band’s 2023 album, Danse Macabre, as well.

Taylor co-wrote several of the band’s biggest hits, including “Hungry Like the Wolf,” “Rio” and “Girls on Film.”

He entered the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame with the group in 2022. His battle with cancer prevented him from attending the induction ceremony, with the group reading a letter Taylor wrote instead.

“I’m proud of everything we’ve achieved together and of the way you have continued,” he said at the time. “As a guitar player in a progressive band from the synth days of the early eighties, literally from the day I met Nick, John, Simon and Roger, they truly valued the contribution of a rather noisy, versatile Northern brat.”

“I’m so very proud of these four brothers,” he continued. “I’m amazed at their durability, and I’m overjoyed at accepting this award. I often doubted the day would come. I’m sure as hell glad I’m around to see the day.”

The news comes weeks after the band confirmed that drummer Roger Taylor would leave the group because of ongoing struggles with tinnitus. In an extensive statement, frontman Simon Le Bon lauded Taylor for his versatility, noting that he “can play it like Mick Ronson, or he can play it like Jimmy Page, he can even play rinky-dink rhythm like Nile Rodgers. But what he does most is play guitar like guitar-genius — Andy Taylor!”

“Me & him had a special bond right from the start,” Le Bon said. “He’s the one who gave me my band name — ‘Charley.’ And after 2006, when he wasn’t touring with us, we spent happy days at the studio in his Ibiza villa, laying down tracks for our latest records. I have so many wonderful, hilarious, tragic memories of our time together: on tour, in the studio, in our little flat on Caroline Road, Balsall Heath at 6 a.m. And through it, & above it all he was in love with his family — most important thing in his life. There’s a massive Andy Taylor shaped hole in my heart. It’ll never close up; I won’t let it. I will miss him & love him for all my days.”

Fellow Duran Duran and Power Station member John Taylor called Andy Taylor “a demon, a killer riff-merchant, melodist, six string textualist (he would hate that term) and a singer with a great voice.”

“What he brought down to Birmingham on that train from Whitley Bay can never be quantified,” he said. “Spirit! What a lark we had, and great records.”

Nick Rhodes paid tribute with a poem that concluded with “Goodbye demon fire, live wire.”

Roger Taylor said that “the sadness cuts extremely deep, but admiration and respect run even deeper for the Taylor that wasn’t related but will forever be my brother. Rest in peace now, Andy.”

The band said Taylor’s family has requested that in lieu of flowers, donations be made in Taylor’s name to Prostate Cancer UK.

Along with his wife, Taylor is survived by his children, Georgina, Isabella, Bethany and Andrew, and his grandson, Albie. “Andy faced the last few years with so much courage and determination,” Tracey Taylor said in a statement. “He never gave up, fighting until the very end, never losing his love of life. The kindness and support shown to him by fans from all over the world throughout his illness brought him immense comfort and strength, and for that we will always be grateful.”

Freshly unboxed and sitting in my hands, the textile-clad Capulet Olive Motorola Signature 27 colorway has impressed me in every way, shape, and form over the past 24 hours. It’s a much longer period of time than I usually get with hands-on opportunities and, yet, the newness of the experience has yet to wear off.

Part and parcel to that experience is the design. For whatever reason, this is the year Motorola seems to have truly figured out what makes a phone nice to hold. The textile back, ribbed metal frame, rounded corners, and curved edges on the front and back of the phone are purely blissful to hold. It’s a reminder that a phone can be as good looking as it feels instead of being a flat, boring, off-balance slab like too many phones are now.

The weight also surprised me. On paper this sits right in the middle of all the Ultra/Pro Max-named phones, yet it feels lighter for some intangible reason. The camera hump makes it slightly top-heavy, and it’s almost as wobble-free as a Pixel (there’s just one off-center camera hump).

The rest of the time, I’ve been either inside or out in overcast weather, all of which it looked brilliant in. And it uses a combination of DC dimming above 20% brightness, and 2160Hz PWM dimming below 20% brightness, making it super comfortable for me.

Motorola’s software has evolved again with this release, and the verdict is still out on whether or not this is actually better than the company’s Android 16 designs. Some people don’t take much stock in DXOMark, but it’s still worth noting that they’ve praised the heck out of the camera and Motorola is also pushing this as a winner in a way they’ve never done before.

The hype for the Motorola Signature 27 certainly seems to be realized, and now only time will truly tell if it can live up to the lofty expectations Motorola’s $1,599 price has set for it. If nothing, it’s certainly off to a good start and has made an excellent first impression.
