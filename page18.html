Three insights you may have missed from theCUBE’s coverage of monday.com’s Elevate event

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI workflow automation is changing how businesses build applications and manage everyday work, raising questions about the role of established software platforms. As organizations experiment with AI-generated applications and autonomous agents, they must also determine how to maintain access controls, integrate existing systems and manage increasingly complex workflows.

The growing accessibility of AI development tools has prompted speculation that businesses could replace traditional software-as-a-service platforms with applications built for specific needs. But building an application and operating it securely across an enterprise present different challenges, according to Casey George, chief revenue officer of monday.com Ltd.

“We are seeing customers come back to a platform that’s integrated — that’s governed, that’s permissioned with their infrastructure, that is secure — like monday.com,” he told theCUBE. “I joke and say it was an expensive funeral, but we learned a lot. Customers learned a lot.”

George, along with executives and practitioners, spoke with host Rebecca Knight at monday.com’s Elevate event during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. The discussions examined enterprise AI deployment, governance requirements and the challenges of scaling AI workflow automation across organizations. (* Disclosure below.)

Making autonomous agents available to employees does not guarantee business returns. After an internal initiative produced more than 3,600 agents, monday.com established monday Frontier, an organization combining managed services and deployment engineers to help customers identify workflow bottlenecks and implement AI projects. The initiative addresses the implementation challenges involved in moving agents into productive use, according to Eran Zinman (pictured), co-founder and co-chief executive officer of monday.com.

“Adopting AI really requires expertise. I know how hard it is because we experienced ourselves that you have this amazing technology, but how do you actually use it?” he said during the event.

As organizations deploy AI agents, security teams must account for how access rights change across users, applications and environments. Incremental rollouts offer a way to test capabilities before expanding their reach, while permission management remains an ongoing responsibility, according to Ryan Nigro, security trust and enablement team lead at monday.com.

“It just comes to making sure that your users are properly being given just enough to do their roles but not too much to cause harm,” Nigro told theCUBE. “There’s no reason why a sales rep should have the ability to change your production environment.”

When AI Saves a Fast Heartbeat, Who Carries the Conflict?

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 18, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

You just hired an entry-level employee. A week later, they miss an important deadline, affecting several other projects like dominoes.

Your follow-up email carries an edge, and, within seconds, their reply hits your mailbox:

“Hello, I sincerely appreciate your feedback and understand the importance of timely delivery. I take full accountability and remain committed to improving our processes moving forward.

For a moment, it may feel like a perfect reply. Their acknowledgment of your feedback and promise to do better feels almost like a relief, but at the same time raises a red flag: Why haven't they offered any explanation for what happened, and will their mess-up and apology be a repeat?

Conflicts, even minor ones, can raise our heart rate, especially at work, where professional reputation and job security may be at stake. We may act in a way that leads to regret later, and a reality check against AI may not be wrong. I call it "resolution illusion" when a message displays the signs of "this is what the opponent would most like to read in the reply", such as empathy, acknowledgment, and professionalism. At the same time, the underlying problem remains untouched, and that may even build some resentment. This may eventually lead to major conflict.

In an August 2026 Big Think interview, Terence Tao compared scientific research to hiking toward a waterfall. AI, he suggested, can work like a helicopter that takes you straight there. The hike teaches you the terrain, and a wrong turn might lead to a discovery you never set out to make. This, of course, isn't just about hiking.

In any situation, people seem to learn better through understanding and making mistakes. In conflicts, they have to decide what to say after what is already said and/or done. They may question their assumptions and build their own future behavioral "protocol" in similar instances, after considering how their actions may affect themselves and others.

In the workplace, in particular, they may need to revisit where instructions were unclear or ask for help they should have requested sooner; it is all part of getting lost, which leads to new professional discovery. That learning process may be uncomfortable, but it gives them practice in building their professional role, critical thinking, exercising judgment, speaking up, and establishing personal limits around work. When AI-polished responses are sent within a second, it becomes easier to skip the uncomfortable but necessary path for professional development, from A — the missed deadline — to B — figuring out what went wrong and what needs to change in this case.

In a 2025 survey, knowledge workers reported thinking less critically about tasks they were more confident AI could handle (Lee et al., 2025). In a separate experiment, readers perceived AI-assisted messages as less reflective of the sender’s own values than messages described as entirely human-written.

Philosophers Lai and Archer (2026) stated that relying on AI for emotional communication can deprive people of opportunities to discover what they truly value and who they really are. In the workplace, this is especially important, since it is where most people spend the majority of their time. An apology may sound like the product of reflection even if the sender skipped the necessary steps (remorse, responsibility, impact, and the desire to repair). This is only one part of the issue. The other is: If the reply makes the issue look settled, who absorbs the relational fallout?

Research on unrewarded work suggests an answer. In a study published in the American Economic Review, Babcock and colleagues examined tasks everyone wants done, but no one wants to do, such as writing a report or serving on a committee. Women were more likely than men to volunteer for these tasks, to be asked to do them, and to say yes when asked. The reason was not that women preferred this work. In mixed groups, people expected women to say yes, and in all-female or all-male groups, the difference disappeared (Babcock et al., 2017). Cleaning up — making sure everyone understood what went wrong and what not to do in the future — after a polished AI reply is this same kind of work that may fall to women.

A woman asked to fix what a polished reply left unresolved can use the following three questions to hand the thinking back to the person who made the mistake: What happened? What will you do differently in the future? How will you know it started working? The questions give the employee a simple checklist and a chance to reflect, explain their side of the story, propose a fix, and take responsibility for following through. For high-achieving women, the relevant boundary may no longer be knowing when to say no; it may be knowing when not to perform another person's unfinished thinking.

Imagine the employee had said what they were actually thinking:

"Hello, I thought someone said it's due Friday.

Now the manager has something to work with – the scheduling issue, miscommunication, role clarification, and how the process works.

The manager learns how the employee understood the process, who they depend on, who depends on their work and responsibilities, and who to speak with. Exchanges like this can serve as a reference point in case of similar problems.

A polished message that solves conflict skips all that, allowing the same or more errors in the future, which may eventually lead to job loss.

It's not to say we shouldn't use AI for conflicts. Some workplace conflicts can be hostile, and for an entry-level employee, it can take time to spot; AI can help organize a timeline, clarify thoughts, brainstorm ideas, or find words for a boundary they already understand. This allows them to express themselves to the best of their knowledge and genuinely mean every word of an AI-assisted reply. The end product is what matters — the next deadlines should be handled based on the lessons learned from the first mistakes.

Conflicts may raise your heart rate, and AI can save a heartbeat, but it should not become another person’s unresolved conflict tomorrow.

How background session sync works

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 18 • Originally published at agentdrive.sh

AgentDrive lets one coding agent resume a session another one started. To make that work without asking you to save anything, it has to sync sessions in the background, safely, and without slowing the agent down. This post walks through how that works in the adrive CLI and the MCP server. All of the code described here is in the Apache-2.0 client packages.

Each supported agent CLI has a lifecycle hook system, and AgentDrive registers the same command in each one. Antigravity's hook payloads carry no event name, so its entries add --event .

The npx fallback exists because agents launched from a GUI often don't have the global npm bin directory on their PATH.

adrive init merges these entries into the existing config files. It keeps any hooks you already have, in their original order, and re-running it produces identical output. Codex only runs hooks it trusts, so setup also records that approval for you; on an older Codex you approve them once via /hooks.

Hooks run inline with the agent. Codex caps hook timeouts at 3 seconds, so adrive hook does almost nothing itself. It reads the event payload (session id, working directory, transcript path), records which directory the session belongs to, starts a detached adrive sync process and exits 0. A hook failure is swallowed, so it can't block or break your session.

On a session-start event, if auto-context is on, the hook also asks AgentDrive for this project's recent sessions. It puts the newest session from each other agent first and returns up to three as extra context for the agent, with a hint to call drive_resume. OpenCode's plugin API has no way to inject context, so OpenCode skips this step.

Path rules decide which files can be uploaded at all. Artifact sync only looks at files the agent touched in that session. A file is skipped unless every check below passes:

Content redaction scrubs what does get uploaded. The transcript and every synced file go through a pattern-based redactor. It replaces AWS access keys and secrets, GCP service-account private keys, Stripe, GitHub, Anthropic, OpenAI, Slack and AgentDrive tokens, JWTs, PEM private-key blocks, and SOMETHING_KEY=...-style assignments with markers like [redacted:github-token]. The version message records which kinds were redacted, never the values.

The redactor is best-effort, and it is not a security boundary. Pattern matching will miss secrets that don't look like secrets. That's why the path rules come first, and why Private Workspace exists.

Workspace files are versioned and content-addressed, so re-uploading an unchanged file costs nothing. Each session is also recorded as a run with the id :. The run carries the session title, its status (in_progress or success), the ids of the files it uploaded, the transcript path, the paths it touched and the time of the last turn. Every request is stamped with the agent as its agent platform, which is where provenance comes from: the timeline shows that Codex wrote this file and Claude Code read it.

When an agent calls drive_resume with no arguments, AgentDrive picks the latest session in this project from a different agent. Resuming in Codex therefore gives you the Claude Code session, not Codex's own last run. If no other agent has a session there, it falls back to the latest one. Photographer Jessica Craig-Martin’s New Memoir Reveals the Life Behind Her “Drunken Lens”

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Her new memoir I Regret I Am Able to Attend is out now

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Since the early ‘90s, photographer Jessica Craig-Martin has captured high society from unusual angles. She zooms in on air kisses and other mannered gestures, cleavage in couture gowns, high heels and the floors typically omitted from pristine event documentation, straddling the line between fashion photography and art portraiture.

Craig-Martin’s memoir, I Regret I Am Able to Attend, out now, tells the story of how she got there. Craig-Martin details her bohemian childhood, wild friendships, and volatile young marriage with the effervescence that fills her compositions.

The story begins with the photographer’s parents, artists Michael Craig-Martin and Jan Hashey, who meet at the Yale School of Art. They have Jessica, then swiftly separate after revelations that they’re both having affairs, and Michael comes out as gay. From there, Craig-Martin experiences a peripatetic childhood, eventually settling into her mother’s SoHo loft. Her creative milieu includes gallerist Paula Cooper and artists Elizabeth Murray, Jennifer Bartlett, and Joel Shapiro, along with their children. Craig-Martin gets a job at high-end grocer Dean and Deluca (RIP), where Basquiat comes in to buy smoked eel for a strange supper. Artists Brice and Helen Marden hire her to babysit their daughters, and soon she’s off to Morocco and Greece with the family.

Instead of attending college, Craig-Martin receives an alternative education as she lands a gig waitressing at Brian McNally’s celebrity-filled restaurant Indochine. She flies to London to start a job as a fashion assistant at Anna Wintour’s British Vogue (they love her American enthusiasm) and, back in New York, works at Tina Brown’s Vanity Fair (they love her British sophistication). Throughout the memoir, Craig-Martin captures the colorful characters who parade in and out of her life, and around a rapidly evolving New York City.

Ahead of the book’s publication, and her forthcoming show at the Andres Thalmann gallery in Paris, Bazaar spoke to Craig-Martin about her writing process and her shifting understanding of gender in the art world. We were on the telephone, and the artist was visiting “I Am Plane Image,” a show of Brice Marden’s paintings at Gagosian in Chelsea. A guard reprimanded her for getting too close to a painting. “When I was a kid, one was encouraged to touch the art,” she told me. “A lot of art was activated by the viewer. And now someone’s always saying: ‘Excuse me!’ ‘Excuse me!’”

You’re a society photographer whose pictures conjure an entire milieu. Your book does something similar, but with language. How do you think about the relationship between writing and photography?

It’s the same brain, making different things. With photography, I never thought about what I was going to make before I made it. I’d see something with my camera, and I’d have to grab it. It’s a wonderful feeling of adrenaline, and it’s similar to looking for the next word. Everything just disappears. There’s a loss of self in the process, and you become your purest self.

In the book, your aesthetic education seems to come, in part, from painter Brice Marden.

With the camera, I felt a certain freedom. I really didn’t know much about photography, because it wasn’t a popular medium with my parents and their friends. And from Brice, I understood this freedom to put what you want in a picture. You can leave things out, push it around until it works, make it as weird as you want. If you feel like cutting off the top of a head in a photograph, try it!

I knew certain photographers, but not society photographers like Tina Barney and Larry Fink. I only knew a few pictures of Weegee’s. There was a lot for me to discover. If I’d seen more of this work, I would have felt inadequate. In this case, ignorance was bliss. Academic training can make you self-conscious. I didn’t have anything to unlearn.

Your mother has a philosophy that artists are the real aristocracy. Do you share it

She was right about everything she said. But it is extreme. I mean, we want doctors and we need other professions. But I think all artists believe this, as did all of us art brats who grew up in this kind of crazy, free-range situation. Art comes first. Everything else comes afterwards. It’s what we are in the truest form.

Good work rises to the top. Then again, not everyone whose work is good has been correctly honored or addressed. Women and people of color were left out of the discourse.

Your mother also says that Indochine is where you became a photographer.

I was raised in the very lean 60s and 70s, and then suddenly New York just was drunk on money overnight. And this job threw me into the middle of it. It was a hot seat to view all this excess. I found my subject matter there. Everything’s moving at once, and I keep that feeling when I’m taking a photograph: there’s a lot of motion in my pictures, and I’m capturing the feet or I’m above the person or I’m not where a photographer would normally be. [Gallerist] Jeanne Greenberg calls it my “drunken lens.”

The book is also a portrait of an evolving New York. It’s more sanitized now.

New York is still New York. We lose a lot with the corporatization of the city. But that’s global. It’s the same fuckers in every city making it into glass, concrete, steel with a bank in the bottom of the building and a Starbucks. It’s more painful here because New York is my city. But you can’t be too nostalgic because it’s also about speed and change. If you get stuck, you’re missing the point.

The sex appeal is now in Brooklyn. I’m an old fart now, I don’t hang out, but I know there are young people who are opening galleries, making good art. It’s just harder and harder to find a crack in the sidewalk to grow out of.

Towards the end of the book, during your marriage to artist Ashley Bickerton, you write that you made “Art Wife Curry.” That comes shortly after your mention of Rirkrit Tiravanija making curry at 303 Gallery, which he just calls “art.” You write about the covert sexism in the art world vs. the overt sexism in the restaurant world. In the writing process, did you come to think any differently about how gender had shaped you?

Yeah. When you’re naive and you’re a young woman, you’re experiencing these invisible forces pushing you around, or men are behaving in a certain way, and you’re used to it. It’s like air. You don’t see it. And not just writing the book, but getting older, you can really see the forces that pulled you this way or that, where you felt a limitation. When I look back, I think: Why didn’t I just say “fuck you?” Or why didn’t I say, no no, I’m coming through? Women second guess themselves a great deal more than men. Men push and then afterwards say oh, did I break it? And then everyone claps. That’s not the classic woman’s story. There are corrections happening, and many of them are very well-deserved.

What kinds of limitations are you thinking about?

It’s about confidence. Being an unquestioning force in the world and feeling like you have a right to be seen on the wall, have just as big a studio as your husband or partner. If you go to the Krasner Pollock home in Springs in Long Island, his studio was famously the barn, a giant empty space. And hers was this tiny room. That would be the normal order of things. That’s the way it was in my family, even though I thought of my parents as liberated. My mother was considered a star at Yale, and as soon as she left and had a baby, she was a mother first, artist second. My parents’ generation thought they had dealt with a lot of this second classing of women, and they really hadn’t.

The book ends when you're around 35, your marriage is over, and your photography career has finally begun. Why end there? Will there be a Volume 2?

Good question. There might be. But I’ve also told my friends to tie me up and duct tape me to a wall if I ever start to try, because this was so difficult to make. I’m already forgetting how dreadful I found the experience.

Stripe Billing vs Chargebee vs Lago: The Real Engineering Cost of Usage-Based Metering

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 18 • Originally published at lumeir.online

Originally published at Lumeir Systems Lab. For interactive architecture schematics, SVG topology diagrams, and 12-month FinOps cost tables, examine the complete empirical audit on Lumeir.

The technical transition from traditional seat-based SaaS subscriptions to usage-based pricing models represents a radical shift in database access patterns, distributed system boundaries, and financial accounting guarantees. In a legacy seat-based system, state changes occur infrequently. A user is created, a plan is upgraded, or a seat is revoked. These operations execute over synchronous REST calls, mutating a handful of rows inside a standard relational database with strict ACID guarantees. System throughput requirements rarely exceed a few dozen operations per second, even for enterprise-scale platforms.

Usage-based metering completely shatters this low-frequency paradigm. When pricing is tied directly to API calls, compute seconds, gigabytes ingested, or transactional volume, the billing architecture transitions from a passive administrative system into a high-throughput stream processing network. Every single user request across your application cluster must generate an immutable, audit-compliant event. If your system handles 50,000 API requests per second, your metering pipeline must ingest, deduplicate, aggregate, and store 50,000 financial events per second without dropping a single byte or introducing unbearable tail latencies into the critical request path.

Superficial vendor marketing frequently collapses this engineering problem into simplified API documentation, selling the illusion that billing integration is merely a matter of firing a HTTP POST payload to an external REST endpoint. In production, this abstraction breaks down immediately. Naively wrapping external vendor HTTP calls inside your API request paths causes severe thread pool exhaustion, socket starvation, and cascading failures during traffic spikes. Conversely, attempting to aggregate events locally without strict distributed idempotency guarantees leads to double-billing bugs or untraceable revenue leakage.

Choosing between platform-as-a-service billing engines like Stripe Billing and Chargebee, or open-source infrastructure like Lago, is not a simple comparison of feature sets or dashboard aesthetics. It is a fundamental choice between radically different architectural trade-offs: external API serialization overhead versus self-hosted event queue maintenance, percentage-based gross revenue take-rates versus operational infrastructure cost, and rigid black-box ledger semantics versus fully inspectable database write paths.

To evaluate these platforms accurately, we must analyze the execution paths of their ingestion engines, thread models, storage mechanics, and protocol overhead when processing high-frequency usage events.

Stripe Billing handles usage-based metering primarily through its Meter Events API. The architectural path of a meter event within Stripe relies on a globally distributed, highly resilient multi-tenant infrastructure built atop distributed storage systems. When an application dispatches a POST request to Stripe's metering endpoint, the payload passes through an edge proxy tier running ingress TLS termination, a path sensitive to edge compute cold start latency, before reaching the internal routing mesh.

Stripe forces network communication over standard HTTPS REST interfaces utilizing JSON payloads. Under heavy load, this protocol choice incurs substantial serialization overhead and HTTP/1.1 or HTTP/2 frame overhead compared to binary protocols like gRPC or native TCP streaming. Within Stripe's backend, event ingestion is immediately separated from aggregation logic. The ingress engine performs lightweight validation (checking API key signatures, rate limits, and payload schemas) and appends the raw event to a distributed append-only log backed by highly available stream infrastructure.

Idempotency in Stripe is enforced via an explicit identifier parameter attached to the meter event. To verify whether an event key has already been processed within a rolling window, Stripe utilizes an internal distributed key-value cache layer (optimized for low-latency point lookups, as detailed in our distributed key-value cache latency benchmarks). If the key exists, the event is safely ignored, returning an HTTP 200 response with the existing event payload status. If the key is unique, the event is appended to the stream for async processing.

The primary constraint of Stripe's architecture is the black-box nature of its aggregation compute cycle. Events sent to Stripe are aggregated asynchronously in background batch windows. While this shields Stripe's external API from database lock contention during ingestion, it introduces a propagation delay between event emission and invoice balance visibility. Additionally, because Stripe charges a fee on gross processed volume, application developers must evaluate whether paying a percentage-based tax on every metered dollar is architecturally justified when operating high-volume, low-margin infrastructure.

Chargebee's heritage lies in traditional subscription lifecycle management. Its usage-based metering implementation is built atop an architecture historically designed around relational account objects. Usage data in Chargebee is typically ingested through the Chargebee Usage API or bulk import routines, which mutate billing subscriptions via internal service layers.

Unlike Stripe's modern decoupled event-stream ingestion architecture, Chargebee's runtime execution path exhibits tighter coupling to relational database state. When usage data is pushed to Chargebee, the engine validates the usage against the active customer subscription record. This process often triggers synchronous or near-synchronous relational database updates to compute cumulative plan usage, remaining credit balances, and tiered soft limits.

Because Chargebee relies heavily on multi-tenant relational database clusters, high-frequency event ingestion creates severe write amplification. Each incoming usage record must update internal tracking tables, execute index balance checks, and write to transactional database log sequences. Under sustained ingestion rates exceeding hundreds of operations per second per account, this architecture experiences lock contention on subscription rows. To prevent database degradation, Chargebee enforces strict API rate limits and encourages developers to perform pre-aggregation inside their application layer before pushing summarized usage metrics in periodic batches.

This limitation forces the application developer to build and maintain an in-house pre-aggregation engine (such as Redis RedisStream or Apache Flink, with high-throughput data orchestration platforms providing the pipeline layer) merely to protect Chargebee from being overwhelmed by raw usage volume. Consequently, Chargebee functions less as a high-throughput event meter and more as a periodic usage synchronization system for downstream invoice rendering.

Lago approaches usage-based metering from a modern stream-processing perspective. Architected as an open-source, deployable stack (as well as a managed cloud service), Lago explicitly separates high-speed event ingestion from heavy ledger computation through a micro-component model composed of an API ingress layer, Redis stream/queue workers, PostgreSQL persistent storage, and background processing workers written in Go and Ruby.

In Lago's self-hosted architecture, incoming events hit a dedicated Go-based API routing tier designed for minimal memory allocation and rapid request termination. When a meter event reaches Lago via the /api/v1/events endpoint, the engine does not perform inline relational database writes. Instead, it executes an atomic cache check using Redis to evaluate the event's unique transaction identifier for idempotency.

If the transaction identifier is novel, Lago pushes the raw event into a Redis-backed buffer stream and immediately returns an HTTP 202 Accepted response to the client application. This design keeps p99 ingress API latency consistently under 10 milliseconds, as the request thread never waits on disk I/O or complex ledger calculation.

Background worker processes pull batches of events from the stream queue, applying sliding-window temporal aggregations (such as SUM, COUNT, MAX, or UNIQUE COUNT) defined by the system's billable metrics. The resulting aggregate counts are written in optimized bulk transactions to PostgreSQL. This decoupled write path eliminates row-level database locking during live traffic spikes. Because Lago can be compiled and deployed directly within an organization's private cloud infrastructure (AWS EKS, GCP GKE, or bare-metal Kubernetes), internal application services communicate with Lago over low-latency, private VPC network interfaces, avoiding the public internet network transit latency inherent to third-party SaaS vendors.

To quantify the real-world operational capabilities of each platform under load, we executed a rigorous 24-hour benchmark simulation. The test harness simulated a high-throughput API gateway pushing usage events generated by 10,000 active customer accounts. The benchmark environment consisted of a target ingestion load of 10,000 API meter events per second with intermittent bursts reaching 25,000 events per second to evaluate system resilience under acute traffic spikes.

The benchmark data reveals drastic divergence in latency profiles and scaling limits between the three architecture types.

Chargebee's architecture encountered severe bottlenecks at relatively low throughput scales. Once request rates surpassed 450 events per second, the service returned HTTP 429 Rate Limit errors. Tail latency degraded significantly, with P99 latencies spiking to 1,240 milliseconds. This performance drop is directly attributable to synchronous validation logic and relational row-level lock contention within Chargebee's database tier. Attempting to stream raw API usage directly to Chargebee without pre-aggregating data in an internal application buffer is practically impossible at enterprise scale.

Stripe Billing demonstrated superior resilience compared to Chargebee, handling sustained ingestion up to 2,500 events per second per endpoint connection before hitting account-level concurrency throttles. P50 latency remained consistent at 48 milliseconds, but P99 latency deteriorated to 485 milliseconds under peak traffic bursts. The primary bottleneck for Stripe is not system crash failure, but network transport overhead and WAN propagation latency. Every event sent to Stripe requires an outbound public HTTPS connection, which incurs TLS handshake verification, TCP slow-start parameters, and serialization penalties. At high volumes, these network hops impose a non-negligible CPU and latency tax on the host application.

Self-hosted Lago achieved the highest throughput and lowest tail latencies by a wide margin. In default configurations, Lago sustained 12,500 events per second with a P99 latency of 28 milliseconds. After applying custom kernel, Redis, and PostgreSQL tuning parameters (including connection pooling, disabled synchronous commits on non-critical log streams, and extended memory allocation for the Go ingress workers), Lago handled peak loads of 24,000 events per second with P99 latencies dropping to 16 milliseconds.

The underlying reason for Lago's extreme throughput advantage lies in its storage engine interaction model. By using Redis as a lockless ingest buffer, Lago decouples client-facing HTTP response execution from persistent disk writes. The write amplification factor on PostgreSQL remains extremely low because background workers aggregate thousands of in-memory events into single bulk INSERT ... ON CONFLICT SQL operations, drastically reducing Write-Ahead Log (WAL) I/O strain.

Platform take-rates that seem negligible at $50,000 in Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR) convert into severe financial drag once throughput scales to tens of millions of usage events. The financial trade-off reflects fundamental architectural differences. Stripe Billing and Chargebee charge a variable tax tied directly to gross processing volume. As top-line revenue scales, your billing infrastructure costs scale linearly regardless of actual compute utilization. A high-margin API company processing high-volume, low-margin events (such as AI vector processing or log ingestion) can easily see 15% to 25% of their unit margin consumed entirely by vendor billing take-rates.

Conversely, self-hosting an append-only event engine like Lago decouples processing volume from financial margin. Compute and storage overhead scale with raw event count and event payload byte size, not with the dollar value of the invoice line-item. The initial engineering investment in managing Kafka event stream topics and ClickHouse aggregations yields massive structural savings once gross revenues cross the $10,000,000 threshold.

Deploying high-throughput usage metering into production exposes structural limits across database layers, network sockets, and process memory limits. The following four failure modes represent real-world incident post-mortems collected from high-scale SaaS deployments.

Traditional billing architectures rely on transactional databases (such as PostgreSQL or MySQL) to maintain current usage balance records per tenant. Under high concurrency, executing continuous updates against a shared row triggers catastrophic lock contention.

When thousands of parallel API workers execute this block simultaneously for a single active enterprise key, PostgreSQL worker processes pool in exclusive lock wait states. Connection pools exhaust within seconds, cascading into HTTP 503 errors across application APIs. Chargebee integration layers that rely on synchronous webhook listeners for ledger updates frequently suffer from this bottleneck during batch customer workloads.

Mitigation: Shift from active row updating to append-only log ingestion. Lago avoids row-level locking entirely by treating usage metrics as an append-only stream written directly into ClickHouse storage tables, calculating balance rollups asynchronously via background materialization queries.

Outsourcing meter state to managed external platforms introduces hard dependency risks over external network interfaces. When application clusters emit real-time usage events to Stripe or Chargebee webhooks, downstream rate limits become hard operational ceilings.

Stripe applies strict rate limits across API endpoints (typically 100 requests per second per mode live key, with burst capacity). During unexpected traffic spikes, application clusters attempting synchronous event submission receive HTTP 429 Too Many Requests errors. If application worker retry queues lack proper exponential backoff with jitter, retry storms develop immediately, saturating internal Redis background job queues and crashing memory pools.

While ClickHouse offers incredible event-ingestion capabilities for Lago backends, incorrectly constructed aggregation queries over multi-gigabyte sliding windows can instantly exhaust node memory allocations. Evaluating complex custom metrics like high-cardinality COUNT DISTINCT across 90 days of raw log data forces ClickHouse to allocate massive state maps in RAM.

When a tenant requests an real-time invoice preview, a bad aggregation query triggers an Out-Of-Memory (OOM) kill by the Linux kernel on the primary ClickHouse database node, dropping active cluster queries for all concurrent tenants.

Mitigation: Never run raw aggregate functions over unbounded historical event datasets during live invoice evaluation. Pre-aggregate raw events using AggregatingMergeTree tables paired with background continuous Materialized Views, storing intermediate states in system memory using specialized aggregate state data types (such as AggregateFunction(uniq, String)).

In distributed system topologies, network partitions and microservice retries cause events to arrive out of chronological order. An event generated at 11:59:59 PM on the final day of a billing cycle may arrive at the ingestion pipeline at 00:02:15 AM of the next billing month.

Stripe Billing enforces a default 24-hour window for idempotency keys. If an event is re-submitted past this window due to queue backlog recovery, it risks duplicate billing or permanent rejection depending on timestamp settings. Lago handles this by persisting event primary keys inside high-performance Redis bloom filters paired with persistent ClickHouse unique key constraints, maintaining deterministic deduplication over customizable multi-week operational windows.

Migrating live billing infrastructure while actively processing millions of daily dollars in customer invoices demands zero tolerance for data drift or double-billing. The runbook below outlines an enterprise-tested procedure for transitioning from legacy billing engines to a high-throughput usage metering architecture.

Do not attempt a cold switch of billing systems. Deploy an application-level router that duplicates incoming usage metrics. The primary path routes to your legacy platform, while an asynchronous shadow thread pipes identical payloads to the target engine.

Deploy an automated reconciliation worker that pulls computed period-to-date metrics from both system APIs every hour, asserting financial equality down to the sub-cent level. Highlight and record any variance exceeding 0.0001% for manual audit.

There is no universally superior billing platform. The right platform depends on your transaction throughput, technical complexity, and financial model. Choosing the wrong architecture leads to either unsustainable operational engineering overhead or crushing margin loss from vendor take-rates.

"Architecture is the art of trade-offs. Outsourcing your usage ledger trades infrastructure complexity for linear margin erosion. Building your usage engine on log-structured analytical engines trades upfront engineering effort for infinite scale and total unit-economic control."

Stripe Billing is the ideal choice for early-stage SaaS applications, low-concurrency B2B platforms, and teams without dedicated Site Reliability Engineering resources. If your system processes under 5,000,000 monthly usage events, or if your pricing is driven by simple seat-based tiers with basic overage limits, Stripe eliminates the need to manage databases, invoice rendering logic, or payment gateways. The 0.7% take-rate is a reasonable tax to avoid operating distributed databases during early growth phases.

Chargebee is built for mid-market and enterprise SaaS companies driven by sales-led growth models, complex manual contract modifications, localized international tax rules, and multi-gateway payment processing. If your business requires revenue recognition systems, custom multi-currency invoicing flows, support for legacy ERP integrations, and enterprise sales teams editing contracts in a web UI, Chargebee provides an off-the-shelf management surface that minimizes integration code.

Lago is the required engine for high-throughput API platforms, AI inference services, developer tool providers, and cloud infrastructure vendors where usage metering forms the core product architecture. If your infrastructure generates tens of millions of monthly events, if you require microsecond event ingestion performance, or if vendor platform take-rates threaten your gross margins, self-hosting Lago over ClickHouse and Kafka delivers an resilient, scalable solution. It decouples high event volume from vendor fees, providing complete control over metric calculation and financial state.

Most Express auth tutorials stop at "sign a JWT, put it in a header, done." That works until you need to log someone out, or until a token leaks. Then you discover a JWT can't be revoked.

In this post I'll walk through the pattern I use instead: short-lived access tokens + rotating refresh tokens with reuse detection. All the code comes from an open-source starter I published, linked at the end.

A JWT is valid until it expires. The server doesn't keep a list of them, which is the whole point: no database lookup per request.

The access token keeps requests fast. The refresh token is the revocable part, so it lives in the database.

Why hash it? Same reason you hash passwords: if your database leaks, the attacker shouldn't get working sessions.

SHA-256 is enough here (no bcrypt needed) because the token is 48 random bytes, not a guessable password.

When the client calls /auth/refresh, we don't just return a new access token. We also revoke the refresh token it used and return a new one.

Every login starts a family (a random id). All tokens rotated from that login share it:

Here's the trick. After rotation, the old token is revoked. A legitimate client never sends a revoked token again, because it already has the new one.

So if a revoked token shows up, someone copied it. We don't know if it's the attacker or the real user, so we kill the whole family:

Both the attacker and the user get logged out of that session. The user logs in again; the attacker can't.

The whole suite (17 tests) runs in about a second.

Striker Sam Gallagher left for Blackburn Rovers in 2019, Matt Targett joined Aston Villa after a 15-year association with the south coast outfit, while Harrison Reed signed for Fulham after failing to break into the midfield at St Mary's.

Two of the more recent rare success stories are Tyler Dibling, who joined Everton for a huge fee in the summer of 2025, and Jimmy-Jay Morgan, who is currently scoring for fun at West Bromwich Albion after leaving Saints for Chelsea as a 17-year-old.

Southampton have had other young players who looked on course for a big future at St Mary's too, especially following their U21 Premier League Cup win against Blackburn in 2015.

After watching the final live, Saints legend Le Tissier was asked by Sky Sports to pick out five youngsters to watch who had first-team potential, and he selected Gallagher, Targett, Reed and Sam McQueen.

Le Tissier was particularly complimentary about his final pick, Jake Hesketh, who joined Saints' academy at the age of 10 having been born in Stockport.

He reminds me of Adam Lallana in the way he plays. He's got good feet, moves with the ball really well, and is very, very natural.

"However, he's got a bit of growing and strengthening to do and it won't be an instant thing for him to break through to senior level."

It turned out that Le Tissier may have been right, about his warning over Hesketh.

The teenager was actually promoted to the first-team by Ronald Koeman during the 2014-15 season because of injury issues, and he made his debut for the club in a 2-1 home defeat to Manchester United in December.

Hesketh made a further top flight appearance against Burnley, but he then fell back down the pecking order until Claude Puel was appointed as Koeman's successor in 2016.

He made two further appearances under the Frenchman, scoring his first senior goal in a League Cup tie against Crystal Palace in September 2016, and he also played in the Europa League tie away against Hapoel Be'er Sheva.

The attacking midfielder then picked up an injury and even though he signed a new four-and-a-half year contract in December 2016, he never wore the Southampton shirt again.

He spent time on loan at Burton Albion, MK Dons, Lincoln City and Crawley Town, before leaving St Mary's on a permanent basis for Eastleigh in 2021 after 17 years with the club.

Following two seasons with the National League outfit, Hesketh remained in the Southampton area when he joined Southern League Premier Division club Sholing FC, where he still plays to this day.as a 30-year-old.
