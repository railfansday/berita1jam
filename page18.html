In China’s Spy Operations, Romance Can Be a Long Game

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jensen Huang’s affair with a woman U.S. officials say is a Chinese spy has drawn attention to the role of intimate relationships in espionage.

At the height of the Cold War, a top official in China’s intelligence agency said that his country would never use seduction to entrap a person.

Such tactics might be for the C.I.A. or the K.G.B., the official named Kong Yuan, of the Central Investigation Department, told a group of agents in training in April 1960, but China was guided by principles. He looked at the few female comrades in the audience. “Rest assured, we will not be asking you to go run beauty traps.”

Despite that pledge, captured in a 2010 memoir by a former Chinese diplomat, China’s intelligence services have for decades used romantic entanglements to compromise or extract information from prominent people.

American officials say that China’s Ministry of State Security, which runs intelligence operations today, recruited a Chinese businesswoman to collect intelligence on Jensen Huang. Mr. Huang, who resigned on Thursday as the chief executive of Nvidia, has been one of the most powerful figures in the artificial intelligence boom.

It was not clear what role the woman, Alice Huang, or Huang Le, might have had with the Chinese spy agency. Ms. Huang was a top executive of a Singapore company that operates data centers in Southeast Asia using Nvidia chips and that rents their computing power to Chinese firms.

Mr. Huang has acknowledged that he had an affair with Ms. Huang. President Trump has said that Mr. Huang told him that he was not aware that the woman was a spy. China has rejected the espionage allegations against Ms. Huang, calling them smears against the country. U.S. officials have not provided evidence for their claims. Ms. Huang has not responded to requests for comment.

Johnny Depp Officially Reveals Which Director He Would Love to Reunite With

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Every adaptation of Charles Dickens’ timeless ghost story has been a delightful blend of festive cheer and whimsical terror, and now, A Christmas Carol returns to the big screen as Paramount Pictures’ Ebenezer, in the hands of horror filmmaker Ti West. After the writer and director, best known for the X Trilogy, was brought on to adapt Nathaniel Halpern’s (Legion) screenplay, he and star Johnny Depp met under bizarre circumstances that led the duo to believe this was a match written in the stars, they tell Collider’s Steve Weintraub.

Depp, who’s spent decades creating unforgettable on-screen characters, stars as the miserly Ebenezer Scrooge, who’s visited by the Ghosts of Christmas Past, Present, and Future on one unbelievable night that will change his life forever. The movie also stars Rupert Grint, Tramell Tillman, Andrea Riseborough, Sam Claflin, Daisy Ridley, Arthur Conti, Ellie Bamber, and Ian McKellen.

In this interview, Depp and West look back at their own pasts, revisiting special moments from their careers, and share the story behind their first meeting for Ebenezer that signaled to the two of them that this collaboration was “meant to be.” Depp also shares his personal approach to scenes, and how he lands on performances for iconic characters like Captain Jack Sparrow, Edward Scissorhands, and more.

JOHNNY DEPP: I think that's a great idea.

TI WEST: You'll be funding it. We’ll be ready to go. Cuts a lot of the legwork out for me. I appreciate that.

100%. Absolutely. My first question is, if you were visited by the ghost of Movies Past and could revisit one day from your career, what day would you love to revisit?

DEPP: Wow, a moment from somewhere in my career? A moment. I'll tell you the truth. Honestly, mostly because I worshiped and I adored him, and we sadly lost him when he was 39 years old, Ted Demme, who directed the film Blow, who was a great pal. I mean, a great pal, and a great, very funny man. I would bring back any day that we were doing the film Blow together because he was magic. He was just a beautiful, amazing, amazingly talented, and down-to-earth, funny, funny guy.

What's amazing is that Teddy’s son, Dexter, and my son, Jack, became friends completely by accident. They just met each other in school when they were, like, eight years old, and they're still best friends to this day. It's incredible. So, Ted Demme. I’d go back to Ted Demme.

WEST: I had a very cool one on MaXXXine where we had shut down on Hollywood Boulevard, which for someone who was like, “Oh, maybe one day I could make movies,” to shut down Hollywood Boulevard, which is a hassle… I remember standing there in the middle of the night, and everyone's pissed off because the road's closed and it's a major thoroughfare, and it's dressed to look like the ‘80s, and I'm standing there with my dog — also no longer with us — and I remember just thinking, “This is crazy.”

Like, I went, “You know what’d be a cool idea for a movie?” and now I'm standing here with Hollywood Boulevard completely shut down, and I have a video store from the ‘80s, and all this stuff, and all these ‘80s period cars, and I was like, “This is all my fault.” Because I went, “You know what might be cool is if we did this?” And then somehow all these great people got together and made that happen, and I don't know that that will ever happen again. So, it was very cool. One of those surreal moments.

And we had that in front of the Chinese Theater, also, which is just a really strange thing. As someone who goes, “It'd be cool to make a movie,” to be doing that is really strange and surreal.

Johnny, if [The Mad Hatter], Jack Sparrow, and Ebenezer Scrooge all got in a room together for 24 hours, who is the first person to lose their mind?

DEPP: [Laughs] So it's The Hatter, Ebenezer, and Captain Jack?

DEPP: Well, at least two of them are already insane. But no, I would have to say, I think it would be Captain Jack Sparrow. He would have to find a way away from those types.

DEPP: Our first meeting, the electricity went out.

When you sit down for the first time, and you definitely want Johnny in the movie, and you probably want this role…

DEPP: I definitely did. I'd seen a number of his other films, and I was very impressed. This guy works. These are not easy things to put together. These are big puzzles, and complicated, and he had done it at least three or four times that I'd seen, and I was very impressed.

But then when we met, my fear meeting folks, directors or whoever that I don't know, is that they're going to be afraid, they're going to already be a little bit worried about what I might offer as the character. That's to say that there's probably a little bit of a tendency to have the fear that I may come into this way over the top or something.

That was a very quick conversation between Ti and I early on because you have to pay respects to Charles Dickens, you have to pay your respects to the screenwriter, the filmmaker, the visionary, and then you have to take care of the character. So, putting all those things together was very easy.

I found Ti instantly ready to just try anything. We would get the takes that we thought would be the right ones, or the right words for this or that, and then you go on, and you try a couple of things. So, he was very, very collaborative on that level. So for me, it was a gas every day.

WEST: But what you should know anecdotally is that I went to a place that he was staying, and it was a non-modern place, a very old-fashioned place, and when we were there, the power went out, and we were stuck there. WEST: And so in comes Johnny with a little candle, and we're sitting there eating some food at his table in pitch black, talking about the movie by candlelight, like two of the least methody kind of people in the most method meeting ever for the movie. DEPP: We were walking from the dining room or whatever back into the sort of hangout room by candle, walking through this old house.

Like when you're sitting with Sir Ian [McKellen], who I worship, I don't know how anything is going to go because I haven't received his… If he gives me a bunch of things, words, we’re having conversations, then I will react.

So essentially, I suppose, yeah, there are times when one has to rev up in a scene — come from here and go to there — but I don't have any preconceived idea of how the scene should go or actually end up. It's really more like how I'm going to behave, and I'm going to react. So, I just don't have any expectations of what might go down, which is a good thing for me because, well, you can be. You can just be in the moment.

WEST: Yeah. You may think from the characters that you have experienced or seen… I mean, I thought, which is what you were saying when we first met, “What am I getting myself into? How’s he going to make this character? How far is it going to go?” and things like that. And there's a remarkable lack of pageantry to it. You can drop into it immediately, and I think it's because there is a genuine truth and emotion to it.

So if I said, “Hey, could you be over here?” “Yeah, no problem.” “Could you be over there?” “Yeah, no problem.” “Do you think you could get the prop on that part?” “Yeah. No problem.” But when it's go time, I think you experience it truthfully.

And as eccentric and as loud as the characters you've played over the years may be — I can only speak from my experience in this movie — once you know where you need to be, whatever happens just sort of happens in a very genuine way. And I think that's a real tribute to whatever it is that you do because it's amazing to watch, because it happens like that.

Supabase vs Firebase: Which Backend Fits Your Startup?

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 12 • Originally published at tjvex.com

Every startup reaches the same fork in the road. You have an app idea, a small team, and a launch date that will not wait. You need user accounts, a database, file storage, and realtime updates. Building all of that from scratch takes months you do not have. Supabase vs Firebase is the comparison most founders end up making. Switching backends later is the kind of migration nobody budgets for.

This post compares the two platforms on the things that actually decide a startup backend: the database, auth, realtime, pricing, and how hard it is to leave.

Firebase is Google's backend platform, and it has been around long enough to be boring in the best sense. It gives you a NoSQL database called Firestore, authentication, file storage, cloud functions, hosting, push notifications, and analytics, all under one roof. You wire up the SDK, and your app talks to Google's infrastructure directly. There is no server for you to manage.

The biggest strength of Firebase is how fast it gets a product out the door. A solo founder or a two-person team can ship login, a database, and file uploads in days, not weeks. The documentation is extensive, the community is huge, and almost every problem you hit has already been answered somewhere.

Firebase also scales without you thinking about it. Your app goes from ten users to ten thousand, and Firestore keeps answering. That is genuinely valuable when you are small, because your team is building features instead of babysitting infrastructure.

Supabase is the newer challenger, and it takes a different approach. Where Firebase is a set of Google services with its own way of doing things, Supabase is built on open source tools you may already know: PostgreSQL for the database, GoTrue for auth, and standard storage and realtime layers on top. It calls itself the open source Firebase alternative, and that framing is accurate.

The practical difference is that Supabase gives you a real relational database. Your data lives in Postgres tables with rows, columns, foreign keys, and SQL queries. If your team already knows SQL, and most backend developers do, Supabase feels familiar from day one. You can write a complex query, join three tables, and get exactly the rows you need, instead of structuring your whole data model around the limits of a document store.

Supabase is also self-hostable. You can run the whole stack on your own servers if you ever need to, which matters to teams selling into regulated industries, or to clients who ask where their data lives.

The database is the decision you will live with longest, so it deserves the most attention. Firestore is a document database. You store collections of documents, each one a bundle of fields, and you query them with the rules Firestore allows. It is fast for simple lookups, and it handles nesting naturally.

The trouble starts with relationships. A marketplace with sellers, products, orders, and reviews needs joins, and Firestore does not do joins. You end up duplicating data across documents, or making several reads where one query would do. That works, and plenty of successful apps do exactly this, but it pushes complexity into your app code.

Postgres, the database behind Supabase, handles relationships natively. Orders link to users, products link to sellers, and a single query pulls the whole picture together. For products with connected data, like booking systems, marketplaces, or CRMs, that difference shows up fast.

There is a counterpoint worth stating plainly. If your data is simple, like a chat app or a feed, Firestore is easier. You will write less backend code and move faster.

Both platforms handle the part every app needs and nobody enjoys building: sign-up, login, password resets, and social login with Google or Apple. Firebase Auth is polished and battle tested, with phone auth and anonymous sign-in built in. Supabase Auth covers the same ground, including social providers, magic links, and phone auth.

One practical difference is how user data connects to the rest of your app. In Supabase, your users table is a normal Postgres table, so linking a user to their orders or subscription is one foreign key. In Firebase, user records live in Auth and app data lives in Firestore, so you connect the two by user ID and keep them in sync yourself.

Both platforms offer realtime updates, which is how a chat message or a booking change appears on screen without a refresh. Firebase built its reputation on realtime, and it shows. Firestore listeners are simple to use and reliable. Supabase realtime is built on Postgres replication, which broadcasts database changes to connected clients. It works well for most cases, but Firebase still has the edge in maturity here.

File storage is similar on both. You upload images or documents to a bucket, set access rules, and serve them through a CDN. Supabase storage is S3-compatible, which makes it easy to move files elsewhere later.

Both platforms offer serverless functions for the logic that should not live in the app: sending an email after an order, charging a card, or cleaning up old data. For a startup, the honest summary is that both are fine. The functions you write in the first year will be small, and either platform runs them well.

This is where founders get caught out, so read the pricing pages carefully before you commit. Firebase charges by usage: reads, writes, deletes, storage, and bandwidth. The free tier is generous, and for a small app the bill can stay at zero for months. The surprise comes with scale. A chatty app that reads a document every time a user opens a screen can rack up millions of reads, and each one is billed.

Supabase charges by project tier, with a free tier and paid plans that include a bundle of database size, storage, and bandwidth. The pricing is easier to predict, because you are paying for a plan rather than counting individual reads. Heavy read patterns that would sting on Firebase are often cheaper on Supabase, since a SQL query that returns a hundred rows is one query, not a hundred reads.

Every backend platform locks you in to some degree. The question is how much, and what leaving costs. Firebase lock-in is real. Your data lives in Firestore, your auth lives in Google, your functions run on Google Cloud. Moving away means rewriting data access code and migrating users, which is a project, not an afternoon.

Supabase lock-in is lighter, because the core is Postgres. Your data is in a standard database you can export with ordinary tools, and you can even self-host the whole stack. That does not make migration free, since your app code still talks to Supabase libraries, but the data layer, which is the expensive part to move, is portable by design.

There is no universal winner, but there are clear patterns. Pick Firebase if you are a small team shipping a mobile app fast, your data is simple, and you are comfortable inside Google's ecosystem. It remains an excellent choice for MVPs and consumer apps where speed to launch beats everything else.

Pick Supabase if your data is relational, your team knows SQL, you expect complex queries and reporting, or your clients care about data portability. It is also the stronger choice when your product is a dashboard, a marketplace, a booking system, or a CRM.

In our experience, teams that choose Firebase for speed and later regret it usually regret the document model, not the platform. Teams that choose Supabase and regret it usually wanted a faster start. Knowing which regret you are more likely to have is half the decision.

9 Developer Tools That Pay for Themselves in a Week

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I tracked what each one saved me. These nine paid back their price in days, not months.

Last Tuesday, at 11:40 at night, Claude Code stopped in the middle of a refactor. Not with an error. With a rate limit. I was three files into a change in Notchy, I had the whole plan in my head, and the tool just said no.

I sat there and did the maths. I'd been paying for that limit with my evenings for months. It never showed up on a bill, only as lost nights.

That night I went through every tool I use to build and ship and asked one question about each: does this pay for itself? Nine of them did, in a week or less. Most are free.

VS Code is the editor and the terminal in one window, and it costs nothing.

I used to keep three apps open to write code: an editor, a terminal, and a browser tab full of docs. Every build meant finding the right window, and half the time I typed the command into the wrong one.

Now the terminal sits underneath the code. I run the build, see the error, fix the line above it. Last month I fixed a crash in Notchy's clipboard code and ran the build forty times in one afternoon. If each run had meant switching apps, I'd still be there.

Checkout here: https://code.visualstudio.com

Privacy: runs on your Mac. It sends some usage telemetry by default, which you can turn off in settings. Your code stays yours.

Raycast is a launcher that does the job of five menu bar apps, and you forget it's there.

Spotlight is fine until you want to do something with the thing you found. I used to open Spotlight, find the app, open it, then go find the file I actually wanted. Three steps for a one-step job. A quick calculation meant opening the Calculator app. For a calculation.

Raycast is one shortcut. Two letters, and there's the app, the file, the command. I launch builds from it, search files, do quick sums. I counted once: about fifty hits a day, ten seconds each. That's eight minutes a day, on a tool I never think about.

Price: free to start. Pro is a subscription, so check the link for the current price. The free version is what I use most days.

Privacy: runs on your Mac, nothing uploaded unless you turn on cloud sync yourself.

GitHub Desktop shows you the diff before you push, so you stop shipping mistakes.

I used to commit from the command line and trust my memory. That worked until the day I pushed a debug print statement and a test file to a release branch, on a Friday. I found out on Monday, from a user.

GitHub Desktop makes the diff the whole point. Before I commit, I see every line that changed. Last month it caught a config change I made while testing and forgot to revert. One line, and it would have broken the build for everyone who pulled it.

Checkout here: https://desktop.github.com

Privacy: runs on your Mac. It talks to GitHub only when you push, pull, or fetch.

DB Browser opens any SQLite database and lets you read it like a spreadsheet.

Guessing what's inside a database is a special kind of miserable. I had a bug once where a count in the app was wrong, and I spent an hour reading code, adding log lines, and rebuilding. The code was fine. The data was wrong. A stale row was sitting in a local database, and I had no way to look at it.

DB Browser is just a window onto the file. Open it, click the table, and there's your data. I found the stale row in about thirty seconds. An hour of guessing, thirty seconds of looking.

Privacy: runs on your Mac, nothing uploaded. Your database never leaves your disk.

Postman lets you poke an API until it confesses, before your code ever touches it.

I used to test APIs by writing the code first and then finding out the endpoint was wrong. That's backwards, and I knew it was backwards, and I did it anyway, because setting up a test felt like work. Then I wired a whole feature to an endpoint that returned a 401 for one kind of request and worked fine for the rest. I found out from a user. Again.

Now I hit the endpoint in Postman first. Last month I was testing a webhook for my referrals site, and Postman showed me the 401 in about ten seconds. Ten seconds, versus a shipped bug and an apology email.

Price: free plan, with paid plans on top. Check the link for the current prices.

Privacy: runs on your Mac. Your requests go only to the API you point it at. Cloud sync is optional, and off until you turn it on.

CleanShot X takes a screenshot, lets you mark it up, and gives you a link. No file to clean up after.

If you have only ever integrated dollar stablecoins, EURC looks familiar: same issuer as USDC, a USDC-style smart contract, six decimals. But a euro stablecoin brings a few traps that dollar-coin code never had to handle. This post walks through them from a developer's point of view, with a small Python script that reads the token straight from Ethereum.

For the non-technical background (who issues it, what backs it, fees, where to buy it and how it's taxed), see this plain-English explainer on EURC, Circle's euro stablecoin.

EURC is a euro-pegged token issued by Circle's French subsidiary as an e-money token under the EU's MiCA rules. Each token is meant to be worth €1 and redeemable 1:1, with reserves held as euro bank deposits. It pays holders no interest and lives on several chains, including Ethereum, Base, Solana, Avalanche and Stellar. If stablecoins are new to you, start with how stablecoins work.

The most common bug is a units bug. Plenty of dashboards and wallets quietly assume every stablecoin equals one US dollar. EURC tracks the euro, so its USD value moves with EUR/USD every day.

The same applies to accounting: for users outside the eurozone, swapping EURC back to their home currency can create a gain or loss purely from FX movement. Our guide to how stablecoins are taxed covers why that matters.

The token used to be called "Euro Coin" with the ticker EUROC. Circle renamed it but kept the same contract addresses, so some explorers and apps still show the old name. That is exactly why you should never identify a token by its name or symbol: anyone can deploy a contract called "EURC". Identify it by contract address, taken from Circle's developer docs, and pin it in config per chain.

Like USDC, EURC on Ethereum uses 6 decimals, not 18. Hard-coding 18 is a classic bug that turns €5 into a rounding error. Read decimals() once, store it next to the address, and do arithmetic with integers or Decimal, never floats.

Also remember that supply is split across chains. The Ethereum contract only knows about Ethereum.

Axis Robotics' ODE Policy Threads a Nut Onto a Bolt, With Three More Assembly Skills Queued

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 12 • Originally published at x.com

Axis Robotics has shipped ODE Policy, a visual policy distillation method that teaches a Franka robot to thread a nut onto a bolt in simulation — the first of four assembly skills the company says it intends to convert into policies that act from camera images. The method is built around learning how actions evolve over time, an approach the company argues gives a distilled policy a more natural way to capture the continuity of robot motion. Axis Robotics describes the early results as strong.

https://x.com/i/status/2120078708879102220

Visual policy distillation is the practice of taking a policy that already solves a task — typically one with access to privileged information such as full simulation state — and compressing it into a student policy that must act from camera images alone. The student is what ships, because cameras are cheap, non-invasive and already present on most industrial cells. The teacher is what makes training tractable, because simulation can supply ground truth that a real cell cannot.

ODE Policy's contribution, as described in the company's own account, is temporal. Rather than supervising the student on isolated action labels, it learns how actions evolve over time, so the policy models the trajectory of motion rather than a sequence of independent decisions. That distinction matters most in contact-rich tasks, where the correct action at any instant depends heavily on the momentum and contact state established a moment earlier. Threading a nut onto a bolt is a compact example: the tolerance is tight, the failure modes are subtle, and a policy that reacts only to the current frame tends to oscillate or stall.

The demonstration reproduces a task the company attributes to SGS, run in simulation on a Franka arm. Axis Robotics says the next step is to widen the set of skills distilled this way, naming gear fitting, peg insertion and connector joining alongside the nut-threading result.

The company's own figures describe a narrow proof of concept and the broader plan behind it.

The ratio is the interesting part. One skill is shown; three more are named in the same breath. Those three are not variations on a theme — gear fitting, peg insertion and connector joining are distinct contact regimes with different failure modes, different tooling and different tolerance budgets. A distillation method that generalises across all four would be doing something meaningfully different from a method tuned to one task.

Contact-rich assembly is the part of factory automation that has resisted the economics of scale. Welding, painting and palletising became commodity robot applications because the motion is repeatable and the sensing requirements are modest. Inserting a peg or seating a connector is not: it demands force awareness, compliance and error recovery, and the traditional answer has been bespoke engineering per part, per fixture, per tolerance. That is why assembly cells remain expensive to commission and slow to change over.

If a policy can be distilled from simulation into a camera-only controller, the marginal cost of adding a new skill shifts from mechanical engineering to data generation and training. Cameras replace instrumented fixtures. Simulation replaces physical trial and error. The cost curve bends in the direction that has made every other branch of machine learning cheaper: more skills per unit of engineering, not more engineering per skill.

The competitive implication is a squeeze on two fronts. Generalist robot foundation model efforts argue that one large policy should absorb many tasks; task-specific distillation argues that narrow, well-supervised policies will reach production reliability first. Axis Robotics is placing a bet on the second, with a method that tries to import the temporal structure of motion rather than relearning it from scratch.

The open question is the one the demo does not answer. Everything shown runs in simulation, and the sim-to-real gap is where contact-rich policies have historically failed — friction models, sensor noise, and the small deformations that simulation approximates poorly. Distilling to camera images is the right target, but evidence that it survives contact with hardware will come from the next set of skills, not this one.

Michael Carrick’s future as Manchester United manager is in severe doubt after overseeing the club’s worst start to the Premier League season in 40 years that has left them in the relegation zone, with Mauricio Pochettino looking set to replace him.

A second Old Trafford comeback of the week, this time battling from two goals down twice to rescue a 4-4 draw with Aston Villa on Sunday, might not be enough to save Carrick’s job and he is at risk of being sacked after just 10 games of the season and with his side 18th during the international break.

United ended a nine-game winless run when they came from two goals down to beat Roma in the Champions League last week but they have not won in eight attempts in the Premier League and have just one top-flight win this campaign.

Carrick survived crisis talks with the United hierarchy after defeats to Como and Bournemouth last month and was given time to turn results around, but speculation over a change begun the day after Sunday’s chaotic draw and The Independent understands contact was initially made with United States boss Pochettino over succeeding Carrick on Monday.

The Old Trafford hierarchy, including Jason Wilcox, Omar Berrarda and Sir Jim Ratcliffe, have met in Monaco to discuss Carrick’s future and the club are on the verge of hashing out a deal with Pochettino, who is reportedly ready to accept the position if United ditch their current boss.

Mauricio Pochettino began his managerial career at Espanyol before moving to Southampton for a 16-month spell in charge.

The Argentine was then appointed as manager of Tottenham in the summer of 2014 and established them as a top four club, taking them to the Champions League final in 2019 before being dismissed less than six months afterwards.

He won three trophies as manager of Paris Saint-Germain including the 2021/22 Ligue 1 title.

In 2023/24 he spent a season as Chelsea boss before being fired in the summer.

Pochettino then replaced Gregg Berhalter as United States Men’s National Team head coach in 2024, following the side’s group-stage exit from the Copa America. He has won 21 of his 35 matches in charge and took the nation to the last-16 of the World Cup this summer.

Peter Schmeichel has accused Manchester United of "disrespectful" treatment towards Michael Carrick as rumours linking Mauricio Pochettino to the club persist.

Former United keeper Schmeichel believes the root problems lie above Carrick, arguing the head coach should be allowed to sign players in the January window.

"I absolutely do feel sympathy for him. I think the way this is playing out is very disrespectful to him," Schmeichel told The Overlap Daily.

"There are so many things that have been going on at Manchester United literally since Sir Alex (Ferguson) and David Gill left the club. And it’s never been corrected. It’s not been the correct people, not the right people in charge, and slowly it’s just gone worse.”

Manchester United have reportedly struck a verbal agreement with Mauricio Pochettino to take over as head coach in the event of Michael Carrick’s sacking.

Carrick took training on Wednesday but it expected to be relieved of his duties before the end of the week, with Pochettino ready to accept United’s proposal.

The Argentinian, who has previously managed in the Premier League for Chelsea, Tottenham and Southampton, will likely remain in charge of the USA for their two games against Haiti during the current international break.

On Sunday evening, Mauricio Pochettino got the call he had been waiting for.

He and long-time assistant Jesus Perez had frequently asked about Manchester United over the last decade. They would often contact friends in the game who were connected to Old Trafford, looking to find out what was happening, whether there could be an opening.

Pochettino came very close to taking one of those openings on at least three occasions: 2018, 2021 and 2024. Now, elements might finally have aligned.

US Soccer was prepared for the prospect of Mauricio Pochettino leaving the United States before the next World Cup, The Independent understands.

A succession plan has been drawn up in the event that Pochettino’s release clause is activated, with Man United thought to be ready to pay approximately £7m for the Argentine coach and his support staff.

The USA face a double header against Haiti during this international break, though it remains unclear if an interim boss will lead the stars and stripes before a long-term successor to Pochettino is confirmed.

USA defender Antonee Robinson says head coach Mauricio Pochettino is focused on their upcoming matches amid links with Manchester United.

Robinson said: "That news kind of got sprung on all of us, like the rest of the world, I'd say. And similar things happened in the past, happened at the World Cup, where huge teams lose their manager and they start looking at possible candidates.

"And for us to have a manager who's linked with those sorts of jobs means we're very lucky, and we know that we're working with a top-class manager.

"But that never seems to leak into the work we do, whether it was the World Cup or whether it's going to be this camp. All that sort of outside noise is nothing to do with him.

"He's very much focused on getting the best out of the team and getting these two wins in the Nations League, and we'll be on the same page as that."

Should Carrick remain in charge or should he be replaced whoever is in the dugout following the international break will have a tricky start.

United’s next fixture is against Liverpool in the Premier League before a run of games against clubs that could firmly place them in a relegation battle should they fail to win them.

Following the end of last season and Man Utd’s third place finish in the Premier League hope was high for a positive season in 2026/27.

The club were back in the Champions League and Michael Carrick had the players firing on the pitch but things didn’t turn out as planned.

A 2-0 defeat to newly promoted Hull City on the opening day of the season kicked off the campaign with the worst possible start and things declined from there.

United’s 5-2 victory over Ipswich was peppered with mistakes while a draw against Everton and then Sabah in the Champions League offered some hope.

That Sabah win came on September 10th and United did not win another game until November 3rd which translated to a nine-match winless run.

In that time they lost to rivals Man City - who played the majority of the game with 10-men - and were knocked out of the Carabao Cup by Brighton despite being 2-0 up at Old Trafford.

A 4-1 defeat to Como in Europe then kick started a run of games which saw United conced 15 goals in five games. Three of those ended in defeats with one win and one draw.

The latter are United’s two most recent matches which were both fraught affairs.

United came from 2-0 down against Roma to triumph 3-2 in the Champions League while the Red Devils twice overcame a two goal deficit against Aston Villa to secure a 4-4 draw in the league last Sunday.

Manchester United have made their worst start to a season in 40 years under Michael Carrick this year.

They’ve won just once in the Premier League, against Ipswich Town, and have gained only eight points from the first 10 matches.

Pressure has mounted increasingly on Carrick over recent weeks though United are unbeaten in their last two games with a 3-2 win over Roma in the Champions League followed by a 4-4 draw against Aston Villa on Sunday.

That win over Roma is United’s only victory, across all competitions, in their last 11 games.

The Independent’s Chief Football Writer Miguel Delaney reports that the club are close to an agreement with Mauricio Pochettino to become their next manager.

It is not yet rubber-stamped that Michael Carrick will be sacked, but that is the expectation following a meeting of the hierarchy in Monaco yesterday, and Pochettino is now lined up as a replacement.
