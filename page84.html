Claude Jira: Built for Tickets, Slow at Team Metrics

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 19 • Originally published at infograph.ai

This was first published on the Infograph blog: https://infograph.ai/blog/claude-jira

Connect Claude to Jira with Atlassian's own connector and it can find tickets, read them, write new ones, comment, and move them across the board, all with your Jira permissions. Atlassian built those tools around single tickets, so work on one ticket or one epic is what this setup does best. It's one connector in Claude, or one command in Claude Code, Anthropic's coding tool (setup below).

Team numbers are harder. Take cycle time: how long a ticket takes from the day someone starts it to the day it's done. Every ticket shows the day it was created, but the day someone started it is only in the ticket's history, its log of every change. So "What was our median cycle time last quarter?" needs the history of every ticket you finished, and Atlassian's history tool reads one ticket at a time.

The shortcut Claude can take is to count from the day each ticket was created to the day it was resolved. That's lead time, which includes the weeks a ticket sat in the backlog, so it's usually a bigger number.

So use Claude for the ticket in front of you, and a dashboard built on every ticket's history for the team's numbers.

Most of what a lead asks of Jira falls into two piles. One is about a ticket or an epic: what's blocking PAY-388, or write these notes up as a story. The other is about the team: where do our tickets wait, and are we getting faster?

Claude with Atlassian's connector fits the first pile. It works where you already are, in a chat or in Claude Code, and it writes and moves tickets as well as reading them. One ticket or one epic takes a few tool calls (each call is one action, like fetching a ticket).

The second pile is where it slows down. A question about many tickets' histories takes a call per ticket. Claude also works each answer out from scratch, so it may pick different dates or tickets from one week to the next. And because it can write, it can move the wrong ticket as easily as the right one.

Infograph, which is what we make, fits the second pile. Our Jira connection keeps every status change on every ticket, so cycle time and time in status are counted across all of them, the same way each week. You get a dashboard: a board the team can look at in standup, and a link you can send to leadership.

Its limits: it can't change Jira, so it won't write or move a single ticket. Its numbers are only as fresh as the last sync, and it needs a one-time setup, including which of your statuses mean waiting.

You can also have both: Claude can ask Infograph's synced Jira history directly (how, below).

What Is LLM Throughput? Tokens per Second, Concurrency, and Batching

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When teams evaluate how a large language model (LLM) will perform in production, two families of metrics come up repeatedly. One family describes latency: how quickly an individual request is served. The other describes throughput: how much total work the system can accomplish in a given period. Throughput is what determines whether a deployment can serve ten users or ten thousand, and it is the metric most directly tied to infrastructure cost per request. Yet it is frequently misunderstood, partly because the headline figure, "tokens per second," can mean several different things depending on whether you are counting one stream or the whole system.

This article explains what LLM throughput actually measures, why it depends so heavily on concurrency and batching, and how it trades off against latency. The goal is a clear and accurate mental model, grounded in how inference hardware behaves.

Throughput is the rate at which a system completes work. For LLM inference, "work" is almost always measured in tokens, the sub-word units the model reads and generates. The most common throughput metric is tokens per second, but it is critical to distinguish two senses of it:

These two numbers can move in opposite directions, which is the single most important and counterintuitive fact about LLM serving. Pushing more requests through the system at once usually raises aggregate throughput while lowering each individual stream's rate. Confusing the two leads to bad capacity decisions, so it is always worth asking which one a given "tokens per second" figure refers to.

Throughput is also sometimes expressed in requests per second, which is useful when requests have similar shapes, but tokens per second is more robust because LLM requests vary enormously in input and output length. A request that generates 10 tokens and one that generates 1,000 tokens are very different amounts of work even though each counts as a single request.

To understand why concurrency raises aggregate throughput, you have to look at what the hardware is doing during generation. Modern accelerators are massively parallel, with enormous arithmetic capacity. During the token-by-token decoding phase of generation, however, the dominant cost is not arithmetic. It is reading the model's weights from memory. For every single token a model generates, it must stream its full set of parameters (or the relevant portions) out of memory and through the compute units.

If the server generates a token for just one request, it reads all those weights to produce a single token. The expensive memory read is "amortized" over one unit of useful work, and most of the chip's arithmetic capacity sits idle. This is deeply inefficient.

Batching fixes this. By grouping many requests together and generating their next tokens in one pass, the server reads the model weights once and uses them to produce tokens for all requests in the batch. The costly memory read is shared across many units of work. As a result, aggregate throughput climbs almost linearly with batch size, up to the point where the hardware's arithmetic capacity or memory for the KV cache becomes the bottleneck.

Concurrency is the number of requests a system is actively processing at the same time. Higher concurrency enables larger batches, which drives aggregate throughput up. But concurrency is not free, and it cannot grow without bound. Two resources constrain it.

The first is memory for the KV cache. Each in-flight request maintains a cache of key and value tensors representing the context it has processed so far. This cache grows with the sequence length and consumes accelerator memory alongside the model weights. The more concurrent requests, and the longer their contexts, the more memory the KV cache demands. When memory runs out, the system can no longer admit new requests, must queue them, or in some designs must evict and recompute context, all of which hurt performance.

The second is compute saturation. As batch size grows, the decoding step eventually stops being purely memory-bound and starts being limited by arithmetic throughput. Beyond that point, adding more concurrent requests no longer increases aggregate throughput; it only increases per-request latency, because each step now takes longer. The system has reached its efficient operating ceiling.

The art of serving LLMs efficiently is largely about operating near the "knee" of this curve: enough concurrency to keep the hardware well-utilized and aggregate throughput high, but not so much that latency becomes unacceptable or memory overflows.

Early batching approaches were "static": the server collected a batch, ran it to completion, and only then started the next batch. This is wasteful because requests in a batch finish at different times. A request generating a short answer finishes early but its slot stays occupied, idle, until the longest request in the batch completes.

Continuous batching (also called in-flight or dynamic batching) solves this. Instead of waiting for the whole batch to finish, the scheduler works at the granularity of individual decode steps. As soon as any request completes and frees its slot, a waiting request is admitted into the batch immediately. The batch is continuously topped up, keeping utilization high and reducing the time new requests spend queuing. Continuous batching is one of the most significant throughput improvements in modern inference servers, often increasing effective throughput several-fold over static batching for mixed workloads.

A simple back-of-the-envelope relationship connects these quantities. If you know your target per-stream rate and your aggregate capacity, you can estimate how many users you can serve concurrently:

Throughput and concurrency relationships aggregate_tps = 2000 # system tokens/sec at the chosen batch size per_stream_tps = 40 # tokens/sec each active request should receive How many streams can run at the target per-stream rate? max_concurrency = aggregate_tps / per_stream_tps = 2000 / 40 = 50 concurrent streams Throughput for a workload, given average output length and request rate avg_output_tokens = 300 requests_per_sec = aggregate_tps / avg_output_tokens = 2000 / 300 ~= 6.7 requests/sec sustained

These are approximations, not guarantees. Real aggregate throughput depends on prompt lengths (long prompts spend more time in the compute-bound prefill phase), output lengths, how uniform the requests are, and memory headroom. But the structure of the calculation is sound and useful for capacity planning: aggregate throughput divided by the per-stream rate you are willing to deliver gives you a rough concurrency ceiling.

The central tension in LLM serving is that throughput and latency pull against each other. Driving the system toward maximum aggregate throughput means running large batches at high concurrency, which lengthens each individual request's time per token and can increase queuing delay before a request even starts. Conversely, optimizing for the lowest possible latency for each user means running small batches, which leaves hardware underutilized and raises the cost per token.

There is no universally correct setting. A batch inference job that processes a million documents overnight should be tuned for maximum throughput, since no human is waiting on any individual result. An interactive assistant should cap batch sizes and concurrency to protect per-user responsiveness, accepting a higher cost per token. Most production systems expose configuration, such as maximum batch size, maximum concurrency, and queue limits, precisely so operators can position the system along this trade-off curve for their use case.

In a deployment serving multiple applications or models, throughput and concurrency are managed at the layer where traffic is proxied, at a gateway that sits in front of the model servers. Concentrating traffic management there allows concurrency limits, queuing, and load balancing across model replicas to be enforced consistently, so that one bursty client cannot starve others and aggregate capacity is used efficiently. This separation of concerns, with the model server focused on efficient batched generation and the routing layer focused on distributing and limiting traffic, is a common and effective pattern.

Lantern Links is a pipe puzzle. A glowing core sits near the middle of a grid, every other tile holds a piece of pipe, and you rotate tiles until light reaches every lantern. We wanted levels that never run out, never need a hand-made level file, and are never unsolvable. This post walks through the generator, which is about 60 lines of plain JavaScript inside a single 71 KB index.html with no libraries.

A short version of the idea is in a devlog on itch. Here we go deeper into the parts that are easy to get wrong: how the tree is grown, what the seed covers, how par is counted, and how the board shape is chosen.

Most "shuffle until solvable" approaches generate a random board and then check it. We do the opposite. We build a finished, fully lit board first, and then scramble it. If you start from a solution and only apply moves that the player can undo, the puzzle is solvable by construction. No solver is needed.

A spanning tree of the grid graph has exactly these properties. It reaches every cell, it uses N-1 edges for N cells, and a connected graph with N-1 edges cannot contain a cycle. So the generator is a random spanning tree algorithm.

Each tile is a 4-bit mask, one bit per side:

Rotating a tile clockwise moves each bit one place left, and the top bit wraps around to the bottom. That is the whole rotation function. The kind of piece falls out of the bit count: one bit is a lantern (a dead end), two opposite bits are a straight, two adjacent bits are a bend, three bits are a junction. There is no separate piece type anywhere in the code.

Carving an edge between two neighbours sets one bit on each tile: the direction bit on the tile we leave, and the opposite bit on the tile we enter. Here is the generator, trimmed only of its state handling:

A plain randomised depth-first search always continues from the newest cell. That makes long, snaking corridors with few branches. Picking a random cell from the frontier instead (the idea behind a randomised Prim's algorithm) gives short, bushy trees with lots of junctions. Neither feels right for this game: the first is too easy to read, the second looks like a mess of crosses.

The line that sets pickIdx mixes the two. With probability 0.45 we extend the newest cell (depth-first behaviour), and otherwise we extend a random cell from the stack. This is usually called the growing tree algorithm. The 0.45 is a single constant. Raising it pushes the look of the levels toward corridors, and lowering it toward bushes. Our README describes the generator as a randomised depth-first search, which is accurate for the 45 percent case only, so we are correcting that here.

A cell leaves the stack when it has no unvisited neighbours left. The loop ends when the stack is empty, and because the grid is connected, every cell has been visited by then.

(X is the core, o is a tile, dashes and bars are carved edges. A cycle would need an edge to a cell that is already in the tree, and the generator never does that.)

Once the tree exists, lanterns need no extra step. A tile with exactly one connection is a leaf, and a leaf is a lantern, unless it is the core:

We build the list of lanterns once per level from the solution mask. That is why the lantern count differs from level to level and from seed to seed: it is whatever the tree happens to produce.

To turn the answer into a puzzle, each tile gets a random number of clockwise quarter turns, and par is the sum of the taps needed to undo them:

First, tapsTo returns the fewest clockwise taps that bring a tile to its target mask, which is 0 to 3. A straight pipe repeats after two turns, so for a straight it is never more than 1; a junction or bend can need up to 3.

Second, there is a guard for the unlucky case where the random rotations leave the board already solved. On a 16-tile board that is not impossible. We turn the first tile that actually changes when rotated.

Third, par is measured against the board we generated, not against a proof of the minimum. We have not shown that every board has only one solved arrangement, so a player who finds a different valid arrangement might beat par. In practice par is a good yardstick, and stars are cheap: three stars at or under par, two up to par plus half of par (rounded up), one otherwise.

Math.random() cannot be seeded, so we use mulberry32, a small 32-bit generator:

The seed is lv * 7919 + 17, so the same level number grows the same tree. The same stream carries on into scramble, so the starting orientation is also the same. There is one catch, and it matters for the next section: the tree depends on the board's columns and rows as well as the seed. Level 12 on a 7 by 8 board is the same puzzle for everyone, but level 12 on a 6 by 10 board is a different one.

Restart is a small exception. It keeps the tree and re-scrambles with a time-based seed, so you get the same network with fresh orientations.

The old version of the game used a square board: 4 by 4 at level 1, growing one row and column every three levels, up to 10 by 10. On a phone held upright, a square board leaves most of the screen empty. So the board shape is now chosen at the start of each level from the free space:

The tile count is kept between k squared and 1.5 times k squared, so difficulty still grows with the level. Inside that range we try every columns-by-rows pair and score it by fill: the share of the free area covered by square tiles, where the tile side t is the smaller of the width and height limits. A new candidate wins if it fills clearly more space; if two shapes are within half a percent, the one with fewer tiles wins. The result is, for example, a tall 4 by 6 board on a phone and a wide 5 by 4 on a laptop.

One more rule keeps this from being annoying. While a level is untouched, resizing the window or rotating the phone re-runs chooseShape and rebuilds the level. After your first move the shape is frozen and the board only rescales. Otherwise the puzzle would change under your fingers.

Win detection is a breadth-first walk from the core. A neighbour joins the lit set only if both tiles have a bit facing each other. The level is solved when the lit set holds every tile. We use the same walk to record each tile's distance from the core, which the renderer uses to make light spread outward along the pipes after a move.

You can play it in the browser at webgame.fun/game/lantern-links, with no sign-up. The whole game is one MIT-licensed file on GitHub, and it is also on itch.io. Open index.html and search for generate to find the code above. If you spot a bug in the generator, or a better way to pick the shape, an issue is welcome.

Anthropic’s Backers Have Another Big Bet: Americans Staying Renters

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic’s stock market debut on Thursday is expected to value the artificial intelligence company at $1.5 trillion, putting some of Wall Street’s biggest investment firms in line to cash in from its rapid rise.

In its pitch to investors, the maker of Claude offers a vision far grander than the 2,800 millionaires the IPO is expected to mint. AI, it says, could “significantly accelerate global growth and dramatically improve quality of life for people around the world.”

But some of its backers are making another bet on a future where renting dominates the housing market.

Among them are Blackstone and Brookfield, two investment giants whose portfolios extend from emerging technologies to the nation's housing stock.

“Amidst continued macroeconomic uncertainty, we believe rental housing is the number one thing you want to own,” reads a 2025 shareholder update from Blackstone Real Estate Income Trust, known as BREIT.

Under a heading touting Blackstone Real Estate’s “Best Ideas,” the fund pointed to record homeownership costs, sluggish construction, and a national shortage of 4 million to 5 million homes. It called those conditions “powerful tailwinds” for its rental housing investments.

To housing advocates, though, those tailwinds are precisely the headwinds they’re working to reverse, says Jordan Ash, housing director at the nonprofit watchdog Private Equity Stakeholder Project.

The investment thesis anticipates a future in which “there are many more people who are just going to be renters for life,” Ash tells Realtor.com®.

“The renter is not their customer,” he says of large private equity landlords. “They're accountable to their investors.”

And now, the rental market is showing signs of turning in those investors’ favor.

Median asking rents rose 0.1% in October—the first annual increase since June 2023—across the 50 largest metros, according to Realtor.com research. The increase was small, but it came as the apartment construction boom that helped hold rents down began to fade.

Meanwhile, households that might otherwise leave the rental market face a steep price for becoming homeowners.

Mortgage rates averaged 7.33% in October, roughly double their 3.69% level in October 2019, notes Realtor.com senior economist Jiayi Xu.

“Elevated mortgage rates are one of the key barriers to homeownership, because they significantly raise the monthly cost of buying,” she explains. Higher ownership expenses have added to the burden.

That financial hurdle is apparent in Dallas–Fort Worth, TX, where Blackstone owns rental homes through Tricon, the operator it acquired in 2024.

Across the metro, the median monthly cost of buying a starter home was $2,741 in October, compared with $1,471 to rent, according to data from Realtor.com. That means making the leap to homeownership would involve finding an additional $1,270 in the monthly budget—after also coming up with a down payment.

It's easy to understand the bet then—with a gap that wide, who would ever be able to afford the jump?

Washington has tried to make that leap easier by limiting buyers’ competition from Wall Street. Following President Donald Trump’s January executive order, Congress enacted restrictions on additional single-family home purchases by institutional investors controlling at least 350 such homes. The ban takes effect on Jan. 7, 2027, with exceptions that include qualifying build-to-rent projects.

But limiting competition from investors does not, by itself, close the monthly gap between renting and owning. And while that gap gives households a reason to keep renting, BREIT expects declining construction to give landlords more room to raise rents.

“Ultimately, lower supply should support rent growth and real estate values over time,” the fund wrote in its Nov. 4 shareholder update, which did not address the institutional investor ban.

Of course, that outcome isn’t assured. Although the multifamily construction pipeline has shrunk from its peak, it remains large by pre-pandemic standards, Xu says. Projects already underway could continue giving tenants more options.

Even so, “If new multifamily deliveries cannot keep pace with rental demand, we expect rents to rise,” she says.

But even buying a home doesn't always end the relationship with a landlord.

Ash points to Brookfield’s acquisition of YES Communities, an operator of manufactured-home communities where residents can own their houses while paying rent for the land beneath them.

Residents’ complaints about rising costs and neglected maintenance predated Brookfield’s purchase. And as the deal approached, some appealed directly to pension funds invested with the firm, seeking assurances that a new owner would improve their circumstances.

“The rent just keeps going up, but no improvements have been made,” Michigan resident Abbie Pedrotte told University of California regents, according to Ash’s reporting.

By October, those concerns had reached Congress.

In an Oct. 13 letter, Rep. Rashida Tlaib (D-MI) and two colleagues pressed Brookfield about its plans. They asked whether it intended to raise home and lot rents by more than 3% annually and whether it would cooperate with residents seeking to purchase their parks. Written answers were requested by Nov. 20.

For its part, Brookfield’s published strategy calls for increasing resident homeownership and expanding tenant programs to “boost operating income and retention.” Owning the house can give a resident a stake in the community while preserving the landlord’s income from the land.

“We own the appreciating part of real estate. It’s fantastic,” Lowell Baron, CEO of Brookfield’s real estate business, told investors in September.

Some of the firms betting on continued demand for rentals are also financing a construction boom of their own.

BREIT had deployed more than $9 billion into data center developments in 2026 by its November shareholder update, helping expand the infrastructure supporting AI and cloud computing.

Blackstone has already identified a potential cost for other real estate—including homebuilding.

On its October earnings call, President Jon Gray said rising construction costs and limited new supply were being intensified by higher interest rates and “crowding out from the AI infrastructure build.”

A construction industry survey offers evidence of that competition. Among firms reporting on data center projects’ workforce effects, 58% cited increased competition for skilled workers, while 49% reported greater wage pressure, according to the 2026 AGC/NCCER Workforce Survey.

Xu cautions against treating those findings as a measure of the impact on homebuilding.

“The survey points to labor pressure where data centers are concentrated, but it does not isolate AI investment from factors like immigration enforcement and tariffs, nor does it directly measure residential construction costs,” she explains.

And to their credit, the private equity firms are also moving to help finance additional housing. Blackstone launched a homebuilder lending platform in May that it expects to support more than 50,000 new for-sale homes annually.

Buying existing homes changes ownership without adding to the housing stock, Xu explains.

“Financing new construction, by contrast, increases the stock, which is the key to expanding supply and improving affordability for both groups,” she says.

Anthropic’s backers have capital to help build the future they describe. Households waiting for a more affordable home need some of that future to include them.
