FTC issues warning letters over deceptive marketing as ACA enrollment opens" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Open enrollment season is getting underway, and the Federal Trade Commission sent warning letters to 25 companies that conduct marketing or generate sales leads for insurers.

The commission said in an announcement that the letters aim to put these companies on notice about deceptive or unfair marketing tactics that are likely unlawful. Activities in question include misrepresenting the benefits or limitations of a plan, or portraying a plan as comprehensive when it isn't.

In addition, FTC also warned against misrepresenting any costs associated with a plan or falsely telling consumers that a plan offers free rewards, cash offers or other incentives.

"Making healthcare more transparent and affordable is a top priority for the FTC," said Christopher Mufarrige, Director of the FTC’s Bureau of Consumer Protection, in the announcement.

"Americans experiencing rising healthcare costs should not also have to worry about whether a healthcare plan or program is what it claims to be," Mufarrige continued. "The FTC will remain focused on monitoring this market and will not hesitate to hold accountable companies that unlawfully market or advertise healthcare plans to consumers."

The FTC letters were largely sent to firms that market plans on the Affordable Care Act's exchanges, where open enrollment opened on Sunday, Nov. 1. Medicare is also in its annual enrollment period, which continues through Dec. 7, and late fall is when most employers begin plan selections for workers.

The agency does not allege wrongdoing in the warning letters, but instead suggests that the companies "conduct a thorough review of their advertisements to ensure they are complying with applicable laws and rules."

The letters include examples of previous enforcement actions against marketers in the ACA space, such as Innovative Partners and Top Healthcare Options Insurance Agency.

In the letters, the agency emphasizes that its staff will be closely watching for potential violations.

"FTC staff will continue to actively monitor this market for potentially deceptive or unfair acts or practices and will take enforcement action as warranted," the FTC wrote in the letter (PDF).

Zocdoc opens infrastructure to AI agents, launches partnership with Instinct" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

As more patients turn to artificial intelligence agents to manage their healthcare, Zocdoc is opening its healthcare access infrastructure to streamline the process.

Beginning Oct. 28, agents can connect patients to care through a variety of channels—including MCP (Model Context Protocol) servers, deep links and more.

Zocdoc Chief Strategy Officer Alex Doyne told Fierce Healthcare that AI agents are “becoming a new starting point for patients seeking healthcare.”

Doyne said traffic from AI agents on the platform has “at least doubled roughly every three weeks since July.”

“AI agents can help patients navigate their healthcare options and take action on their behalf, but they're only as reliable as the information and systems they can access,” Doyne said. “By connecting agents to Zocdoc's infrastructure, we can ensure they are booking the right appointments for patients.”

Zocdoc has over 175 scheduling integrations, with more than 10,000 insurance plans and 200,000 verified providers in its network, according to Doyne.

“Opening that infrastructure to agents means they now have access to a reliable booking experience,” Doyne said.

Doyne added that healthcare is “particularly challenging,” for AI agents since “so much of the information available online is fragmented, outdated, or inaccurate,” which can have “real consequences for patients who may already be struggling to access care.”

“That's why it's critical that agents have access to reliable provider and insurance information, accurate availability and infrastructure that ensures the booking is integrated with a provider’s calendar and will hold on the day,” Doyne said.

“We've established a two-way promise that sets clear expectations for Zocdoc and the agents connecting to our infrastructure,” Doyne said. “Zocdoc commits to welcoming agents that register and identify themselves, while agents are expected to obtain explicit patient consent before booking appointments and protect patient and provider data.”

The company also announced a partnership with AI personal assistant Instinct to let agents use Zocdoc’s healthcare access infrastructure to find vetted providers, verify insurance benefits and book appointments.

“Instinct is built to genuinely improve your life and handle important everyday tasks,” said Ben Schwerin, Instinct chief business officer, in a statement. “Since healthcare is more complicated than ordering groceries or booking travel, the stakes are higher. By partnering with Zocdoc, Instinct can navigate that complexity—so getting an appointment can be as simple as sending a text.”

Instinct will join Zocdoc’s growing Care Access Network, which also includes Google Gemini, Amazon Health AI, Yelp, Healthgrades and more.

Last month, Zocdoc acquired Sohar Health in its push to evolve beyond an appointment-booking marketplace into broader healthcare access infrastructure.

Sohar Health helps healthcare organizations simplify insurance workflows, and its APIs automate insurance discovery, eligibility and benefits verification, network status checks and cost estimates.

It marked the company's first acquisition in its nearly 20-year history, executives said.

Why Cancer Biomarker Testing Is Personal for Mark-Paul Gosselaar

Known for his iconic roles on “Saved by the Bell,” “NYPD Blue,” “Franklin & Bash,” and “Found,” Mark-Paul Gosselaar knows how to grab an audience.

Now, he’s using that star power to raise awareness about cancer biomarker testing while sharing how he prioritizes his own health and well-being.

“Health is a huge thing for my wife and I, and our family,” Gosselaar told Healthline. “We are constantly aware of new technologies.”

Having seen people in his life navigate cancer diagnoses, Gosselaar is now raising awareness about the role of biomarker testing in cancer treatment through his partnership with AstraZeneca and its Meet Your Mark campaign.

The campaign aims to educate people facing cancer diagnoses and their loved ones about how biomarker testing may help doctors make more informed, personalized treatment decisions. It also encourages patients to discuss testing with their healthcare teams.

“Biomarkers may tell doctors the specific type of cancer present and if targeted therapies or immunotherapy might help,” Mark Socinski, MD, oncologist and Executive Medical Director of the AdventHealth Cancer Institute, told Healthline.

He explained that biomarkers are typically genes or proteins that doctors measure through blood or tissue tests. They are used to help guide treatment in numerous diseases but are especially powerful tools in many types of cancer.

“They help decode why cancers behave differently, even when two people have the same cancer type,” said Socinski. “However, while biomarker testing provides useful information, it does not guarantee a specific outcome.”

Despite those limitations, Socinski sees promise in the shift from one-size-fits-all cancer treatment to care tailored to each patient’s specific cancer characteristics.

“Biomarker testing does play a critical role here, and it is important for people to be aware of the potential benefits,” Socinski said. “It may help change the course of a person’s treatment journey.”

Gosselaar hopes that greater awareness of biomarker testing can help people facing a cancer diagnosis feel more informed about their treatment options.

“A lot of the time, when a loved one or someone who has been diagnosed with cancer [it can] feel like your world is in a tailspin,” he said. “I wanted to get the word out for anyone who’s going through a cancer diagnosis, or a caretaker, to know that biomarker testing is an option, because even though two cancers are similar, their tumors can act very differently and biomarker testing may help the medical expert with more personalized treatment.”

As part of the campaign, Gosselaar took part in a commercial, something he hadn’t done since before his days on “Saved by the Bell.”

“This was the first time that I actually had to be on set and not be a character; I had to be me talking about this very serious issue,” he said.

While it was a challenging experience, he said it was also fulfilling.

“I enjoyed the process, and to be able to use my platform to be able to spread the word; it means a lot to me,” he said. “I’m very selective of the things that I want to attach myself to, and this seemed like the right fit.”

He particularly enjoyed recreating Zack Morris’s signature “timeout” gesture from “Saved by the Bell,” which allowed his character to freeze time and speak directly to the camera.

“Using something from the past that was such a symbolic gesture to so many people of my generation that are now my age and possibly going through [cancer] or knowing someone who’s going through [cancer to] direct them to [this campaign], means a lot,” he said.

Beyond his efforts to raise awareness about cancer biomarker testing, Gosselaar is equally focused on taking care of his own health.

Getting quality sleep is one of his top priorities. He sleeps on a high-tech mattress that monitors sleep stages, heart rate, heart rate variability, and respiratory rate. He also uses a biometric health and fitness tracker, prioritizes healthy eating, and uses saunas and red light therapy.

“We do everything that we possibly can, but I’m less about longevity and more about just living a healthy lifestyle,” Gosselaar said. “I want to continue doing the things that I’ve done since I was a child…without feeling held back by my health.”

As he embraces his 50s, he said he’d like to live a long life for his children and future grandchildren. He started taking his health seriously in his 40s after undergoing shoulder surgery.

“[It] really made me realize [that] all the damage I had done in my 20s and 30s sort of adds up later in life, and that’s probably something that I would tell the younger me… to preserve a little bit more and take [an] approach that recovery is just as important as the workout because I did not use my recovery very well; it was not a priority for me,” said Gosselaar.

He also makes time for himself every day. He said doing so makes him a better person and more able to care for his wife and four children.

“[It’s] something that I believe in; that I need to do something for myself before I can do something for someone else. Or if I do something for someone else, then I have to equalize that by doing something for me,” he said.

Daily physical activity also helps support his mental well-being.

“I need to do something where I challenge myself, and it could be as simple as a walk. It could be as simple as just getting outside and lifting something heavy [or] taking bags of groceries and taking the long way into your house,” Gosselaar said.

“Like challenging yourself with picking up things, you know, taking backpacks from the car from your kids and seeing if you can carry three in one hand…or going into the gym and spending an hour in the gym, or 30 minutes, or just moving.”

When he’s in the sauna, he works on breathing and clearing his thoughts.

“Some people need to meditate, some people need to talk to somebody. I encourage all of that because I think all of that helps,” he said.

“I’ve done them all. I’ve done the cold plunging, I’ve done all that stuff…some of it works, and some of it doesn’t; it’s whatever you believe in. So I just encourage people to stay healthy and move, get outside, and help other people.”

UnitedHealthcare eliminates prior authorization requirements on 2K pediatric services" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

UnitedHealthcare will eliminate the need for prior authorization on more than 2,000 codes for pediatric services beginning on Dec. 1, the company announced Monday.

Earlier this year, the insurance giant said it would ease prior auth requirements on an array of pediatric care, and now it's detailing the codes that will fall under that initiative. Services range from diagnostic imaging to cancer treatment to orthopedic care and more.

Examples include sleep studies, MRI scans, joint procedures, ear and tonsil procedures and some chemotherapy services, UHC said in an announcement.

The company said that it "chose to fast-track services that are widely considered in the medical community to be safe and effective," per the release. The changes will reduce prior authorization volume in pediatrics by nearly two-thirds, and apply across commercial, individual market and community plans where eligible under state law.

The changes aren't limited to services provided at pediatric facilities, UnitedHealthcare said.

“Every child deserves a health care experience centered on their needs,” said Tim Noel, CEO of UnitedHealthcare, in the announcement. “These changes will help make that experience simpler for children, their families and the physicians who care for them.”

UnitedHealthcare is among the insurers nationwide that signed on to a pledge in the summer of 2025 to ease the administrative burdens associated with prior authorization and embrace electronic submissions and decisions. Since then, the insurer has taken multiple steps to reduce the requirements on prior auth.

On Oct. 1, the company eliminated prior authorization on 1,700 services across its insurance lines. In May, UHC said it was aiming to eliminate 30% of prior auth requirements by the end of the year.

UnitedHealthcare is set to eliminate close to two-thirds of pediatric prior authorization requirements by the end of the year.

The insurance giant announced Friday morning that it will nix prior auth on an array of diagnostic tests, routine surgeries and specialty services, including cardiology, neurology, pulmonology and orthopedics.

UnitedHealth also said it will offer authorization waivers for certain procedures when they're performed at leading pediatric hospitals as a reflection of their "consistent use of well-established care practices." The insurer said that the facilities in this program represent a broad national network across medical and surgical specialties.

“Parents should be able to spend less time having to navigate the health system and more time focusing on their children as they get the care they need,” said Tim Noel, CEO of UnitedHealthcare, in the announcement.

“These changes are part of our broader efforts to simplify healthcare and allow families—and their doctors and nurses—to pursue routine care with far fewer administrative steps, while higher-risk procedures continue to undergo reviews," Noel added.

The company is undertaking a broad review of all pediatric prior authorizations to determine which can be rolled back while maintaining safety and quality. It said it plans to eliminate pre-approvals for some diagnostic imaging, sleep studies, routine outpatient tests and some surgical services and therapies that are approved consistently.

UnitedHealthcare said prior authorization will remain in place for services with high clinical complexity or variability, such as experimental treatments, or where it is required by regulations.

The company said the changes will take effect across its commercial and Medicaid plans.

The move is the latest from the company as it aims to reduce prior authorization requirements. For example, last month, UnitedHealth said it would exempt rural providers from most prior auth requirements.

The company has also made a push to reduce a wide swath of reauthorizations at its pharmacy benefit manager, Optum Rx.

Losing Weight in Your 50s May Protect Your Heart Health Later in Life

📅 Nov 02, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Losing weight in your 50s, even a modest amount, may help protect your heart health later in life, according to new research.Adults who lost weight in their early 50s saw their blood pressure, “good” cholesterol and inflammation change at about the same rate as adults who stayed at a healthy weight, according to a study from researchers at University College London and the University of Glasgow.Adults who gained weight in their early 50s saw those same measures worsen faster by their early 60s, the researchers reported. The authors posted the study on a research-sharing website on September 4. Other scientists have not yet peer-reviewed the work.

Charis Bridger-Staatz, PhD, a senior research fellow at UCL, led the study. She told Healthline the main takeaway for adults over 50 was that “it’s not too late to try and do things that are going to improve your health.”Nearly half of U.S. adults ages 40 to 59 had obesity from 2021 to 2023, the highest rate of any adult age group, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Among all U.S. adults, around 31.7% were overweight, according to the CDC.

The findings can apply to Americans because British and U.S. adults share similar weight patterns, said Solveig Cunningham, PhD, a professor of global health at Emory University’s Rollins School of Public Health. Cunningham, who was not involved in the research, told Healthline that Americans tend to gain weight across adulthood.

“By the time you get into your 50s, close to the majority of Americans already have obesity. Many others have overweight,” she said.The World Health Organization (WHO) calls obesity a chronic disease. Healthcare professionals diagnose overweight and obesity using a person’s weight and height, or body mass index (BMI).

Bridger-Staatz said the team set out to learn who lost weight and kept it off before GLP-1 weight loss medications became available, and whether that weight loss improved health over time.

She said researchers had long assumed that “once people had become overweight or developed obesity, people didn’t really lose weight and certainly not sustained it.”The team also asked, she said, “can we try and say something positive about health that isn’t just this kind of like doom and gloom?”The researchers used the British 1958 National Child Development Study, which has followed about 17,500 people born during one week in 1958. Of those, 6,309 took part in a health check in their early 60s, according to the report.The team compared each person’s highest weight between ages 23 and 42 with their weight between ages 50 and 55. Researchers sorted people by whether they gained or lost more than 5% of their body weight and kept the change. For a 200-pound person, 5% equals 10 pounds.To track heart health, the team compared blood tests and blood pressure readings at ages 44 and 62 with those of people who maintained a healthy weight.“We know everyone’s heart health is kind of going to get a little bit worse over that time because you are aging and that’s expected,” Bridger-Staatz said. The question, she said, was, “Does it slow the rate at which people’s heart health is getting worse?”

Leading Medical Groups Team Up to Redefine Female Heart Health

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A quartet of medical organizations has published a comprehensive set of reports calling for a coordinated plan of action to improve heart healthcare for women throughout their lifetimes.

The reports detail how cardiovascular health issues can be prevented, monitored, and treated from the early years of menstruation to peak fertility age to pregnancy to perimenopause to postmenopause stages.

The report authors call for healthcare professionals to identify heart health risks early in girls and women and to coordinate care across medical specialties.

They stress that females have specific biological factors that increase their risks of cardiovascular disease. Those components include hormonal and emotional factors.

The authors note that heart disease is the number one cause of death for females in the United States, as well as the number one cause of death during pregnancy. Yet, only 44% of females are fully aware of the dangers they face from heart disease.

The organizations issuing the reports are the American Heart Association (AHA), the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine.

The groups presented their findings in a trio of reports published in the AHA’s flagship journal, Circulation on October 26.

“Women experience their healthcare across clinical specialties, but it often is fragmented or disconnected,” said Sadiya Khan, MD, the chair of the collaborative writing group for the reports and a professor of cardiovascular epidemiology at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine in Chicago, in a news release.

“Specifically, these reports connect the dots for clinicians, for patients, and across health domains so that care is holistic and begins in adolescence, recognizing that women’s heart health is uniquely shaped by biological transitions not experienced by men — periods, pregnancy, perimenopause, and menopause.”

Experts not involved in the reports told Healthline the updates were a long time coming.

Nicole Weinberg, MD, a cardiologist at Pacific Heart Institute’s Women’s Heart Center in Santa Monica, California, said the reports present a positive framework for women’s health.

“Women have unique cardiovascular risk factors related to pregnancy and menopause, and certain traditional risk factors, including diabetes, may increase their risk of heart disease more significantly than they do in men,” noted Nissi Suppogu, MD, a cardiologist and medical director of the Women’s Heart Center at MemorialCare Heart & Vascular Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California.

“Recognizing these differences is essential to identifying women at risk earlier, improving prevention strategies and ensuring they receive appropriate treatment,” she said.

“I am incredibly excited to see these new reports on women’s heart health,” added Svati Shah, MD, the president-elect of the AHA, as well as the associate dean of Translational Research and the director of Precision Genomics Collaboratory in the Duke School of Medicine in North Carolina.

“They are a call to action for increasing education, research, and coordinated clinical care for women’s heart health,” Shah said.

The organizations spelled out their recommendations in three reports.

The first report is a presidential advisory that lays out a “female-specific life course approach to the prevention and management of cardiovascular disease.”

In it, the authors state that “girls and women uniquely experience fragmented care” across medical fields, including pediatrics, obstetrics, and gynecology.

The authors call for a “unified life course approach that addresses the female-specific interconnectedness of hormonal, psychosocial, and cardiovascular health.”

This report breaks down the life cycle for girls and women into five stages.

“Emerging data suggest that unique biologic transitions or life stages experienced by girls and women are linked with changes in [cardiovascular disease] risk, which may be due in part to physiologic changes in sex hormones,” the authors wrote.

Weinberg said this life-stage breakdown is important, especially for risk reduction.

She said issues with menstruation during the teen years may be a harbinger of heart health problems later in life. The same is true for health issues during pregnancy, especially high blood pressure or high blood sugar levels.

“We need to be aware of these heart disease risks so we’re not caught by surprise 10 to 20 years down the road,” Weinberg said.

Suppogu agreed. “Medical professionals should emphasize that heart health is a lifelong priority and prevention should begin early. Encourage girls and women to establish healthy habits, understand their cardiovascular risk factors, and maintain regular checkups, even when they feel healthy.”

“Women’s heart health has a unique constellation of risk factors and manifestations that are shaped by biological transitions that vary across a woman’s lifetime,” said Shah.

The second report is a science advisory that recommends a comprehensive plan for women’s cardiovascular care.

In it, the authors call for a system that “integrates assessment of hormonal, psychosocial, and cardiovascular health across life stages.”

They propose a “national infrastructure” of centers that would coordinate clinical care, education, training, research, and community engagement around women’s heart health.

“These centers can reduce fragmentation, enhance earlier risk identification, and expand opportunities to increase representation of women in clinical research,” the authors wrote. “In doing so, they offer a bold opportunity for a scalable and equitable model to improve cardiovascular health for all women.”

To illustrate their point, the authors noted that only about 60% of women with hypertensive disorders during pregnancy in the United States have a follow-up visit with a healthcare professional about their condition within the first 6 months after giving birth.

“Given that pregnancy is a cardiovascular stress test and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are risk-enhancing factors for long-term [cardiovascular disease] risk, this represents a major missed opportunity to address cardiovascular risk,” the authors wrote.

“Pregnancy and the postpartum period are important windows into cardiovascular health throughout the life course,” said William Grobman, MD, the vice-chair of the writing group for these reports and a professor of obstetrics and gynecology at The Warren Alpert Medical School of Brown University in Providence, Rhode Island, in the press release.

“An adverse pregnancy outcome can unmask increased cardiovascular risk, and that information should guide care well beyond pregnancy,” he continued.

The third report is a scientific statement that suggests a “consensus guideline for the female-specific prevention and management of cardiovascular disease.”

In it, the authors state that there is “growing recognition that the optimal prevention and management of cardiovascular disease in girls and women requires attention to sex-specific considerations relevant to the interconnected domains of hormonal, psychosocial, and cardiovascular health across the life course.”

The authors note several age-related warning signs of heart issues. These are spelled out in the AHA press release.

Females comprise around half the population, and their cardiovascular health “must be a part of everyday care across medicine,” said Mariell Jessup, MD, the chief science and medical officer of the American Heart Association, in the press release. “These publications give clinicians and health systems a shared foundation for recognizing risk throughout a woman’s life.”

In a scientific statement published in February 2026, the AHA projected that 60% of females in the United States will have at least one type of cardiovascular disease by 2050.

The organization also predicted that 25% of U.S. women will have diabetes and 60% will have obesity by that year if current trends continue.

Letter From the Editor-in-Chief: What a Decade of Bedtime Stories Taught Me About Health

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Bezzy communities provide meaningful connections with others living with chronic conditions. Join Bezzy on the web or mobile app.

I’ve read to my children each night at bedtime for as long as I (and they) can remember. We started with the likes of “Goodnight Batcave” and the lesser-known-in-the-US but oh-so-wonderful collection of verse-driven classics from former UK Children’s Laureate Julia Donaldson.

We moved on to B.J. Novak’s anarchic love letter to the power of words (and to great typography), “The Book With No Pictures.”

We eventually worked our way up to Roald Dahl’s wickedly dark back catalog, discovering more complex characters, language and plotting as we went.

When my eldest, Amelia, hit 6, it was time to roll out the big guns.

Over the last 5 years (she’s now 11), I’ve read the seven Harry Potter books aloud twice, complete with all the voices: once to Amelia on her own, once to both her and her brother, Henry, two years her junior.

I had feared she wouldn’t be keen on another helping, but Amelia immersed herself once more in the world of Hogwarts with no hesitation and took immense joy in watching Henry discover for himself every twist and turn on Harry’s journey.

We have done the same thing with Tolkien’s “The Hobbit” and his fantasy masterpiece “The Lord of the Rings,” and are currently midway through “The Two Towers” on our second go around.

There’s a huge amount of reciprocal joy we take from this nightly ritual, beyond the practical benefits of providing a calming demarcation between the end of the day and the wind-down to sleep.

For me, it comes as either a break in my working day or a signal that it’s over, depending on how hectic things are.

For the kids, it’s an all-too-rare chance to get me all to themselves, no deadlines, screens, or other distractions to pull me away from being anything but 100% present.

For all three of us, it’s a literal (and literary!) warm blanket — a chance to escape under the covers to familiar worlds and familiar faces. It is, in short, incredibly good for our well-being.

And yet, in the world of health information — including on this site — thousands, if not millions, of reading hours are dedicated to the benefits of nutrition, sleep, and exercise as the building blocks of good health. Reading itself rarely makes that shortlist.

For me, it unequivocally deserves a place on it. Research seems to support that case. Even if we need more of it to really understand how and why.

A recent editorial in Psychological Medicine co-authored by Barbara Sahakian, Professor of Clinical Neuropsychology at Cambridge University, draws on a broad range of research linking reading for pleasure to better mental health and greater empathy.

The positive impact of reading on young people — just like Amelia and Henry — lso carries evidence.

Among more than 10,000 US adolescents, those who started reading for pleasure early had fewer mental health problems, spent less time on screens and slept more. Although, per an earlier column of mine, I can’t vouch for the sleep side of things when it comes to early bird Henry.

For many, reading can be a form of therapy that improves well-being. Bibliotherapy is a complementary therapeutic tool for emotional release and reduced stress and anxiety, with its roots traced back to ancient civilizations.

There’s even research suggesting a link between a love of reading and a lower risk of cognitive decline.

In a 2003 study of people aged 75 and over, reading was associated with a 35% lower risk of dementia over roughly five years.

A separate 14-year study from 2022 found that people who read at least weekly saw less cognitive decline, whatever their level of education.

A happy (and healthy) by-product of this love of being read to is that both my kids have now started reading to me at bedtime. Henry recently read “Jeffy the Burglar’s Cat” to us, a lesser-known but no less compelling chapter book by Ursula Moray Williams that was a staple of my own childhood. And we’ve enjoyed several books from Amelia, including the wistful tale of friendship and the circle of life, “Charlotte’s Web” by E.B. White – not a dry eye in the house for the final few chapters of that one.

They’ve also gone off and re-read the Harry Potter books and “The Hobbit” to themselves.

The research above links reading to many health benefits: better attention and memory, fewer mental health problems, more sleep, less time on screens, and greater empathy. I haven’t measured any of those impacts empirically in our house. Nor am I naive to the fact that as they get older, they’ll ultimately grow out of me reading to them, with school, extracurricular activities, their friends, and inevitably screens competing for more and more of their time.

Come what may, though, I know that a child who considers missing a reading session as the most cardinal of cardinal sins, and who scrambles for the chance to read aloud to their dad at bedtime is, at that moment, concentrating, empathizing, discovering and growing.

Researchers are clear that studies espousing the benefits of reading show association rather than proof, and it’s evident that more experimental work and deeper research are needed to ensure the complex cause and effect stands on firm scientific ground.

Regardless, it’s a healthy habit Amelia, Henry and I are very happy to keep, and with a bit of luck, I’ll get to pass it on to their kids one day.

Best Buy is holding a midnight launch for the upcoming Zelda game and its associated Nintendo Switch 2 system and Pro Controller tonight, on Nov. 4. Fans who already preordered but want to get in on the fun can have their goods shipped to a local store for pickup during the midnight launch. Fans who haven't yet secured the foil cover edition or its hardware accessories have good reason to show up as well. The Best Buy website says that a limited number of consoles and Zelda controllers will be available for purchase in store.

Fans have taken that warning to heart, it seems. Social media pictures of stores across the United States show that people are lining up hours, if not days, in anticipation of the new Nintendo console. Some onlookers have assumed the lines must be for Pokémon cards releasing on Friday, or for Black Friday deals. Most likely, the lines are for the hot products that are imminently releasing, not ones that are days away. In some cases, Best Buy locations have two lines: one for pre-orders, and one for people who don't have one.

Stock will vary by location, but early estimates show that stores are receiving somewhere between 10 and 20 Switch 2 units. Given the lines (and the high resale prices), those units are bound to sell out quicker than physical game copies or accessories like controllers. Elsewhere, fans are being told that stores initially listed as midnight release locations won't actually be getting any Switch 2 stock on Wednesday night. The pre-orders aren't canceled — the affected stores just won't be selling them early.

Though Best Buy hasn't hosted any recent midnight launches, the rising popularity of Pokémon cards means many customers are already used to lining up outside the store ahead of hot releases. Fans should double-check that their local store is participating and assess line lengths at least a few hours before midnight. That way, folks who have struck out online won't waste time at stores that are already packed with customers.

Best Buy won't have any electric ocarina instruments to purchase on Nov. 5, which is a shame since the accessory is sold out on Nintendo's website. Fans shouldn't lose hope, though.
