14 TV shows canceled or ending in 2026, from Palm Royale to Ridiculousness

14 of the 26 series on TV Insider's list of 2026 cancellations and endings, from Palm Royale to The Steve Wilkos Show.

Most viewers picture a cancellation as a sudden decision, made in the middle of a season, that takes a show off the air before anyone sees it coming. Many of this year's endings don't fit that picture. CBS announced in March 2025 that The Neighborhood would get one last season, according to Variety. The CW announced a final season for All American in June 2025, according to TheWrap. Both networks gave their shows a full season to close their stories, so anyone who counts them as 2026 cancellations is looking at decisions made in 2025. For these shows, 2026 is the year the goodbye airs, not the year the network decided. Their fans had months of notice.

Other cancellations turn on more than a show's own numbers. Networks weigh each series against the new shows they want to launch, and a network can't add a show without dropping another. "We aggregate all the numbers, and we have to make those tough decisions in order to make room for new shows," CBS Entertainment President Amy Reisenbach said, as reported by The Washington Times. Ratings still count in that calculation, but so do the network's plans for the next season. A show that ends is not always a show that failed, and a weak show is not always the only one at risk. The single word "canceled" covers endings with very different causes, and readers learn more by asking what pushed each show out than by counting the losses.

TV Insider set out to catalog the series that were canceled or are ending during 2026, from Ridiculousness and The Late Show with Stephen Colbert to The Way Home and Palm Royale. The catalog counts endings of every kind, so scheduled farewells appear alongside abrupt cuts. That mix is worth keeping in view, because a comedy in its debut season and a staple that has aired for decades end up under the same heading for very different reasons. The slides that follow take each ending on its own terms. They look at what it cost the network to keep the show, what the network wanted in its place, and whether the audience ever had a say.

Apple $AAPL TV ended Palm Royale early, even though the comedy had drawn plenty of Emmy attention. The streamer gave the series only two seasons, according to TV Insider. Its first season received 11 Emmy nominations, 9to5Mac reports, and its one win went to its theme music. The show was also nominated for Outstanding Comedy Series, but that nomination did not bring it a trophy. Palm Royale's single award honored its opening credits rather than its writing or its cast.

Online ratings put Palm Royale squarely in the middle, with a 60% average on Rotten Tomatoes and a score of 6.8 out of 10 on IMDb, according to 9to5Mac. Those scores show mild approval but little excitement, and they fall well short of the enthusiasm that surrounded Apple's comedy hits, such as Ted Lasso and The Studio. The second season ended in January, and Apple chose not to order a third. Fans who finished the latest episodes now know that those episodes were the last.

Viewers are left to weigh the nominations against the lukewarm ratings on their own. Together they describe a comedy that impressed Emmy voters but never won over audiences to the same degree.

Anne Rice's Talamasca: The Secret Order is a short, finished show that a viewer can watch from start to end in a weekend. AMC gave the series one season, according to TV Insider. That season ran six episodes and ended with a Nov. 23 finale, The Hollywood Reporter reports, and news of the cancellation came out on March 28, 2026. The show counts as a 2026 cancellation because word of its end arrived that year, not because it aired then. Viewers who finished the season in November learned months later that no more episodes were coming. Those six episodes are all there will be.

AMC is cutting a spinoff, not walking away from Anne Rice. A canceled spinoff can look like a network losing faith in a property, but AMC's main Anne Rice series, Interview With the Vampire, returns under its new title, The Vampire Lestat, for a third season June 7 on AMC and AMC+, according to The Hollywood Reporter. Belonging to the same Anne Rice franchise didn't save Talamasca. AMC is committed to the franchise, not to every show in it. The network does leave room for parts of the series to return, and it said in a statement that it expects "at least some of these characters, and the organization itself" to appear "in future expressions of the franchise." That promise covers pieces of Talamasca, not a second season. Fans who want more of the secret society will have to look to AMC's other Anne Rice shows.

CBS decided DMV's future while the comedy's first season was still on the air. The network ended the show after a single season, TV Insider reports, and the cancellation became public on March 27, 2026, according to TVLine. CBS made that call with only part of a first season to judge, and it chose not to wait for the rest before passing on a second. DMV had little time to settle into its time slot.

Viewers tend to read a fast cancellation as a sign that nobody was watching, but DMV had a large audience. The comedy averaged 5.7 million viewers, according to The Washington Times, which means millions of people were making time for a first-year show that CBS still dropped. An audience that size would count as a success on many schedules, yet it did not earn DMV a second season on CBS. On this network, a new comedy needed more than millions of weekly viewers to return. A shortage of viewers is hard to name as the reason for the decision.

The speed of the decision also shapes what fans get to see. TVLine reports that DMV's last episode will serve as the series finale. Fans will get one season and no more, and the episode that closes that season now has to stand as the end of the show. Viewers who are still watching DMV this year know where the series stops before they reach it.

The Late Show with Stephen Colbert was a late-night staple on CBS for 11 seasons before the network canceled it, according to TV Insider. Colbert was not leaving a fading show. When his final episode aired in May, no other late-night talk show on broadcast TV drew better ratings, according to the Chicago Sun-Times. CBS did not end the show for lack of an audience, and that makes the network's stated reason worth testing.

CBS called the cancellation "purely a financial decision," as reported by the Chicago Sun-Times. The word "purely" leaves no room for any other motive. A show can lead its time slot and still cost a network more than it earns, so strong ratings alone can't prove CBS wrong. The network's choice of replacement shows what its money case means. Comics Unleashed took over the 10:35 p.m. hour, and its host, Byron Allen, makes payments to CBS for the right to broadcast it. The network gave up a show it paid for and took on one that pays the network. On those terms the financial explanation holds together, but it doesn't show that money was the only consideration.

The doubts about CBS's reasoning come from the calendar. Colbert publicly described the $16 million that CBS paid Trump to settle a lawsuit as a bribe, and news of the cancellation followed just two days later, according to the Chicago Sun-Times. A gap that short invites people to read the cancellation as retaliation. Paramount $PARA Global's merger with Skydance Media was awaiting federal approval at the time. That gave the company a reason to avoid friction with the Trump administration, though it doesn't prove a political motive.

CBS's financial case and the timing can both be true, and neither the ratings nor the Comics Unleashed deal settles which one drove the decision.

CBS decided to end The Neighborhood while viewers were still watching it in large numbers. The network kept the comedy on its schedule for eight seasons, according to TV Insider, and viewers stayed with it through that long run. In its seventh season, the show was averaging 6.4 million viewers an episode by CBS's 35-day multiplatform count, according to Variety. With an audience that large, the reason for the ending has to lie somewhere other than the ratings.

CBS's own leadership praised the show's audience in public. "’The Neighborhood’ is a strong ratings performer for CBS and one of the top comedies on TV," Amy Reisenbach, president of CBS Entertainment, told Variety. Yet The Neighborhood was missing from the group of nine shows that CBS brought back in February 2025. A network that praises a show's ratings and then passes on it at renewal time is weighing something other than demand.

The reported reason is money. Variety reports that sources pointed to the cost of continuing the show as the main factor. Those sources are unnamed, and CBS's own announcement said nothing about cost. If the cost explanation holds, the show's popularity did not protect it. Its strong ratings earned The Neighborhood a last season, not a longer life.

CBS may not be done with the show's characters. A spinoff centered on the roles Marcel Spears and Sheaun McKinney play is in development at the network, according to Variety, but CBS has not given it a formal go-ahead. Fans should treat the project as a possibility rather than a promise. If it moves forward, viewers who followed the original show for eight seasons will have a reason to stay with CBS after the finale.

CBS gave Watson only two seasons, according to TV Insider. The Washington Times reports that CBS confirmed the end of the Morris Chestnut drama in late March 2026, several weeks after its second season returned on March 1. The network did not give the show a farewell season. It ended the series a few weeks into the second season's return, before fans had settled back into the new episodes.

Watson's audience over the full season was large by any ordinary measure. The drama averaged 6.4 million viewers in the 2025-26 season and ranked No. 74 among the season's 100 most-watched shows, according to Variety figures reported by The Washington Times. Most viewers would call a show with that many viewers a hit, and few would expect a network to cancel it after its second year.

CBS judges a show against the rest of its own schedule, and its standard is steep. Amy Reisenbach, CBS Entertainment President, told The Washington Times that "it's a high bar on CBS." A drama can draw a large audience by industry standards and still fall short on CBS's lineup. A place among the season's 100 most-watched shows did not clear the bar the network sets. CBS measured Watson against its own programs, not against television as a whole.

For Watson's fans, the show's healthy season numbers offered no protection on CBS.

All-American lasted through years of upheaval at the CW. The network gave the drama eight seasons, according to TV Insider. In 2022, shortly before Nexstar's deal for the network went through, the CW dropped 10 of its original series, TheWrap reports, and that was triple the number it cancels in a typical year. A cut that deep shows a network reshaping its lineup, and All-American stayed on the schedule for years afterward.

Viewers were not leaving All-American when the CW decided to end it. They were arriving. The drama finished its seventh season with a 71% larger audience of adults ages 18 to 49 than it had when the season began, according to TheWrap. Networks most want to reach those younger viewers. The CW answered that growth with a farewell season rather than a longer run, which suggests the network set the end point for some reason other than the size of the audience.

The CW also chose not to extend the franchise beyond the original series. It canceled the spinoff All American: Homecoming, TheWrap reports, so the main show ends without a sister series to carry its world forward. Nothing in the drama's ratings points to a show the CW had to drop. The ending looks more like a decision about the network's own direction than a verdict on All-American, though the CW's exact reasons remain its own. Fans get a final season to see the story through, which is more than a show in decline usually receives.

Strong reviews were not enough to keep Going Dutch on the air. Fox canceled the comedy after only two seasons, according to TV Insider. Of all the scripted shows on Fox, Going Dutch alone faced real doubt about a return, ScreenRant reports, and Fox settled that doubt with a cancellation. Fox had cast Denis Leary in the lead, but a familiar star could not draw the audience the show needed to last.

Critics and viewers reached very different conclusions about Going Dutch. Reviewers gave it an 85% critics score on Rotten Tomatoes, which means most of them recommended it, according to ScreenRant. Viewers judged it far more harshly, and their audience score of 54% counts as rotten on the site. Fox's decision matched the audience's negative verdict rather than the reviewers' praise. A network has to fill its time slots with viewers, and a show cannot stay on the air on good notices alone.

The cancellation shows how few people watched Going Dutch, and it says little about the quality of the show. Fans who enjoyed it have no reason to treat Fox's decision as a judgment against their taste. Anyone curious about the comedy that critics recommended can still watch it. Fox will not make a third season, but Hulu streams the second, according to ScreenRant.

The Hallmark Channel is ending The Way Home after four seasons, according to TV Insider, and that is a long run for a drama that began at another company. The Arizona Daily Star reports that the series was originally developed at Netflix $NFLX before it came to Hallmark. A project that started elsewhere became a Hallmark series that lasted for years, so its ending closes a show that found a home rather than one that never caught on.

The series reached Hallmark because one executive was willing to vouch for it. Lisa Hamilton Daly brought the show to the network, the Arizona Daily Star reports, and her support carried real risk for a channel with firm habits. Andie MacDowell, the show's star, said the series "was a reach for Hallmark. It sort of broke down some boundaries they had and really took a chance on something a little bit juicier." MacDowell has worked with Hallmark for seven seasons, so her judgment comes from someone who knows the network's limits from the inside.

The Way Home has never tried to settle every mystery it raises. Executive producer Alexandra Clarke has described a series that answers some of its questions and leaves others open, according to the Arizona Daily Star. Viewers heading into the last episodes may want to keep that in mind, because the show's makers have treated open questions as part of the story from the start.

MTV's cancellation of Ridiculousness ends production of the clip show while keeping it on the air. The network has built a deep archive of the series, airing 46 seasons, according to TV Insider. KTLA reports that the show premiered on Aug. 29, 2011, and MTV produced that entire run of seasons in the years since. That stockpile is why Ridiculousness carries a 2026 end date at all. MTV stopped production in 2025, and episodes it had already finished will air to close out the 2026 season. The network made its decision in 2025, and it will run out of new episodes to air in 2026. MTV is spending down a backlog of episodes made before production stopped, rather than presenting a final season written as a goodbye.

Ridiculousness will remain available after MTV airs its last first-run episode. Viewers can keep streaming select seasons on Paramount $PARA+, according to KTLA, so fans can keep watching on demand long after the show leaves MTV's lineup of new programming. For a viewer, this cancellation means no new episodes. It does not take away the episodes MTV already made, and a fan who missed years of the show still has a large catalog to work through.

MTV is winding Ridiculousness down rather than erasing it.

Law & Order: Organized Crime ended in stages rather than in one sudden cut. TV Insider counts it as a five-season NBC series, yet its final season never aired on NBC. That season streamed on Peacock instead, according to Variety. NBC moved the show off its broadcast schedule before it announced any cancellation. Viewers saw the last episodes through streaming rather than on broadcast TV.

After that last season, the show spent most of a year neither returning nor officially ending. Variety reports that the cancellation came out on April 16, 2026, about ten months after the final season ended. By then the news confirmed the fate of a show that was already off the air. It did not cut one that was still running. The move to Peacock turned out to be the show's last stop, and the April announcement was a formality for fans who had long since watched the final episode.

The cancellation ends the spinoff, while the older series where its lead character began keeps going. Christopher Meloni played Elliot Stabler, according to Variety, a character who first appeared on Law & Order: Special Victims Unit before getting a series of his own. Special Victims Unit is in its 27th season, so the series that introduced Stabler has outlasted the one built around him. Fans of the character still have that show on the air.

NBC's crime franchise goes on without this branch.

Access Hollywood is the kind of program viewers expect to find on their TV schedule indefinitely, a fixture of the daily lineup rather than a series anyone watches for signs of an ending. Viewers have had 30 seasons of the show to settle into that habit, according to TV Insider. In the summer of 2025, the show's future still looked secure. CNBC reports that as of July 2025, Access Hollywood was set to continue its broadcasts. Nothing public explains when that outlook changed or why.

The easy explanation is that NBCUniversal is clearing out its older entertainment-news shows and Access Hollywood was simply next, but the most recent cut points the other way. When the end of E! News was announced in July 2025, both Access Hollywood and its spinoff Access Daily were slated to keep broadcasting, according to CNBC. That decision spared the Access shows rather than starting their decline. The two brands are also headed to different corporate homes. E! is moving to Versant, the Comcast $CMCSA spinoff, while Access Hollywood is listed under NBCUniversal. With the two endings on opposite sides of Comcast's split, tying Access Hollywood to the fate of E! News would mean drawing a link that the record doesn't show.

Without that borrowed explanation, what remains is a three-decade run ending with no public reason, and no cause or final air date accompanies the news. For a show on the air this long, that silence leaves viewers with nothing to weigh.

The fair response is to hold off on a verdict rather than borrow one.

Karamo is ending as an established show, not as a new series that never found its footing. It aired for four seasons, according to TV Insider, well past the point where a network has to decide whether a new show deserves a second year. A series reaches a fourth season only if someone chooses to bring it back after each of the earlier ones. Keeping a show on the air that long takes a fresh decision to continue at the end of every season, not one early bet that is then forgotten.

The length of that run changes what the ending means for viewers. The people who watched Karamo are losing a show that had been part of their schedule for years. They aren't losing a pilot they might have skipped or a debut they barely had time to sample. For them, the cancellation breaks a routine, and that kind of loss is harder to shrug off than the end of a show that came and went within a few months.

Karamo is an NBCUniversal show, and its cancellation comes this far into its run. An ending at that stage concerns where the show could go next, not whether it ever worked. Four seasons had already answered that question.

Karamo goes off the air as a show that made it.

Some of this year's endings came before a show had a chance to settle in, but The Steve Wilkos Show is ending after a run that most series never reach. The talk show was on the air for 19 seasons, according to TV Insider. That is a long career for any program on television. This cancellation closes a show with years of episodes behind it, not one that disappeared after a brief tryout. Fans who watched it lose a program that had been on the schedule for a long time. They are not losing a debut they barely had time to sample, and the show's full run is already on the record.

The Steve Wilkos Show belongs to NBCUniversal's group of programs. That tells viewers which company the show sat under, but it does not tell them why the show is ending. The length of the run does not explain the timing either. A show can last for many seasons and still end for reasons that have little to do with its record. Cost, ratings and plans for the time slot all remain open questions, and none of them should be filled in by guesswork. Until a reason is made public, the one certain fact about the cancellation is how long the show lasted.

The cancellation does not shorten that run. The show lasted, and few TV shows can say the same.

SmartMLS Introduces New Ownership Verification Tool to Help Combat Seller-Impersonation and Land Scams

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

With security and insurance symbols prominently featured, the property house cautioned against falling victim to scammers and fraud.

Connecticut’s SmartMLS has announced that it will introduce a new Ownership Verification tool from Property Shield designed to help real estate professionals combat seller-impersonation and vacant-land scams.

According to a release, the new technology, expected to become available to SmartMLS next week, provides an additional way for real estate professionals to verify the identity of someone claiming to own a property before accepting a listing.

Seller-impersonation fraud typically begins when a scammer poses as a property owner and contacts a real estate professional seeking to list and sell property the scammer does not own. These schemes can be particularly difficult to identify when the property is vacant land or when the purported seller communicates remotely.

“Seller-impersonation scams can be difficult to identify.” said Kathy Elson, CEO of SmartMLS. “We want to give our users a tool to help verify a prospective seller’s identity and identify a potential problem as early in the listing process as possible.”

An additional step when something doesn’t look right

Property Shield’s Ownership Verification tool is designed to help determine whether the individual claiming to be the property owner is who they say they are.

The verification process uses identity verification and facial-recognition technology in conjunction with property ownership information to provide an additional layer of protection when questions arise about the identity of a prospective seller.

Rather than relying solely on documents, emails, telephone conversations, or information supplied by the prospective seller, real estate professionals will have a tool available to help evaluate whether the person attempting to list the property is legitimate.

“Real estate professionals are often on the front line when seller-impersonation fraud is attempted,” Elson said. “The challenge is that a sophisticated scammer may have enough information about the property and its owner to appear legitimate. Ownership Verification gives our users another way to ask an important question before proceeding: Is this really the property owner?”

Helping stop fraud before a listing enters the MLS

Ownership Verification complements the Listing Fraud Protection technology SmartMLS has already introduced through its partnership with Property Shield.

The two technologies address fraud at different points.

Listing Fraud Protection helps identify when legitimate SmartMLS listing information is copied and used in potentially fraudulent advertisements across the web.

Ownership Verification is designed to help identify potential seller impersonation before a fraudulent listing enters the MLS.

Together, the tools provide SmartMLS users with additional ways to identify and respond to real estate fraud.

“Seller impersonation and vacant-land fraud are difficult problems because the person committing the fraud may never meet the agent face-to-face,” said Alexander Fahsel, CEO of Property Shield. “Ownership Verification gives real estate professionals another layer of information to help determine whether the person they are dealing with is actually connected to the property they are attempting to sell.”

Technology supporting professional judgment

Ownership Verification is intended to provide an additional fraud-prevention tool, not replace the judgment and due diligence of the real estate professional.

A successful verification should not eliminate other appropriate steps when circumstances surrounding a prospective listing raise concerns. Similarly, an issue identified during verification can provide an important signal that additional investigation may be warranted before proceeding with the listing.

“Our goal isn’t to replace the due diligence brokers and agents already perform,” Elson said. “It’s to give them a better tool when they need it. If something about a prospective seller or listing doesn’t add up, we want our users to have another way to investigate before moving forward.”

For more information, visit smartmls.com and propertyshield.co.

Hera spacecraft photographs asteroid from 37 miles away

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 6, 2026 3:19 PM EST

In September 2022, NASA’s Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) spacecraft smacked into a cosmic rock named Dimorphos at a speed of around 14,000 miles per hour. But mission engineers weren’t crestfallen by the news—they were thrilled. That’s because DART was specifically designed for this head-on asteroid collision. Since then, researchers around the world have poured over data from the multimillion dollar, car-sized craft’s smashing success to find ways of improving planetary defenses in the event that Earth ever finds itself in the crosshairs of a rock like Dimorphos.

In the meantime, the European Space Agency (ESA) sent its own observer, Hera, to examine the collision scene. On November 5, the ESA released the first image taken by the spacecraft. It was about 37 miles from Dimorphos’ much larger binary asteroid, Didymos. Although DART’s crash site is hidden in the shadow of the roughly 2,559-foot-wide rock, the perspective is allowing astronomers to begin examining how debris from a direct strike affects not only the primary asteroid, but any nearby objects in its vicinity.

“The first thing we said was, ‘Wow!’” Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel said in a statement. “This is the other side of Didymos from the one that DART’s camera showed us shortly before impact, so this feels like looking at a whole new world.”

Although Didymos exhibits the same central equatorial ridge visible from DART’s earlier views, the surface initially appears to be extremely different from its moonlet asteroid, Dimorphos. Because barely half a mile separates the two objects, astronomers are curious to see how much of Didymos’ features come from debris impacts.

“We have exciting work ahead of us to sort it out,” added mission scientist Michael Kueppers. “We look forward to seeing much more of both bodies and systemically mapping all the changes we identify.”

The ESA plans to release even more detailed photos of both asteroids on November 25.

Stock market hours and futures trading hours: 10 timing answers

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

What time does the stock market open, and when do futures trade? 10 answers on U.S. stock hours, extended sessions, futures schedules, holidays and overseas hours

The opening bell at 9:30 a.m. Eastern is the best-known time on Wall Street, but it is not when trading begins. By the time the bell rings at the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq $NDAQ, prices have often been moving for hours. Some of that activity happens in extended sessions for stocks. Much of it happens in futures contracts that trade nearly 24 hours a day, five days a week.

That is why a simple question — what time does the stock market open — has more than one useful answer. The regular session for U.S. stocks runs from 9:30 a.m. to 4 p.m. Eastern, Monday through Friday. Pre-market and after-hours sessions stretch that window on either side. Each broker decides how much of that extra time its customers can reach. Stock index futures follow a separate clock. CME $CME Group's Globex platform opens the futures trading week on Sunday evening and closes it on Friday afternoon, with a short break each day.

The timing matters for practical reasons. Companies often release quarterly earnings after the closing bell. Economic data, central bank decisions and overseas events can also arrive while U.S. exchanges are closed. Trading conditions change by session, too. Fewer buyers and sellers are active outside regular hours. The gap between bid and ask prices is typically wider as a result.

The calendar adds another layer. U.S. exchanges close for a set list of holidays each year, and some sessions end early. Futures markets keep their own holiday schedules, which do not always match the stock exchanges. Time zones matter as well. A trader in California sees the opening bell at 6:30 a.m. local time. The gap between New York and London shifts for a few weeks each year because the U.S. and Europe change their clocks on different dates.

This guide covers 10 timing topics. It explains regular stock market hours, pre-market and after-hours trading, and the schedules for stock index, commodity and Treasury futures. It also explains why futures move before the open and how index futures expire. The final slides cover the days the market closes, how the open translates across U.S. time zones and when trading begins in London and other overseas markets. The regular trading session on the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq $NDAQ runs from 9:30 a.m. to 4 p.m. Eastern time, Monday through Friday. That window lasts six and a half hours. It is the schedule most people mean when they ask what time the stock market opens.

Both exchanges keep the same core hours, even though they operate in different ways. The NYSE combines a physical trading floor at 11 Wall Street in Manhattan with electronic trading. Nasdaq has no trading floor and runs entirely on electronic systems. A share of Apple $AAPL, listed on Nasdaq, and a share of Coca-Cola $KO, listed on the NYSE, both trade in the regular session on the same clock.

The trading day starts with an opening auction rather than an ordinary trade. The auction gathers buy and sell orders submitted ahead of time and matches them at a single price. Nasdaq calls this process the Opening Cross. On the NYSE, designated market makers oversee the opening auction for the stocks assigned to them. The result is an official opening price for each listed stock.

The session ends the same way. At 4 p.m., a closing auction pools the orders marked for the close and sets one closing price per stock. Nasdaq calls its version the Closing Cross. Traders who want to take part send market-on-close or limit-on-close orders before the auction runs.

Funds and index providers rely on those closing prices. U.S. mutual funds generally calculate their daily net asset value as of the 4 p.m. Eastern close. Index providers use the official closes to set end-of-day index levels. Many index funds trade in the closing auction to match the levels their benchmarks record.

The bells that mark the open and close are also public events. The NYSE rings an opening bell and a closing bell each trading day, often with executives or guests from listed companies on the podium. Nasdaq holds its own opening and closing ceremonies at its MarketSite studio in Times Square $SQ.

Trades that take place before 9:30 a.m. or after 4 p.m. fall outside the regular session. Prices from those hours do not count as the official open or close.

Pre-market trading lets investors buy and sell U.S. stocks before the regular 9:30 a.m. Eastern open. On major exchange venues, including Nasdaq $NDAQ and NYSE Arca, the session can begin as early as 4 a.m. Eastern.

That 4 a.m. start is the earliest the exchanges allow, not the time every investor can trade. Brokerage firms set their own pre-market windows, and many open later than the exchanges do. Some platforms do not offer pre-market access at all. A broker's website or trading platform lists the hours that apply to its accounts.

Pre-market orders are matched electronically rather than through the opening auction that starts the regular day. Prices can differ from one venue to another before the open. A single large order can move a stock further than it would after 9:30 a.m. A trade can also fill at a price some distance from the last quote.

Brokers limit order types for that reason. Most accept only limit orders before the open. With a limit order, the investor sets the highest price to pay or the lowest price to accept. Market orders, which fill at whatever price is available, are generally not permitted in the session. Some brokers also require an order to be marked specifically for extended hours. Their rules differ on whether an unfilled pre-market order carries into the regular session.

Scheduled economic data is one reason the early session gets attention. The Bureau of Labor Statistics releases its monthly jobs report and the consumer price index at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, an hour before the opening bell. Many companies also publish quarterly results before the market opens. Pre-market trading gives investors a way to act on that information before the regular session starts.

A stock's pre-market move is usually shown as a change from the previous day's 4 p.m. close. That figure rests on a smaller pool of trades than the regular session produces. The official opening price is set separately when the regular session begins at 9:30 a.m. Eastern. A stock can open at a different level from where it traded minutes earlier.

After-hours trading lets investors buy and sell U.S. stocks once the regular session ends at 4 p.m. Eastern. On major exchange venues, including Nasdaq $NDAQ and NYSE Arca, the extended session runs until 8 p.m. Eastern, though each broker sets its own access hours.

The session draws attention mainly because of when companies release news. Many publicly traded companies publish quarterly earnings shortly after the 4 p.m. close rather than during the trading day. That timing lets investors read the results before the next regular session begins. It also means the first market reaction to those results often plays out after hours.

An earnings release is commonly followed by a conference call with analysts, often held in the late afternoon or early evening. Remarks from executives on that call, including guidance about coming quarters, can push a stock's after-hours price further after the initial release.

Other corporate announcements also tend to arrive after the bell. These can include mergers, leadership changes and plans to sell new shares.

Price quotes need careful reading during this window. A stock's official closing price is set at the 4 p.m. close and does not change because of later trades. Financial sites and broker apps typically show after-hours moves as a separate figure next to that closing price. The next day's percentage change is measured from the official close, not from the last evening trade.

Benchmark indexes follow the regular session as well. The S&P 500 is calculated from trades during regular hours. An evening jump in one of its largest companies does not alter the index's closing level.

Access is the other practical question. Brokers that offer extended hours often require an order to be marked specifically for the extended session. The hours a broker supports may also end well before 8 p.m. Eastern. The broker's stated window and order rules determine what a customer can trade after the close.

Trading on Nasdaq and NYSE Arca then pauses until the pre-market session opens the following morning.

E-mini S&P 500 and E-mini Nasdaq $NDAQ-100 futures trade on CME $CME Group's Globex electronic platform nearly 24 hours a day, five days a week. The trading week opens Sunday at 6 p.m. Eastern and closes Friday at 5 p.m. Eastern.

From Monday through Thursday, trading pauses from 5 p.m. to 6 p.m. Eastern for a daily maintenance break. It then resumes for the next session. That leaves 23 hours of trading on a typical weekday.

The break also marks the boundary between trading days. A trade placed at 7 p.m. Eastern on a Tuesday counts toward Wednesday's session on CME's calendar. In the same way, the Sunday evening open is the start of Monday's trading day, not a separate weekend session.

CME Group is based in Chicago, and its contract pages list hours in Central time. In that format the week runs from Sunday at 5 p.m. to Friday at 4 p.m. Central. The daily pause runs from 4 p.m. to 5 p.m. Central. Both versions describe the same window.

The two contracts track different benchmarks. The E-mini S&P 500, which trades under the ticker ES, follows the S&P 500 index of large U.S. companies. The E-mini Nasdaq-100, ticker NQ, follows the 100 largest nonfinancial companies listed on Nasdaq. CME launched the E-mini S&P 500 in 1997 and the E-mini Nasdaq-100 in 1999.

Contract size is set by a multiplier on the index level. One E-mini S&P 500 contract is worth $50 times the index. One E-mini Nasdaq-100 contract is worth $20 times the index. In 2019, CME added Micro E-mini versions of both at one-tenth of those sizes. The micro contracts run on the same Globex schedule.

These hours stretch well beyond the 9:30 a.m. to 4 p.m. Eastern stock session. Index futures keep trading for one more hour after the stock market's 4 p.m. close, until the daily break begins. They reopen at 6 p.m. Eastern, well before pre-market stock trading begins on major exchange venues.

Holiday weeks follow a separate calendar published by CME Group. The hours above describe a standard trading week only.

Stock index futures keep trading after U.S. stock exchanges close, so their prices change overnight as traders respond to news. By the time the 9:30 a.m. Eastern bell rings, futures have often already absorbed hours of information that the regular stock session has not yet priced.

That gap is why morning market coverage leads with futures. Contracts such as the E-mini S&P 500 and E-mini Nasdaq $NDAQ-100 trade through the evening and night. The regular sessions of the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq are closed during those hours. A futures move before the open reflects trades by participants willing to act on events while the cash market waits.

Scheduled U.S. economic data offers a clear example. The Labor Department's monthly jobs report and the consumer price index are released at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. That is an hour before the opening bell. Futures trade straight through those releases, and their prices adjust to the numbers before the regular stock session begins.

A futures contract is priced against an index, but it is not the index itself. The S&P 500 is calculated from the prices of its component stocks. The futures contract is an agreement whose value at expiration is tied to that index level. Before expiration, the two prices can differ.

The expected difference between them is known as fair value. It accounts for two forces. Holding the stocks in the index ties up money that could otherwise earn interest. That cost pushes the futures price above the cash index. Owning the stocks also pays dividends that a futures holder does not receive. That pulls the futures price lower. When interest rates are higher than the index's dividend yield, futures typically trade above the cash index even when no news has moved prices.

A positive futures reading does not by itself signal a higher open. Pre-market commentary often compares futures with fair value rather than with the previous close. A futures price above fair value points to a higher opening for the index. A price below fair value points to a lower one.

The gap between futures and the cash index narrows as expiration approaches. With less time remaining, the interest and dividend effects shrink.

The indication is not a guarantee. The index's actual opening level comes from the first trades in its component stocks, and news that breaks just before 9:30 a.m. can change the picture. Futures show where traders are positioned overnight, not where the market must go once the bell rings.

E-mini S&P 500 and E-mini Nasdaq $NDAQ-100 futures expire four times a year, in March, June, September and December. They are cash settled, which means no shares change hands when a contract ends.

Each quarterly contract has a fixed expiration date: the third Friday of its contract month. A trader holding a June contract, for example, has a position tied to an index level set on the third Friday of June.

Final settlement uses the Special Opening Quotation of the index on the third Friday of the contract month. That figure is calculated from the opening prices of the stocks in the index on that Friday. It is not the index value displayed at 9:30 a.m. It is built stock by stock as each component opens. A stock that starts trading late still enters the calculation at its own opening price.

Trading in the expiring E-mini contract stops at 9:30 a.m. Eastern on the third Friday. After that, the contract's final value depends only on how the component stocks open.

Cash settlement avoids a practical problem. Physical delivery would require handing over every stock in the index in its exact weight. Each open position is instead marked to the final settlement price. The difference is then paid or collected in cash.

Traders who want to keep exposure past expiration do not wait for settlement. They close the expiring contract and open a position in the next quarterly contract. This step is known as rolling. It shifts trading activity from one contract month to the next in the days before expiration.

Futures tickers identify contract months with single letters. H stands for March, M for June, U for September and Z for December. A ticker such as ESZ, followed by a year digit, refers to a December E-mini S&P 500 contract.

The third Friday of each quarterly month also has a name in market slang. It is traditionally called quadruple witching. The label referred to four kinds of contracts expiring on the same day: stock index futures, stock index options, stock options and single-stock futures. Single-stock futures are no longer listed on the major U.S. exchange that offered them. The term triple witching is also widely used for the same quarterly date.

Many CME $CME Group futures, including WTI crude oil, gold and U.S. Treasury futures, trade on the Globex platform from Sunday at 6 p.m. to Friday at 5 p.m. Eastern. From Monday through Thursday, trading pauses for one hour each day, from 5 p.m. to 6 p.m. Eastern.

That schedule gives these markets 23 hours of trading in each session. The daily break marks the line between one trading session and the next. A trade placed on a Monday evening at 7 p.m. Eastern counts toward Tuesday's session, not Monday's.

The three markets sit on different CME Group exchanges. WTI crude oil futures are listed on the New York Mercantile Exchange, known as NYMEX. Gold futures are listed on COMEX. Treasury futures are listed on the Chicago Board of Trade. All three trade through the Globex electronic platform and share the same weekly window.

CME Group, which is based in Chicago, lists its trading hours in Central time. In that format, the week runs from 5 p.m. Sunday to 4 p.m. Friday Central. The daily break runs from 4 p.m. to 5 p.m. Central. Anyone comparing an exchange schedule with a broker's platform should confirm which time zone each one displays.

The long hours matter because the news that moves these contracts does not follow the New York stock clock. Oil prices respond to supply decisions by OPEC and its allies, which can be announced while U.S. exchanges are closed. Gold trades heavily in London and across Asia during the U.S. overnight hours. Treasury futures often react to U.S. economic data released at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, such as the monthly jobs report. That release lands an hour before stocks begin regular trading.

The general window does not cover every contract. Grain futures such as corn and soybeans trade shorter sessions, with a pause between the overnight and daytime segments. Individual contracts can also carry their own rules around expiring months and holidays. The contract specification page on CME Group's website lists the official hours for each product. It is the place to check before trading a specific contract.

The New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq $NDAQ close for 10 full holidays a year: New Year's Day, Martin Luther King Jr. Day, Presidents Day, Good Friday, Memorial Day, Juneteenth, Independence Day, Labor Day, Thanksgiving Day and Christmas Day. Neither exchange holds a regular session on those days.

Most of the stock market calendar follows federal holidays, but the two lists are not identical. Good Friday is not a federal holiday, yet both exchanges close for it. Columbus Day and Veterans Day work the opposite way. Federal offices and the Federal Reserve close on those dates, while stock trading runs on its normal schedule.

Juneteenth is the newest entry. It became a federal holiday in 2021, and the stock exchanges first closed for it in 2022.

Weekends change the closure dates. When a holiday falls on a Saturday, the exchanges generally close on the Friday before. When it falls on a Sunday, they close on the Monday after. New Year's Day is the usual exception to the Saturday rule. If January 1 lands on a Saturday, trading typically goes ahead on Friday, December 31, as a normal session.

A few sessions end early instead of being canceled. On those days, the regular session closes at 1 p.m. Eastern rather than 4 p.m. The day after Thanksgiving is the most consistent early close. In some years, July 3 and Christmas Eve also end at 1 p.m. Those two dates close early only in certain years, depending on where they fall in the week.

The bond market keeps a separate calendar. SIFMA, the trade association for U.S. securities firms, publishes recommended full and early closes for bond trading. That schedule does not always match the stock exchanges. SIFMA typically recommends a full bond market close on Columbus Day and Veterans Day, for example, while stocks trade normally.

Futures follow yet another schedule. CME $CME Group publishes its own holiday hours for products on its Globex platform. Those hours differ from the stock exchange calendar. On some days when NYSE and Nasdaq are shut, certain futures contracts still trade a shortened session. The exact hours vary by product and by holiday.

The specific dates shift every year. The official holiday calendars posted by NYSE, Nasdaq and CME Group are the reference to check before planning trades around a long weekend.

The U.S. stock market's 9:30 a.m. Eastern open is 8:30 a.m. Central, 7:30 a.m. Mountain and 6:30 a.m. Pacific. Those conversions hold year-round in most of the country. The Eastern, Central, Mountain and Pacific zones all change their clocks on the same dates.

The New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq $NDAQ set their schedules in New York local time. The opening bell rings at 9:30 a.m. on a Manhattan clock in January, when New York is on standard time. It also rings at 9:30 a.m. in July, when the city is on daylight time. A trader in Chicago, Denver or Los Angeles sees the same one-, two- or three-hour offset in every season.

The closing bell converts the same way. The 4 p.m. Eastern close is 3 p.m. Central, 2 p.m. Mountain and 1 p.m. Pacific. On the West Coast, the regular session runs from early morning to early afternoon.

Alaska sits one hour behind Pacific time and observes daylight saving time on the U.S. schedule. The open arrives at 5:30 a.m. in Anchorage throughout the year.

Two states break the pattern. Most of Arizona stays on Mountain Standard Time all year and does not observe daylight saving time. From March to November, Phoenix shares a clock with Los Angeles. The open arrives there at 6:30 a.m. In winter it returns to 7:30 a.m.

Hawaii also skips daylight saving time. The open lands at 3:30 a.m. in Honolulu while the mainland is on daylight time. It lands at 4:30 a.m. while the mainland is on standard time.

Extended sessions shift by the same offsets as the regular session. On major exchange venues, the post-close session runs until 8 p.m. Eastern, which is 5 p.m. Pacific. Brokers set their own extended-hours windows. Anyone trading outside regular hours from another time zone should check the broker's schedule in local time.

The U.S. moves its clocks forward one hour on the second Sunday in March. It moves them back one hour on the first Sunday in November. Many other countries change their clocks on different dates, and some never change them at all.

Britain and the European Union move their clocks on the last Sunday in March and the last Sunday in October. Those dates do not line up with the U.S. calendar. For a few weeks each spring and autumn, the gap between New York and London differs from its usual size.

During those weeks, the habitual offset is not a reliable guide. A reader tracking the London close or European economic releases should check a current time conversion before acting.

The London Stock Exchange's main trading session runs from 8 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. London time on weekdays. The Tokyo Stock Exchange trades from 9 a.m. to 3:30 p.m. Japan time, with a midday break.

London's session lasts eight and a half hours, two hours longer than the regular session on Wall Street. London uses Greenwich Mean Time in winter and British Summer Time in summer. For most of the year, that puts it five hours ahead of U.S. Eastern time. On that clock, the London open falls at 3 a.m. Eastern and the close lands at 11:30 a.m. Eastern.

That timing leaves a two-hour stretch, from 9:30 a.m. to 11:30 a.m. Eastern, when stocks trade in London and New York at the same time.

Tokyo splits its trading day into two sessions. The morning session runs from 9 a.m. to 11:30 a.m., and the afternoon session runs from 12:30 p.m. to 3:30 p.m. The exchange moved its close from 3 p.m. to 3:30 p.m. in November 2024. That change lengthened the afternoon session by 30 minutes.

Japan does not observe daylight saving time. Japan Standard Time sits nine hours ahead of Coordinated Universal Time all year. During U.S. standard time, Tokyo's open arrives at 7 p.m. Eastern the previous evening. During U.S. daylight saving time, it arrives at 8 p.m. Eastern. The Tokyo close falls at 1:30 a.m. Eastern under standard time and 2:30 a.m. Eastern under daylight saving time.

Those conversions show how the trading day passes from Asia to Europe to North America. Tokyo's session ends before London opens. A U.S. investor checking prices at breakfast is often seeing the result of a finished session in Asia and one still under way in Europe.

Neither exchange follows the U.S. holiday calendar. London closes for U.K. bank holidays. Tokyo closes for Japanese national holidays and a New Year break. Each exchange publishes its own trading calendar, which is the place to confirm hours for a specific date. Exchanges in other cities set their own sessions and midday breaks. Their official calendars are the reference for those markets.

Carrie Coon on ‘The Gilded Age’, ‘The Liberation’, and Parenting

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Carrie Coon and I are supposed to be talking about her new film, The Liberation—a gripping drama about a suburban mother who leaves her family to join an all-woman cult in the 1980s. But when we meet for tea at the London West Hollywood hotel, it doesn’t take long for our conversation to turn to general doom and gloom. Between the moment I order a custom-made ginger tea—which I’m now calling “the Carrie Coon special”—and when it arrives at our table, we’ve already started talking about fascism in America, how hard it is for an actor to find enough work to earn health insurance, global warming, and the devastating fires in Los Angeles.

“I don’t do small talk anymore. I’m not good at it,” says Coon, taking a sip from her own ginger tea—a blend she started having this restaurant make for her after getting hooked on it while filming The White Lotus in Thailand. “I’m just like, ‘So we’re all going to die. How you feeling about that?’”

But even when she’s talking fire and brimstone, Coon’s charming warmth keeps the conversation from being too dark and stormy. If Coon were leading a cult, I might join. She’s funny and empathetic; she makes this sweet, quick “aww” sound if you compliment her or share something she’s sympathetic with. Maybe Coon, who was raised in Ohio, simply can’t shed her Midwestern sensibility, even though she transforms onscreen into characters with much harder, and sometimes pricklier, outer shells. Coon has earned four Emmy nominations for playing the stubborn small-town cop Gloria Burgle in Fargo, the lonely lawyer Laurie Duffy in The White Lotus, and twice for the ambitious social climber Bertha Russell on The Gilded Age, which just debuted its fourth season on HBO.

Carrie Coon and Morgan Spector in season 2 of The Gilded Age.

She reveals a different side of herself in The Liberation, Guy Nattiv’s drama that opens in limited (and Oscar-qualifying) release November 6 before going wide January 8. Her character, Rita Cooper, is a Holocaust survivor whose trauma and stagnation in her family life cause her to abandon her husband (Tracy Letts, Coon’s real-life husband) and two daughters (Bella Ramsey and Odessa Young) for an all-female spiritual group led by a charismatic leader named Ricki (Lily James).

The film is a strong showcase for Coon as she transforms both physically and emotionally. When Rita joins the commune, she loses weight and her identity as she falls further under Ricki’s spell. “People are struck by me playing someone who feels weaker or more frail, because I’m often put into roles where I’m strong, verbal, intellectual, brassy,” says Coon. “I think it’s unusual for them to see that level of vulnerability in my performances. But of course, I think of myself as capable of all that.”

I nervously admit to Coon that when I watched The Liberation, I was troubled to find that, as a mother of two young kids, I could understand Rita’s choice—to leave behind all the demands and burdens of family and household, to run to a place where you could focus solely on yourself. It’s hard to say this out loud.

Coon makes that sympathetic “aww” sound. “We all have that moment. It’s a fantasy, but haven’t we all thought, ‘I could leave right now; what would happen if I just didn’t come back?’” she says. “That’s why I found her so compelling, because she walked away from her family and chose to invest in her self-actualization and her healing in the ’80s. It’s a really risky choice, but, I suppose, just undeniable for her.”

Coon and Letts, who married in 2013—in a hospital where Letts had just received emergency gallbladder surgery—have two kids together who are now five and eight years old, just a few years ahead of my own. Juggling life as a working mother can be challenging, especially in invisible ways. “I think a lot of the cognitive load is so heavy, and people don’t really understand,” says Coon.

Carrie Coon, Leslie Bibb, and Michelle Monaghan in The White Lotus.

When Coon was cast in the third season of HBO’s hit The White Lotus, playing a woman who goes on a luxury vacation with her two longtime best friends, she was gone for a total of 10 weeks. At the time, her daughter was only three. “That was very hard, and that was a very difficult decision,” she says. “I’m not sure it was a great decision, ultimately. I don’t know if we would do it again the same way.”

She’s had other moments of feeling spread too thin, especially just before she went to film His Three Daughters—the 2023 movie that cast Coon, Natasha Lyonne, and Elizabeth Olsen as three sisters who reunite to care for their ailing father. “I turned to Tracy and said, ‘I can’t do this anymore. I can’t do both things,’” she remembers. Maybe as her kids get older— “little kids, little problems, big kids, big problems,” she says—she will actually have to shift her focus. “In this world where we have no schema to navigate the challenges they’re facing, I would have to take a step back again and be with them,” she says. “I don’t know. It’s hard to say.”

Elizabeth Olsen and Carrie Coon in His Three Daughters.

Coon and Letts’s kids joined them for part of the time while they filmed The Liberation in Vancouver. During the first half of the movie, Coon wore hip pads and other body-shaping additions to make Rita seem more weighed down by her responsibility and the trauma of having been a child in a concentration camp. Coon says she could also lean into the exhaustion she already felt: “We were co-sleeping in a little apartment, and it was not easy,” she says. “And so I was like, ‘Great, I don’t have to look like anything. I could just be tired.’ So I kind of allowed that exhaustion I normally feel, which I try to overcome in my daily life, just to creep in more.”

When Rita leaves her family for the isolated commune, Coon’s environment also shifted: She moved to a new filming location, and her family left town. In the mornings, she’d swim or stretch. She’d also lost 10 pounds and shorn her hair for the role, transforming from Rita into Naya—the new name Ricki gives her character, a common practice in cults. “Carrie’s a very, very strong, physical actress,” says Nattiv. “Rita is the matriarch of her family. She’s a strong woman, intelligent—and then she’s not. She loses everything. It was amazing to see how she shifts between Naya and Rita.”

Nattiv based The Liberation on his grandmother Rita—who really had been in a concentration camp as a child, and joined a commune in her adult life. Rita had given testimony to Yad Vashem, the World Holocaust Remembrance Center; Coon watched it as she prepared to play the role. She also watched and listened to hours of testimonies of women who had been in concentration camps in Poland, learning “the sort of very specific way that particular trauma is held in the body, the challenge of speaking the unspeakable.”

I wondered if watching so many hours of these stories, so much horror and pain, was difficult. “Well, if I’m really honest, I found that I was drawn to listening to them instead of the news,” says Coon. “I started listening to them as a comfort, because they were a story of resilience. Thinking about the unspeakable atrocities that were happening around me in my very own country with my own tax dollars—and yet here are people on the other side of something like that, articulating those stories and what they learned.”

The present political climate is ever-present in Coon’s mind, as well as on her social media accounts. (A few of her more recent X posts: “Failed president,” “Close the camps,” “The president has nothing but ‘tranny’ jokes. How pathetic. How empty.”) For the past few weeks, she’s been encouraging people to vote, especially her fellow Ohioans. As she admitted, she’s not one for small talk.

Carrie Coon (center) with Odessa Young, Bella Ramsey, Sharon Taylor, and Tracy Letts in The Liberation.

“It’s absurd. It’s untenable,” she says. “We’re in a really precarious moment because people’s lives are untenable all across the board.” She recognizes her own privilege—that she and her husband are both able to work, that they can afford childcare—but says she’s worried for actors who can’t find roles in this shrinking economy. “Even just the labor of making tapes, the free labor, there’s no protection,” she says. “We have these rules in our union, but no one’s really following those rules. What young actor is going to make that phone call and complain?”

She’s also noticing how few female-centered films get told on the biggest stages, with the biggest budgets. “What happens when you’re living in an authoritarian regime is everybody gets scared, and everybody gets safe, and everyone’s making really safe choices. It’s not what people want,” she says. “Women are tremendous economic drivers in this economy, and we still aren’t making art for them.”

Coon will do her part, with films like The Liberation. She’s wrapped work on another independent movie, Honeymoon/Funeral, a romantic comedy from Fresh Off the Boat creator Nahnatchka Khan that also stars Rebecca Ferguson and Greta Lee. Next year, Letts heads to London from April through October to do Death of a Salesman at The National, which means more maneuvering for their family unit. “We’re thrilled for him. And yet that’s going to be a really interesting time for our family,” she says. “So I don’t have as much work lined up, and I’m okay to be patient. I feel good about being patient right now.”

But she does have one wish for the future: She’d like to do a comedy. “No one’s letting me do that. My family is surprised by my career because it’s just not the way I occurred to them as a person at all. People are expecting to meet a very serious and lugubrious actress that needs a lot of special drinks or something,” she says. While taking another sip from her custom, off-menu ginger tea.

New Laws Could Add an Estimated 103,000 Homes a Year, Study Finds

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

New state laws to encourage homebuilding could bolster the nation's home production by six figures in coming years—but there’s a small catch.

The American Enterprise Institute tracked 10 state bills passed this year and estimates they could potentially lead to 103,000 new homes a year across the country. It's positive progress to solving the nation's housing supply shortage, which is complicated by red tape.

AEI’s data, though, shows that about half of those housing units are accessory dwelling units, or ADUs. But dozens more bills and legislative efforts put forth this year could bring more progress, AEI said at a housing conference in Washinton, DC, this week.

This has been the “year of the starter home,” said Ed Pinto, co-director of AEI’s Housing Center. Thank the bipartisan 21st Century Road to Housing Act, the largest housing reform package in decades. Americans are broadly concerned about affordability, and many young people have grown pessimistic that owning a home will be attainable.

The two largest contributors are California's AB 956 and Washington state's SB 6026.

California's AB 956 loosens requirements around building ADUs. AEI thinks that could result in 48,000 new units a year. Behind that is Washington state's SB 6026, which allows homes in commercially zoned areas. AEI thinks it could mean 37,000 new homes a year.

Other big contributions come from Idaho's SB 1352, which allows homes on smaller lots and could mean 5,000 new homes a year. Kansas' SB 418 brings similar potential in that state. Maine, Virginia, and North Carolina also passed new pro-housing laws.

Tobias Peter, co-director of AEI’s housing center, noted that doesn't necessarily mean every new home will come to fruition. Other kinds of reforms could also unlock hundreds of thousands more homes a year.

At the same time, AEI released a study that found broad support for changes to housing policy. Two rounds of polls of about 5,000 people this year found about 83% support a state law allowing more affordably priced starter homes on smaller lots in new neighborhoods.

AEI found 61% said they’d rather see a torn-down home replaced with two smaller homes, compared to a larger and more expensive home. But people’s opinions trading housing development versus individual property rights can be complicated.

Two of the most popular pushbacks on housing reform fit into two categories. “Supply skeptics” don’t think more homes will improve affordability. Others oppose change to the character or scale of established neighborhoods. Bur rather than housing reform being a "YIMBY versus NIMBY" debate, AEI pictures a spectrum of reform supporters and skeptics.

Some of the largest efforts have focused on the legal red tape around where homes can be built. For instance, several states have put forth laws to allow homes on smaller lots.

But more legislation is needed to correct the slowing construction of starter homes in the nation. Freddie Mac found the U.S. built an average of 418,000 new starter homes a year in the 1970s, 34% of all new housing starts. By 2020 that fell to 65,000 starts, or less than 10%.

“You really need a local champion, a quarterback who organizes at the state level,” Peter said, noting some successful bills where the governor, lieutenant governor, or a group of legislators shepherd a bill. “And it’s also important you have the evidence in your back pocket to counter some of the narratives."

Affordability also requires local governments to respond—because zoning and building code reform, by and large, happens at the local level.

Local government controls some of the most significant levers of affordability, says Yadira Ramos-Herbert, mayor of New Rochelle, NY. And that city's updates to its zoning code have meant incremental progress over time.

New Rochelle developed a form-based code that allows developers to build with fewer requirements around building use. It also streamlined its approval process for new developments, with some taking as few as 90 days to achieve approvals.

The program has helped it approve 4,500 new homes since 2020. New Rochelle, a winner of the University of Utah's 2026 Ivory Prize for Housing Affordability, has seen its median rent decline as national averages surge.

"Our core objective is to ensure New Rochelle remains affordable, accessible, and economically resilient," Ramos-Herbert tells Realtor.com. "Affordability remains the defining challenge of our time, and cities have a responsibility to respond with urgency."
