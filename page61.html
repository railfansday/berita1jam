Adam Driver: ‘No One Was Paying Us’ to Develop ‘The Hunt for Ben Solo’ and ‘Star Wars’ Boss Said ‘We Have a 100% Approval’ to Make It, Then Disney Killed It

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Adam Driver and Steven Soderbergh didn’t get paid for the two-and-a-half years they spent developing “The Hunt for Ben Solo,” a standalone “Star Wars” movie that would’ve centered on Driver’s fan favorite character Kylo Ren/Ben Solo. The actor said on the “Talk Easy” podcast that money was not even top of mind since they were given the upmost assurance from then-Lucasfilm president Kathleen Kennedy that the movie would happen, and thus the pay of it all could be figured out once it was greenlit. “We were gonna do a Ben Solo movie and that didn’t happen. And we worked on it for a long time,” Driver said on the podcast. “Soderbergh did that for nothing, because we were given a lot of confidence. Like Kathy [Kennedy] said we have a 100% approval rate [from Disney]. We worked on it. No one’s paying us to work on it. The only reason we were working on it was we fucking loved the idea, how we wanted to do it, and I loved that character. And that was it. That was our guiding principle. All the money stuff came later. We were trying to figure out how to make it cheap so they could let us do what we wanted to do. So that was kind of a bummer.”

Driver revealed the existence of “The Hunt for Ben Solo” in an October 2025 interview with The Associated Press, explaining how Disney executives pulled the plug on the project because they couldn’t wrap their heads around the character coming back after he presumably died in 2019’s “Star Wars: The Rise of Skywalker.”

“I always was interested in doing another ‘Star Wars,’” Driver said at the time. “I had been talking about doing another one since 2021. Kathleen [Kennedy] had reached out. I always said: ‘With a great director and a great story, I’d be there in a second.’ I loved that character and loved playing him.”

In an AP interview earlier this month, Driver said it was “criminal” for Disney to scrap “The Hunt for Ben Solo” after also saying on “The Rich Eisen Show” around the same time that the project is officially dead and he has no interest in trying to revive it (“I think the ship has sailed though,” Driver said).

“For me it was: Steven Soderbergh wants to make a ‘Star Wars’ film. What are we debating?” Driver told AP. “It’s what it is. It’s criminal. Or it felt criminal. We’re all moving on. On to the next. And Steven already has something else that’s one of the best things he’s ever written.”

Driver originally told the AP in 2025 that he loved “The Hunt for Ben Solo” script, calling it the “standard” of what a “Star Wars” movie “should be” and comparing it to the “handmade and character-driven” feel of “The Empire Strikes Back.” He added that Lucasfilm execs Kennedy, Dave Filoni and Cary Beck backed the project until Disney shut it down.

“We presented the script to Lucasfilm. They loved the idea,” Driver recalled. “They totally understood our angle and why we were doing it. We took it to Bob Iger and Alan Bergman, and they said no. They didn’t see how Ben Solo was alive. And that was that.”

Kathleen Kennedy expressed her own frustration with Disney pulling the plug on the project during her Lucasfilm exit interview. Soderbergh told BK Mag soon after that he was not surprised to read about Kennedy’s annoyances.

“We were all frustrated,” Soderbergh said. “You know, that was two and a half years of free work for me and Adam and [writer] Rebecca Blunt. When Adam and I discussed him talking about it publicly, I said, ‘Look, do not editorialize or speculate about the why. Just say what happened, because all we know is what happened.’ The stated reason was, ‘We don’t think Ben Solo could be alive.’ And that was all we were told. And so there’s nothing to do about it, you know, except move on.”

“I’d kind of made the movie in my head, and just felt bad that nobody else was going to get to see it,” the director continued. “I thought the conversation was strictly going to be a practical one—where they go, what is this going to cost? And I had a really good answer for that. But it never even got to that point. It’s insane. We’re all very disappointed.”

Air New Zealand Denied All Slots For New 27-Hour Route: Here's Why

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Air New Zealand has been looking to return to the UK market for some time. The last time we saw its black-and-white livery was in March 2020, when it operated its final flight from London Heathrow Airport (LHR) to Auckland International Airport (AKL). Since then, passengers traveling between the UK and New Zealand have had to rely on connecting flights through hubs in the Middle East, Asia or Australia.

That being said, for the upcoming summer 2027 season, the New Zealand flag carrier had sought to secure slots at Heathrow. According to Airport Coordination Limited's (ACL) Initial Coordination Report for London Heathrow, the Star Alliance member requested 434 slots for the season. However, none of these were approved.

Air New Zealand had been flying to London for several decades. The airline initially served London Gatwick Airport (LGW) from Auckland, with stops in Papeete, Tahiti, and Los Angeles. In 1994, it moved its operations to Heathrow, where it continued to fly via Los Angeles. Between 2006 and 2013, the airline also offered flights via Hong Kong.

However, the Star Alliance member eventually decided to discontinue its London service due to low passenger numbers. It is unclear what routing it would have used had it secured the slots for summer 2027. Los Angeles appears to have been one possibility, as a spokesperson previously told Simple Flying that the airline would most likely operate the service via the US city using Boeing 777 aircraft.

Interestingly, Air New Zealand had also applied for slots at London Gatwick for the previous summer season and was actually successful in securing them. However, the airline never launched the flights, as it was facing aircraft availability issues at the time. Up to four of its 14 Boeing 787-9 Dreamliners were grounded simultaneously due to problems with Rolls-Royce Trent 1000 engines and maintenance delays.

These aircraft have since returned to service. Nevertheless, aircraft availability remains a concern. According to ch-aviation, Air New Zealand currently has nine 777-300ERs and 16 787-9s, although only 11 of the latter are in active service. The airline is also awaiting deliveries of three additional 787-9s and five 787-10s, with its outstanding Dreamliner orders expected to be fulfilled by 2028.

However, Boeing has faced difficulties increasing 787 production to its target of ten aircraft per month, partly due to supply-chain delays involving General Electric GEnx engines. These issues have affected deliveries to customers, including Air New Zealand, although planespotters.net data shows that the airline received two Dreamliners in October 2026.

Apart from new aircraft deliveries, the Star Alliance member is also refurbishing its existing widebody fleet. As of late October, 13 of its 14 787-9s had completed cabin upgrades, which include replacing the older inward-facing Business Premier seats with private suites, updating Economy cabins and introducing bunk-style sleeping pods on selected aircraft.

The final Dreamliner is expected to return to service before the end of the year. The airline is also scheduled to begin refurbishing its 777 fleet in March 2027. All of which are expected to undergo the upgrades by the end of 2027, meaning aircraft will need to be temporarily withdrawn from service while the work takes place.

Air New Zealand is one of the principal airlines operating in the South Pacific, with a fleet for NZ's domestic needs and ultra-long-haul routes.

The exact reason why Air New Zealand did not receive any slots has not been confirmed. However, Heathrow is one of the most slot-constrained airports in the world, and securing additional slots is difficult. According to ACL's report, airlines requested a total of 26,832 slots for summer 2027, but only 151 were allocated, just 0.6% of the requests.

Xiamen Air, which recently launched a weekly service between Xiamen and Heathrow, received most of the approved slots. However, several other airlines were unsuccessful. These included Alaska Airlines, which only began flying to Heathrow from Seattle in May and requested 434 slots for next summer. Emirates also failed to secure any of the 1,920 slots it requested, while Virgin Atlantic, Delta Air Lines, Air India and Riyadh Air were denied all 1,798, 556, 1,736 and 868 slots they sought, respectively.

US adds Megaspeed, at center of Nvidia scandal, to a trade blacklist

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Trump administration has added Megaspeed to a trade blacklist after tensions over its failure to do so raised questions. The news came the same day that Jensen Huang was reinstated as Nvidia's CEO.

The Trump administration added Megaspeed International, the firm at the heart of the Nvidia scandal, to a trade blacklist on Monday.

That means that the Singapore-based firm will not be able to obtain US technology, including semiconductors, without obtaining a license from the US government.

The news was reported by Semafor, an American news website.

The US Commerce Department had been investigating whether Megaspeed was smuggling Nvidia chips into China for more than a year, according to the Financial Times.

But tensions at the Commerce Department broke into the spotlight after Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang admitted to an extramarital affair with an executive at the Singapore-based firm earlier this month.

Politicians criticized Commerce Department Secretary Howard Lutnick for not having previously added Megaspeed to the department's so-called "Entity List" despite US intelligence warnings that the company was a possible front for smuggling sensitive AI chip technology to China.

Huang briefly lost his job over the scandal as US officials said the woman, Alice Huang, was a Chinese spy.

Jensen Huang was reinstated as CEO on Monday, in a stunning reversal after his resignation just two weeks ago.

The New York Times reported last month that US authorities had concluded that Alice Huang was a Chinese intelligence asset who passed information to Beijing while developing a close relationship with Jensen Huang.

Beijing has denied those claims and called the spy accusations "groundless."

Nvidia shares soared more than 30% after the company announced it reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO.

Some people were happy to see him back as president of Nvidia, as videos posted to social media show.

House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries, who is on track to be the next speaker of the House, called the decision to reinstate Huang "indefensible, irresponsible, and incomprehensible" and vowed Congress would reinvestigate any wrongdoing by the company and the Commerce Department.

Put out of mind the scenes of cheering you may have seen of Jensen Huang’s return to the headquarters of perhaps the world’s most important company, Nvidia, marking his personally triumphant comeback from being unanimously ousted by his board.

Get out of your thoughts any happy notions that you have reason to celebrate just because your money, like the money of so many millions of Americans and people well beyond the country’s borders, is tied up in the U.S. stock market, which soared at this surprising turn in the news after slumping badly in response to the scandals that had swirled around Huang.

What has happened in recent days, culminating in Huang’s return in the face of allegations he sustained a secret affair with a woman who was in touch with China’s spy agencies, is bad news for an economy that has grown increasingly imbalanced with the enormous growth of artificial intelligence. Far better for the U.S. economy and for everyone who has a stake in it would have been a reckoning for Nvidia that forced it to undertake the hard work of corporate reform and rebuilding trust.

Nvidia, for those who have not been paying attention, makes the most indispensable product in the world of AI: graphics processing units, a specialized type of chip, so capable and advanced that it has few real competitors, including, crucially, among well-funded Chinese companies. Nvidia, however, is much more than even this. As I have written before, it sits at the center of a highly incestuous economy of AI companies, including most of the big service providers, which it funds to keep purchasing its chips. The circular funding arrangements that Nvidia sustains with its largest customers—companies with extraordinary financial leverage and seemingly bottomless capital needs—is what keeps the AI economy’s party going. Without this, the merry-go-round of this new sector would grind to a halt and make what many thought looked like a bursting bubble in recent days seem like a minor blip.

Add to this the fact that the U.S. government and President Donald Trump have seemingly become convinced that everything and anything must be permitted with Nvidia, and with AI in general, or else China will overtake the United States in the most important technology of the near future. What this gives you is something more frightening than too big to fail: a company declared too strategically important to question or to hold to account.

The reality is that almost everything about the present picture is messed up and deeply worrisome. Let’s start with the fact that Trump, who already leads a government largely unconstrained by Congress and with wildly inflated executive powers, has taken so freely to intervening in the running of major companies. In Huang’s case, he played a central role in his reinstatement, as Monday’s news revealed.

There is nothing reassuring about this, but it gets worse. There is no reason to believe that Trump has an even remotely sophisticated grasp of the technologies at the heart of Nvidia’s business or its race against competitors. What we do know is that Trump has a weakness for very powerful and wealthy men, and Huang fits this billing. In the president’s universe, he is a winner, and Trump loves to associate himself with anyone invested with such an aura. Trump even posted an AI-generated photo of Huang intended to mirror a famous image of himself after surviving an assassination attempt, with the big difference that Huang was shown hoisting a red bra.

Trump, furthermore, is not known for a deep concern about ethics, and that is putting things gently. Where do ethics enter into this? Everywhere, from Huang’s honesty and the extent to which he was forthcoming about his affair with the suspicious Chinese national and shadowy customer, Alice Huang, as well as with at least one other woman who was his direct subordinate at Nvidia, in violation of corporate rules. Ethics are also involved in the way Huang ran his company, with a historically supine board, minimal internal controls on his behavior and oversight, and the way he promoted his family members. What matters is that nothing, inside the company or outside it, checked him. In fact, it seems likely that Trump, with his own record of serial marriages, rumored affairs, and children on the make, can relate.

But even these are mere starters. If competition with China deserves inclusion in any serious conversation about the future of AI, Trump’s treatment of Huang’s situation at Nvidia has been a running chronicle of flagrant disregard for national security. Let’s count the ways. First, when the U.S. government began to look into Alice Huang’s behavior on the suspicion that she might be a spy, it never informed Jensen Huang or gave him what would have been typical: a so-called defensive briefing so that he would know what to look out for and avoid giving away valuable trade secrets or selling his company’s most advanced chips without adequate controls preventing them from being shipped onward to China.

More broadly, historians may come to see Trump’s casual disregard for normal considerations of national security to be one of the most important and unusual hallmarks of his time in power. The examples are numerous. When shown evidence that Russia or China or some other powerful adversary is conducting secret operations against U.S. interests, Trump has often waved away concerns—in effect saying, well, we do that to them, too.

Last week offered the spectacle of Trump mugging in China with that country’s leader and Russia’s Vladimir Putin, with no hint that he had complained to them about offering targeting information to Iran that has enabled that country to attack U.S. bases in the Middle East, killing U.S. troops, as is the case with Russia, or supplying drone components to Iran, as with China. Trump went yet further, though, looking like a kid in a candy shop as he entered a room to greet an awaiting Putin and exclaiming, “He’s my friend.” Lest the message be lost on anyone, he later posted a picture of the meeting with this same message on social media.

Second, there is further reason to distrust the judgment of the U.S. government in the unfolding Nvidia saga. It lies in the vague and unforthcoming way its officials have spoken about Huang and his erstwhile Chinese mistress since their affair became public knowledge, always carefully avoiding clarity about whether or not she might have been a spy or whether his behavior damaged U.S. national security interests.

In fairness, the question of whether she was a spy involves some nuance. Alice Huang could very well have compromised U.S. national security without being a member or agent of any Chinese intelligence service in a formal sense. The lack of openness and straight language about the other question leaves little wiggle room, though. Through the Singapore-based company she worked for, Alice Huang was purchasing Nvidia’s most advanced chips, which fall under explicit U.S. export controls to China. This puts the public in a bewildering place. On the one hand, people are asked to believe that the United States must beat China in a zero-sum race toward the AI future. On the other, given the lack of candor about these Nvidia sales and the damage they may have caused to U.S. interests, we are being told, in effect, one of Trump’s favorite messages: Smile, and be happy. Lots of money was being made.

Taken together, Nvidia and Jensen Huang seem to concentrate enormous and growing risk for the entire public, as does the broader AI sector. Remember that Anthropic, the leading AI provider that went public recently, has warned of the potential power of its systems becoming so awesome as to having the potential to lead us all to an extinction-level event. And when the world’s top AI power is led by someone with such a casual disregard for safeguards, for strong governance, for ethics and for transparency, a company too important to question invites a peril that risks falling on us all.

Stalwart is now a drop-in replacement for Microsoft Exchange

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

For more than twenty years, “the mail server” in most companies meant Microsoft Exchange, and “the mail client” meant Outlook. Today we are releasing Stalwart 1.2.0, and with it Stalwart becomes a drop-in replacement for Microsoft Exchange.

Stalwart now implements the protocols that Exchange clients speak: MAPI over HTTP, Exchange Web Services (EWS), Exchange ActiveSync and Autodiscover. Outlook, Apple Mail and the mail apps on phones and tablets connect to Stalwart as they would to an Exchange server, with mail, calendars, contacts, tasks and notes in one account. Users type their email address and password, Autodiscover does the rest, and they keep working in the client they already know.

Exchange support is part of the open source Community edition, under the AGPL.

Before going into what Stalwart now does, it is worth looking at where self-hosted Exchange stands today, because it explains why so many organisations are looking for a way out.

Microsoft has not announced the end of Exchange Server. On paper, on-premises Exchange still exists. In practice, every road Microsoft is building leads to Exchange Online:

None of these steps forces anyone to move on its own. Together, they leave an Exchange administrator with a shrinking set of choices: keep running a server that will not grow, or move the organisation’s mail into Microsoft’s cloud.

For many organisations the second option is not acceptable. Email is the record of a company’s contracts, negotiations, legal advice, HR matters and customer data. Placing it with a US provider means placing it within reach of US law, including the CLOUD Act, which allows US authorities to compel American providers to hand over data they control wherever it is stored. Public administrations, healthcare providers, law firms, defence contractors and many European companies cannot accept that, or no longer want to. Data sovereignty means that you decide where your data lives, under which jurisdiction, and who can read it. A mail service in someone else’s cloud cannot offer that, and an on-premises server whose vendor is steering its customers toward that cloud offers less of it every year.

The obstacle to leaving Exchange has rarely been the server. It has been the client.

Many people have spent their whole working life in Outlook. They have years of muscle memory in it: their folder layout, their rules, their calendar views, their shared mailboxes, the way they book meeting rooms. Many organisations also have workflows built around it, such as delegated calendars managed by an assistant. Replacing Exchange with a standard IMAP and CalDAV server has always meant telling every one of those users to switch to a different mail client, or to reconfigure Outlook into a reduced IMAP mode where calendars and contacts no longer sync. For a migration project, that is often where the plan dies.

With Stalwart 1.2.0 the users do not need to change anything. The administrator moves the mailboxes and points the DNS records at Stalwart, and users connect with the same Outlook they used before.

The Exchange protocols are not a separate silo inside Stalwart. They are another door into the same accounts. A message filed in Outlook is in the same mailbox an IMAP or JMAP client sees, a meeting accepted on a phone appears in the calendar that CalDAV clients read, and a contact added in Apple Contacts shows up in the CardDAV address book. A user can keep Outlook on the office desktop, the native mail app on a phone and a JMAP webmail in the browser, all on one account.

Rules created in Outlook’s Rules and Alerts are stored on the server as mail rules, which run as Sieve when the message is delivered. They apply when Outlook is closed and whatever client the user opens next. Delegates and folder permissions set in Outlook become regular Stalwart shares, so they apply to every protocol. Shared mailboxes open automatically in Outlook from group membership, as they do with Exchange.

Not every part of an Exchange deployment has a counterpart. Stalwart does not offer public folders, archive mailboxes, mobile device management, RPC over HTTP (Outlook 2010 and earlier) or NTLM and Kerberos sign-in: clients authenticate with a password, an app password or OAuth. The Exchange documentation covers configuration, DNS and Autodiscover, each family of clients, and the differences from Exchange that administrators should know about before a migration.

One Outlook is missing from the list above: the new Outlook for Windows, the one that Windows now installs by default and that classic Outlook keeps offering through a toggle in its title bar.

The new Outlook does not work with Stalwart, and it does not work with any self-hosted server, Exchange included. It is built to work through Microsoft’s cloud. Microsoft does not support on-premises Exchange accounts in it at all.

The new Outlook does accept IMAP accounts, and that might look like a way around the problem. It is not. When an IMAP account is added to the new Outlook, the connection to the mail server is not made from the user’s computer. As heise and c’t reported in November 2023, the new Outlook sends the IMAP server name, the user name and the password to Microsoft, and Microsoft’s cloud connects to the mail server on the user’s behalf. Every message then passes through Microsoft’s infrastructure on its way to the screen. Microsoft does not only see the mail; it holds the password to the mailbox.

For an organisation that runs its own mail server to keep its data under its own control, this defeats the purpose of the whole exercise. We advise against using the new Outlook with any self-hosted email deployment, whether it runs Stalwart or anything else. Users who see the “Try the new Outlook” toggle in classic Outlook should leave it off.

The same applies on phones. Outlook for iOS and Android reach on-premises mailboxes through a Microsoft cloud service as well, which requires a hybrid Exchange Online setup. On mobile devices, the native mail, calendar and contacts apps, which connect directly over ActiveSync, are the better choice.

The obvious question is why Stalwart is adding Exchange protocols now. EWS is being removed from Exchange Online, Microsoft’s own future for Outlook is a cloud client, and classic Outlook is reportedly supported until 2029. Why build support for protocols that will be obsolete in a few years?

Because the companies that run Exchange today cannot wait a few years, and because those protocols are the bridge they need to cross.

An organisation with a few hundred Outlook users cannot replace its mail server and its mail client in the same weekend. It can replace the server, though, if nobody notices. With Stalwart speaking the Exchange protocols, the server moves first: mailboxes are migrated, DNS records change, and on Monday every user opens the same Outlook and the same phone apps they used on Friday. The organisation’s mail is back under its own control from day one, and no one’s work is interrupted to get there.

The client can then change at the pace that suits the users. Over time, as classic Outlook reaches the end of its life and the new Outlook remains tied to Microsoft’s cloud, we expect users to move gradually to third-party email clients built on open standards, and in particular on JMAP. Outlook will stop being an option for anyone who cares about where their email lives, and by then the people who used it will have had the time to choose something else without a deadline over their heads.

Exchange support is not where Stalwart is going. It is how organisations get from where they are to where Stalwart is.

Exchange compatibility is not a small piece of work. It adds about 200,000 lines of code to Stalwart, and we know that some of you would rather not carry that weight if you never plan to see an Outlook client. The migration guide for hosted services walks through the whole process, from the first test account to the cutover. With the Exchange protocols enabled on Stalwart, Outlook and the other Exchange clients then connect through Autodiscover and find the migrated mail, calendars and contacts.

Making Sense of Chaotic Supernova Ejecta With the JWST

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Finding order in the chaotic aftermath of a supernova explosion isn't easy. But the JWST has the tools to do it. Its Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI) and its Medium-resolution Spectrometer (MRS) were built for situations like this.

Massive stars play an important role in the evolution of the cosmos. They're responsible for synthesizing astronomical metals, the elements heavier than hydrogen and helium that are necessary to form everything from rocky planets to living organisms. Without massive stars, the cosmos would look much different. It would be a simple mixture of hydrogen, with a little helium mixed in, and trace amounts of lithium. Nature can't build planets or create life with that limited palette.

Astrophysicists can't see inside stars to learn more about this process, so they have to wait until they turn themselves inside out and then examine the results.

Massive stars live fast and die young, then explode as supernovae. When that happens, the explosion sends their contents, including their astronomical metals, out into the interstellar medium. Once there, it's available not only for planets and life, but for the next generation of stars.

When a supernova explodes, it leaves behind a supernova remnant, the matter ejected by the explosion in an expanding shockwave. The shockwave heats up the expanding shell, creating some of Nature's most stunning displays. The shocks can also rebound, creating an inward shock wave. It can create a real mess, but there are still patterns to be discerned. Within the exanding shell, molecules come form that tell astrophysicists what happened inside the star prior to its explosion.

One of the most well-known supernova remnants is Cassiopeia A (Cas A). It's about 11,000 light years away, and it was observed by Earthlings around 1667. Astronomers are interested in it because they can learn a lot about the exploding star that created itâthe progenitorâby analyzing its contents.

The JWST was built for just such an endeavour. In new research in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, a team of researchers used the JWST's MIRI and MRS to determine Cas A's chemical makeup. The research is "JWST Resolves Dust Emission from Individual Ejecta Clumps in Cassiopeia A: Dominant Grain Compositions Point to a Shell Merger in the Progenitor," and the lead author is Tea Temim, a research scientist from Columbia University.

"We present a study of dust emission in the supernova remnant (SNR) Cassiopeia A (Cas A) observed with the MIRI Medium-resolution Spectrometer (MRS) on board JWST," the authors write. "The unprecedented spatial resolution at mid-IR wavelengths allows us to isolate dust emission from individual arcsecond-scale clumps of supernova (SN) ejecta and circumstellar material (CSM) and identify the dominant dust species in each chemically distinct region."

Massive stars fuse different elements in layers, and that's reflected in the remnants they leave behind when they explode. Increasingly heavier elements are forged in the core, up until iron is reached. Lighter elements are fused together in the surrounding layers. These layers tell the star's history, but it all gets blown into space when the star explodes as a supernovae. To make matters more complicated, these shock waves can bounce back as reverse shocks.

The clumps in the supernova ejecta that the authors refer to are huge, as large as our Solar System. In studying these clumps with the MRS, the researchers were able to identify different dust species region by region, rather than as one mixed up jumble of spectra. They learned that the progenitor star underwent a shell merger.

âMaterial within these shells churns and circulates, which can cause the shells to merge shortly before the supernova explosion,â lead author Temim said in a press release. âSo, for example, this would be something like the oxygen- and carbon-burning shells in a star merging together and changing the structure of the star significantly. And that would then change the chemical abundances that you would see after a supernova explosion in the ejecta of a supernova remnant.â

P2 is centered on what astronomers call Cas A's "Green Monster" structure, which is the green structure near the center. It's emissions are attributed to circumstellar matter emissions on Cas A's near side, not material from Cas A's progenitor. It's likely made of mostly Mg2SiO4, which helps astronomers determine that the progenitor's dust and the CSM dust are distinct from one another.

P3 is another reverse-shocked region. It has very bright neon emissions compared to P1. That favours another shell merger, this time between a carbon shell and an oxygen shell.

Taken together, the more complete picture shows that the progenitor's shell mergers were underway prior to its explosion as a supernova.

Jensen Huang’s return is what Silicon Valley wanted—but things won’t necessarily be the same

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jensen Huang’s affair with an alleged Chinese spy led to his ouster from Nvidia. 11 days later, he’s back—the outcome Silicon Valley founders, watchers, and luminaries wanted all along.

Within hours of Huang’s original (and ignominious) exit from Nvidia earlier this month, a “Stand With Jensen” petition circulated online, demanding Huang’s return to the company he’d cofounded in 1993. Signatories included Elon Musk, Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney, Shopify CEO Tobias Lütke, Atreides Management CIO Gavin Baker, and Databricks CEO Ali Ghodsi, who wrote in the comments “Nvidia runs on Jensen! Bring him back!”

Travis Kalanick, Uber’s pugnacious cofounder and former CEO, was both a signatory and especially vocal in his support.

“Jensen is one of the great builders of our time,” Kalanick—himself ousted in 2017 from Uber amid compounding scandals—wrote in a public post on the “Stand With Jensen” site. “Liberty and civilizational progress depend on Jensen,” Kalanick declared. “He has erred, yes—to err is to be human—but I do not believe anything he has done rises to the bar of separating him from his company. Jensen deserves fairness in adjudication and we must presume innocence until that adjudication proves otherwise beyond a reasonable doubt.”

Huang’s return is a Rorschach test of sorts. Some critics see a corporate governance nightmare, while much of the wider public has memed or expressed horror at the circumstances of Huang’s initial resignation (widely described as potentially raising national security concerns). In Silicon Valley though, Huang’s ouster was an injustice that trumped any other aspects of the story . To tech, Huang remains the most indispensable of founders, a nearly god-like figure in a culture that worships “founder mode.”

So, Huang’s return, however questionable it may be, is cause for celebration. Even the very circumspect Sam Altman, who’d previously expressed cautious sympathy for Huang (and who of course, went through a very similar ordeal), tweeted on X on Monday, “Jensen!” Musk, meanwhile, tweeted: “The mistake has been corrected.” Sarah Guo, the plugged-in founder of Conviction VC, welcomed Huang back. The chorus from tech was, in its way, relatively unanimous about just how positive this news was. (The markets agreed vigorously, Nvidia closed yesterday up about 31%, after last week’s massive tech rout.) Meanwhile, for Nvidia’s board, it’s a dicey U-turn.

“Six days after NVIDIA’s board said it had no intention of reinstating Jensen Huang as CEO, he’s back—before the board’s own independent investigation has reported anything, before he answers questions from Congress next week, and with no conditions attached (or at least none that anyone outside the boardroom can see),” said Nicole Bestard, founder of strategic communications firm Quarter Horse Strategies, via email. “From a reputation standpoint, this is a huge mistake. It asks investors, employees, and the public to take the board’s judgment on faith.”

The move from here, in part, is likely to attempt to bury this, David Tepper, CEO and cofounder of Ascerta, predicts.

“It very likely means that Nvidia will be putting out their next GPU cycle (the 6000 series) much sooner than otherwise expected,” he said via email. “They will be eager to bury the news cycle with Nvidia’s continued dominance in the space versus keeping the attention on his infidelity.”

Nevertheless, things are not likely to be the same from here on out, said Umesh Padval, managing partner at Seligman Ventures.

“No one can replace the vision he has,” said Padval. “With him back, he may not go back to 100% immediately, he’ll likely be more cautious. It will still be significantly better than a few weeks back.”

Translation: We still can’t assume Huang will continue to be as aggressive an investor, operator, and guarantor as he may have been before, even with a supposed return to normalcy.

The private markets ecosystem has to stand by itself,” said Padval. “This was driven by Nvidia a lot in the past, the whole AI ecosystem. If participation is less, the venture community needs to step it up with conviction. We all need to believe in the companies we’re backing. And if we believe in the companies, [we] don’t have to wait for the signal from Nvidia.”

In This Seaside Rhode Island Family Home, the Interiors Have an Ever-Changing Color Palette Inspired by the Ocean

“It was like Frankenstein,” says the husband of the shingled postmodern house on Rhode Island’s rocky coast that he shares with his wife and their three young-adult children. Built by his father in the 1980s, the house occupies an extraordinary site overlooking the Atlantic ocean and an expansive marsh. But successive additions had left it architecturally disjointed. The Manhattan-based couple, fresh from updating a Litchfield County weekend retreat with AD PRO Directory designer James “Ford” Huniford, asked him to take another look. “We were thrilled to have him try to rethink this house for us,” says the wife, “which may have been easier in retrospect to just knock down.”

In a favorite sitting room, which Huniford reconfigured from a triangle into a rectangle, rattan Audoux-Minet armchairs and a custom sofa flanked by 1930s Kittinger pedestal tables wear cushions covered in a Colefax and Fowler chintz. The walls, clad in Scalamandré wallpaper, display 1950s ceramic plates from Italy’s Amalfi Coast, while an antique French chandelier hangs overhead. The coffee table is custom and the carpet is from Sacco. “I was impressed with [Ford’s] ability to take risks and do things that were different than he had done,” says the wife, pointing to the chintz fabric, “and we were very happy with the outcome.”

The French wicker barrel chair with a Jasper-covered cushion and a custom sofa, upholstered in Pierre Frey, complete the seating arrangement around a vintage coffee table. Layered beneath are rugs from Doris Leslie Blau and Sacco, the former a midcentury Scandinavian design. Rococo side tables each support a 1930s Camille Tharaud vase lamp, while the 1961 patinated-brass ceiling lights by Jacques Quinet are from Galerie Apaganthe.

Rather than impose a conventional beach-house aesthetic, Huniford sought to evoke the early-20th-century summer houses of New England while preserving the existing structure’s character. His references ranged from the work of architect Fred Lincoln Savage on Maine’s Mount Desert Island to Kilkare, a Victorian oceanfront estate in Wainscott, New York. New rooflines, structural bump-outs, covered porches, traditional divided-light windows, and detailing brought a sense of accumulated history, even as the house was winterized and its systems updated. Expanded interiors and improved flow were achieved with remarkably little change to the overall footprint. “To me,” says Huniford, “the luxury is in the detail of the craft.”

Garden-inspired Ferrick Mason wallpaper enlivens the jewel-box powder room, where a vintage pedestal sink is paired with a Samuel Heath faucet. Antique Italian sconces flank a vintage Italian mirror from Donzella, while a green 1970s pendant by Hans-Agne Jakobsson for Staff and a Rug & Kilim rug complete the space.

In the dining area, Carlos Mendoza chairs upholstered in a Pierre Frey fabric surround a custom table draped with an antique coarse-linen buggy blanket and topped with Vallauris woven plates and a 1960s Émile Tessier cachepot. Above hangs a 19th-century Dutch brass chandelier from Brownrigg, while Josef Albers color case studies enliven the walls. “What I love about Ford is: I was like, ‘Are you sure that we should paint the walls yellow?’” the wife recalls of Huniford’s custom paint selection. “And he’s like, ‘I don’t know. This is a whole new world for me, but yeah, I think we should.’”

Inside, the designer took his cues from the wraparound vistas and shifting natural light, particularly during the summer months, when the family spends most of its time here. “The ocean can be dark black and then it can be blue and then it can be green,” says Huniford. “I love the sea. It’s such a magical thing.” That ever-changing palette informed rooms of relaxed refinement, where the mix includes English, French, and early American furnishings, many slipcovered in upholstery washed to achieve an effortlessly lived-in quality. “They don’t like clutter, and they don’t like fussiness,” Huniford notes of his clients. “They like function, and they love form.”
