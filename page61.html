United Airlines Coming Back To A Hub It De-Hubbed Over A Decade Ago Could Be Its Biggest Domestic Bet In Years

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Wikimedia Commons, Wirestock Creators | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

United Airlines has buoyed its presence at Cleveland Hopkins International Airport (CLE) recently. Several route additions this year have helped expand the US-based legacy carrier’s footprint in Northern Ohio to its largest in over a decade. They have also left locals with ample choice of United hubs and leisure destinations, making Cleveland an ever more important part of the airline’s network.

But there is a sense of irony in the move. United largely turned its back on the airport, unceremoniously winding down operations 12 years ago. This cut what was a major hub in itself from United’s network. So with the carrier now appearing to bet on the site once again in what looks to be a full-circle moment, the question is: What has changed?

United’s move back to Cleveland is particularly noteworthy because of its historical relationship with the airport. After a merger with Continental Airlines in 2010, Cleveland became one of nine hubs for the expanded carrier, which retained United’s name. Unfortunately for the airport, this would turn out to be just one of a host of factors that would see it fall out of favor with the new, combined entity.

Not only was Cleveland the smallest of the nine hubs, but it was also relatively close to United’s existing gateways at Chicago O'Hare International Airport (ORD) and Washington Dulles International Airport (IAD), as well as another former Continental base: Newark Liberty International Airport (EWR). While these fit nicely into the larger network and remain in use as hubs by United to this day, Cleveland failed to fill a significant role in the wider operation. Not least was Chicago—a huge airport in comparison, serving a far greater market—being only 315 miles (275 km) down the road.

Behind all of this was the fallout from the 2008 financial crisis, which had hammered profitability across the industry. After United and Continental joined forces to cut costs, the unviable, loss-making Cleveland base became an easy target in the now-unified United and Continental brand’s drive towards efficiency. This came in 2014, with its de-hubbing (which was actually the second time in its history that United moved away).

The de-hubbing did not mean United left Cleveland altogether, though. Rather, the carrier drastically scaled back on departures and rerouted connecting services elsewhere. Under plans laid out at the time, United said average daily departures would be cut by around 60%. Some 72 peak-day flights serving 20 destinations nonstop were to be retained, it added, down from 200 and 61 beforehand, respectively.

These fell further with time, too. By 2025, just 30 or so United flights were departing Cleveland daily. That is, until the carrier opted to expand its offerings from the base once again this year. Las Vegas’ Harry Reid International Airport (LAS), which United last flew to from Cleveland in 2024, would be served year-round with five flights a week starting in September, an announcement said in May. A winter service, running between December and April next year, would then see Miami International Airport (MIA) reappear on United’s list of destinations from the airport for the first time since 2014.

* Myrtle Beach: 11 flights from June 4, 2027.

** San Juan: 26 flights from December 17, 2026, to January 11, 2027.

*** Sarasota: 4 flights from March 13 to April 3, 2027.

Owing to the additions, United’s winter schedule for this year going into next is set to cover 14 nonstop routes from Cleveland. This will be the most among any of the 10 passenger airlines that regularly operate out of the airport during the period. It will also leave United flying out of Cleveland almost 40 times a day in the meantime, taking capacity of the airport to its highest in over a decade. No doubt, this is in response to strong local demand.

The carrier is betting big on Cleveland's travel market with the announcement of two new nonstop routes to Miami and Las Vegas.

As Scott C. Carr, a City of Cleveland Department of Port Control assistant director, commented on the news of United’s route additions in May.

“Strong demand for leisure travel continues to create opportunities to grow service and connect Northeast Ohio travelers to destinations they want most.”

This is by no means new, however, with the airport having enjoyed progressively stronger footfall for some time now.

In fact, the airport’s own data shows a clear trend of improving traffic in almost every year since United’s large-scale departure in 2014, aside from an obvious slump during the pandemic. Though passenger numbers naturally dipped as United slashed services, it only took a few years for other airlines to move in, pick up the slack, and push the figures higher once more.

Among the flurry of airlines to begin or expand operations in response were low-cost carriers Frontier Airlines and Spirit Airlines, as well as close United rivals Delta Air Lines and American Airlines, to name a few. Simply put, demand was always there, but capturing it without losing money required a different approach than the one United had committed to thus far.

Prior to 2014, Continental — and later United following their merger — primarily used Cleveland as any other airline would: to funnel passengers originating elsewhere onto flights headed elsewhere. But such an operation is complex and can come with caveats.

A major consideration is whether what are known as banked connections can be sustained. Here, airlines effectively sort their arrivals and departures into waves so that passengers have time to disembark one flight and board another. The downside is that all aircraft involved have to remain grounded for a period to give travelers time to get through the airport, which can be costly for a carrier. Alongside this, airport infrastructure and ground crews must be in place to support sudden spikes in activity as planes come and go in waves.

Clearly, the practice was ultimately deemed unsustainable at Cleveland by United post-consolidation with Continental. Instead, a scaled-back, arguably simpler operation targeting those originating in or traveling to the area has taken its place. The bonus for United is that the fewer moving parts in a point-to-point system, compared to a hub-and-spoke model, make reacting to demand simpler — especially when the personnel and infrastructure to accommodatechanges are already in place.

Newark is the main United hub, while it has other major hubs in other airports across the country.

Indeed, United drastically cut back on services from Cleveland, but it did not fully scrap them. In fact, the airline remained its largest by passenger traffic, which gives some context into the weight of the de-hubbing decision on the airport. Either way, that meant United retained everything it needed to fly from the site — including a pilot base, cabin crew domicile, and maintenance facility.

But despite having the means in place to easily expand its schedule from Cleveland, United has found itself in a far less dominant position there today. Yes, it is still the airport’s primary carrier, but that is by no means as clear-cut as before the de-hubbing.

United accounted for a 28.84% share of Cleveland’s passengers over the year to August 2013, according to the US Bureau of Transportation Statistics. ExpressJet was the airport’s next-largest operator at the time, with a 14.60% share. Come the 12 months to August this year, United’s share was a more moderate 23.77%, while the gap to the second-largest - now Frontier with 21.47% - had narrowed drastically.

In other words, competition has heated up, and a string of nonstop routes passengers can choose from in Cleveland are serviced by more than one carrier — hence the risk United has had to accept in moving back in. But another factor may have contributed to the decision.

Spirit took the axe to its network prior to its collapse this year. This involved closing its base in Cleveland, where it had historically held a near 10% market share and from which it had even flown to the likes of Miami and Las Vegas. How much that has influenced United’s decision-making is debatable. Regardless, it has no doubt left a gap to be filled.

5 Funniest ‘Far Side’ Comics About the Meaning of Life

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

If I were forced to find the meaning of life in a daily newspaper comic strip, the first ongoing series I’d probably consult would be Calvin and Hobbes. Despite the fact that Calvin was just a six-year-old, Bill Watterson infused the boy with a deep thoughtfulness and insight that sometimes wrestled with the biggest questions about life, including its very meaning.

My second choice would probably be Peanuts, as Charles Schulz captured that slice-of-life realism better than anyone else. His characters, while not quite as philosophical as Calvin, would sometimes stumble onto something brilliant in the same way that Charlie Brown’s puny tree could somehow perfectly embody the spirit of the Christmas holiday.

I most certainly would not consult The Far Side.

Gary Larson’s single-panel comic strip was no less brilliant than those aforementioned comics, and Larson was no less a genius than​​​​​​​ Watterson or Schulz, but pondering life’s big questions is just not what the guy did. Gary Larson was a gag man, a joke maker, a humorist, not a philosopher. His humor was incredibly dark and maybe even nihilistic at times. The Far Side was smart, often subtle, but not deep and certainly not the kind of outlet where you’ll discern the meaning of existence. It’d be like reading Garfield in the hopes of finding healthy eating habits.

Still, having already located the deeper, meaning-of-life-type comics done by Watterson and Schulz, I thought it'd be an interesting exercise to attempt the same feat with The Far Side. The results only confirmed what I already thought — and loved — about Gary Larson’s humor.

To find Larson’s views on the meaning of life, I thought it would make sense to check out the comics where he depicts God, as there are a number of them. This one pretty much seems to sum up the seriousness Larson has towards the Almighty. His “Acts of God” depicts God putting on performances like a third-rate vaudeville star, doing things like juggling and getting shot out of a cannon. If you compare this with Linus’ sermons from A Charlie Brown Christmas, you’ve got two very different comic strip creators there.

In this comic strip, Larson depicts God at his computer, watching a man walk under a piano, as he’s about to tap the “smite” button on his keyboard. While the previous entry depicted God as a desperate entertainer, this one somehow seems worse as it portrays God as a disconnected, indifferent deity who deals out death with frightening casualness.

And this comic strip takes it to yet another level, going from indifference to outright cruelty, with God adding jerks to the world “just to make it interesting.” While I don’t think the meaning of life can be discovered in this strip, it does make you wonder why are there so many jerks in the world? And if God does exist, why did he create so many jerks?

This one, I think, gets more at what Larson actually thinks about life more than his depictions of God do. It shows two ants sitting on a mushroom, looking up at a starry night sky. One of the ants says to the other, “Just look at those stars tonight. … Makes you feel sort of small and insignificant."

The way I read this joke has a couple of different levels to it. First, I think the surface-level joke is, “haha, they’re ants, their lives are meaningless and they are small.” However, I also wonder if the ants are supposed to represent us. In other words, we’re​​​​​​​ the insignificant little ants staring up at an indifferent universe above.

Maybe there’s more depth to Larson than I gave him credit for.

The way most Far Side comics worked was to have the image work as the setup to the joke and the punchline was the caption below. Because of that, this comic appears to have no punchline at all. It’s just two scientists staring at a complex equation on a chalkboard, with one scientist saying,​​​​​​​ “No doubt about it, Ellington — we’ve mathematically expressed the purpose of the universe. God, how I love the thrill of scientific discovery!”

That isn’t funny and it isn’t the punchline.

This is one of those Far Side comics where you have to go looking for the joke, and I’m convinced the joke is that this complex equation ends in “=0.” Zero, of course, means nothing​​​​​​​, which I’m pretty sure is Larson’s way of saying that there is no purpose​​​​​​​ to the universe and that existence is meaningless — which certainly is a possibility.

Millwall have helped Vincent Tan dodge £2.1m Cardiff City transfer bullet

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cardiff City haven't been the busiest side in the EFL in the transfer market over the past few seasons, so need their new signings to hit the ground immediately. Therefore, they'll be happy that Millwall beat them to the signing of Yanis Issoufou.

The Bluebirds only brought in four new faces to the Cardiff City Stadium this summer, and even then, of the four, only two of them were actual new players who hadn't made their debuts for the club yet.

One of them, Nathan Trott, had a loan move made permanent, and another, Callum Scanlon, was signed on loan again from Liverpool after spending the second half of last season in South Wales.

That summer window followed the one last season where only Trott, Gabriel Osho and Omari Kellyman were signed in Brian Barry-Murphy's first window at the club. An admirable tactic to focus on promoting youth, but it puts the onus on those few who do sign to make an instant impact.

One player who Cardiff were linked with this past summer who didn't end up signing for them was 19-year-old Yanis Issoufou, who had broken through at Montpellier after their relegation to the French second tier last season.

He spent the entire first half of the season with the club's second team, appearing as a substitute in the early rounds of the Coup De France, but in 2026, Zoumana Camara introduced him more into the first-team fold.

Issoufou started 11 league games in the second half of the campaign, scoring two and assisting two. His breakout performance came in the Coup De France Round of 32, where he scored and assisted in a 4-0 win over Ligue 1 side Metz.

He was raw but exciting, and has plenty of potential, the kind of player that Cardiff boss Brian Barry-Murphy excels at getting the best out of. However, their reported pursuit was in vain, as Issoufou ended up moving to Millwall.

Millwall, a side that has excelled in recent times at plucking young, promising European talents and cultivating them into top Championship stars, secured the services of Issoufou for €2.5 million, equal to £2.1m.

The former French Under-19 international spent his entire career so far in his native country, coming through at Nimes Olympique before moving to Montpellier. This is his first venture into English football, and he hasn't yet settled in.

Issoufou has played just 105 Championship minutes so far for Millwall, with all five of his appearances coming off the bench. He's failed to make a matchday squad in four of the last five league games.

There is good reason for that, though. Millwall boss Alex Neil spoke in late October about how the 20-year-old came over to England as a teenager with limited English and how he's still getting used to football and life in another country.

In the meantime, Issoufou is getting reps in with the club's Under-21s side, while helping him communicate better off the field and get up to speed with the intensity of Championship football.

Vincent Tan hasn't been the most benevolent owner in recent years at Cardiff, and the Bluebirds haven't ranked atop the player sales charts recently either, barring Mark McGuinness' move to Luton Town in the summer of 2024.

Cardiff boss Brian Barry-Murphy did express some disappointment post-window at not being able to sign a winger or a striker on deadline day, stating that there were players they wanted to bring in, but it wasn't possible.

Ultimately, with marquee summer arrival Jack Moylan only arriving from Lincoln City for an initial £3 million, there's potential that the Bluebirds simply couldn't afford to spend £2.1 million on a winger who may not be ready to play regularly until January, or perhaps even next season.

Millwall, as a club, are in a position where they can afford to take time with some of their new signings, given the personnel that they have alongside Issoufou in the squad.

Right now, it looks like Cardiff are better off for not spending a seven-figure fee on Issoufou, but that doesn't mean they won't have any transfer envy in the years to come.

It's hard to really gauge the 20-year-old's ability right now, with just one shot, one chance created, and seven progressive carries in 105 minutes of football, but from his time at Montpellier, it's clear there's a player in there.

Matthew Briggs became an instant pub quiz question in his very fist game in senior football.

By coming off the bench against Middlesbrough in May 2007, the Fulham left-back set a new Premier League record as its youngest-ever player.

Briggs inherited the distinction from Izzy Brown and would give it up to Harvey Elliott 12 years later, before Ethan Nwaneri set the mark at 15 years, 181 days in 2022.

But as Briggs told FourFourTwo: "Being the Premier League’s youngest player was one of the greatest moments and achievements of my life, but it came with a lot of pressure.

"When I eventually lost the title to Harvey Elliott after 12 long years - the longest anyone has had it - it felt like the pressure had been lifted and I could just be me.

"People had always been wondering why I wasn’t doing this and that, why I wasn’t achieving what they believed I should have been achieving.

"It’s a lot of noise to deal with. I have sympathy for Harvey Elliott now, too.

Could being bored save your creative career?

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A couple of weeks ago, it was my 43rd birthday, and I spent a good few days doing absolutely nothing. My wife, Kate, knows me really well, and freed up my long weekend so I could mooch around as I pleased.

I went for long walks by myself, we went for short walks together, we ate, danced, cuddled on the sofa a little with our son . . . you know, the good stuff. A younger version of me might have thought that sounded incredibly boring.

These days, it’s exactly how I want to spend my time.

And it’s got me thinking about that b-word: boredom.

I believe this particular b-word has a bad reputation. Especially in the world of business, freelance, and entrepreneurship, where we are continuously being encouraged to hustle and fill all of our waking time with moneymaking activities. Keep learning, keep posting, just so you can keep the lights on.

Today, we’re surrounded by more creative tools, access to inspiration, and potential new opportunities than at any other point in history, but I wonder whether all this stimulation is coming at a cost.

And the more I think about this, the more I wonder whether we are becoming so good at distracting ourselves that we’re forgetting how to think for ourselves. 

Have we become allergic to doing nothing?

I often find myself in random little moments trying to fill the empty space.

Waiting for a bus? Pick up your phone.

Standing in a queue? Pick up your phone.

Going to the bathroom? Best take your phone.

As a species of humans, we’ve become incredibly good at filling every available second with . . . something. And it’s not all our fault. We have these little machines with incredibly distracting toys on them that have been built to make distraction convenient.

Perhaps more so than many others, the creative industry is among the most susceptible to the stimulation machine. Jobs and roles rely on us being inspired and on top of trends and other fluffery.

We design and create for others, and need to check and develop with devices and humans in mind. I don’t know the stats, but I would hazard a guess that due to our industry, we tend to use the tools, apps, and devices more on a daily basis than anyone else. I would hate to think how long I personally spend looking at a screen of one sort or another every day.

But something happens when you spend your time looking at a device that continuously shares the thoughts and opinions of others. You start to think the same, envy what you don’t have yet, and struggle to think for yourself. 

Over my 20 years in this industry, I’ve learned that the most important part of being a designer isn’t how quickly I can make something, although the bros on the internet seem rather adamant that I’m soon to be cooked.

It’s the deliberate nature of how I think about what I’m making.

Thinking requires something we’re becoming increasingly uncomfortable with.

Silence.

What did our brains do before smartphones?

When I work, I often sit in silence.

When I drive, I often sit in silence. 

When I go for a walk, sometimes for four, five, or even eight hours, I like silence. And when I’m on a train, I sit and stare out of the window, usually looking at all the other people looking at their phones.

I am an exciting chap, I know.

But I find these quiet moments immensely valuable.

A few years ago, I came across the work of Harvard professor Arthur C. Brooks, who got me intrigued about what happens when we give our brains a little extra room to do their own thing.

There’s some fascinating, geeky neuroscience behind this stuff that I’ve enjoyed learning about.

Scientists have identified something called the default mode network, or DMN. It’s a collection of interconnected brain regions associated with internally directed thinking—you know, remembering things, imagining possible futures, reflecting on your past experiences, daydreaming, and all that stuff you do in the shower.

Sounds a little like Narnia, doesn’t it?

Interestingly, the DMN can only switch on when you’re not focused on external tasks.

A 2024 study published in the journal Brain provided further evidence that this network plays an important role in creative thinking. Researchers recorded brain activity during spontaneous and divergent thinking tasks, finding evidence that the default mode network contributes to creative idea generation.

Now, that doesn’t mean sitting around doing nothing will magically turn you into a creative genius. If only that were true. But it does suggest that some of the mental processes involved in creativity happen when we allow our attention to move away from the task directly in front of us.

Maybe there is a weird little irony in the fact that sometimes the best way to think about something is to stop actively thinking about it.

Maybe there is actual science behind ‘I get my ideas in the shower’

Surely we have all heard someone say this at least once. I know Albert Einstein was famous for figuring out complex problems on the long walks he took daily.

Monotonous tasks like washing the dishes, walking the dog, doing the laundry, or even mowing the lawn have all sparked brilliant business ideas for people. So while doing a little more digging, I learned about a study involving something called creative incubation.

Participants in the study were given a set of creative problems. Then they were given different activities to do before returning to those problems.

A funny thing occurred.

The people who took a break to do something simple came back with better ideas. They even outperformed those who had taken a full-on rest or spent their break doing something more demanding. The researchers concluded that giving our minds a chance to wander could actually help us think more creatively.

Also, Stanford researchers have found that walking increases creative output by an average of 60% compared with sitting. The meaningless movement or task is what allows our DMN to perform for us. Essentially, when we wander our minds are free to wonder.

Perhaps doing nothing is exactly what we need to do

I think there’s something here for all of us, somewhat hidden among the shower thoughts and hum of boredom. Whether we’re designers, artists, entrepreneurs, business owners, or whatever we choose to call ourselves, we now participate in a world that’s continuously telling us to do more, share more, make more, and be more productive. It’s conditioned us to see every empty moment as another opportunity to fill our brains with stuff.

But perhaps it’s a trap.

Maybe the long walks without headphones, the train journeys without our smartphones, and the quiet weekends with our families are exactly what we need more of. The nothingness. The meandering. The pensive stimulation of being ever-present in any particular moment.

In our rush to be productive, we’re actually limiting our brain’s capacity; we’re getting in the way of it doing its much-needed “hoovering”: to daydream, reflect, question things, and imagine what could be one day.

Now, I am no creative genius, but I wonder whether the lack of originality we see in the creative industry today has something to do with how much time we spend consuming other people’s thoughts, on a little black device, rather than giving ourselves the opportunity to develop our own.

Maybe boredom will help you think about your next move.

Maybe it will help you reflect on how awesome you actually are.

And maybe, just maybe, that next long walk in silence will change your life.

InfoQ Homepage News NetWasm 0.10 Speeds Up C# on WebAssembly, Now Faster Than Mono AOT

Nov 19, 2026 3 min read

The team behind NetWasm has published a write-up of its 0.10 optimisation work, spanning releases 0.10.1 to 0.10.8. The changes touch exception handling, array bounds checks, WebAssembly SIMD and integer division. On the project’s own benchmark set, NetWasm now comes out ahead of Mono AOT and gets close to desktop .NET on some workloads.

NetWasm is a compiler and runtime that compiles C# programs ahead of time into small, standalone WebAssembly files. It takes the IL that Roslyn produces and emits Wasm, using its own runtime and a CoreLib ported from .NET. The yardstick is the Mono AOT build for WASI, which runs .NET on the same engine. A new garbage collector had already put NetWasm in front on allocation-heavy code, but plain loops were still slower: a quicksort of 100,000 integers took 77.2 ms, against 58.0 ms for Mono AOT.

Much of the early gain came from exception handling in 0.10.1. Wasmtime adds extra register save-and-restore code around any function that can throw or catch, floating-point registers included. Methods with null, bounds or checked-arithmetic checks paid that price on every call. Throws now go through a shared function and the catching handler releases the frames, which cut UI churn from 17.6 to 7.9 ms.

Version 0.10.2 inlines small straight-line methods in loops, tuple swaps included, and skips checks already performed on the same array and index. The quicksort fell to 54.0 ms. DoomSharp, a C# software renderer for Doom that ships a NetWasm build, served as a second test case. Value-type accessors now compile to plain field loads, and at O3 small branching helpers are inlined inside loops. Together these brought a 120-frame render from 3,595 down to 1,934 ms.

SIMD arrived in 0.10.7. Vector128, Vector and PackedSimd lower to 128-bit Wasm instructions, and element-wise array loops handle four or more elements per iteration, with a scalar fallback. Hoisting loop-invariant Math calls allowed a solver for Burgers’ equation, a fundamental partial differential equation from fluid mechanics, to vectorise: its first test dropped from 497 ms to 71 ms, against 186 ms for Mono AOT and 93 ms for desktop .NET.

Version 0.10.8 targets division and range-checked arithmetic. A remainder that follows a division now reuses the quotient and skips repeated zero-divisor and int.MinValue / -1 checks. A trial-division test fell from 1,376 to 765 ms, while Mono AOT needed 862 ms.

Across the 2,213 cases, 0.10.8 at O3 with NetWasmGC takes 0.41 times Mono AOT’s time as a geometric mean, excluding five cases where Mono’s LLVM removes the work. Against desktop .NET 11 RC3 JIT the ratio is 0.87, down from 2.15 for 0.10.6. By family, the ratio to Mono AOT runs from 0.34 for intrinsics to 0.81 for math. The 120-frame DoomSharp render takes 1,623 ms on NetWasmGC, compared with 2,371 ms for Mono AOT and 1,053 ms for desktop .NET JIT.

Those figures come from Wasmtime 49.0.3 on an Apple M1 Max, using cases from dotnet/performance plus five NetWasm workloads. The authors also found that identical loops at different addresses could differ by up to 15 per cent, so their A/B tests align every function to 4 KiB.

The article is open about what is left. Desktop .NET is about 3.1 times faster on vector benchmarks, because it can use host SIMD directly while Wasm is limited to fixed 128-bit operations. Math.Pow is 1.4 times slower than on Mono AOT, and CopyBlock64 is 3.3 times slower. One workload, NewtR, regressed to 1.9 times slower than in 0.10.6, which the authors blame on Wasmtime’s register allocator keeping a value in a stack slot around the loop.

Since the numbers are self-reported and come from one machine and one runtime, results elsewhere may differ. NetWasm can be tried in the online playground, and local setup is covered in the compile and publish guide. Developers can find the documentation is in the official GitHub repository.
