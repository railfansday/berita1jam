Most small shops I talk to in Sri Lanka don't need an "app". They need three boring things to work: customers can find them, customers can order without a phone call that gets missed, and the owner knows what sold and what's left at the end of the day.

You can get all three without a big upfront bill. Here's the order I'd do it in, with free options first.

Before a website, before anything, claim your Google Business Profile. Google says it plainly: "creating a Business Profile and listing your business on Google is free."

Then ask your regular customers for a review. Don't buy reviews and don't write fake ones. A few honest ones are worth more.

Your customers are already on WhatsApp. The mistake is running a business from a personal account where orders get buried between family chats.

Every department generates valuable data, but that information often lives in separate systems.

The procurement manager has one report. The warehouse manager maintains another spreadsheet. Finance uses a separate application, while sales data is stored somewhere else.

When management asks a simple question—How much inventory do we actually have, which suppliers are delivering late, and where are we spending the most money?—getting a reliable answer becomes difficult.

I never once mentioned the term 'repo rate' except in response to your confusing source. I'll just lay it out one more time for future reference of people looking at the thread.

The index call price is per B-S formula N(d1)*S*e^-(d*t)-N(d2)*K*e^-(r*t)
'r' is often called 'the risk free rate' but is actually the rate at which the 'market clearing participant' can finance the physical stock basket of stocks using them as collateral. So it's somewhat specific to that underlying (the SPX in this case) and market. It doesn't need to be equal to what individual investors are (naturally) used to thinking of as the 'risk free rate', the T-bill yield, nor somewhat equivalent rates they're less familiar with (again for good reason) like the SOFR swap rate. 'r' for box spread 'is what it is' and we just need to evaluate it if we're borrowing (no credit risk to us) compared to other alternatives, also considering tax (the 'interest' will manifest as a Section 1256 mark to market capital loss, again for SPX options, 60% long term/40% short term regardless of holding period, plus whatever state tax). If instead we're investing via box spread, we need to consider the actual credit risk we're taking (risk of collapse of the broker dealer *and* the options exchange) v how much higher the rate is than T-bill (again after tax, T-bill ordinary income Fed but no state tax).

'd' is the dividend yield of the index.

The word 'repo' hasn't appeared in this or my other direct explanations and needn't. Again your source IMO is inherently confusing in adopting this term as a general monicker for the rate discounting the underlying, because the term 'repo rate' much more often refers to the rate at which *US treasuries* can be financed using those treasuries as collateral which has nothing directly to do with prices of SPX box spreads. And in the SPX case again, the discount rate attached to 'S' is the *dividend yield*, there's no reason to call it by any other more general name.

The risk-free rate associated with the dollar value of the options spread is in the term that contains rf only, and represents the financing rate of dollars borrowed or invested.

EUGENE — One of Oregon football's most important defensive players is done for the season.

Ducks senior defensive end Teitum Tuioti suffered a season-ending knee injury in Oregon's 41-28 win over Ohio State, Dan Lanning announced on Monday.

"Teitum has been unbelievable for us this year, and every year he's been here," Lanning said. "Just watching him grow as a player and person has been a lot of fun for us. But unfortunately Teitum's done for the year, he had a knee injury, he'll end up having surgery."

Tuioti was among the Ducks' leaders in tackles with 44, and ranked second on the team in sacks with 5.5 on the season. The former Sheldon High School star sits at No. 8 all-time for Oregon with 22.5 sacks across his four seasons, providing steady leadership on a talented and experienced defensive front.

In the win over the Buckeyes, he also showed off his arm: Tuioti completed a pass to fellow defensive end Matayo Uiagalelei on a memorable fake punt which shifted momentum in the game.

"I hate it for him, because (the injury) was a routine, weird play that it happened on," Lanning said. "He left it on the line there on the field, and I don't know if he'll get another chance to play as an Oregon football player, but whoever gets to have that guy on their team in the future is gonna be really, really lucky."

Should Tuioti forgo his fifth year of eligibility and enter the NFL Draft, he is a projected second- or third-round pick according to some mock drafts.

Rocky’s Best Line Is Still the Greatest Quote in Franchise History

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Sylvester Stallone’s 1976 Oscar-winner Rocky carved itself into Hollywood history thanks to its timeless tale of underdog spirit, and a catalog of memorable moments. Stallone’s script was filled with lines that are quoted to this day – the triumphant cry of “Adrian!," Mickey (Burgess Meredith) declaring, “You’re gonna eat lightnin’ and you’re gonna crap thunder!” or Apollo Creed (Carl Weathers) joking “Apollo Creed versus the Italian Stallion. Sounds like a damn monster movie.” There’s one quote, however, that stands out among the rest, one that sums up everything about Rocky Balboa, and the franchise that tells his story, is all about. It’s not said in the ring, or on top of the famous steps, but in one of the boxer’s most private moments.

The scene in question comes the night before the climactic fight against Creed. Rocky (Stallone) walks around the arena, then back home to his girlfriend Adrian (Talia Shire). Sitting on the edge of the bed, he quietly confesses that he can’t beat Apollo, before adding: “It really don't matter if I lose this fight. It really don't matter if this guy opens my head, either. 'Cause all I wanna do is go the distance. Nobody's ever gone the distance with Creed, and if I can go that distance, you see, and that bell rings, and I'm still standin', I'm gonna know for the first time in my life, see, that I weren't just another bum from the neighborhood.”

At that one moment, The Italian Stallion’s journey comes into perspective. He is a fighter from the street, going up against the heavyweight champion of the world. The odds are against him, and even he can’t see himself knocking out a man who is at the peak of a sport when he had only ever been a manager. However, there’s a way to win even if he loses. That speech becomes the key question of the movie, and every sequel that came after it: can you go the distance?

Anthony Ippolito's uncanny performance and a heartwarming script make this an unmissable film.

Rocky isn’t the story of the preparation for a boxing match. It is the story of personal triumph, of one man who seemed like he didn’t have a future, whose own trainer called his choices “a waste of life,” betting on himself to go toe-to-toe with the best in the world. It’s about becoming more than you thought you could be, pushing the limits of what people expect of you, and shocking the world not by winning or losing, but by going the distance.

It’s a mindset that has prevailed in the six Rocky movies. Rocky II, arriving in 1979, saw the boxer strive to prove the first fight wasn’t a fluke. The third film saw him made complacent by money and fame, having to recapture his underdog spirit when he comes up against a fighter as hungry as he used to be. The fourth film deals with personal grief and revenge, while the fifth sees Rocky turning away from his life’s work in order to be a father and husband. Even his final fight, in 2006’s Rocky Balboa, is less about stepping into the ring than it is proving he still has something to give. Whether on the top of the mountain or haunted by the past, going the distance was always the goal.

It’s easy to assume every Rocky movie ends in glorious victory, but some of the key moments in the franchise are moral victories where someone else’s hand is raised. The first film sees Apollo win on a points victory, with the press and fans surrounding the champion, while Rocky and Adrian embrace each other as if they had won, because the underdog went the distance. Rocky III ends with a victory, but it is defined by a loss, as Rocky underestimates Clubber Lang (Mr T.) and the resulting defeat reignites the spark. Rocky IV sees Apollo die in the ring, a devastating end to a defining relationship, while Rocky Balboa comes full circle as, now in his 60s, he still finds victory in defeat, going all 12 rounds with a much younger champion.

While he stood across formidable men in the ring, Rocky’s most formidable opponent was always himself. Going the distance meant overcoming flaws and demons and becoming who he knew he could be. In the first two Creed movies, where Rocky becomes the trainer of Adonis Creed (Michael B Jordan), the son of his old rival-turned-friend, he imparts that same wisdom onto his protégé.
