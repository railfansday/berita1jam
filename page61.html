To deploy an AI-built app to your own server, you do five things: get the code into a Git repository, find out whether it is static files or a server, decide where its backend lives, build it into a container image with the right environment variables, and run that image behind a reverse proxy that serves your domain over HTTPS. Lovable, Bolt.new, Replit, v0 and Claude Code all take this path; what differs is the stack each one produces and the backend it leans on.

Every builder fact below comes from the builder's own documentation, checked on 11 November 2026. The Dockerfiles and the Caddy and Docker Compose files in the builder guides were built and run in Docker the same day, on Node.js 22, against fresh apps from the Vite, TanStack Start and Next.js starters and a small Express and PostgreSQL app.

Every builder hosts what it builds: Lovable publishes to lovable.app with custom domains and a managed backend, Bolt has Bolt Cloud hosting with .bolt.host addresses, Replit publishes to its own Google Cloud infrastructure, and v0 publishes to Vercel. Electric Vehicles Now Make Up Nearly 1/3rd of Global New Car Sales: Study

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The conversion of the automotive industry over to electric vehicles, sold as a foregone conclusion by many car brands in the early 2020s, may have largely stalled out in America, but the picture is far different globally. According to data from the Commonwealth Bank of Australia, EVs now account for 31 percent of all passenger vehicle sales.

And the global increase came despite falling sales in China. The major driver of electric vehicle sales has been Europe; European EV sales have risen 37 percent year over year so far in 2026, climbing to 43 percent of the new vehicle sales market. CommBank data also showed substantial growth rates in countries such as Brazil (EV sales up 164 percent, to a 13 percent market share) and India (EV sales up 76 percent, to a 9 percent market share). Even in Japan, where manufacturers have been notably EV-reticent, sales jumped 80 percent year-over-year to 5 percent of the market.

Meanwhile, electric vehicle sales have declined in the United States this year. According to Cox Automotive data, EV sales made up 6.4 percent of the new car market in Q3 of 2026, down from more than 10 percent in Q3 of 2025. Fuel prices have been elevated this year, but that has led Americans to gravitate toward hybrids rather than fully electric vehicles. Total electrified vehicles now account for nearly 25 percent of the American market.

Much of the decline has been a direct result of Trump administration policies. Last year, the U.S. eliminated a $7500 federal tax credit for electric cars, which—due to a commercial vehicle loophole—made them especially cheap for buyers to lease; the 10 percent share of sales in Q3 2025 came from a glut of sales as buyers rushed to take advantage of the tax credits before they expired. Congress is also considering imposing an annual federal tax on electric cars.

The Trump administration has also effectively eliminated enforcement of America’s CAFE standards, which would have effectively forced manufacturers to sell EVs to avoid hefty fines. American laws and tariffs also effectively prohibit importing affordable Chinese-made EVs, which have been a major reason behind the sales increase in Europe.

SpaceX Just Raised $32 Billion for AI Chips. Lenders Want Elon Musk to Keep Them on Earth.

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

SpaceX had to pay a yield above 8% on the biggest piece of a $32 billion bond sale to buy Nvidia chips.

By Johnny Rice – Nov 20, 2026 at 12:28PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Space Exploration Technologies ›

Space Exploration Technologies Corp. (SPCX -7.02%) sold $32 billion of bonds through a trust set up to buy Nvidia (NVDA -6.81%) chips on Thursday, with the largest piece paying investors a yield above 8%.

And, according to reporting from The Financial Times, the lenders involved wanted guarantees that SpaceX would keep the financed chips on terra firma, apparently worried about the logistics of repossessing chips circling the Earth at about 17,500 mph.

The stock was down 6.7% as of 11:39 a.m. ET on Friday as the whole market sees red.

The bonds are part of a more than $40 billion financing package SpaceX announced in October. To try to keep the debt off its books, the financing runs through a separate entity -- a trust that sells the bonds, buys the chips, and leases them to SpaceX. Similar maneuvers are increasingly common across the AI data center market as companies look for ways to deal with ballooning debt balances.

Functionally, however, SpaceX is still the one borrowing, and its quarterly lease payments are what pay the bondholders back.

The largest piece, about $25.2 billion, was priced at a yield of 8.259%. The average yield on high-grade U.S. corporate debt was 5.95% that day, so investors are being paid about 2.3 percentage points more than usual for bonds that are supposed to be relatively safe. Bonds considered "junk" were yielding an average of 8.3% that day.

The loans are secured by the chips in the same way your mortgage is secured by your home. If SpaceX ever defaulted, the lenders' fallback would be to repossess the GPUs and sell them. That's obviously quite difficult if they're in space. It's not clear if the lenders were able to get anything binding in the final documents, but SpaceX says these chips are for its "terrestrial compute build-out."

The chips, destined for SpaceX data centers, are expected to start arriving in January. They're part of the massive build-out SpaceX's AI division is in the thick of, which has driven enormous growth on its top-line. The AI unit nearly tripled its sales between Q2 and Q3, growing revenue from $2.6 billion to $7.6 billion. Costs are high, however: the business spent $16.4 billion in capex in its Q3 -- the lion's share of the company's overall $19.6 billion capex spend.

While the terms are less favorable than past deals, SpaceX got the money it was seeking. CFO Bret Johnsen said in the company's October financing announcement that SpaceX is seeing "less than one year payback periods" on its infrastructure investments.

But I'm still skeptical long-term. Debt market investors are incredibly sophisticated, and watching spreads -- the gap between what a U.S. Treasury pays and what the bond yields -- widen for SpaceX doesn't do much to convince me that the economics make sense. Right now, the payoff looks incredible. I don't think that's going to hold up.

Meerkats cross the desert together, but what happens when one finds itself alone?

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Researchers tracked 20 meerkats in the Kalahari Desert to see how they navigated familiar territory after being separated from their groups.

Meerkats leave their burrows together each morning and forage as a group. How well can one find its way alone?

In South Africa’s Kalahari Desert, researchers carried 20 adult meerkats up to 0.9 miles from where they caught them.

After release, 18 reached a familiar spot or found their group. Fourteen headed first to the burrow where the group had slept.

The team found no clear advantage for older or more experienced animals.

The researchers suggest that daily group travel may give meerkats enough experience to navigate alone.

The study was led by Mathilde Martin of the University of Zurich. The team worked alongside the Kalahari Meerkat Project, which follows about 15 wild groups accustomed to people.

The project is run from the Kalahari Research Centre, which is known as the KRC.

“At the KRC, our work with meerkats allows us to distract them with a water bottle and fit their collar in a few seconds while they stand upright drinking; a mere formality for them,” Martin said.

A GPS tag on each collar logged the position of the animal once every second. A radio transmitter was also fitted, along with a small audio recorder for the meerkat’s calls.

The team tested a total of 20 adults from five groups between January and March 2025. Fifteen of these animals were male, whereas only five of them were female.

Each meerkat was caught while its group was foraging and was released up to 0.9 miles (1.5 kilometers) away.

Such a distance is greater than the ground usually covered by a meerkat group in a whole day.

The release sites were located inside the territory of each group. The team chose areas that the group had not visited for at least 30 days.

Homing experiments of this kind have previously been carried out on honeybees and desert ants. Highly social animals that travel in tight groups have rarely been tested in the same way.

This is because the knowledge of one animal is difficult to separate from the decisions of the group.

Eighteen of the 20 meerkats left the release site. The sleeping burrow, the capture spot or the group itself was reached by all 18 of them.

Some of the meerkats departed almost at once, whereas others circled the release site for nearly 90 minutes.

The area covered by them ranged from half an acre to almost 17 acres (2,050 to 68,200 square meters).

A meerkat rarely strayed from its course once a direction had been chosen. Sixteen of the animals aimed for a fixed place.

The sleeping burrow was visited first by 14 of those 16.

On average, the straight-line distance to the target amounted to 82% of the distance actually walked by the meerkat.

The release areas had not been visited by the group for a month. The team therefore concluded that the meerkats had been navigating from long-term memory.

Two males never left the release site, and they were carried back to their groups after 90 minutes. They were no younger or less experienced than the others.

The authors suspect that stress kept them there, and not a lack of knowledge.

Some meerkats had been expected to navigate better than others. Subordinate males sometimes leave the group and go alone in search of mates.

Other females are occasionally driven out for a while by the dominant female.

Solo trips of this kind could be counted as practice. The researchers also considered the age of each animal, together with the time it spent on guard duty.

Attention was given as well to how often it scent-marked the territory or dug at shelters.

None of those traits predicted how long a meerkat explored. Neither did they predict how accurately it set off or how straight it travelled.

No clear difference was found between males and females either, although only five females took part.

The authors think that the explanation lies in the territory itself. It is small and stable, with 0.3 to 0.6 miles (0.5 to 1 kilometer) covered by a group daily.

The whole territory has probably been crossed several times by each member by the time adulthood is reached.

The authors wrote that pigeons of different ages are likewise able to find their way home equally well.

On the way home, the meerkats spent about two-thirds of their time searching for cues. Most of the remaining time was spent moving.

Few of them stopped to eat, and those that did only grabbed millipedes crawling on the sand.

The animals stood upright or climbed a bush or tree trunk in order to scan the landscape. Overall, they spent 10 times longer on looking than on sniffing.

This balance between looking and sniffing was altered late in the trip. By that stage, a meerkat had reached its target and was searching for its group. Sniffing then took up more than 20% of its cue-searching time.

Along the way, meerkats were often seen sniffing around boltholes. These are the small shelters that the animals dig across their territory. An earlier experiment had found that meerkats remember where their boltholes are.

The authors suggested that boltholes may serve as landmarks in a meerkat’s memory of its territory.

The search was not ended by reaching the burrow, because the group could have moved on since the capture.

Thirteen of the 20 meerkats rejoined their groups on their own. The slowest took almost three hours.

The burrow or the capture site was reached by five others. The group had nevertheless not been found by them when the three-hour limit ran out.

These animals were caught again and returned by the team.

The researchers could not explain why some meerkats succeeded while others did not. Success was not predicted by a straighter trip to the burrow.

Nor was it predicted by the number of scent marks left behind by the group.

The tracking of a group was compared by the authors to a predator following its prey. The target keeps moving and its route is not known to the searcher.

Twenty animals make up a small sample, as the authors wrote. All of them were subordinate adults older than 10 months, so pups and dominant animals were not tested.

Every release was also made inside familiar territory. The results therefore do not show how a meerkat would manage in a place it does not know.

Two reasons for looking around could not be fully separated either. A lone meerkat that scans the landscape may be checking for predators as much as searching for landmarks.

If many group members know the territory, the next question is who leads when the group moves.

The authors want to know whether certain traits influence which animal takes the lead. The physical needs of an animal and its social rank are two examples.

Co-author Benjamin Robira said the team now wants to understand what makes up a meerkat’s mental picture of its territory.

The researchers also intend to study how the animals get from one point to another.

The full study was published in the Journal of Experimental Biology.

Some patients who appear unresponsive can control a computer with their thoughts

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

One in three ICU patients who appeared comatose changed a beeping tone by thinking, and that sign of hidden consciousness predicted recovery.

After a severe brain injury, a patient may show no visible response when doctors ask them to move. But their brain waves can sometimes reveal that they’re following the command.

In a New York intensive care unit, 13 of 41 patients who appeared unresponsive changed how fast a sound beeped by trying to open and close a hand.

Electrodes on the scalp recorded the patients’ brain waves. A bedside computer detected signals linked to the attempted movement and adjusted the beeping the patients heard through headphones.

The 13 who could do it were also far more likely to regain consciousness by hospital discharge.

A year later, they were far more likely to be able to look after themselves for hours at a time.

Jan Claassen, a professor of neurology at Columbia University’s Vagelos College of Physicians and Surgeons, led the study.

He also runs the neurocritical care unit at NewYork-Presbyterian and Columbia University Irving Medical Center, where the patients were treated.

In a 2019 study, his team recorded brain waves while unresponsive patients were told to open and close a hand. A computer program found that about 15% of them were trying to comply.

Some patients showed brain activity consistent with following a command even though they did not visibly move. Researchers call this cognitive motor dissociation.

“Our brain-computer interface is only a first step, but it shows that, in principle, we could hopefully in the future give some of these patients a voice,” Claassen said.

Most brain-computer interfaces that let paralyzed people type, play games, or move a robotic arm use a chip implanted on the brain, and they need long training.

Neither suits someone who was healthy last week and isn’t responding now.

So the team used the 21 EEG electrodes already glued to each patient’s scalp.

Through headphones, a recorded voice said “to make the beep sound faster, keep opening and closing your right hand,” then “to make the beep sound slower, stop opening and closing your right hand.”

A bedside computer read the brain waves as they came in and changed the beeps when it detected the effort.

Each session was two five-minute practice rounds, during which the program learned that patient’s signals, and then a ten-minute test with no help.

The voice encouraged patients when the beeps changed and congratulated them when they hit the target.

The 41 patients were tested a median of five days after their injuries. Their median age was 65, and most had suffered a stroke, a brain bleed, a cardiac arrest, or a head injury.

Thirteen of the 41 patients, or 32%, passed the test on at least one try. Six of them did it within the first week after their injury, which surprised the researchers.

For comparison, all eight healthy volunteers controlled the tone, and so did nine of 22 conscious patients in the same unit. The conscious patients who failed were mostly sedated, agitated, or unable to speak after a stroke.

The unresponsive patients who succeeded were slow. Their brain signals showed up about 50 seconds after each instruction.

Conscious patients took about 17 seconds and healthy volunteers about 15. A patient who looks like they are ignoring a command may simply be answering it late.

Each patient also took the team’s older command-following exam, the one from 2019. Eight of the 41 passed it. Every one of those who was tested on the tone the same day could control it.

But nine of the 33 patients who failed the older exam could control the tone anyway.

The authors wrote that the beeping feedback may simply be more engaging than a voice repeating the same command.

“EEG is used in clinical practice every day in the ICU,” Claassen said.

“The only sophisticated part is the computer program we wrote, which analyzes the data in real time and modulates the feedback to the patient.”

Four of the 13 patients who controlled the tone regained consciousness before leaving the hospital.

Among the 28 patients who could not control the beeping, only one had regained consciousness by hospital discharge.

A year later, six of the 13, or 46%, could be left alone for at least eight hours a day without help. Two of the 28 others, or 7%, could. Patients who had failed both tests did worst of all.

Of those 24 patients, not one recovered that much. By then 17 of the 41 had died, 14 of them after life support was withdrawn.

The deaths after withdrawal are why the authors wrote at length about ethics.

Four of the 13 patients who controlled the tone died after life support was withdrawn. The authors cautioned that a negative result cannot rule out awareness.

The study covered one hospital and 41 patients with very different injuries, some of them sedated during testing.

Patients who recovered were also examined more often, which could have made recovery easier to spot in them. The authors wrote that the result needs repeating at other centers before it guides care.

Controlling a tone is also not talking. The study didn’t try to get a yes or a no from anyone. The authors wrote that doing so may need a level of awareness beyond what this test detects.

Families and the conscious patients were asked what they wanted.

All 37 who answered supported the idea, and they asked for a loved one’s voice in place of the recording, and for ways to signal pain, hunger, or simple awareness.

“Our goal is to refine the system so that patients can tell their doctors and loved ones how they are feeling – if they are in pain or hungry, for instance – with more direct communication,” Claassen said.

For some patients who showed no visible response at the bedside, the beeps offered evidence that they could follow a command.

The full study was published in the journal Brain.

The Privacy Policy URL Nobody Was Watching

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at meridianbuild.dev

The URL pointed to a GitHub Pages site for the app's repository. A few weeks earlier I had restricted what that Pages site served, for good reasons, and later it stopped serving the policy at all. Nothing in my setup knew that a store console depended on that one path. The site changed, the link broke, and the only system that noticed was Google's review pipeline, weeks later.

The App Store listing had a different URL: a GitHub gist. It worked, it loaded, Apple had accepted it. The quick fix was obvious: paste the same gist URL into Play Console, both stores aligned, done. I did that.

It dated from March, from an earlier version of the app. It mentioned collecting an email address and using PostHog for analytics. The current app does neither. It uses anonymous accounts with an animal pseudonym, and RevenueCat for the one in-app purchase. The real, current policy existed. It was in the repository as an HTML page, written in French and English in October, covering both Apple and Google. It just was not at any URL either store knew about.

So for months the App Store had pointed parents at a policy describing data collection the app no longer did, and for some time Google Play had pointed them at nothing. And for a few hours on November 20, after my "fix", both stores pointed at the outdated one.

I wanted a URL I control, on a domain that is not tied to how one repository happens to be published. This blog already runs as a static site with automatic deploys, so the policy now lives here:

https://meridianbuild.dev/kidcapital/privacy/

It is a plain copy of the HTML page from the app repository, dropped into the blog's public/kidcapital/privacy/ folder, so the static build serves it as is. No framework, no layout, nothing that a future blog redesign can break by accident.

The cost of this setup is that the policy now exists in two places, the app repository and the blog. Any future change has to be made in both. I wrote that down where I will see it, because "two copies" is exactly how the gist went stale.

Fixing the URL surfaced the next layer. Apple's privacy label for the app, the "Data collected" section on the App Store page, still declares the categories from March: contact info, diagnostics, usage data. Those match the old gist, not the app. Play's Data safety form and the current policy say something different. That is the next thing to realign, and it matters more than the URL, because the label is what most parents actually look at.

I am mentioning it because it is the honest state of things. Fixing one field in a store console tends to reveal the three next to it that were filled in the same week, by the same tired person, for a version of the app that no longer exists.

If you ship an app, open your store consoles today, click every URL you declared, and read what is on the other end. It does not take long. I had not done it since the day I filled those fields.

Shrinking My Photos Did Not Cut My Gemini Bill

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at meridianbuild.dev

Every time someone adds a piece of clothing to their wardrobe in OutfitMaker, a background job sends the photo to Gemini on Vertex AI. The model returns a small JSON object: category, colour, a description, a few tags, translated labels, and which way the garment is rotated in the photo. That is the whole job. It runs on every upload, so it runs a lot.

On November 20 I read the Google Cloud bill line by line. The top line was image input for that analysis call, about 15 euros over 30 days. Not a disaster, but it was the biggest one, and it grows with every new user who photographs their closet.

Phones take large photos. My first idea was the one everybody has: resize the image to something modest before encoding it, send fewer bytes, pay less.

Before writing that code I checked how Gemini counts image tokens. It does not bill by bytes or by pixels in the way I assumed. An image becomes a fixed number of tokens determined by the resolution setting of the request, and the model scales whatever you send to fit that budget. A huge photo and a small photo of the same shirt land on the same token count.

So resizing on my side would have cost CPU on the worker, added a dependency on image processing in a code path that already had its own share of trouble with missing binaries, and changed nothing on the invoice. Smaller uploads are still nice for storage and bandwidth. They are just not a Gemini cost lever.

The lever is mediaResolution in generationConfig. It tells the model how much detail to keep from each image, and the token count follows. The values are MEDIA_RESOLUTION_LOW, MEDIA_RESOLUTION_MEDIUM and MEDIA_RESOLUTION_HIGH, and if you do not set it, you get the default.

Lowering detail on an image classifier sounds risky, so I did not pick a value from the docs. I ran the real call in production on 12 garment photos at each setting: default, MEDIUM, LOW. For each photo I compared category, colour and rotation, and read promptTokenCount from the response metadata.

The prompt token count includes the text of my prompt, which does not change between runs. So the drop on the image part alone is larger than the 40% you see in the table.

LOW was tempting. It is cheaper again, and on 11 of the 12 photos it was right. But colour is the field users notice first. If the app calls a navy sweater black, every outfit built on it inherits the mistake, and the user's trust in the whole wardrobe drops with it. One error in twelve is too many for the field people check with their own eyes. MEDIUM gave identical answers, so MEDIUM it is.

Someone refactors the request builder, the field is dropped, every test still passes because the model answers fine at default resolution, and the bill goes back up without a single error. A test that pins a cost setting is the only alarm you get for that kind of regression.

My estimate is a saving of around 6 euros a month at the current volume. That is small in absolute terms. It is also free, permanent, and it scales with uploads, which is the one usage pattern I hope keeps growing.

The more useful outcome for me is the habit. Before optimising an AI call, I now check how the provider actually counts the units I pay for. My intuition said bytes. The answer was a setting I had never read, sitting one level below maxOutputTokens in the same config hash.

If you send images to Gemini, look at your generationConfig. If mediaResolution is not there, you are paying for the default on every call. Run your own handful of real inputs at MEDIUM and LOW, compare the fields your users actually look at, and pick the lowest setting that gives the same answers.
