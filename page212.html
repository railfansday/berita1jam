How The Southlands Turns Into Mordor (LOTR Map & Full Explanation)

📅 Oct 05, 2022  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power showed how the peaceful, sleepy region of Middle-earth known as the Southlands became Mordor, the seat of power for the Great Evil that defined the Third Age. J.R.R. Tolkien never offered much insight into Mordor before Sauron turned the region into a smoking hub of villainy, and while The Rings of Power tried valiantly to hide its big twist, plenty of viewers quickly deduced the Southlands was actually Second Age Mordor.

Essentially, an ancient key switches on a dormant Southlands volcano. The resulting cataclysm brings forth ash, steam, fire, rock, water, and, most of all, misery, and by the end of The Rings of Power season 2, the transformation of the Southlands is well underway. However, even for a being as powerful as Sauron, morphing a relatively pleasant land of plains and mountains into hell on Middle-earth is no simple feat. One does not simply turn the Southlands into Mordor — it's a process of epic proportions that begins with a disgruntled tavern owner and ends with a permanent scar upon the map.

The — quite literal — key to turning the Southlands into Mordor proves to be the broken black sword kept hidden by Morgoth loyalist Waldreg. The weapon slots into an ornate hole at the Elven watchtower of Ostirith and begins a chain of events that give rise to Mordor — but who constructed this mechanism, and for what purpose?

Although Ostirith is occupied by Elves when The Rings of Power begins, the magical black sword and skull mechanism obviously aren't their doing. The Southlands fought for Morgoth during the War of Wrath, so Ostirith was once under the control of the dark forces of Middle-earth, meaning the sinister key and its corresponding doom-switch were likely forged during this period.

It can also be assumed this dark machinery built into the mountain wasn't just for some mundane purpose like opening the dam or changing water levels. Not even a showman like Sauron would craft a magic black sword with a giant skull lock for such an ordinary reason. Whoever made this lock-and-key combo apparently had the formation of Mordor in mind from the beginning.

The Rings of Power hasn't yet offered enough information to say whose mind the Mordor master plan originated from. The black sword dates back to Morgoth, but Morgoth wasn't the type to plan for his own downfall, which leans more toward Sauron as the chief architect. The Southlands-shaped sigil on Finrod's corpse proves Sauron held an interest in the area for centuries, and the Lord of the Rings villain could've carried out his designs in secret.

Going into Rings of Power season 3, Sauron's evil kingdom is fully established, and he is set to forge the One Ring in the fires of Mount Doom. As this story continues, the show may provide further details regarding Sauron's overarching plan for the Southlands, including when he first set to work on the idea and just how much came from his own mind.

The Rings of Power's Ostirith tower is located on the eastern side of the Ephel Dúath mountain range (red circle on the map above), meaning the offending waterway could be either the Poros or some tributary of Middle-earth's huge River Anduin. Regardless, turning the black sword lowers the wall holding this water back, and the cascade courses down the rocky cliff, destroying part of Ostirith's battlements along the way (further proof that the release system wasn't designed for regular dam control).

Once the water hits the bottom level, it flows into a cavern and through a system of tunnels and trenches. These are the very same tunnels and trenches that Adar's Orcs have been digging ever since their Southlands invasion began. Their purpose, as confirmed in The Rings of Power season 1, episode 6, was to direct the flow of river water along a specific course through the Southlands.

Thanks to the scenes of destruction witnessed by Galadriel and the other victorious warriors, it's known that the water passes through the village Tirharad (yellow circle), as well as Hordern (green circle), which was excavated by Orcs earlier in The Rings of Power. All the various underground tunnels feed into a central overground trench dug by Arondir and the other prisoners, which carries the water north to an inactive volcano — the future Mount Doom.

Weirdly for a world that boasts immortal wizards and ghost armies, The Rings of Power's volcanic eruption is more or less plausible, scientifically speaking. The meeting of cool water released from Ostirith with the magma bubbling peacefully beneath Halbrand's Southlands kingdom creates a massive amount of steam within a confined space, which in turn leads to a violent phreatomagmatic eruption churning out magma, ash, and pyroclastic fragments, as well as generating extraordinary heat.

Even the thunderstorms triggered within the eruption's ash cloud are semi-based in scientific fact, since such weather typically occurs due to warm air and water particles rising. Warm air and water are exactly what's rising in The Rings of Power's explosive season 1, episode 6 ending, so while the thunderstorms may happen very quickly, they're not just present for dramatic flair.

It's also important to remember that this particular eruption was triggered by a magic black sword, so there's very likely an element of sorcery to The Rings of Power's volcano compared against real-world phreatomagmatic eruptions. Mount Doom is, after all, where Sauron's One Ring will be crafted, making it special even within the context of Middle-earth. The eruption itself may be triggered by science, but one cannot rule out Morgoth or Sauron weaving a touch of dark magic into the process.

The main purpose of The Rings of Power's eruption — at least for Adar — is generating a massive plume of steam and ash that covers the sky, thus blocking out all sunlight, which Orcs have a bad allergic reaction to. Even during Frodo's journey, Mordor is renowned as a realm cloaked in darkness, and those days begin when Waldreg turns the sword-key in The Rings of Power.

Darkness isn't the only outcome of Adar's Southlands transformation project, however. Where Orcs tunneled upwards into settlements such as Tirharad and Hordern, water now bursts through the cavities, turning the Southlands' smooth plains into Mordor's craggy landscape. The trench carved out by Adar's prisoners isn't vanishing anytime soon either, leaving a huge permanent gash all the way from Ostirith to Mount Doom. Any Southlanders who think they could endure a few potholes and a surprise river should think again.

The flying rocks and clouds of steam emanating from Mount Doom's eruption will destroy settlements, while the oppressive heat will make living and farming in the area all but impossible. Even during the War of the Ring, Mordor is largely inhospitable for any race but Orcs, and intense heat isn't the only factor behind the Southlands' newly-barren landscape — the volcanic activity could produce sulfur dioxide, stunting nearby plant growth.

In many ways it's obvious why the Southlands becoming Mordor in The Rings of Power is important. Mordor is, after all, the final destination of Frodo's quest in The Lord of the Rings, and where the final battle against Sauron takes place in both of the wars against him. However, there are some important points to consider when it comes to the impact the change had in-universe for the citizens of Middle-earth who existed beyond the Southlands themselves.

Thanks to Morgoth, the races of Middle-earth were no strangers to threat. However, once Mordor was created, the danger existed right on their doorstep in a way it never had before. Perhaps only the dwarfs, whose digging beneath their fortress-cities brought them forever close to some of the more dangerous creatures of Middle-earth, knew how to exist with evil so close and (literally) on the horizon.

Being able to see the fiery Mount Doom on the skyline would have brought evil closer to the hearts and homes of most of the inhabitants of Middle-earth in a way few would have known before (especially humanity). For those alive during the creation of Mordor in The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power, the time prior to the eruption of Mount Doom would likely come to be known as an era of, if not literal peace, but peace of mind at the very least, and one that wouldn't be seen again for many, many millennia.

You may not be able to control where your digital data ends up. But you know exactly how you can securely dispose of your paper records. The trick is deciding which shredder will best satisfy your serious or casual shredding needs, which usually comes down to a handful of features.

Once you start your shredding chore, you’d rather not be interrupted by problems with your shredder. Runtime tells you how long you can keep feeding paper into a shredder before its motor overheats and shuts down, forcing you to wait 30 to 45 minutes, depending on the model, for the machine to catch its mechanical breath.

A small personal shredder may run for only five or 10 minutes, which is plenty if you dispatch only a few documents at a time. If you’re regularly confronted by larger piles to pulverize, look for a runtime of 30 minutes or more.

Once you choose a shredder with longer runtimes, don’t obsess over the advertised cooldown time. You may fill the bin before the shredder overheats and, by the time you’ve disposed of all that confetti, the motor already has had a chance to cool before you return with an empty bin.

A shredder is still largely a mechanical device, a motor driving a set of cutters, augmented in many models by sensors and electronic controls. And as with any mechanical object, failure is often less a question of if than when. Long-term owner shredder reviews are especially useful for spotting recurring problems with motors, sensors, switches, and other parts that may not misbehave during short-term testing.

Unfortunately, getting help directly from some shredder brands can be harder than clearing a paper jam. During our ongoing shredder research, we’ve encountered multiple manufacturers failing to respond to repeated emails and phone calls. Some brands don’t even provide a U.S. customer-service number.

That doesn’t automatically make shredders from these contact-resistant brands a bad buy. Purchasing from an established store or online retailer with a clearly stated return or replacement policy gives you a more reliable resource to turn to if your new shredder turns out to be a lemon. Before buying—particularly from an unfamiliar brand—check both the manufacturer’s warranty and especially the retailer’s return/replace policy.

Unlike the shredders you may remember from old movies, most modern machines no longer turn documents into long linguini-like strips. Cross-cut shredders slice paper both lengthwise and crosswise into small rectangular scraps. Micro-cut models slice and dice those scraps into even tinier bits, leaving hundreds or even thousands of petite particles.

For most home and home-office use, either cutting method provides plenty of protection. Most shredders these days offer micro-cut machines with either P-4 security, which makes reconstructing a document the most puzzling jigsaw puzzle ever, or P-5 security, whose even more diminutive document destruction methods are favored by MDs, CPAs, JDs, or by other professionals with initials in their titles.

The larger and more powerful your shredder, the more sheets it can generally chew through at one time. Just don’t treat the number printed on the box as a dare.

Manufacturers’ rated sheet capacities are best-case numbers, usually based on neatly aligned standard-weight bond paper. Thicker stock, folds, staples, uneven piles, and stuffed envelopes can quickly turn a “12-sheet” machine into something more comfortable with eight or nine. Feeding a few sheets below the rated maximum is one of the easiest ways to avoid jams and unnecessary strain.

Manual shredders require you to stand there feeding paper in batches. Auto-feed machines let you load a stack, walk away, and get something else done while the shredder eats.

Here again, don’t take the rated auto-feed sheet capacity too literally. An “80-sheet” or “200-sheet” auto feeder may accommodate that many pristine sheets under ideal conditions, but real paperwork is rarely pristine. Wrinkles, folds, different paper weights, staples, and uneven stacks can reduce how much you can reliably leave it to swallow unattended. Plus, the bin on a high-capacity auto-feed model may fill before the maximum number of sheets are fed into it.

Auto-feed mechanisms also add more rollers, sensors, and jam-clearing compartments. And more moving mechanical parts create more places for something to eventually go wrong.

However, if you routinely accumulate large piles, the time a highly rated auto-feed shredder saves can easily outweigh the added complexity and potential mechanical issues.

A bigger bin means fewer trips to empty it, but gallon capacity tells only part of the story. Pull-out drawers are lighter and easier to clean and empty. Bins that require lifting the often heavy shredder mechanism off the top of a wastepaper basket can potentially scatter loose particles still in the cutter mechanism.

Also pay attention to what happens when you pull that drawer out. Paper confetti has an impressive ability to cling to plastic, pile up behind a bin, and otherwise escape the container designed to hold it. An easy-to-remove bin that keeps most of its shredded paper actually in the bin will save you emptying and cleanup aggravation, especially if you don’t wait until the bin is filled to its brim.

Shredders may look sturdy and impervious, but most are actually quite finicky eaters. Nearly every shredder will eventually jam if you feed it too much at one time or feed it the wrong type, shape, or mix of paper. More advanced models with more advanced anti-jam systems sense an overload before one occurs, or either automatically reverse or allow you to manually reverse to relieve the jam before you need to perform paper-removal surgery from the cutters with tweezers or needle-nose pliers.

The cheapest jam protection, however, is restraint. If a machine claims 12 sheets, regularly feeding it eight or nine instead will lower your odds of spending quality time violently tugging mangled paper back out of its teeth.

Most current shredders can swallow staples and paper clips, and many can also chew up plastic credit cards. CDs and other thicker media such as metal cards are less universally welcome.

Check the manufacturer’s instructions before you buy for what non-paper materials a shredder can successfully chew and before feeding anything other than paper into its cutters. A shredder capable of mincing a stack of tax records can object like a microwave forced to cook metal—something it wasn’t designed to eat.

Probably less than you think. For most people who dispatch a few bills, receipts, financial statements, or junk-mail offers every few days, well under $100 can buy a perfectly capable shredder.

Spending more generally buys stamina, convenience, and durability: longer runtimes, larger bins, higher sheet capacities, auto-feed, better jam protection, sometimes finer-cut security, and years if not decades of service. Those features become worth paying for as the amount and frequency of your shredding increase.

As with nearly all things, don’t be penny wise and pound foolish. You will find you will shred more than you anticipated—it’s just human nature to use a new device more than you though you would. Overall, shredders costing several hundreds of dollars make the most sense for people who regularly devour large stacks or have a specialized need. Don’t decide what to spend simply by asking how private your paperwork is; ask how much of it you need to turn into confetti and how often both now and in the future.

The most important shredder “spec” may be how little time it forces you to spend with it. Sheet capacity, runtime, security level, and bin capacity are the headline numbers I’d look at first. Runtime is particularly useful because it tells you something about how much work the machine can comfortably handle before demanding a rest.

But some of the things that most determine whether you’ll love or hate a shredder aren’t specs at all. Nearly every decent modern shredder can reduce ordinary documents beyond casual recognition. The bigger differences are how often it jams, how easily those jams clear, how cleanly the bin empties, and how much confetti winds up everywhere except inside it.

Check owner reviews for recurring complaints about jams, overheating, finicky sensors, messy bins, and other annoyances. AI agents on online sites can summarize customer reviews to answer many of these before-you-buy questions.

With shredders, old doesn’t necessarily mean obsolete. Sometimes it means proven.Unlike most consumer electronics, shredders don’t become outdated because they lack this year’s faster processor, new operating system, wireless standard, or other bleeding-edge technology. An older model that’s still attracting buyers and good reviews may actually offer something a brand-new machine can’t: years of accumulated evidence about how well it holds up.

Before buying an older model, check whether the manufacturer has introduced a newer or revised version. When shopping online, search the manufacturer’s current lineup or, if the retailer provides an AI or shopping assistant, ask whether there’s a newer version of the model you’re considering. Then compare old to new rather than automatically assuming new means better.

The best shredder maintenance is self-restraint. Don’t continually stuff the cutters to their rated maximum, allow the bin overflow, or feed the machine materials it wasn’t designed to digest.

Lubrication requirements vary. Some manufacturers recommend shredder oil applied at regular intervals, others suggest pre-lubricated oil sheets, and some models claim to require little or no lubrication at all. Follow the instructions for your particular machine rather than assuming every shredder requires the same type of mechanical TLC.

Beyond that, maintenance should be pretty painless: empty the bin before the paper snow reaches the cutters, occasionally clean dusty sensors if the manufacturer recommends it, and clear jams rather than repeatedly hammering the reverse button and hoping for divine intervention.

Despite the name, most modern “shredders” no longer shred paper into long strips. While cross cut and micro cut are clues for how small the ultimate shred pieces will be, P-ratings, part of the DIN 66399 security standard, put an actual number on “small.”

P-ratings run from P-1 through P-7; the higher the number, the smaller the pieces produced. For ordinary household paperwork, P-3 is already plenty secure. A cross-cut P-3 machine reduces a document to hundreds of pieces, which is more than enough discouragement for anyone contemplating reconstructing your bills, junk mail, receipts, or other everyday paperwork. After all, a document pieced back together like a taped mosaic isn’t likely to fool many people.

P-4 is the sweet spot for buyers who want their paperwork diced considerably finer without spending much more, and P-4 is common among consumer micro-cut shredders. P-5 turns documents into even tinier bits and makes sense for especially sensitive financial, legal, medical, or professional paperwork. P-6 and P-7 go further still, into security levels intended for documents whose contents matter considerably more than last month’s Visa bill.

For most of us, then, P-3 is plenty, P-4 buys extra peace of mind, and P-5 is for paperwork you’d really rather nobody put back together.

The most famous, most powerful, most precious Ring of Power is, of course, Sauron's One Ring, forged in the fires of Mt. Doom as a master controller for the other 19. Crafting the Ring required Sauron to pour a large portion of his own power into the melting pot, creating a symbiotic bond between the item and its master. Sauron would never be truly killed while the Ring was still whole, but neither could Sauron wield his full Maia power without the Ring on his finger.

As recounted in The Lord of the Rings, Sauron used the One Ring to bring Middle-earth to his knees, until the weapon was cut from his hand by Isildur during the Last Alliance of Elves and Men. Over 2000 years, the Ring remained in its watery resting place until, as shown in The Return of the King's movie adaptation, Sméagol the stoor found it while fishing. The Ring passed from Sméagol to Bilbo Baggins, from Bilbo to Frodo Baggins and, following a brief stint with Samwise Gamgee, into the fire from whence it came.

While the One Ring was the most powerful, there were 19 other Rings of Power in Middle-earth, and each of them was a considerable force in its own right. There are a few things you need to do to make sure your gas snowblower runs smoothly through the season:

Change the spark plug at the beginning of each season to ensure it starts smoothly. A dirty spark plug can make it difficult to get your snowblower going, especially in extreme cold.

Lastly, small gas engines require plenty of airflow to run smoothly, so check the air filter once a season and replace it if it's dirty.

Snowblowers use unleaded gasoline—the same thing you put in your car—which begins to degrade after about 60 days. Running a snowblower with old fuel can clog the carburetor and make the engine hard to start, so you should never store your snowblower with fuel left in the tank.

Before putting your snowblower to bed for the season, either run the fuel tank dry or add fuel stabilizer, which keeps gasoline fresh for up to 24 months, to the tank. If you use fuel stabilizer, run the snowblower for a minute or two after adding it to the tank to circulate it through the engine.

Sex And The City: How Old Carrie Was At The Beginning & End

📅 Jul 17, 2021  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The Sex and the City franchise follows Carrie Bradshaw through decades of her life, from a single woman in her 30s to a widow in her 50s. Considered one of HBO's best series, Sex and the City was revolutionary in its depiction of sexually active, unmarried women in their thirties, as Carrie taught generations of women what it meant to be "single and fabulous."

The life of Carrie Bradshaw was so beloved that Sex and the City spawned two movies, a prequel series, and a sequel series. And Just Like That ended in 2025, and the polarizing show sadly didn't quite capture the experience of being a 50-something woman the original series did with Carrie's 30s. Still, our Carrie has learned, grown, and loved a lot in the 27 years that we've known her.

The timeline of the original Sex and the City series makes it fairly easy to determine Carrie's age while also setting up a useful bit of information to determine it in the rest of the franchise. She states that she is 32 years old at the beginning of season 1, which takes place in 1998, so her birth year is 1966.

Carrie's Age In The Sex And The City Franchise

Her birthday is also established in the series as October 10. The show's timeline typically follows the passage of time in real life, so Carrie should age one year in each Sex and the City season.

She then turns 35 in season 4, meaning that when Sex and the City comes to an end in 2004, Carrie is 38 years old, which is also confirmed by the title of the season 6 episode, "Catch-38". Once the movies come into the picture, the timeline and the corresponding characters’ ages get fuzzier.

The first Sex and the City movie premiered in 2008, seemingly four years after the events of the series. The film centers on Carrie and Mr. Big's impending wedding, which provides some context for her age. A magazine editor asks Carrie to pose as the “40-year-old bride,” and if the film takes place in the year it premiered, she would be about 41-42.

The timeline is a bit confusing, considering the film is supposed to take place over two years, so you can assume it begins in 2007, or at least before Carrie’s 41st birthday, and finishes in 2008 when she’s 41-42.

It's also clear that the magazine article is about 'the 40s' as a decade of age, so the line could mean a 40-something bride, rather than Carrie being 40 exactly. She also says to her assistant, "your 40s are to pay for the drinks," confirming that she is in her 40s at this time.

Sex and the City 2 is the next movie in the franchise and continues to show what Carrie Bradshaw's life is like, now that she and Big are finally married. The movie takes a look at Carrie settling into married life with Mr. Big, missing some of the excitement of her younger years.

Released in 2010, Sex and the City 2 picks up two years after the first movie, likely putting Carrie Bradshaw's age at 43-44 years old. However, her age is not a big plot point in this film.

Though short-lived, there was a Sex and the City prequel series that looked at Carrie's teen years. Played by AnnaSophia Robb, Carrie Bradshaw's age in The Carrie Diaries is revealed to be 16 years old, and she's a junior in high school at the start of the series.

Carrie lives in Castlebury, Connecticut, and the prequel series shows her secretly working at a magazine company while interning at a New York City law firm. In season 2, Carrie celebrates her 17th birthday and enters her senior year of high school.

Following the cancellation of the third Sex and the City movie, the franchise returned to the small screen for a sequel series, And Just Like That. The show explored how times have changed and what life is like for Carrie and her friends as middle-aged women, with it taking place 11 years after the second movie and seeing Carrie as a 55-year-old woman.

Carrie is shown to be more conservative when talking about sex compared to the younger generation, and seems to have settled into a quieter life than she had in Sex and the City. Before Big's tragic death in the And Just Like That pilot, she's even shown to (somewhat) enjoy cooking with him. This is a far cry from the 30-something Carrie who used her stove for shoe storage.

The depiction of Carrie's aging in the sequel series didn't go over well with viewers, many of whom felt frustrated with storylines like Carrie undergoing hip surgery. Still, there was some excitement that we might get to see Carrie celebrate her 60th birthday, should the show have continued into 2026.

Alas, that's not what happened, as it was abruptly announced that And Just Like That season 3 would be its last, a mere two weeks before the finale. And Just Like That's final episode takes place over the American Thanksgiving holiday, which is celebrated in November, so it can be assumed that Carrie had recently celebrated her 59th birthday the month before.

Sex and the City follows Carrie and her friends across just over half a decade of their lives, with the movies covering the interim years between the original show and the spinoff, And Just Like That, which picks up decades later.

Given this vast timeframe, it's no surprise that Carrie's age factors into many storylines in Sex and the City, And Just Like That, and even The Carrie Diaries. The Carrie Diaries makes obvious use of Carrie's age, since it's a spinoff set during her younger years, but the other two shows also weave age into the exploits they portray.

Sex and the City is primarily about two things — dating and growing up. Carrie starts the show in her early 30s, and many episodes have her wrestling with figuring out how she wants to spend the rest of her life against the backdrop of most of her friends settling down and starting families of their own.

As an experience, dating also changes as one grows older. The wild and spontaneous relationships of Carrie's youth weren't appealing to her as 40 approached, and she wanted a stable life with Mr. Big.

Age also became important in And Just Like That. Following Carrie in her mid-50s created yet another unique variety of storylines that were only possible due to her age. However, despite And Just Like That's backlash, none of the shows have ever preached any toxic messages about aging or have claimed that age is important when finding a romantic partner.

With Carrie's age comes wisdom, and in the And Just Like That finale, she realizes that she spent most of her life either looking for or with a partner, and that this may no longer be her path anymore. The very last scene sees her dancing around her beautiful home, happy to be in her own company. This never would have happened in Sex and the City, when Carrie was in her 30s.

It was indeed a surprise to learn that And Just Like That was seemingly closing the book on Carrie Bradshaw, but the ending, as controversial as it was, seemed definitive. In the original Sex and the City finale, Carrie said, "The most fabulous relationship of all is the one you have with yourself," and that's a lesson she wholly embodies in And Just Like That.

There are currently no known plans to continue the story of Carrie Bradshaw, but that doesn't mean we'll never see her again. When addressing the potential for a return to TVLine, And Just Like That writer and executive producer Elisa Zuritsky said, "She's alive, so it could happen."

"Sarah Jessica Parker is [And Just Like That showrunner] Michael Patrick King’s muse," Zuritsky continued, "and the reason he says ‘I’ve said goodbye before and I’ve come back’ is because it’s true." Parker also noted that she was saying "farewell" to the character, not "goodbye." (via People)

So, while we likely won't be seeing Carrie Bradshaw on our TV screens anytime soon, anything is possible, and in 20 years, we very well may be catching up with the Sex and the City heroine in her 70s. Now that would be fabulous.

A camping chair’s durability is evaluated based on the frame, the material of the seat and back, and overall weather-resistance. Most of the camping chairs we write about are made with steel tubing that’s powder-coated or treated to resist rust and corrosion. This style of frame holds several hundred pounds without any bowing or bending and is still light enough to carry in your hands.

Similarly, seat durability relies on sturdy materials. Woven polyester is one of the most common options. Camping chair materials are often combined with a water-resistant coating so any spilled drinks or rain drops don't damage the fabric. This also protects any interior cushioning from absorbing water.

Great camping chairs are also treated to resist UV light. This helps prevent the fabric from fading over time. Similar to the metal frame, the fabric should also be strong enough to offer a weight capacity of 300 pounds at minimum.

Camping chairs are specifically designed to be portable. That said, the relative portability between them can range drastically.

Small, lightweight camping chairs pack down for easy transport and work well for backpackers or anyone traveling by foot. These typically come with carrying straps or collapse small enough to latch onto or pack into a large backpack. Ideally, they should weigh less than eight or nine pounds.

Larger chairs can weigh more than 10 pounds, which makes them far more cumbersome to carry and better suited to car camping and backyard lounging. For hikers and backpackers, smaller, lighter chairs are the obvious choices. If you’re using the chair for the beach, weather- and UV-resistance generally increase its longevity. It's also worth considering chairs with shorter legs so you can stretch out your own in the sand.

You could just drive over to your local hardware store and buy a large portable generator to use as a home backup, but we wouldn’t recommend it. You want to make sure you’re getting a generator that can back up your home and power all the appliances, lights, and electronics inside. (Plus some extra watts in case you need them down the line.)

In addition, there are often state and local laws or homeowners association rules and restrictions around using them in residential neighborhoods. Choosing poorly or setting up your backup generator incorrectly could damage the generator or short out your house power at best, or lead to fire, electrocution, or carbon monoxide poisoning at worst.

Picking the best generator for your home starts with a little math. Ideally, you want to calculate how much energy you need to keep your house running, and buy a generator that can handle everything you want to power in an emergency, with room to spare.

The two key specs to pay attention to are the running wattage and starting wattage (e.g., 9,500 running watts / 12,500 starting watts). Running wattage is effectively your generator’s capacity: It tells you how much power a generator can produce steadily without any possibility of an overload. Starting wattage is the generator’s max output, or how much power it can produce for short, peak bursts when appliances first switch on.

When you are making your list, note what items are 120-volt and which ones are 240-volt appliances. It’s not hard to identify them. Refrigerators, small appliances, and most electronics run on 120-volt power and plug into standard outlets. Anything that requires 240 volts—electric dryers, well pumps, and HVAC systems—is either hardwired into your electrical system or has a dedicated 240-volt outlet. If you need to power the latter, the installation of a transfer switch and a generator that outputs at least 30 amps/240 volts may be required.

If you’re just looking to keep the fridge, lights, and a few modest appliances going through a short blackout, you can usually get away with a generator that supplies 3,000 to 5,000 watts. If you want to run the heat, hot water, and several appliances at once, plan on using at least 6,000 to 8,000 watts. If you think you’re going to be adding large appliances such as laundry machines and dishwashers, expect to need 10,000 watts or more.

A piece of advice we have: Once you figure out how much you need, opt for buying a generator that outputs around 20 percent more than that. It’s not uncommon to realize you need more than you thought you would.

It’s also important to consider that, while generators are certainly a hefty investment, they usually stand the test of time. Consumer-grade portable generators can run anywhere from 1,000 to 3,000 hours, sometimes more. If you run one for 100 hours per year, it should last for 10 to 30 years.

There are four basic types of generators, each with advantages and disadvantages.

The most traditional of the generator category, these run on carbon-based fuels like gasoline, propane, and natural gas. Some allow you to switch between them. Dual-fuel models add the option of either propane or natural gas, while tri-fuel versions offer all three fuel options. These generators can be small, offering as little as 1,000 watts of power output, or run very large, as much as 20,000 watts or more.

Different fuels offer different power capabilities. For example, a tri-fuel generator may offer 9,500 running watts and 12,500 starting watts with gasoline, but only 8,500/11,200 with propane and 7,500/9,500 with natural gas. Regardless of the fuel you choose, this type of generator must always be operated 5 to 20 feet away from the home or any window or door, because the carbon monoxide exhaust is deadly. Even a garage with an open door is not safe.

Often this type of generator is called portable, but that's a relative term: Almost all of them weigh more than 100 pounds, and many are upwards of 200. Many are on wheels, making them relatively easy to store in a garage and wheel outside when you need them, but actually moving one will likely require at least two people.

Inverter generators look and operate the same as other portable generators, but have an added component–a power inverter– that converts the alternating current (AC) power generated by its fuel source to DC (Direct Current), then inverts it back to AC before putting it out.

Generators without an inverter generate varying degrees of distortion in the sine wave of the alternating current, which can trigger power surges and other issues in some electronics. The inverter stabilizes the power, almost like a filter, “cleaning” it for more sensitive electronics like TVs and laptops.

So why doesn’t every generator have an inverter? Inverters are expensive, so the generators are, too.

Realistically speaking, you don’t need an inverter generator to power major appliances and your house power, but if you’re running a full home backup (or if you count a TV or laptop among your essential electronics), holding out for an inverter is a smart move.

While often referred to as “solar generators” because they can be paired with solar panels, power stations are not really true generators. They're advanced battery banks that store power, which you precharge using AC power or solar panels. They can’t generate electricity in real time.

But because they can offer many of the same electrical outputs as a fuel generator, they can function similarly to a traditional generator for as long as the batteries retain a charge. Some stations are very small and meant only for off-grid adventures like camping trips, but some are large enough to run whole-home systems that can produce 14,400 watts or more with expandable batteries.

Since power stations don't run on fuel or have an engine, they can be used indoors without the risk of carbon monoxide poisoning and operate with almost no noise.

The downside is that once the power is depleted, the power station must be recharged. If the power grid is still unavailable, it can take many hours in ideal sunny conditions if all you have are solar panels. You can use a traditional generator to recharge a power station, but some may view that as defeating the purpose.

Standby generators are permanently installed generators designed to automatically take over for your house power in the event of an outage. You can set them to power your whole house, or only specific circuits, giving you precise control. Standby generators start at around 10,000 watts (10 kilowatts) but can go up to much higher. For the average home, a range of 10 to 25 kilowatts is usually enough.

They typically require professional installation, making them the most costly option. Since many large portable generators can effectively back up a house for a few hours for a lot less money, standby models are still considered a more niche option.

When you draw too much power from your generator, its performance will start to decline. At home, your lights will start to dim or flicker, and appliances may turn off momentarily. The generator itself may also start to get louder, or sound like it’s working extra hard to keep up.

If you notice these signs, start turning off and unplugging non-essential power sources immediately, or turn the generator off entirely for ten minutes or so to reduce the power load.

This is an urgent problem, so do not wait to take action: If you overwork your generator for too long, it’s only a matter of time before it starts to overheat, produce smoke, or break down. The damage from an extended overload could permanently damage the generator or, worse, start a fire.

With the right size for your home, a portable or standby generator can run as long as you keep feeding it fuel and stay within its running wattage capacity; however, you should not run them indefinitely. Manufacturers recommend you allow them to rest for 30 minutes or more after at least 24 hours of use. Check the manual of your generator for its specific recommendations.

You will get more time from each tank of gas if you draw less power, so it helps to pay extra attention to your electricity usage when you’re running the generator.

Make sure to turn your gas and/or propane generators off when it’s time to refill the tank. Failing to do so could lead to a fire.

Power stations, since they rely on electricity or solar power, can almost always be charged while in use.

Never run a fuel-based generator (gasoline, propane, natural gas) indoors. Burning fuel releases carbon monoxide (CO), which will quickly build up to deadly levels in an enclosed space.

Set your generator up outside, between 5 and 20 feet away from your home and/or any garages, sheds, or other walls. Position it as far as possible from doors, windows, and vents, with the exhaust positioned away from the house, to minimize the possibility of fumes wafting in. If you keep it outside all year round, find a naturally covered position where snow won’t naturally pile up around it.

My generator is situated near my home’s power box, which is located on a windowless side of my home. It stands about five feet from the exterior wall, with plenty of space on all sides, but close enough to sit under the roof peak overhang 20 feet above.

There are two types of 3D printer built with home users in mind: FDM and resin. (While other types of 3D printers are available, they’re intended for industrial use, with a learning curve and price tag to match.) As a rule of thumb, FDM printers are faster and produce more rugged prints than resin, while resin printers excel at fine detail.

Fused deposition modeling printers (FDM) are like a hot glue gun for plastic. They feed a strand of plastic filament through a heated metal nozzle, laying out the print onto a heated metal bed. They’re cleaner and easier to use than resin, and require less safety precautions. They tend to have larger print volumes, but they don’t produce prints that are as highly detailed as resin.

Resin printers, on the other hand, create layers of material by dipping a flat bed into a vat of liquid resin, then curing a layer of material with UV light. (It’s similar to the process of making candles, where you dip a wick into wax repeatedly.) They are typically more complicated to use, as the models have to be washed and cured after printing is complete. Printer resins are toxic until cured, and using resin printers requires significant safety precautions. Resin printers produce highly detailed prints, prized by users ranging from tabletop gamers making miniatures to the dental offices, creating surgical implants and guides.

Both types of printing have their pros and cons, depending on your space, your style, and what items you want to print. Generally speaking, FMD printers are good for larger items, items that need to be more durable, or when you need to turn around prints quickly. They are also a good choice if you’ll be printing in areas with less ventilation or if you’ll have children or pets around while printing. Resin printers are a better choice if you want outstanding detail, and have room to set up a cleaning station for your prints.

FDM printers are usually described by the head movement (either CoreXY or bed-slinger) and whether or not it’s enclosed or open frame. CoreXY printers move the head left to right, and front to back. On a bed-slinger, the head moves left to right, but front to back adjustments are made by moving (“slinging”) the bed itself.

Enclosed printers are just that–enclosed on all sides. These are typically CoreXY printers, and allow for higher temperatures and provide filtered vents for filaments that produce more fumes. They also usually cost more than open-bed printers.

To start printing, you’ll need some 3D models and software (called a slicer) to send them to your printer. Luckily, some of the best options are free.

Every 3D Printer uses some type of slicer software, which allows your printer to analyze objects and create a blueprint for making a 3D printed copy. True to its name, it creates a digital model and “slices” it into anywhere from one to a few thousand printable layers, creating the building blocks of a 3D print design. The result is a detailed set of instructions for printing, called G-code. When you load a design, your printer follows the G-code to assemble the design, layer by pre-sliced layer.

Printer manufacturers provide a proprietary slicer or an open-source slicer customized for their specific machines. For testing purposes, I used the slicer provided by the manufacturer. Honestly, once you get used to one slicer, they’ll all feel pretty similar.

Creating your own print designs isn’t necessary, though, if you don’t feel compelled. There’s no shortage of sites with free or paid 3D print designs that you can download and print. Once you have a little bit of experience printing, there’s a whole world of printable tools and products to explore within the 3D printing community.

When a plastic part breaks, there’s a good chance someone else has run into the same issue and created a 3D version of that part to download and print. Many printer manufacturers support some kind of community site, where users can upload, remix, and review 3D models.

Resin printers pose more risks, as resin itself is toxic, and should not be allowed to touch unprotected skin, and should only be used in an area where the fumes can be safely vented outdoors. Resin printers also use UV light to cure the prints, which can damage your eyes if not properly filtered.

FDM safety precautions are fairly straightforward. First, don’t stick your hand in the print area while the printer is running. This may sound obvious, but it’s surprisingly tempting to do, since sooner or later you’ll spot a bit of loose filament or some other bit of debris laying on the bed in the middle of a print. It’s very easy to reach in and grab that debris without pausing the print -- but the print head moves very fast and without warning. That nozzle is probably around 300 degrees celsius / 580 Fahrenheit, and having it slam into your knuckle can leave a puncture and a burn.

Second, make sure the room where you operate the printer is well ventilated. Proper ventilation is especially important when using high VOC filaments. These include acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) and acrylonitrile styrene acrylate (ASA), but the fumes will vary by manufacturer, so err on the side of caution.

Resin requires more extensive safety precautions. You’ll need to invest in PPE such as nitrile gloves, a respirator (use filters rated for VOCs and not just dust debris), and possibly UV safety glasses. When resin printing, venting is even more important. In most cases it’s easy to safely vent your 3D printer, but if you’re concerned, consider getting an air quality monitor to ensure that you’re working in a safe environment.

Your primary expense using a 3D printer is your printing material, which will be plastic filament for these printers, but could be other materials with more advanced models. You can buy a one-kilogram reel of PLA filament for about $12 to $19. If you need a specialty filament, you’ll likely pay double that amount, and prices can go much higher for premium materials.

Then there’s the hit to your electric bill. FDM printers typically use about 50 to 100 watts to operate, depending on what material you’re printing and the ambient temperature. If your electricity is 20 cents per kilowatt-hour, your printers will likely cost less than 2 cents per hour to operate.

Lastly, there will be other costs over time: You may need replacement bed plates for FDM printers, or post-processing supplies for resin printers. Other parts may break and need replacing, as well.
