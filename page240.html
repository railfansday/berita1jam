AI Is Dividing Product Designers: Exploring vs. Building

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A week ago, I attended PUSH UX in Munich, Europe's largest conference on product design and user experience, featuring speakers from Anthropic, Wise, Google, IKEA, n8n, and other prominent organizations. On my way home, I kept thinking about one recurring theme: product design teams are approaching AI from fundamentally different perspectives.

Some are cautiously experimenting with AI while trying to fit it into established processes. Others have already integrated it into their daily workflows, connecting research, prototyping, design, and code with fewer handoffs. The difference goes beyond tool adoption. It reflects how teams understand their work, organize collaboration, and measure product value.

Paulo Oliveira from IKEA highlighted a fundamental problem with the way organizations approach AI:

"Most efforts start with tools, copilots, and automation, and mistake faster cycles for value. The result is a pile of pilots nobody adopted, and a lot of people wondering what happened to the work they were good at. The alternative: to build something that fits, lasts, and makes an organization better at what it already does well. That starts by refusing the AI first, AI everything instinct."

I think this captures one of the central challenges in product design today.

Adding AI to an existing workflow can make individual tasks faster without improving the product or the way a team operates. Organizations can accumulate tools and experiments while leaving the underlying processes unchanged.

Meaningful adoption starts with understanding where teams lose time, which handoffs create friction, and where AI can improve how products are researched, designed, built, and validated.

For years, product design and software development operated as distinct stages. Designers worked in Figma, documented interactions, prepared specifications, and handed their work to engineers for implementation. This division made sense when building and testing functional interfaces required substantial engineering resources. AI-assisted development is changing that relationship.

After years of designing fintech and blockchain-based products, I kept encountering the same limitation: I could design an experience without having full control over how it was implemented.

About a year ago, I started building websites and applications from scratch with AI tools. As I described in my previous HackerNoon article, Claude Code Finally Made Me an Engineer After 10 Years in Figma, my workflow shifted from almost entirely Figma to roughly 90% Claude Code.

Working closer to production code lets me build, test, and iterate on interfaces directly, with fewer handoffs between design and engineering.

For product teams, this opens up several possibilities:

This doesn't mean every designer needs to become a full-time software engineer. Deep specialization remains essential, particularly in complex products. However, designers can work closer to implementation and contribute beyond the traditional boundaries of their roles.

Another exciting development is how AI is changing prototyping. Think about how difficult it used to be to create an interactive prototype for a specific device, such as an Apple Watch or an in-car infotainment system. Even without writing production code, designers can now use AI to build interactive prototypes tailored to specific hardware and explore how their ideas work in real-world contexts.

As AI makes prototyping, coding, and exploring alternatives easier, the focus of design work is shifting toward decision-making and product quality. Understanding whether it solves the right problem, meets user needs, and works reliably requires experience and critical judgment.

This is where faster workflows can create lasting value. Teams have more opportunities to test assumptions, explore alternatives, and identify problems before committing significant resources to a solution. However, individual productivity gains aren't enough. Organizations need to connect design, engineering, and product teams around shared workflows, clear quality standards, and meaningful evaluation of AI-generated output.

I'm particularly interested in how this can shape an AI-first product culture. The goal is to encourage experimentation while maintaining the technical rigor, security, and reliability that deep tech products demand.

Many designers argue that AI-generated product experiences lack quality. Are they right? Sometimes. Can the results improve? Absolutely.

AI-assisted research and discovery can produce shallow or generic results, especially when designers rely on AI without applying their own expertise and critical judgment. The quality of the outcome depends on how thoughtfully people guide, evaluate, and refine the process. Julia Bastian from Alasco explored this challenge in a session on AI discovery slop, examining how to recognize and address low-quality AI-generated research and insights.

The PUSH UX conference reinforced how differently the design community is responding to AI. Some teams are still finding their footing, while others are already changing how they work. My own experience has made me more comfortable working across design, product strategy, and engineering, but every team will need to find its own approach.

The important shift is to reconsider established workflows, explore where new capabilities can make a difference, and take greater ownership of business outcomes.

Michael John Roberts Dies: ‘TripTank’, ‘BoJack Horseman’ Animator, Director & Producer Was 48

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Michael John Roberts, an animator and director of such series as Comedy Central’s TripTank and Netflix’s BoJack Horseman, died of glioblastoma Wednesday, November 11, in Los Angeles. He was 48.

His death was announced by his family, including his wife, the One Life to Live and Gossip Girl actor Bree Williamson. The announcement states that Roberts died peacefully at home surrounded by his family.

“With heavy hearts, we share the passing of Michael John Roberts,” the announcement reads. “Michael saw the world a little differently. A gifted artist with an extraordinary talent, he could find something worth exploring in what others might overlook. His creativity was not limited to what he put on paper; it shaped the way he thought, questioned, and understood the world around him.”

Born December 20, 1977, in Toronto, where he was raised, Roberts got his start as an animator in the late 1990s and early 2000s on such children’s series as the animated Angela Anaconda and the animation-live action hybrid The Hoobs, both produced by Canada’s Decode Entertainment (The Hoobs was co-created and co-produced by The Jim Henson Company). Although starting in children’s television, Roberts would become best known for his work on animated comedy geared to adults. He co-created and directed Comedy Central’s 2014-2016 sketch comedy series TripTank, which featured voice work from Bob Odenkirk, Larry David, Niecy Nash, Zach Galifianakis and others, and he animated the 2015 opening sequence of BoJack Horseman (he would direct two episodes of the Netflix series).

He subsequently directed episodes of, among others, the Bill Burr series F Is For Family, the TBS series Final Space, MrBeast Lab, the 2023 Apple TV series Strange Planet and, most recently, a 2026 episode of Masters of the Universe: Tales From Eternia. As a producer, his credits included the 2018 John Cena-Kat Dennings YouTube Red series Dallas & Robo, the 2024 series Hot Wheels Let’s Race and a 2026 TV special from the Hot Wheels franchise.

In addition to his wife, who played the popular character Jessica Buchanan on One Life to Live from 2002 to 2012, Roberts is survived by son McGreggor; sister Jane Roberts; niece Gwen; nephew Vaughan; and cousin Hrothgar Cashmore, whom he saw as a brother.

“We were supposed to have more time,” his family wrote. “More conversations over a good cup of coffee. More spirited debates about matters both profound and wonderfully insignificant. More laughter, more stories, more music and more ordinary moments that would someday become extraordinary memories.

“Although his illness came suddenly and the time that followed was heartbreakingly short, it cannot define the man he was. His life is better remembered through what he created, the conversations he inspired, the friendships he nurtured, and the love he gave so freely to those closest to him.”

‘La Bola Negra’ Creatives Faced a Ticking Clock to Finish the Film for Cannes, But Waiting for Snow Almost Kept the Ending From Being Shot

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

“La Bola Negra” editor Alberto Gutiérrez knew he was up against time — the plan was that they wanted to take the film to the Cannes Film Festival, and the deadline was March.

Filming began in November, and Gutiérrez was editing the movie. But there was one problem. The film didn’t have its ending, and it was a pivotal scene.

Directed by Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrossi (known as Los Javis), the film, now playing in theaters, is based on an unfinished play of the same name written by Spanish playwright Federico García Lorca in 1936. Tragically, Lorca never got the chance to finish the play. He was shot dead by nationalist forces after being imprisoned at the beginning of the Spanish Civil War. The film navigates three timelines. In one, Carlos (Milo Quifes) is a young man trying to join a casino group in Granada, Spain. His membership is decided by a vote using white balls for acceptance and black balls for rejection. He is rejected because he is gay. At the heart of his story is conflict, as he has to make choices in life and deal with his family.

When he goes to the casino to plead his case, he denies he’s gay. He wakes up covered in snow. As is thematic with Lorca’s poems, snow means death. He hears his grieving mother and asks her for a second chance after realizing that he’s dead. The snow is a metaphor about the cycle of life.

That key part with snow had yet to be shot. The filmmakers were hoping it would snow in Madrid in December. It didn’t. Gutiérrez says, “We went into January thinking it would snow.” Again, it didn’t.

But they were determined to include the snow sequence and not fake it because snow was integral to Lorca’s plays.

The clock was ticking on whether they would meet the deadline to finish the film and submit it to Cannes.

It still hadn’t snowed in Madrid. But it did snow in Granada, the southern region of the country. They decided they would go there to shoot it. “But it was February and we had one month before the Cannes deadline,” Gutiérrez says.

Gutiérrez explains, “We couldn’t do screenings of the movie because we couldn’t get people to see two hours of a movie and then tell them to watch the ending in theaters.”

The team had one month to work together to pull it off. “We said, ‘We have to trust each other and our instincts because if we start listening to everybody’s opinion on the film, we won’t make it to Cannes.’”

They worked around the clock in that last month to finish the film.

One person they did trust was Penélope Cruz. In the film, she plays Nené Romero, a cabaret singer, and they brought her into the equation.

But Gutiérrez felt immense pressure. “I had this huge responsibility because the script was so good, and I had to be as good as the rest of the film,” he recalls, crying in the next room as Cruz watched. “When the film stopped, someone knocked at my door. Javier Ambrosi told me, ‘I want you to meet somebody,’ and suddenly Penelope entered the room. She asked me, ‘Oh, but why are you crying?’ I said, ‘I’m not sure about the movie. I’m not sure if this is the best editing.’ She told me to stop crying because it was perfect.’”

With Cruz’s stamp of approval, they decided to send “La Bola Negra” to Cannes and it was accepted.

“We had two weeks to make changes, and I remember even then we were not sure about it,” he says.

Gutiérrez remembers the film premiering at the Lumiere Theater, with Tilda Swinton seated in front of him. He laughs, “I was watching the movie through the eyes of Tilda Swinton for two and a half hours. And I thought, ‘OK, this is the movie. This is what we’ve made.’”

After the film ended, it received a 16-minute standing ovation and the Best Director Award.

“We decided there that this was the final edit of the movie,” Gutiérrez says. “I think it’s good because maybe if we had continued editing, we wouldn’t have this special movie that we have now.”

“La Bola Negra” will stream on Netflix on Dec. 2.

This weekend marks Xbox's 25th birthday, and while any celebration of the milestone is going to be muted in view of the layoff bloodbath Microsoft is currently engaging in, the gaming division is taking steps to keep us thinking about the brand in a positive light. That includes a fresh expansion to the list of original Xbox games that are now backwards compatible on PC.

After the four original Xbox games that Microsoft brought to PC earlier this year, another 21 have joined the list – a total of 25 games for the console's 25th birthday. The new list of games is an utterly bizarre one, largely dominated by LucasArts titles. That includes plenty of beloved Star Wars titles, alongside largely forgotten obscurities like Secret Weapons Over Normandy and Thrillville.

Some of the titles you might recognize, like Star Wars classics Battlefront 2, Jedi Outcast, and Knights of the Old Republic, are odd choices in themselves, since those games are already available on PC. Assuming Xbox is using the same emulation-based solution to port these games, they're all going to be substantially worse experiences than simply picking up the native PC versions for the same price on Steam.

That goes double for The Elder Scrolls 3: Morrowind, which is also on the list of newly added games. The Xbox version of Morrowind was a stunning technical achievement back in 2002, but it was notoriously janky even at the time, filled with bugs and technical limitations that have been robustly addressed on PC. I struggle to come up with a compelling reason anyone would want to play this version over the PC original today, especially with all the mods available for the RPG.

There are two games I absolutely will not complain about, however: TimeSplitters 2 and TimeSplitters: Future Perfect. Developed by the Rare veterans at Free Radical Design, the TimeSplitters series was a spiritual successor to classic N64 games GoldenEye and Perfect Dark, refreshing the FPS gameplay for a new generation with fun weapon gimmicks and charmingly goofy characters.

TimeSplitters never moved beyond cult classic status, and attempts to revive the series ended in failure. None of these games ever got PC ports, which makes their inclusion arguably the most notable part of this Xbox backwards compatibility update.

Emmylou Harris, Sheryl Crow, Elvis Costello, More Celebrate Gram Parsons at the Troubadour

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Psych-country pioneer Gram Parsons’ induction into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame this week has been a long time coming. Few cult heroes have had as much of a lasting impact as Parsons, who in the decades since his death in 1973, has inspired literally generations of musicians, despite never having achieved commercial success. That was evident in the lineup for “A Song for Gram,” a tribute concert held Wednesday at the Troubadour in Los Angeles, which featured disciples and devotees of the “cosmic American” sound Parsons pioneered.

The concert (produced by Parsons’ daughter Polly along with Vector Management, the Rock Hall, and Rolling Stone co-editor in chief Shirley Halperin) brought together collaborators (Emmylou Harris), longtime champions (Elvis Costello, Sheryl Crow), and acolytes (Jesse Welles, Margo Price, Madison Cunningham), alongside musical scholars like Jim Lauderdale and Black Crowes, who regularly perform songs from the Parsons canon. With “cosmic accompaniment” by stellar house band Beachwood Sparks, the totality of talent channeled the singer-songwriter’s magic while exploring the entirety of his deep-but-short catalog. Selections came from his solo work and his time in the Flying Burrito Brothers, the Byrds, and early days with the International Submarine Band.

Polly Parsons kicked off the night with a speech that set out the vision of Gram’s music. “He looked at the landscape of American song,” she said from the stage, “and saw a map where all the roads connected … It was a seamless tapestry that wove together the deep, raw heartbreak of traditional country, the spiritual urgency of soul, the gritty energy of rock, and the storytelling of folk.”

Neo-folk songwriter Jesse Welles, for his part, said he realized that country music could also include rock energy by discovering Parsons’ catalog. His solo version of “Hickory Wind” was Dylanesque in the best way — complete with harmonica — while his duet with Margo Price on “Return of the Grievous Angel,” faithful to the original with Emmylou Harris, sounded like the two walked off the record onto the stage. Considering the L.A. setting, where Parsons spent his later years living and recording, the sweetest moment of the night was surprise guest Sheryl Crow, who describes singing “Juanita” with Emmylou Harris as being “like heaven.” The duo had recorded a version of the song for 1999’s  Return of the Grievous Angel: A Tribute to Gram Parsons, and nearly three decades later, their voices blended so beautifully that a rapt crowd turned silent to give their full attention. Harris was rightly the performer emeritus of the night, crooning alongside everyone from Elvis Costello (“Sleepless Nights”) to Jim Lauderdale (“The King of Broken Hearts,” recorded in 1991 as a duet with Harris) to Mike Campbell, who conjured the spirit of his late bandmate Tom Petty for “Love Hurts,” another highlight of the night. 'Avatar: Seven Havens’ Season 2 Release Date Announcement Includes Some Shocking Good News

Book 2: Discovery will feature a big reunion and more answers about Korra

Book 2: Discovery will feature a big reunion and more answers about Korra

Avatar: Seven Havens spent its entire first season in the Haven of Ellora, but as the show's title makes clear, that's just one of many Havens spread across this post-apocalyptic landscape. Paramount Plus announced Thursday that Book 2: Discovery premieres surprisingly soon, while also confirming that Avatar Pavi’s (Saheli Khan) journey to repair the broken world is about to pick up speed. The announcement, which also confirms Avatar: Seven Havens Book 3 will come soon after Book 2, includes a synopsis that answers some of our most pressing questions following the season 1 finale: Is this now Pavi’s story? What will happen to Nisha (Aishu Devan)? Where will the plot take them and when will they reunite? Already, we’re getting answers to these and other questions.

Spoilers follow for Avatar: Seven Havens season 1.

Like Book 1: Survival, the next season of Seven Havens consists of 13 episodes released over four consecutive Fridays:

In Book 2, entitled ‘Discovery’, Pavi continues her chakra journey to the Floating Isles and learns more about what happened to Korra. Meanwhile, Nisha discovers vital information about the mysterious spirit energy Pavi released from Ellora. The girls are brought together in New Ba Sing Se, along with all their unresolved issues.

Throughout Book 1, Ellora was plagued by the Anomaly, a sort of spiritual affliction associated with fear that manifested as a diamond-shaped entity made of red energy. After a huge concentration of that energy exploded near Ellora, it created a large dome that threatened to consume the entire Haven. Only by unblocking her own root chakra was Pavi able to redirect the energy through her body and into the sky, where it manifested as an aurora. Based on a vision she received right after, another Haven called the Floating Isles needs her help. So at the end of Book 1, Pavi sets out into the Harshlands with Jae (Major Curda) at her side.

While inside the Anomaly dome, however, Pavi had learned the truth about her and Nisha’s past: Their parents had tried to surrender Nisha to the White Lotus, fearing her powers. In a fit of despair, Nisha emitted a jolt of red lightning that caused a rockside to seemingly crush her parents and sever Pavi’s leg. The twins then have a fight, and Pavi decides to leave Nisha behind. That accounts for the aforementioned “unresolved issues.”

My best guess about the “mysterious spirit energy Pavi released” has a lot to do with the state of the world. Bender birth rates are on the decline, spirits are virtually nonexistent, and the land itself is in a sorry state of decay. It would appear that spiritual energy worldwide has ceased flowing properly. Which is why the series has put so much emphasis on chakras. If the flow of energy in the world has stagnated into gross pockets of stale liquid like the red pool seen underneath Ellora, then transforming it back into its natural state could be precisely the mission that the Avatar(s) must accomplish to fix the world.

Pavi learning more about what happened to Korra in Book 2 is a welcome detail that feels like a must. One of Book 1’s more compelling sub-plots involves a flashback that the twins experience of Korra seemingly destroying Republic City. Everybody blames her for the Cataclysm, but there must be a deeper explanation there. What really happened to Avatar Korra?

The official description for Book 2 confirms, like I theorized, that the next stop on the Avatar’s journey after the Floating Isles is New Ba Sing Se. Pavi’s visions draw a clear connection between saving the Seven Havens and unblocking her own seven chakras. Ellora is the red earth chakra associated with survival and blocked by fear. Since the Floating Isles is associated with the color orange, we know it’s the second chakra which focuses on pleasure and is blocked by guilt.

In the Seven Havens world map shown during the show’s opening credits, the Haven associated with the yellow chakra is located in the northeast corner of what used to be the Earth Kingdom, which is exactly where Ba Sing Se was located until the Cataclysm. The third chakra is associated with willpower and is blocked by shame. That makes New Ba Sing Se a fitting place for the twins to reunite and grapple with “all their unresolved issues.” Think of the shame Nisha feels for what she did to their family. Think of the shame Pavi probably feels for abandoning Nisha after everything they’ve both gone through. And think of the shame they both feel for being the dual reincarnation of Avatar Korra, the person everyone thinks destroyed the world.

Letting go of all this shame seems like a natural place for Book 2 to go, which also means we’re probably going to get some cleaner answers about what actually caused the Cataclysm and what Korra’s real role was in it all.

To hear that Nisha has a parallel role that intersects with her sister again in Book 2 is a relief. Plenty of fans have voiced concerns about Seven Havens becoming “the Pavi show.” The finale positioned Pavi as the Avatar setting out to fix the world, while Nisha simply stayed behind and was praised as the hero of Ellora. For them to reunite seems like a wise decision.

Book 1 also established a practical reason for Nisha to travel. When members of the White Lotus tested her bending powers, they said that she needed to train in other Havens to sharpen her skills. And when Nisha exposed herself as the Avatar to fight Agam (Sakina Jaffrey) during the summit, Agam made a point to say that Nisha only knows Elloran bending styles. Each Haven seemingly has its own bending techniques, which could position New Ba Sing Se as a training destination that puts her on a collision course with Pavi, rather than somewhere she visits specifically to find her sister. Although, whatever information Nisha uncovers about the energy Pavi released could be so important that she has to find Pavi to tell her.

Paramount Plus hasn’t announced a premiere date or title for Book 3, but the streamer did confirm that it will also consist of 13 episodes and be released later in 2027. Book 4 will conclude the series, with release details still to come.

The accelerated release schedule means we may see substantially more of this world before the end of next year. If Book 2 covers the Floating Isles and New Ba Sing Se, four Havens remain for the final two seasons.

Following the chakra sequence, the next two would be green and blue: the heart chakra, which deals with love and is blocked by grief, and the sound chakra, which deals with truth and is blocked by lies. Feather Mountain and Akasha are possible matches, though their respective colors remain unconfirmed. Beyond those lie the light chakra, concerned with insight and blocked by illusion, and the final thought or crown chakra, associated with cosmic energy and blocked by earthly attachment. The light chakra appears to be located in or near the ruins of the old Fire Nation, and Republic Forest is almost certainly the final stop.

That gives the remaining story a framework, but it doesn’t dictate who visits each location or when. The twins could continue together after New Ba Sing Se or go their separate ways to help the remaining Havens separately. Saving Ellora felt like a big deal that took an entire season, but we’re about to step into a much bigger world in May when Book 2 begins.

Early humans may have kept souvenirs half a million years ago

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Early humans may have collected keepsakes 500,000 years ago, a habit researchers suggest could have helped shape memory and a sense of self.

Somewhere in your house there’s a stone from a beach. A shell from a holiday. A pinecone your kid picked up on a walk three years ago that you can’t bring yourself to throw away.

It does nothing but it means everything. And according to a new study, you’re not the first human to feel that way. Not by about half a million years.

The study was led by Ran Barkai and Yoav Ram at Tel Aviv University.

The team argues that early humans in the Levant were already picking up colorful pebbles, fossil-studded rocks, and old weathered tools and carrying them home for reasons that may have gone beyond practical use.

According to the scientists, the habit of anchoring memories to objects didn’t just reflect the human mind. It may have helped build it.

The most striking evidence comes from the Sakhnin Valley in Lower Galilee.

Out of more than 200 surveyed handaxes at this Acheulean landscape, roughly ten were shaped around fossil imprints or unusual geological formations embedded in flint nodules.

Those nodules were harder to knap. Toolmakers normally avoided them.

But someone chose them anyway, then went out of their way to position the fossil or geological oddity at the center of the tool’s face.

The researchers argue that these choices were deliberate.

At Qesem Cave, small round colorful pebbles showed up starting around 400,000 years ago and kept showing up for the next 200,000 years. Their practical purpose remains unclear.

Then there’s something stranger. At Revadim, Jaljulia, and Qesem Cave, people picked up old flint tools that had been lying in the landscape long enough to develop a visible patina.

They resharpened the cutting edges, but they kept the old surfaces: the scars, the color changes, the story written into the stone.

Under magnification, wear traces show these recycled tools were barely used in their second life.

The fresh edges made them functional; preserving the patina may have held personal meaning.

The researchers call them “memory objects.”

Most discussions of weird prehistoric objects land on the same explanations. Art. Ritual. Status symbols. Shared cultural meaning.

This study does something different. It argues these objects worked at the personal level first.

No audience was required and no shared convention needed. Just one person who remembers what the stone means.

The authors lay out four things that separate a souvenir from a decoration: it’s tied to a specific episode in someone’s life, it helps verify a real experience against an imagined one, keeping it takes deliberate effort, and its meaning is private.

That last part matters. A keepsake points to something that happened to you, and only you need to know its meaning.

So how does holding a rock help you remember? The mechanism the authors point to is source monitoring. That refers to the brain’s way of sorting real experiences from dreams, hearsay, and imagination.

The hippocampus reconstructs episodes from partial cues. The perirhinal cortex handles familiarity, and the parahippocampal cortex fills in contextual detail.

The authors propose that a keepsake’s physical features could engage these memory systems together.

That matters more than it sounds. In small societies built on reciprocity, people who could reliably distinguish real experiences from imagined ones may have had an advantage.

Over enough generations, routine souvenir-keeping may have created a “souvenir niche” that favored brains better wired for autobiographical memory and sense of self.

“The disappearance of large animals, particularly elephants, required early humans to develop new hunting methods and become familiar with hunting grounds inhabited by animals such as fallow deer and wild cattle,” Barkai said.

“Objects taken from these places may have helped them maintain a personal connection with the places that would henceforth provide their livelihood.”

The elephant vanished from the Levant around 400,000 years ago. So did Homo erectus and  the Acheulean tool tradition.

Everything shifted. Smaller, faster prey meant learning new territories from scratch.

Familiar landscapes couldn’t be taken for granted anymore. Portable pieces of those places became worth carrying home.

The authors are clear this isn’t proof. Not every odd stone was a keepsake.

But the pattern is there. Objects with no clear practical role were carried far from their source, curated over long use-lives, and found near hearths and living areas rather than tool-making debris.

Aesthetics and raw material shortage don’t explain it cleanly.

“When early humans brought a colorful stone or an old tool they had found in the landscape back to their cave, that object may have helped them remember a significant event or encounter for the rest of their lives,” Ram said.

“In this way, keepsakes may have become a kind of ‘external memory device,’ supporting the evolution of brain mechanisms responsible for autobiographical memory, which is so characteristic of us as humans and so important in shaping our identity.”

The study is published in the journal Evolutionary Anthropology.
