Basque Cinema Expands Its (Rapidly) Growing Footprint in Tallinn

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

With Basque filmmakers in the Tallinn Spotlight section and buzzy competition title 'Caballé,' the never-healthier regional production hub is reaching territories not yet conquered: "It opens different doors."

This year, Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF) is letting Basque cinema bask — pun intended — in the spotlight.

Through dedicated investment over the last 10 years, the Basque film and TV output has never been stronger. The region has attracted productions from all over the world thanks to an alluring 60 percent tax rebate — increasing to 70 percent for projects shot in the Basque language, Euskara — allowing the area to lay claim to Catherine Zeta-Jones’ Kill Jackie and Nanni Moretti’s It Will Happen Tonight, among a slew of Spanish projects that catapulted the region to a record 45 Goya nominations earlier this year. This week, Tallinn’s Spotlight section represents the booming Basque audiovisual in territory not yet conquered. “It opens different doors,” begins Laura Lizuma, a Latvian producer based in San Sebastian who comes to the festival as part of the Basque delegation this year. “If the Basque country was in the spotlight in Argentina and Spain, that would be understandable — but the Basque industry is very small and when we think about global cinema… It’s a great place to discover new possibilities of co-production because [when else] would a Basque producer come to Estonia or the Baltic states?”

Lizuma arrives in the Estonian capital as a producer on The River Woman, one of three Basque projects selected as part of the Tallinn Spotlight to receive development support. The feature film, directed by Tamara García Iglesias and co-written with Jon Bilbao, is described by Lizuma as “a rather unconventional western — one that takes the familiar codes of the genre and turns them around to tell a story from a woman’s perspective.” García’s film is one of many Basque films hitting this Tallinn edition, joined by the likes of Daniel Romero Bueno’s debut feature The Daughters (Las Hijas) in the Midnight Shivers section and Joana Moya Blanco’s La Koreana from the festival’s industry program.

But the buzziest title of all competes in Tallinn’s official competition selection. Caballé, from Venezuelan filmmaker Patricia Ortega, is a biographical portrait of Spanish opera diva Montserrat Caballé that shot mostly in Bilbao. The movie is a Spain-led co-production between Set Màgic Audiovisual, La Claqueta, Basque company Amania Films, La Charito and Belgium’s Menuetto Film, with Film Factory Entertainment handling international sales.

“I was interested in her contradictions,” Ortega begins to The Hollywood Reporter about what drew her to the famed opera singer, depicted in the film by Begoña Alberdi. “Her strength and vulnerability, her doubts and insecurities, but also her determination to make her way in a world dominated by men.”

Ortega describes the Basque contribution as “an essential part of bringing Caballé to the screen.” This doesn’t just refer to the production side of things, but also to the talent, commitment and “generosity” of those who worked on the film. “Montserrat Caballé was not only a legend, she was also a deeply multicultural woman,” explained the director. “She spoke several languages and worked with people from all over the world. In a way, that is something we experienced while making the film in the Basque Country: people from different places and backgrounds coming together around a shared creative vision. I think she would have appreciated that spirit of collaboration.”

She vouches for the work of the Basque crews and support from the region’s government, saying that Bilbao’s logistical strengths made the filming process “much easier.” Ortega added: “There was also the experience of living in the city for several weeks. We found a place that welcomed us with open arms, with its remarkable variety of landscapes, the generosity and warmth of its people, and its rich and diverse cultural life.”

Those virtues are on display and then some in Tallinn, where international producers are able to get a sense of the strength of the Basque audiovisual industry and production capabilities. That’s according to Esther Cabero Gonzalo, the shorts programmer at Zineuskadi, a company that works strategically to promote the Basque audiovisual sector, partnering with every production output in the region and working with the local government to help facilitate co-productions, distribution and sales deals, as well as securing films a coveted slot at major film festivals.

“Having a delegation in Tallinn creates opportunities to meet people, establish direct contacts and develop potential collaborations,” says Cabero, who adds that a representative for the Etxepare Basque Institute, the organization leading the promotion of Basque culture internationally, will be talking in Tallinn about the tempting tax incentives. “I think all these elements help demonstrate just how much can be achieved in such a small territory,” says Cabero.

The Basque shorts program, Kimuak, had one film — Raúl Barreras’s Meanwhile, Then — premiere in the PÖFF Shorts section over the weekend. Barrera’s feature follows five researchers from the future who arrive in a valley in the Basque Country to record gestures and emotions that have disappeared over time. Through interviews and observations of daily life, they delve into the private lives of the inhabitants — but soon the framework underpinning their research begins to crumble.

Six shorts from previous editions are set to screen through the rest of the festival as part of the Basque Spotlight. Cabero is full of pride showcasing such a broad range of work from male and female filmmakers, as well as documentaries, animations and more, to the crowds in Tallinn. “We can promote our culture, our language, our identity, and make connections with international audiences,” she says. “But it’s also a way to promote those names, the directors, so they can find opportunities abroad and co-productions.”

For Cabero, it isn’t just the draw of Basque talent, production companies, studios and technical facilities that has helped make the Spanish region a thriving film and TV hub, but the historic towns, industrial environments, as well as forests and dramatic natural scenery.

Interestingly enough, it’s also Lizuma’s appreciation for the Basque Country’s sweeping landscapes that will help The River Woman achieve high-scale cinema at a lower cost. “With this project, in particular, the first idea is to shoot in the Basque Country because of the nature,” says Lizuma. “We are planning to shoot between the Basque Country and Navarre, but this project is developed from the beginning to be an international co-production. So we are looking for other territories that we could film [in], […] and we really have the intention to get those woods or those rivers here in [the] Basque Country or the Baltic states,” she says. Basque cinema, she adds, often capitalizes on its “pretty strong” relationship with nature.

Lizuma has lived in Spain for 10 years now and can measure in great depth the success of the concentrated backing of the Basque film and TV sector. “There are a lot of projects coming [to the] Basque Country […] because they can get this tax incentive. So there are a lot of projects that come there with this interest, but also for Basque directors and producers, there are a lot more projects that are not so small anymore. When I came, there were a lot of self-discoveries — very small, very local. Now there are projects that raises three million [euros] in funding. That was impossible before.”

And while Ortega’s award hopeful filmed mostly in one of the region’s busiest and most vibrant cities, what strikes her about Basque cinema is its ability to simultaneously straddle a particular place and culture while speaking to audiences far beyond it. “There isn’t just one Basque cinematic voice,” she says, “there are many filmmakers with distinct sensibilities, and that diversity is one of its great strengths.”

She touts the work of David Pérez Sañudo, who recently presented the brilliant Sacamantecas, Alauda Ruiz de Azúa with the five-time Goya Award-winning Los domingos, and Estibaliz Urresola’s 20,000 Species of Bees, shortlisted for best international feature at the 2024 Oscars. “They are examples of the quality of the work coming out of the Basque Country,” says Ortega.

“I would also like to mention Nerea Torrijos, who was the head of costume design on Caballé. She is a wonderful example of how Basque talent extends across every area of filmmaking,” Ortega adds. This particularly personal testament is indicative of the kind of relationships the Basque audiovisual industry has so far managed to cultivate. Will Tallinn also succumb to the region’s undeniable charm?

Why your fine-tuning job takes longer than the FLOPs say

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Predicting end-to-end fine-tuning time on shared GPU clusters, from 272 production runs.

A preview of our upcoming paper, Predicting End-to-End Fine-Tuning Time for Generative Language Models. The full version will be on arXiv soon.

GPUs are among the most expensive and scarcest resources in AI today. Anyone running a shared cluster has to decide, every day, which jobs go on which GPUs and in what order. That plan is only as good as the estimate of how long each job will take. When a job finishes hours early, GPUs sit idle until the next one is dispatched. When it runs hours late, everything queued behind it slips, experiments wait and delivery dates move.

Better duration estimates turn this into planning instead of guesswork: what runs, when, and on how many GPUs, with less idle capacity in between. Yet the estimate most teams use is a back-of-the-envelope FLOP calculation. It says nine hours, and the job finishes the next afternoon.

We wanted to know how far off that math really is, and whether we could do better before a job is even dispatched. So we took 272 real supervised fine-tuning (SFT) jobs from our production pool of NVIDIA H200 GPUs, about 7,382 GPU-hours in total, and compared analytical estimates against learned predictors.

The short version: the usual FLOP-based estimate explains little of what we observed (R² = 0.31, MAE 12.41 h). A CatBoost regressor that only sees information available before dispatch reaches R² = 0.98, with a mean absolute error of 1.71 h and a median error of 0.51 h in 5-fold cross-validation. The gap comes from two things a closed-form estimate cannot do. The regressor sees more training-specific signals, such as evaluation cadence, checkpoint policy, per-GPU load and fine-tuning regime, and it learns the nonlinear interactions between them from past runs.

For a dense Transformer, training costs roughly 6·P·D floating-point operations, where P is the parameter count and D the number of training tokens. Divide that by what your GPUs can deliver and you get a training-loop time:

Here N_GPU is the number of GPUs, FLOPS_peak is the peak throughput of one GPU from its spec sheet, and MFU (Model FLOPs Utilization) is the fraction of that peak the training actually achieves. Real jobs never run at peak because of memory traffic, communication and kernel inefficiencies, so MFU typically sits somewhere between 30% and 60%, and the right value for a given job is not known in advance.

This is a good description of the optimization loop. A job is more than its loop, though. Before the first step there is environment setup, model download and loading, and tokenization. During training there are periodic evaluations and checkpoint uploads. At the end, the final model goes to object storage. On a multi-tenant cluster, all of it competes with other jobs for network and storage I/O.

These overheads depend on configuration, and they do not scale neatly with workload or GPU count. Two jobs with the same nominal compute can finish hours apart.

To keep the comparison fair, the FLOP baseline we test counts the scheduled evaluation tokens as well as the training tokens, and we sweep MFU over a wide range.

We treat end-to-end duration as a supervised regression problem. Given a feature vector built from the job configuration and model metadata, predict the wall-clock time between the moment the job gets its GPUs and the moment it is done. Queue time is excluded.

We compared Linear Regression, Ridge, XGBoost and CatBoost. Feature selection combined correlation filtering, mutual information and permutation importance across 18 candidate feature sets, followed by a grid search with MAE as the criterion. The final CatBoost model uses 24 features.

Distribution of end-to-end duration across the 272 runs. Mean 23.43 h, median 15.29 h.

All numbers come from stratified 5-fold cross-validation. The first four rows are analytical and involve no fitting.

No single MFU value fixes the FLOP baseline. We swept MFU from 0.25 to 0.65. The best setting, 30%, still leaves a mean error above 12 hours on jobs that last 23 hours on average.

A more detailed analytical model does not close the gap. As a second baseline we took Calculon (Isaev et al., SC '23), an analytical performance model for LLM training from prior work. It builds an explicit layer graph and accounts for compute, memory traffic and inter-device communication. We extended the public implementation to cover LoRA, grouped-query attention, fused attention, bfloat16 and SwiGLU. Its estimates still correspond to a median of only 11% of the observed wall-clock time. That says nothing against Calculon as a step-level tool, which is what it was designed for. It shows how much of a production job happens outside the optimization step.

Linear models help, and then stall. Ridge brings MAE down to 7.23 h. The relationship between configuration and duration is not well captured by a purely linear model.

Gradient boosting does the heavy lifting. CatBoost and XGBoost land within one standard deviation of each other on every metric. We carry CatBoost forward because it has the lowest mean error across the board.

CatBoost's feature importance concentrates in a handful of features. Model and workload variables are there, as expected, but so are evaluation and checkpoint variables. num_eval_data, the total number of samples processed in evaluation over the life of the job, ranks first, followed by num_trainable_params, batch_eval_size_percent, num_all_params and num_gpus*batch_train_percent, with num_params_saved close behind. SHAP values tell the same story: high evaluation volume consistently pushes the predicted time up, while trainable parameter count has a nonlinear effect that only becomes large for the biggest models.

Mean feature importance of the CatBoost model across folds (PredictionValuesChange).

Feature importance is not a causal claim. Still, the pattern lines up with how a job is actually spent. How often you evaluate, how large the evaluation set is and how many checkpoints you persist all shape the wall-clock time, alongside model and dataset scale. These are choices people often make by habit when writing a training config.

One caveat on reading the plots: larger GPU allocations show positive contributions to predicted time. In our data, bigger allocations go with bigger jobs, so num_gpus acts partly as a proxy for job scale. It does not mean adding GPUs makes a job slower.

Looking at all 272 out-of-fold predictions, the median absolute error is 0.51 h. 80% of predictions are within 2.20 h and 90% are within 4.31 h. Broken down by job duration:

Up to 60 hours, the median error stays at about one hour or less. The long tail is where the model struggles, and with only 13 runs above 60 hours we read that as a data limitation.

Out-of-fold absolute error against real duration, colored by duration range. Errors stay low up to 60 h and widen in the sparse tail.

More data should still help. On a fixed 20% holdout, MAE drops from 1.63 h to 1.50 h when going from 85% to 100% of the training partition, with no clear plateau.

In practice, the predictor gives a scheduler a duration it can plan around before a job starts: which jobs to run together, when GPUs will free up, and whether a run will finish in time for its deadline.

What does transfer is the method: overhead-aware features, multicollinearity-aware selection and the comparison protocol. The full paper will report the feature formulas, the selection procedure, the search spaces and the final hyperparameters in full, so the pipeline can be rebuilt on any execution-log dataset with a similar structure.

Next on our list: hardware-specific descriptors and validation on other GPU types, transfer learning for configurations with few observations, other training objectives, and more data on long runs.

Nvidia said its AI data center loan guarantees won't kick in until facilities are built

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The company's maximum gross exposure on guarantees totaled $118 billion as of Oct. 25, and it has made no payments under them

Nvidia $NVDA said on Tuesday that its guarantees for data center buildouts take effect phase by phase, once each related building is completed and ready for service.

The largest guarantee, entered into in August, provides credit support on the land, power and shell buildout for about 4.25 gigawatts across nine buildings at SB Energy's PORTS-Pike Technology Campus in Ohio, the company said. The campus will host Nvidia infrastructure under 20-year leases to OpenAI, subject to limited exceptions.

The first guarantee is expected to take effect in fiscal 2029, Nvidia said. Its exposure builds as each building comes online, up to a maximum of $105 billion, then declines over each 20-year lease term as OpenAI makes lease payments. Nvidia has the option to provide credit support in phases for about 3.8 additional gigawatts as the site scales.

Maximum gross exposure on all of Nvidia's guarantees totaled $118 billion as of Oct. 25. That figure includes $105 billion in SB Energy credit support guarantees, $9.5 billion in power and systems credit support guarantees and $3.5 billion in land, power and shell guarantees for neoclouds. Nvidia said it has made no payments under its guarantees.

Nvidia said separate commitments to neoclouds take effect once the neoclouds secure financing to buy the data center infrastructure and the related compute is deployed, made operational and accepted by Nvidia. If those conditions are not resolved on time, certain commitments could be reduced or eliminated, the company said.

Interim CEO and CFO Colette Kress told analysts on Tuesday's earnings call that a guarantee ends if a customer is delayed or runs into trouble building, according to TechCrunch. "Our guarantee doesn't exist anymore," she said. Asked whether the terms were new, Kress said they simply spelled out conditions that already applied and did not signal a shift in direction.

Kress said Nvidia will treat help with land, power and shell for data centers as a top priority while reviewing which types of companies it will help finance, according to TechCrunch.

Nvidia reported fiscal third-quarter revenue of $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year earlier, on Tuesday. The results were its first since co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as CEO last week after admitting to an affair with a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, a major Nvidia customer. Kress took over as interim CEO while keeping her CFO role, and the board began a search for a permanent chief executive.

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Newsmax says the combined Fox-Roku would hold more than 70% of free ad-supported streaming TV, according to its complaint

Newsmax sued Fox in California federal court on Wednesday to stop the company from integrating Roku $ROKU into its operations, arguing the $22 billion merger harms competition, according to Bloomberg.

According to Bloomberg, the complaint says combining Roku with Tubi, Fox's free streaming service, would hand Fox over 70% of the free ad-supported streaming TV market. Newsmax argues that Fox, owner of the Fox News channel, has a motive to put Newsmax at a disadvantage in streaming television.

Fox completed its acquisition of Roku on Nov. 6. The Justice Department closed its antitrust investigation the same day and said Fox had committed to keeping Roku an open and neutral content partner.

The commitments include keeping non-Fox content available on Roku for a minimum of three years and refraining from discriminating against others' content in search results, according to Bloomberg. The Justice Department did not file the commitments in federal court, which skipped a public review by a judge.

Newsmax calls the commitments inadequate and unenforceable by a court, according to Bloomberg. Newsmax says it requested that Fox sign a binding contract, but Fox refused. Fox did not respond to a request for comment.

Fox announced the deal in June. In September, the company said it expected the deal to close in the first half of 2027, after the Justice Department sent second requests for information to Fox and Roku.

Under the terms, Roku stockholders receive $95.30 in cash and 0.9799 shares of Fox Class A common stock for each Roku share. Fox said it expects about $400 million of run-rate cost synergies within two years of closing.

"Beginning today, our top priority for the combined company is to continue to relentlessly serve audiences and partners, while reinforcing the openness and choice that is the essence of the Roku platform," Fox Executive Chair and CEO Lachlan Murdoch said in a statement at closing.

Should You Buy Walmart Stock Before Nov. 19? History Has a Clear Answer.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Walmart's earnings days are a coin flip, but its long-term record tells a much clearer story.

By Micah Zimmerman – Nov 18, 2026 at 1:51PM EST

Pop quiz: What did Walmart (WMT -0.16%) stock do the last four times the company reported earnings?

If you guessed "went up, because it's Walmart," you're one for four. Shares jumped about 6.5% after the November 2025 report, slipped about 1.4% in February, dropped about 7% in May, and tumbled about 9% in August.

Walmart reports again before the opening bell on Thursday, Nov. 19, with results due around 7 a.m. ET and a call with management an hour later. So should you buy before then? History gives a pretty clear answer -- just not the one you might expect.

Those four reactions had more to do with the forecast than the quarter. In May, sales beat expectations, but cautious guidance amid soaring gas prices sent shares lower. In August, Walmart raised its full-year outlook and still fell as Wall Street fixated on weaker-than-expected U.S. same-store sales. Walmart tends to lowball that forecast, too. After February's soft outlook, Baird analyst Peter Benedict pointed to the company's "history of conservatism."

Thursday's bar is high. Shares trade at about 46 times earnings and sit near their 52-week high after climbing about 7% in the past month. That's a rich P/E ratio, and CFO John David Rainey noted in August that Walmart was absorbing more than $2 billion in extra fuel costs "in arguably a softer consumer environment."

Zoom out, though, and those scary drops shrink fast. Someone who bought two days before August's plunge is still ahead today, and shares are up about 26% from a week before last November's report.

The longer view says the same thing. In its latest annual report, Walmart showed that $100 invested in February 2021, with dividends reinvested, grew to about $272 by early 2026, versus about $201 for the S&P 500. The interesting part is that the first two years were dull, with that $100 worth only about $106 by early 2023. That's the trouble with market timing. The payoff often shows up while you're not looking.

Walmart has also raised its dividend for 53 straight years. This move of over 50 straight years of raises makes it a Dividend King.

What keeps me interested is what Walmart actually does in the 90-odd days between earnings reports. Furner, a three-decade Walmart veteran, took over as CEO on Feb. 1, and he's betting on speed. "Speed isn't simply a fulfillment metric; it's an acquisition strategy," he said in August, adding that fast-delivery customers shop more often and are more likely to join Walmart+.

The newest piece arrives by air. On Nov. 5, Walmart agreed to add 300 Zipline drone delivery sites nationwide. "We see customers try drone delivery for the novelty and then continue to come back to it for the convenience," said Greg Cathey, who leads Walmart's e-commerce fulfillment transformation.

Walmart is also quietly making money on a lot more than groceries. Its advertising business, which includes Walmart Connect and Vizio, and its memberships both continued to grow last quarter. "The math isn't simply one plus one equals two," Furner said of how those businesses feed each other.

Then there's artificial intelligence. Walmart already sells through ChatGPT, Google's Gemini, and others, and it's rolling out Agent Profiles that let shoppers set a spending limit for an AI agent. "We price products, not people," the company wrote, promising agents see the same prices humans do. This holiday season, its Sparky assistant can even make an item's shelf tag light up in the store so you can find it.

So, should you buy Walmart stock before Nov. 19? History says the date barely matters. If you're buying just to catch a pop, the last four reports say you're flipping a coin. If you want to own Walmart for the next decade, buy when you're ready, maybe in pieces, so a rough Thursday becomes a chance to add more instead of a reason to panic.
