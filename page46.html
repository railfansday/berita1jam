You Can Buy a Fujifilm GFX Eterna 55 for $6,500 Less Than Before

Filmmakers will soon be able to get their hands on the large-format Fujifilm GFX Eterna 55 cinema camera for considerably less money than before.

As NewsShooter reports, a “body-only” version of the GFX Eterna 55 has appeared for preorder at some retailers, including B&H. It costs $9,999, compared to $16,499 for the standard GFX Eterna 55 package.

Saving $6,500 is significant, so what does that extra money actually get video creators?

The standard GFX Eterna 55 includes a custom 5-inch external touchscreen monitor with a mount and hood, a dedicated top handle with customizable controls, and AC power cables. Whether that bundle is worth $6,500 will depend on the individual user, of course.

PetaPixel YouTube host Dmitry Koshutin has used the GFX Eterna 55 extensively and believes the top handle is arguably the camera’s best feature.

“The top handle is probably the best feature of the Eterna,” Koshutin says. “It’s very sturdy, and the controls are very handy. I would still want that, knowing how well it’s built.”

As of yet, the GFX Eterna 55’s dedicated top handle is not listed as an optional accessory or available for separate purchase. At least for now, the only way for filmmakers to get it is by buying the standard GFX Eterna 55 kit for $16,499.

Koshutin is also a fan of the Eterna’s included external monitor, especially for filming fast-paced action, noting that its proprietary connection works very well. However, he adds that third-party monitors using SDI or HDMI can also be strong alternatives, offering larger or brighter displays.

“Having said that, $10,000 is quite a deal,” Koshutin concludes. “For filmmakers who already own top handle accessories and a monitor, shaving that extra $6,500 off makes a lot of sense without losing much functionality.”

The Fujifilm GFX Eterna 55 (Body Only) is available for preorder now at $9,999. It is also listed on Fujifilm’s own store, though it is marked “out of stock” and not yet available for preorder there. B&H currently lists the body-only GFX Eterna 55 as “coming soon.”

In 2024, when the Warriors and Steve Kerr agreed to a two-year extension paying him $17.5 million a season, other coaches celebrated — he had driven up the market price overall for coaches.

While other coaches' salaries may have climbed, Kerr remains the highest-paid coach in the league, according to Kurt Badenhausen of Sportico, who did the research on coaching contracts (which are not publicly available). Here are the top 10 highest-paid coaches, all making eight figures a year:

Steve Kerr (Warriors): $17.5 millionErik Spoelstra (Heat): $15 millionTyronn Lue (Clippers): $15 millionRick Carlisle (Pacers): $11 millionIme Udoka (Rockets): $11 millionJoe Mazzulla (Celtics): $11 millionMike Brown (Knicks): $10 millionQuin Snyder (Hawks): $10 millionTaylor Jenkins (Bucks): $10 millionDusty May (Mavericks): $10 million

A few thoughts from the Sportico research:

• Kerr, Spoelstra, and Lue also happen to be the NBA's three longest-tenured head coaches with their current teams.

• Jenkins and May were hired this summer with those eight-figure salaries, with Dallas paying up to get May out of Michigan (where he had just won a national championship). They got hired at higher salaries than established, very successful coaches such as Mark Daigneault in Oklahoma City and Chris Finch in Minnesota.

• The story reports the standard starting salary for an NBA head coach is at least $4 million a season. Two coaches are below that number: Doug Christie in Sacramento, and Micah Nori in Portland, who is on a much-discussed (and despised by other coaches and basketball people) one-year deal with less than $2 million guaranteed, and with options after it. The Best Celebrity Red Wines, According To Our Editors

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

These bottles have some serious star power.

Celebrities have taken over the liquor store, with everyone from rappers to actors to athletes launching their own bottles. And the section with the most star power is definitely the wine aisle.

Whether they own their own vineyards or simply slap their names on a label, celebrities sell wines from almost every corner of the world. To see whether any of them are worth the A-list endorsements, we tasted a selection of their most popular red wines. Here are the ones that won over Delish editors.

Cameron Diaz's wine brand, Avaline, specializes in bottles made with organically farmed grapes and minimal additives. Avaline's red blend is made in France with Rhône varietals like Grenache and Syrah.

Our editors appreciated its light- to medium-bodied flavor and bone-dry finish. Notes of red fruit and light spice add just enough depth to keep each sip interesting. And at $24 per bottle, this is a solid table wine that tastes much more expensive than it is.

Reality TV legend Lisa Vanderpump is no stranger to the food and beverage business. In addition to operating six restaurants, she’s also the mind behind her eponymous wine brand. And LVP’s Sonoma County Cabernet Sauvignon is as elegant and sophisticated as she is.

While other Cabernets can be bold and intense, this wine's beauty lies in its subtlety. One editor described it as a “summer Cab” because of its soft, delicate fruit profile. The flavor is complex without feeling too rich or heavy, making it an ideal red for dinner. And at $26 a bottle, it’s a good deal, too.

Famed film director Francis Ford Coppola isn’t just known for his Oscar-winning movies—he’s also a prolific winemaker. He sold his namesake winery to Delicato Family Wines in 2021, but the lineup is still just as strong as it was under his leadership.

We enjoyed all of Coppola’s reds, but the Archimedes Cabernet Sauvignon was a clear standout. Made with California-grown grapes and aged for 20 months in French and American oak barrels, this wine delivers what our editors described as the quintessential Cab, with a full-bodied, smooth flavor and notes of dark fruit and tobacco.

Depending on the vintage and retailer, prices can start around $70 and climb past $100. But if you want to impress both the film buffs and wine snobs in your life, it’s worth the splurge.

2027 Toyota Sienna Now Gets Up To 37 MPG Combined, And It'll Be Better To Drive Too

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you're still hating on minivans in The Year Of Our Lord 2026, the joke is officially on you. Minivans are exceptionally versatile vehicles that are objectively the best option for most modern SUV buyers, but their lack of a perceived cool factor prevents many buyers from even considering them. We love minivans, and one of the most compelling minivans on the market today is the hybrid-only Toyota Sienna. It's getting even more compelling for 2027, since Toyota has bestowed it with some significant drivetrain improvements including the automaker's sixth-generation hybrid system that uses high efficiency electric motors, a retuned engine, and a more energy efficient lithium-ion battery.

The 2027 Toyota Sienna isn't visibly distinguishable from the 2026 model since no changes were made to its exterior, but minivans have always been about what's beneath the skin, anyway. In addition to the new powertrain, all 2027 Siennas get the larger 12.3-inch infotainment screen and the larger 12.3-inch digital gauge cluster as standard, Toyota's latest suite of active-safety features, an integrated dash cam, a redesigned steering wheel and column, and new LED interior lights.

The Sienna's new hybrid powertrain has been totally recalibrated to operate the 2.5-liter four-cylinder at lower engine speeds when accelerating through the low and mid-range, and all Siennas now have a 1.15-kWh lithium-ion battery and a 166-horsepower electric motor. The powertrain produces a combined 251 horsepower, 6 more horses than last year, and Toyota reckons it's good for a 0.3-second quicker 0-to-60 sprint of 7.5 seconds in front-wheel-drive form. It can still be had with all-wheel drive, which adds a rear-mounted 54-hp electric motor, but its 0-to-60 time is actually a tenth slower than the front-drive model. Toyota now estimates both FWD and AWD Siennas will return 39 mpg in the city, 35 mpg on the highway, and 37 mpg combined, improvements of 3 mpg for FWD and 4 mpg for AWD.

Toyota says the new steering system is more direct and has better on-center feel while reducing steering effort at low speeds for easier parking thanks to a new steering gear box, motor control unit, and torque sensor. The Sienna's brakes have also been reworked with a new electronic control system, offering improved feel and response with less body roll. The latest Toyota Safety Sense 4.0 suite of active safety features has upgraded sensors and hardware for improved obstacle detection capabilities, and in case that all fails, a built-in dash cam comes standard.

Other little changes include the cancellation of the Limited model, now leaving base LE, XLE, Woodland, XSE, and top Platinum trim levels. Toyota has not yet release pricing for the updated 2027 Sienna, but we expect its prices to increase slightly from their current starting point of $42,915.

The Elizabeth Holmes documentary “You Can See Everything” takes place in the Theranos founder’s former Del Mar, CA, rental—and the property’s location in an exclusive, gated community could be just part of the reason the film’s unexpected release was such a smash.

The project—which is now available to stream on demand and was directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim—was kept secret for the four years it was in production, leaving viewers in awe after the documentary’s surprise Sept. 6 premiere at Telluride Film Festival.

Holmes and her partner, Billy Evans, invited the film crew—which Oppenheim told Variety was intentionally kept intimate to keep the project a secret—to reside in the guesthouse of the home for the 34 days before she reported to Federal Prison Camp in Bryan, TX, on May 30, 2023.

“Keeping it under wraps was very important because we never wanted to get in front of what we were capturing,” the director shared.

The crew would typically shoot for 16-hour days, with Fielder telling Variety that cameras were strategically positioned to “cover the whole house” so nothing would be missed.

The four-bedroom, four-bathroom beachfront property is located in an ultraprivate community less than a half-hour from San Diego. Called Sandy Lane, it features just about a dozen residences.

“This is basically a hidden community. It’s a guard-gated community, which is so rare on a beach, which is why you have a lot of high-profile individuals up and down this strip,” celebrity real estate agent Josh Altman explained in a home tour of another property on the community’s sole street.

Given the home's private location—and considering California’s anti-paparazzi legislation protecting private property from capture—it’s plain to see why Holmes and Evans opted to rent the 2,200-square-foot dwelling in the time leading up to her imprisonment.

The New York Times also previously reported that the couple and their two children moved to the area to be nearer to Evans’ family. His parents, William L. Evans and Susan Evans, are the hotel magnates behind Evans Hotels, a San Diego–based hospitality company.

Although the property is currently off-market, a previous listing indicates the single-story home was built in 1948 and includes direct access to the private beach.

As seen in the film, the abode features tall ceilings with exposed-beams and sizable windows connecting the home to the serene surrounding landscape. Midcentury modern design is present throughout the home, which is paneled in honey-hued wood.

While Realtor.com® estimates the property’s current market value to be about $5.8 million, it’s unclear just how much Holmes and Evans shelled out to reside there temporarily. The most recent publicly listed rent was $5,650 a month in 2015, which is a decrease from the $5,950 rent it was listed for in 2012.

The Sandy Lane spot is not the only prominent rental property Holmes has retreated to since her legal troubles began. During her trial from Aug. 31, 2021, to Jan. 3, 2022, she resided in one of the seven homes of San Francisco’s notable Green Gables.

The sprawling 74-acre property was owned by the Fleishhacker family for over a century before it was sold for a record-breaking $85 million in 2025.

Before Theranos came crumbling down, Holmes and her former business and romantic partner, Ramesh “Sunny” Balwani, purchased a $9 million French provincial manor in Atherton, a wealthy enclave of Silicon Valley.

Privacy was likely a factor in that decision, as well, as the five-bedroom, 5.5-bathroom mansion was set behind a gated entrance and tall trees.

The home was considerably more lavish than the beach bungalow that serves as the backdrop for “You Can See Everything,” with a 6,800-square-foot main home accompanied by gardens, a pool, and a pavilion on a 1.16-acre lot.

Along with the couple’s young children, Evans has since relocated from California to Texas, so the family can visit Holmes in prison, where she is serving an 11-year sentence for three counts of wire fraud and one count of conspiracy to commit wire fraud.

The sentence was reduced to nine years for good behavior, and Holmes has since been approved for placement in a community correction center, ABC News reported, citing an update from the Department of Justice's Victim Notification System.

Holmes is scheduled to be placed in the Austin Transitional Center in De Valle, TX, in August 2027.

‘Naughty’ vs ‘Nasty’: Universal and Warner Bros. Battle Over Titles for New Jennifer Aniston, Jenna Ortega Movies (EXCLUSIVE)

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Two similarly titled movies are causing a war of words between Universal and Warner Bros. Pictures. The legacy studios are locked in a stalemate over dueling projects that sound alike and are set to hit theaters around the same time, five sources tell Variety.

The first is “Naughty,” Olivia Wilde’s raucous Christmas comedy starring Jennifer Aniston. The other is “Nasty,” Mary Bronstein’s psychological drama led by Jenna Ortega, which will roll out under Warner’s new indie label Clockwork. Universal slated “Naughty” for Nov. 5, 2027, after registering the name with the Motion Picture Association in 2023, according to two sources. Clockwork, the nascent shingle run by Neon veteran Christian Parkes, has not officially set a date for “Nasty,” though four sources say the film is also eyeing a November 2027 release. Related Stories ‘PAW Patrol’ Spinoff Series ‘Skye’s Pup Squad’ Set for 2027 Release

Generally, studios go to great lengths to avoid delivering movies with similar-sounding names — much less launching them in the same corridor. “Naughty” and “Nasty” aren’t alike in genre or subject matter. “Naughty,” featuring Aniston and Peter Dinklage, follows a single mom who has to convince Santa Claus to testify at her divorce hearing. “Nasty,” Bronstein’s follow-up to “If I Had Legs I’d Kick You,” revolves around an elite gymnast competing for a spot on the Olympic team while dealing with her manipulative coach.

Yet insiders at Universal and Warner Bros. feel the fact that the titles are basically synonyms could confuse moviegoers. Further complicating the matter is that LuckyChap, Margot Robbie’s prolific film and TV production shop, is backing both projects. Universal and Clockwork declined to comment.

The spat is headed toward a formal review by the MPA, which has a 101-year-old department called the Title Registration Bureau to manage such conflicts. Arbitration involves a panel of studio intellectual property lawyers at the ready to hash it out. Although titles are not copyrightable, the TRB is armed with a 50-page document outlining rules of play and precedent, per one source, and its rulings are legally binding. The MPA declined to comment on the matter.

“Movies want to distinguish themselves, and the first way they do that is the title,” says Jeff Bock, an analyst with Exhibitor Relations. “If they sound similar, there could be confusion.”

Universal believes it should have dibs since it was first to file with “Naughty.” That would mean “Nasty” either changes its name or shifts its release date. Insiders note Clockwork filed two alternative titles for “Nasty” with its MPA documentation, but the filmmakers are against using the backups.

Meanwhile, sources close to “Nasty” feel that Universal is “punching down” at Clockwork because the indie drama doesn’t have the same broad commercial appeal as the Aniston-led holiday comedy. (Though Clockwork is a specialty label, it is owned and operated by the vast Skydance Motion Picture Group). Wilde is content to let “Nasty” proceed as titled, according to a person familiar with her thinking. LuckyChap is working toward a solution that sets up both projects for success without entering MPA arbitration, two sources add.

Disputes like these rarely become public knowledge, insiders say. And most often, studios work it out without involving the Title Registration Bureau. The group’s last two arbitrations were in 2018. That’s when Disney and Lucasfilm settled on “Solo: A Star Wars Story” rather than “Solo” to distinguish the Han Solo origin story from the 2018 climbing documentary “Free Solo.” And Universal agreed to change its raunchy comedy to “Blockers” from “The Pact,” a name that was already used by a 2012 horror film.

Renaming movies can benefit both parties. Ask Paramount and Sony, which released “No Strings Attached” and “Friends With Benefits” a few months apart in 2011. Both romantic comedies — one starring Natalie Portman and Ashton Kutcher and the other with Mila Kunis and Justin Timberlake — had an identical premise involving two friends who agree to casual sex without dating. “No Strings Attached” was originally called “Friends With Benefits,” but the studio acquiesced to the title change. Any plot similarities didn’t dilute the films’ respective box office, with each one grossing $149 million globally.

To that end, movies with similar themes or ideas are less of a problem for studios. Bock points to 1998’s “Armageddon” and “Deep Impact,” two disaster films about comets on a collision course with Earth. Both were theatrical winners, with “Armageddon” earning $553 million globally and “Deep Impact” generating $350 million globally.

“Consumers might be fooled by something like that, but it didn’t affect the box office,” he says. “They turned out to be different, even though the synopsis was the same.”

Montel Williams is getting candid about the state of daytime talk show, saying viewers’ attention spans have changed a lot since he ended his own talker in 2008.

“It’s a sad commentary that we lost our attention span in the last 15 years,” he said in a conversation with We’re Out of Time podcast host Richard Taite, debuting Tuesday. “The talk shows that I did and the talk shows of that era were hourlong talk discussions. We don’t have the patience anymore to listen to a topic for an hour. He said the format has morphed quite a bit since his show ended. (The Montel Williams Show ran from July 1991 to May 2008, spanning 17 seasons.)

“So the talk shows of my era became the reality shows of today,” he opined. “I’ll say that again, the talk shows of my era became the reality shows of today. We took the dysfunction that we put on display and turned those into individual shows of their own, to the point now that the talk that’s out there right now, what do they really even talk? Other than selling products and hawking a new movie or hawking a new record, what do they do? Is there any discussion that makes man better?”

Williams was quick to say he’s not criticizing anyone in particular, just nothing how times have changed.

“I’m not casting aspersion on my peers of the day,” he said. “But I am saying that at least back when we talked about issues, we talked about issues that changed the world, that I believe changed America. We were talking about AIDS before any news stations were going full reports on it. We were talking about civil unrest, we were talking about racism, we were talking about hate, we were talking about all those things that we didn’t even talk about it around the dinner table. We talked about it. Some of us did it in the salacious way. But at least they had a discussion.”

Williams added that the goal with his show was to educate people and put a spotlight on a topic that viewers perhaps hadn’t been introduced to or thought that much about. In other words, something that would “make you stop and think,” he said.

Does he think that kind of talk show would work today?

“It could, if it was formatted differently,” he says. “I think we can go an entire hour, but we have to go in that hour introducing every single piece of that topic, not just one piece, but we have to introduce it all and add to it information that people can take away.”

Taite likened that to scrolling on your phone, where you’re staying engaged by absorbing different information — and a lot of it — in a short time span.

“That’s right, and [keeping] you stimulated on the same topic instead of scrolling, or swiping up and down, instead of doing that and getting a different topic every single second, and that’s what they’re trying to do,” Williams replied. “They’re trying to give you something different every five or six minutes where they never really even solved the first five minutes. I say that every single topic out there can be broken into 20 different topics. Somebody needs to just program it that way.”

He added, “I think we may gravitate back to that as soon as we realize that we pushed attention span to a point where it’s no longer attention span.”

Strength-Train Your Hair

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Can the slew of products designed to fortify weakened strands really made a difference? Dermatologists and beauty scientists detail what to look for when you’re shopping the haircare aisle

Women's Health may earn commission from the links on this page, but we only feature products we believe in. Why Trust Us?

WE’D ALL LIKE to imagine our hair as strong, resilient, healthy. And there’s no shortage of products marketed toward reaching that end point. You’ve got your bond-building treatments, your protein masks, your strengthening serums—heck, there are now entire product lines geared toward promoting robust, rugged strands.

But what exactly does it mean to have ‘strong’ hair?

We tend to equate strength with the kind of glossy, thick, bouncy strands you'd see in a shampoo commercial. But there’s more to the science than that. For starters, hair that looks healthy isn't necessarily strong, and hair that's naturally fine or thinning isn't automatically weak. Plus, there's a difference between making hair “feel” stronger and actually improving its ability to withstand breakage.

Meet the experts: Sherry Maragh, MD, is a board-certified dermatologist based in Virginia. Nathan Puksta is the founder and CEO of FILAMENT Sciences, a haircare brand focused on the science of stronger hair.

So, what counts as strong hair, and can all these products really deliver on their promises? We asked two experts to explain.

Scientifically speaking, hair strength is all about resilience, meaning how well an individual strand withstands stress from brushing, stretching, styling, and other everyday activities without breaking. That strength largely comes from two parts of the hair shaft: the cuticle, which acts as its protective outer layer, and the cortex, which contains keratin proteins responsible for much of its structural integrity, explains Sherry Maragh, MD, a board-certified dermatologist based in Virginia.

Another important factor is elasticity, or the ability of hair to stretch and return toward its original shape without snapping. One of the most useful measurements is tensile strength, which quantifies how much force a strand can withstand before breaking, says Nathan Puksta, the founder and CEO of FILAMENT Sciences, which creates products and ingredients dedicated to the science of stronger hair. Think of it as the difference between a brittle piece of dry spaghetti and a flexible rubber band.

Tensile strength is an important part of this conversation because it’s something that laboratories can actually measure, using calibrated equipment that pulls individual hair fibers until they break. Other tests evaluate elasticity, breakage, and resistance to combing. But strength isn't synonymous with thickness, density, or shine. "Hair can absolutely be strong while still being fine, thin, or less reflective," says Dr. Maragh. "Similarly, hair that appears thick and glossy is not necessarily resistant to breakage." In other words, those naturally fine strands might be remarkably resilient, while locks that epitomize the “glass hair” trend could be surprisingly fragile.

Unfortunately, the things we regularly do to make our hair look good can also compromise its strength. Puksta identifies five major contributors to weakened hair: chemical processing, mechanical damage, heat, environmental exposure, and aging.

Chemical treatments, particularly bleaching and certain straightening processes, can be especially damaging because they alter the hair's internal structure. But everyday habits matter, too. Those can look like:

So, can you actually make your hair stronger? Yes, but there's an important distinction between improving damaged hair's performance and returning it to its original, undamaged condition. "The visible hair shaft is made up of nonliving cells, so damaged hair cannot biologically heal in the way skin does," says Dr. Maragh.

That doesn't mean strengthening products are useless. Certain ingredients can improve hair's physical properties, reduce friction, reinforce weakened areas, and help prevent additional breakage, with some technologies even working beneath the cuticle. Puksta explains that bond-building treatments target chemical interactions within hair, while certain peptides and proteins can help reinforce its keratin structure. However, effectiveness varies considerably between formulations.

Dr. Maragh cautions that an ingredient's ability to penetrate the hair shaft doesn't automatically prove that it repairs structural damage. So, what can different strengthening products realistically accomplish?

And remember that a product claiming 80 percent stronger hair isn't necessarily saying what you think. Dr. Maragh recommends examining what was measured, the comparison group, and whether results came from laboratory testing or consumer feedback. Protein retention, for example, isn't the same measurement as tensile strength.

Here's the good news: You don't need naturally thick, glossy hair to have strong hair. And if your strands are damaged, there are meaningful ways to improve their resilience. The key is understanding what products can—and cannot—accomplish. A conditioner that reduces breakage through easier detangling is valuable, even if it doesn't rebuild internal bonds. Likewise, a treatment that improves measurable tensile strength isn't necessarily reversing every aspect of previous damage (translation: it can’t recreate virgin hair).

Ultimately, maintaining strength is as much about preventing damage as treating it. Consistent conditioning, gentler styling practices, and choosing products suited to your hair type can help protect existing strands while healthy new hair grows.

And that's worth remembering the next time you see a bottle that promises stronger hair: The goal is helping the strands withstand whatever you put them through—not just have shampoo commercial-worthy hair.
