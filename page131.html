The U.S. Department of Labor proposed new policy this week that would end fast-track green cards for healthcare professionals like nurses and physical therapists—the latest in a string of policies from the Trump administration raising the bar for foreign-born workers and students across healthcare and other industries.

Currently, nurses, physical therapists and other workers “with exceptional ability in the sciences or arts” are granted an exemption from an administrative test in the permanent labor certification program (PERM), a months-long administrative process in which an employer must prove there are no able, willing, qualified and available U.S. workers for a permanent job ahead of sponsoring a foreign worker.

The exemption, referred to Schedule A, is given to occupations where the DOL recognizes a nationwide shortage of U.S. workers. Nurses and physical therapists have been listed under Schedule A for more than 30 years.

The Department of Labor, in its newly proposed rule, said it wants to eliminate Schedule A as it cannot “demonstrate that the occupations currently listed in Schedule A are still shortage occupations.”

It cited in the proposed rule a late 2023 request for information published under the Biden administration seeking input on potentially expanding Schedule A. Despite more than 2,000 responses, DOL now says it didn’t receive the information it would need to establish a methodology for regularly reviewing and updating Schedule A occupations, and questioned whether it has the statutory authority to make such occupational exemptions in the first place.

“The elimination of Schedule A would enable the Department to better ensure that U.S. workers have an opportunity to apply for available job opportunities, and adhere to the statutory requirements to protect U.S. workers assigned to it, as intended by Congress under the [Immigration and Nationality Act].”

The proposed rule, which is open to public comment for 60 days, also includes requirements for employers to advertise their open jobs more broadly and for a longer period of time to demonstrate a good-faith effort to recruit American workers before turning to a green card sponsorship. These and other proposed changes would be the first “comprehensive” reforms to the PERM program in almost two decades, the Department of Labor said.

If finalized as-is, employers who want to sponsor professional nurses, physical therapists and other affected roles “would be required to go through the lengthier and costlier PERM labor market testing process, adding time, cost and administrative work to a pathway that hospitals, nursing homes and rehabilitation providers have historically relied on to fill persistent shortages,” Caroline Tang, an Austin shareholder at labor and employment law firm Ogletree Deakins, told Fierce Healthcare.

Industry associations as well as other sections of the government have widely projected shortages in the healthcare workforce, including nurses, for the coming years. The Heart Resources and Services Association’s National Center for Health Workforce Analysis, for instance, projects (PDF) national shortages of registered nurses through at least 2038, with a projected 8% shortage in 2028 that is more severe in non-metro areas.

The proposed policy change is among several brought by the Trump administration in line with an order from the White House to prioritize domestic hiring—though many have been stymied in the courts.

Among the most prominent is a $100,000 supplemental fee the Department of Homeland Security attached to H-1B petitions that was vacated by two separate judged, and in August was succeeded by the somewhat altered proposal of an additional H-1B petition fee of $103,265. Another policy from the same department tightening how long nonimmigrant students may remain in the country (referred to as “duration of status”) was temporary blocked hours before implementation in September and fully vacated on Monday (PDF), though the former decision is already being appealed.

The Department of Labor has also proposed increasing the prevailing wage floors for several green cards that legal experts expect will be challenged by employers that would shoulder the resulting financial burden.

Anthropic IPO raises over $81 billion in largest offering on record, topping SpaceX

📅 2026-11-18  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 per share on Wednesday, above the expected range, setting the stage for the Claude creator's eagerly awaited Nasdaq listing and a historic moment for the artificial intelligence industry.

The company, along with its seven co-founders, upsized the offering, selling about 980 million shares, according to a press release. That brings the amount raised to $81.3 billion, surpassing SpaceX, which held a record IPO in June. Elon Musk's company raised $75 billion initially and a total of $85.7 billion after underwriters exercised their overallotment.

Anthropic's fully diluted market cap sits at around $1.5 trillion. The stock is expected to begin trading on the Nasdaq on Thursday under the ticker symbol "ANT."

The listing is a major test for the AI industry, which has experienced a set of recent shocks, including a dramatic backlash that was felt at the polls in the midterm elections and last week's ouster of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. The market was already jittery on concerns that rising interest rates will make debt unaffordable for many data center developers and neoclouds.

After publicly unveiling its prospectus in October, Anthropic updated the document last week with an expected price range between $70 and $78 per share.  The company had been planning to sell 825 million shares.

Tech's biggest companies have significant exposure to Anthropic, as an investor, partner or customer. Amazon and Google both own stakes valued at over $100 billion, and a group of tech companies, including Google, Microsoft, Nvidia and Advanced Micro Devices agreed to collectively invest $26 billion at the IPO price. Anthropic, meanwhile, is a buyer of costly systems from Nvidia, Google, Amazon and AMD.

"I think if we do this right, we can create an age of miracles where humanity is able to solve problems that have eluded us for millennia," Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei said in Anthropic's roadshow video. "We have to make sure that all the risks, all the ways that things can go wrong with this technology, we very carefully address those risks as we bring the benefits and as we have a viable business at the same time."

Amodei co-founded Anthropic in 2021 alongside a handful of other researchers less than six years ago, after the group collectively defected from OpenAI. The company is best known for developing a family of AI models called Claude, and it primarily sells its products and services to other businesses.

According to the latest prospectus, each co-founder is selling 3.38 million shares for total proceeds of $1.96 billion.

Anthropic has been racing to maintain an edge over OpenAI and Google in the enterprise market, and it experienced a period of explosive growth in 2026, driven largely by the popularity of its AI coding tool, Claude Code. OpenAI has confidentially filed for an IPO and has said a debut is likely in 2027.

For the nine months ended Sept. 30, Anthropic said, it generated $33.24 billion in revenue, up roughly 1,200% from the same period last year, according to its prospectus. The company reported a $104 billion net loss during the nine-month period, though most of that was due to the change in fair value of convertible notes and derivative liabilities. Anthropic lost $25.1 billion over the same stretch in 2025, the filing said.

Anthropic said in an amended filing that its run-rate revenue was roughly $81 billion at the end of October, up $10 billion from the previous month.

The company has faced plenty of challenges during its meteoric rise over its positions on safety, regulation and national security, leading to two high-profile disputes with the U.S. government this year. Its prospectus included more than 90 pages of risk factors for investors to consider ahead of its IPO.

"Our aim is to help humankind chart a safe course through this technological evolution, open new frontiers of discovery, and bring forth an age of miracles," Anthropic's co-founders wrote in the company's prospectus. "Everyone who owns our stock, whether an institution or an individual, becomes part of that effort."

Real REMAX Group confirmed additional organizational changes after its REMAX acquisition. Executives previously disclosed a 14% headcount reduction and more than $40 million in annualized gross cost savings tied to integration efforts.  AI Summary

After a merger or acquisition closes, companies almost always look for cost synergies. These efforts frequently result in layoffs, which they did at the recently formed Real REMAX Group, a RealTrends Verified firm.

During the company’s third-quarter 2026 earnings call with investors and analysts, which occurred a little over two months after The Real Brokerage closed its acquisition of REMAX, executives said the company reduced its combined headcount by more than 14%, eliminating 150-plus positions across the combined organization.

This came as the company said it found more than $40 million in annualized gross cost savings and expects to realize more than $30 million in adjusted EBITDA savings in 2027, which could increase to more than $40 million in 2028.

At the time of the earnings call, executives said the initial phase of headcount restructuring was largely complete but that they were still looking for ways to simplify operations and improve efficiencies.

But based on a recent slew of posts on LinkedIn, it appears that headcount reductions are not over just yet. Over the past week, several individuals who claim to be former REMAX employees shared posts stating that they’d been impacted by a recent round of layoffs at the company.

A spokesperson for Real REMAX Group told HousingWire via email that following the acquisition, “Real REMAX Group is continuing to evaluate opportunities that streamline operations, create efficiencies and better integrate the two organizations.”

“As part of this ongoing process, we can confirm that additional organizational changes have taken place at the company,” the spokesperson wrote. “We will continue to assess our business needs to ensure we have the right structure and resources in place to support our long-term strategic priorities and better serve real estate professionals.”

The spokesperson declined to share details regarding the size of the layoffs, which departments were impacted or if the layoffs were performance related.

Real REMAX Group is not alone in laying off employees as part of an attempt to rightsize after a merger.

Earlier this year, Compass International Holdings announced it was laying off 110 employees after it closed its acquisition of Anywhere Real Estate. Compass has estimated $500 million in total net cost synergies over the next three years.

It’s been almost seven months since artificial intelligence disease detection and care coordination platform Viz.ai entered into a partnership with the National Rural Health Association (NRHA).

The partnership aims to help rural healthcare leaders better understand and implement AI tools across systems. That's been a personal and professional passion of Viz.ai Chief Clinical Officer Andrew Ibrahim, M.D., who discussed with Fierce Healthcare at HLTH his key takeaways from the collaboration thus far and forward-looking goals as it approaches its one-year mark.

Ibrahim said that the two organizations have done three educational events for rural health systems since the partnership kicked off in April. These series aim to show rural health administrators the specifics of AI—and whether they should adopt it, he said.

He noted there’s “sometimes a very big mismatch” when smaller rural health systems onboard multiple vendors.

“We've learned [that] many rural hospitals don't have the IT expertise and infrastructure to adopt some of these tools, and so it's actually been a force function to strengthen their hub spoke relationships,” Ibrahim said.

Strengthening these relationships, according to Ibrahim, has been key.

“It's actually been beautiful to see the hub-spoke relationship strengthened with the AI tool as the conduit,” Ibrahim said. “And it gives them a reason to interact more, and a way for the rural hospitals to strengthen their capabilities.”

He continued, “there's an authenticity about the moment where it's like ‘Yeah, we do need to help each other out.’ We're all kind of figuring it out as we go.”

In addition to the educational series, Ibrahim said frameworks are in development to aid rural health systems in navigating, implementing and deploying AI—which will be built into a toolkit.

One of Ibrahim’s personal goals for the one-year mark of the partnership is to ensure any rural health administrator can understand what an AI tool is when pitched.

“I think that would be a huge milestone,” he said. “Getting to 100% of hospitals at year one would be ideal—probably unrealistic, but that's probably the ultimate goal.”

And when it comes to metrics for measuring the collaboration, Ibrahim said he's looking for numbers that show participants are “keeping more care local” and retaining their workforces.

“It would be great to be able to connect the dots [and understand whether] adoption of an AI tool give[s] rural clinicians confidence, enthusiasm, willingness to stay in their community as opposed to dropping the services and moving back,” he said.

Another initiative from Viz that has “disproportionate benefit in rural communities,” according to Ibrahim, is its new partnership with Wolters Kluwer Health to integrate the Viz.ai One platform into UpToDate Expert AI.

“I'm pretty excited about being able to bring their level of clinical decision support into our rural hospitals,” he said.

The latest for gun hunters and competitive shooters.

It’s never been easier to get a suppressor for your hunting rifle. But choosing one, for a first-time buyer, can be intimidating. There are thousands of options on the market and narrowing it down to the best can for your hunting rifle isn’t easy if you don’t know what to look for. So many new suppressors are hitting the market that what was a top-of-the-line can a couple years ago is just average today.

I’ve been shooting every new suppressor I can get my hands on for years, and these are the best ones that I have used.

This is one of the best hunting rifle suppressors I have ever used. Like many others that are leading the pack, it’s 3D printed from titanium, making it both strong and economical. It measures 5 inches long and weighs just 5.75 ounces. Despite its lightweight profile, it’s very quiet. In fact, it’s substantially quieter than several other popular suppressors — something I noted on a recent antelope hunt.

This is one of my top picks because it’s simple, rugged, quiet, and you’ll hardly notice it on your muzzle. It is direct thread only and, because threads are cut into the suppressor itself, they cannot be changed like a HUB-compatible suppressor. For a hunting suppressor, that’s a feature, not a bug. At $900, this is a really good value. Many top-level hunting suppressors will cost you $1,000 or more.

In the year since its introduction, Airlock has released 30-cal and 7mm versions of the Zero Gravity, as well as a 3.7-ounce Zero Gravity Nano, which is designed for bolt-action centerfire .22 cals like the 22 ARC, 22 Creed, and .22-250 Rem. It’s slightly larger than a rimfire suppressor, but satisfyingly quiet.

The Nomad Ti XC is another excellent option for a hunting suppressor. Its 3D printed design allows for modern baffles and reduces weak spots. The front and rear of the can are threaded and it is compatible with HUB rear mounts. I prefer using a Silencerco Ti direct thread adapter on the rear, and the standard .308 front cap. Other companies like Salmon River Solutions are making accessories like a directional brake that threads into the front cap to further reduce recoil and tame down cartridges like the .300 PRC. As pictured, the suppressor weighs less than 10 ounces and works wonderfully for anything 30-caliber and under. A nice feature is the inclusion of deep tool notches which makes removing the HUB mounts easy without worry of damaging the suppressor.

Another great option, if you want a shorter overall length for your rifle, is the Nomad TiOTB, which has a chamber that extends back over the barrel. This suppressor has the same interchangeable endcaps, but works exclusively with a thread-over-brake mount.

I’ve been shooting the TiTrex 300 Ti for several months now, and this is one of the best options for a general-purpose hunting suppressor out there. After all that shooting, it still only weighs 9.5 ounces, and it offers great mounting and front-cap modularity.

A couple years ago, the Silencerco Scythe Ti was one of few .30-cal silencers that light, but it’s been plagued by weld failure issues. The TiTrex 300 Ti is also made of titanium, is slightly lighter, and uses HUB mounts at the rear and an open-source front cap that works with the Silencerco Bravo front mounts. You can use a normal endcap or the short endcap brake, which helps reduce recoil, and both have great spanner wrench notches. It’s also compatible with some aftermarket Endcap brakes like the Salmon River Solutions Grim, which can help cut recoil even more.

The TiTrex has a 3D-printed body and baffle system that is rugged, durable, and even full-auto rated. This is a great option to use with a front brake as a competition can, but that can also work well on your hunting rifle. It would also be a solid choice as your first suppressor. A unique feature is that the serialized portion of the suppressor is a stainless-steel band at the rear of the can, that can be moved to a different core if you manage to wear the original out or damage it somehow.

There are three big trends in hunting suppressors in 2026, and the Buckskin 30 from Tenet tackles all of them. First, it’s 3D printed. Additive manufacturing has allowed companies to quickly develop and prototype suppressor models while reducing weak points like welds on thin titanium.

Second, it incorporates a front brake. Suppressors reduce recoil by slowing gases as they exit the muzzle, and incorporating a brake on the front tames them even further. The Buckskin features a built-in brake that is non-adjustable and won’t come loose.

Third, it’s light. It’s printed with titanium and mine weighs 8.5 ounces — making it the lightest .30-cal suppressor I own. I’ve used this suppressor on my 20-inch 300 PRC, several 6.5 Creedmoors, and have been putting a lot of rounds through it on a 16-inch 7mm Backcountry. It does a great job at reducing recoil while adding minimal extra weight.

If you want more adjustability and increased recoil reduction, the Tenet Limited 30 is another suppressor to look at. These are more expensive, but feature a removable front brake that cuts your rifle movement dramatically. Mine has a two-port brake and, while the first generation of brakes tended to blast your face with gas, the recently-released gen 2 brake has small steps in the curved brake to disrupt that gas impulse. It’s much more pleasant. If spotting your impacts with larger cartridges is important to you, a can like this fits the bill.

In the days of year-long waiting periods, modularity was king for hunting suppressors. While modular cans have their place, a simple dedicated hunting silencer is hard to beat. This fully 3D-printed titanium can from Unknown Munitions weighs about 11 ounces and features a reflex design, meaning the rear portion of the suppressor goes back over the barrel. That reflex expansion chamber allows a shorter portion — about four inches — to stick out in front of your muzzle. This improves balance and gives you a more compact package than the average 8-inch suppressor. The reflex design also creates a noticeably duller sound signature, whereas some produce a very sharp, snappy sound.

These are available in .30 caliber as well as 6.5mm and 6mm options, and models and fit over 0.75-inch diameter steel barrels and the OGL model fits larger carbon-fiber barrels. This suppressor isn’t modular, so you need to ensure compatibility before you order. Also check out their Reaper suppressor, which I haven’t had the chance to use, but should be another top contender.

I’ve used several of B&T’s Print-X series of titanium 3D-printed suppressors and have been impressed by all of them. Their modular VERS36 Ti model, however, deserves special recognition. To my knowledge, this is the lightest modular 36-caliber suppressor on the market, and it’s way cheaper than similar, more primitive cans were several years ago. If you’re wanting to get a suppressor to start out with, or to use on multiple hunting rifles, this is your jam.

It’s got 36-caliber baffles and comes with a .360 front cap. The rear is HUB-compatible and B&T makes nice titanium direct-thread adapters. You can get smaller front caps as well. If using on smaller cartridges or you just need a shorter overall package, the front section is removable. This is a flow-through design with spiral baffles that works well on bolt actions and gas-guns alike. It’s ideal for cartridges like the .350 Legend, but will work on anything with a .360-diameter bullet or smaller. I received mine through Silencer Central and it can be delivered to your door.

The StingerWorx Evo Hunter is a lightweight suppressor that’s more affordable than the 3D printed titanium cans, and it’s also much more serviceable. A downside of most printed suppressors is that there isn’t a great way to remove large amounts of carbon buildup from the baffles. Soaking and ultrasonic cleaners help, but a lot of shooters want a suppressor that can be disassembled and manually cleaned.

The Evo Hunter is just over 10 ounces, and it gets there by using multiple materials. It has a titanium blast baffle and chamber to provide a long service life, but uses an aluminum baffle stack downstream of that — where heat and abrasion isn’t as intense. If damaged, that front baffle stack is replaceable. Additionally, the Evo Hunter is compatible with StingerWorx’s front brake if you’re looking for additional recoil reduction. It’s got HUB threads at the rear and the blast chamber is deep enough to work with a variety of over-brake HUB mounts that you might already have. For the light weight, versatility, and serviceability, this can is a great value.

There are lots of suppressors out there and, because many of them are so similar, lots of good ones can get lost in the mix. Because we cover a lot of leading-edge ultralight cans, don’t think that we don’t pay attention to budget options, too. There are bottom-tier suppressors that are very cheap — often made of mostly aluminum — that will work, but can also leave buyers dissatisfied. When it comes to bang for your buck, stainless steel is still one of the best materials for a more affordable can. The first suppressor I ever bought was a large .30-cal stainless can. It was durable and affordable and its weight was the only downside.

The Banish HNT 30 SS is a great example of a modern great value. It’s compact and, at 13.8 ounces, not excessively heavy. It’s suitable for virtually any hunting rifle .30-caliber and smaller, and similar in size and form to the lightweight Banish Backcountry titanium can.  It’s got hub threads at the rear and comes with a direct-thread adapter. If you’re looking to try a suppressor for hunting and want a good-quality can, this is a great place to start.

If you’re looking for maximum suppression of your big-bore cartridges, the Goliath Gen 2 from Liberty Suppressors is an excellent option. This beast of a silencer is 10.25 inches long by 2 inches in diameter and designed specifically for cartridges like the .458 Socom, .450 Bushmaster, and .45/70 Gov’t. While big, this can is relatively light. It’s got twice the volume of suppressors like the Silencerco Hybrid 46, but weighs only about 4 ounces more (22 ounces total). This is a perfect fit for anyone hunting with a short-barreled .458 or .450 Bushmaster, or a trapper-style .45/70.

The suppressor has a titanium sleeve and titanium baffle stack that’s removable and serviceable so you can shoot cast lead bullets and clean out lead fouling — unlike sealed suppressors. I’ve had a Liberty .22 rimfire can for many years, and this is essentially a much larger version of that. I’ve been shooting it on my .45/70 and it’s working wonderfully on my .338 ARC SBR with subsonic ammo.

One of the best values in lightweight modular suppressors is the BDE from Primary Weapons Systems. I’ve been shooting the 5.56 model for the last several months, and it combines smart manufacturing with durability, value, and the kind of modularity a lot of hunters want. The BDE has hub threads at the rear, a blast chamber segment, and a 3D-printed main baffle segment. Forward of that, it has removable individual threaded baffle segments topped with an end cap.

This is a sealed suppressor, but baffles are designed to allow gasses to flow better and reduce backpressure, making a great option for both a bolt gun and AR-style semi-auto. Each segment of the can has wrench notches making disassembly easy, and you can customize to the platform you’ll be shooting. This has been living on my .223 practice bolt-action and the irregular surface contours do seem to help break up mirage. The BDE is also available in .30- and .36- caliber models, all with customizable baffle configurations.

Picking through the many different suppressor options can be daunting, and most good suppressors perform similarly. To find one that you’ll be happy with, consider details like construction and weight, but also the level of modularity you desire, or whether you’re just looking for a good value. If one of these isn’t quite what you’re looking for, I hope that seeing these options and features will help you home in on the model that you will be most satisfied with. The suppressor market is constantly evolving, and we will continue to use and test suppressors with the goal of helping you make the most informed decisions possible.

Jensen Huang's departure was only a symptom of a bigger problem in the AI economy.

Though Nvidia’s earnings call Tuesday offered some reassurance to investors after Jensen Huang’s departure as CEO, it did little to resolve the political, regulatory and leadership questions hanging over the entire AI economy.

The semiconductor giant at the heart of the AI infrastructure boom saw its shares plunge 35% from last month’s high following the scandal around Huang — wiping out nearly $2 trillion in market cap and triggering Wall Street panic around a broader AI reckoning.

Nvidia’s finance chief and interim CEO, Colette Kress, sought to project stability, telling investors it was “business as usual” and raising Nvidia’s fiscal 2028 outlook. Though the chipmaker’s stock price rebounded after reporting better-than-expected results, it’s likely to face a rocky future, as concerns linger around national security violations amid illegal export deals. Market confidence is also shaky amid supply chain backlogs for advanced chips and servers that remain critical to AI compute and the training of large language models.

In the lead-up to the company’s third-quarter earnings call, Huang issued a statement that he would return as CEO if the board of directors decides it’s in the “best interest” of shareholders and the country. Despite calls from President Donald Trump and other tech executives to reinstate the former GPU boss, Harvey C. Jones, Nvidia’s appointed first-ever chair of the board, said there is no intention to reinstate Huang, and no successor was named.

Prior to the call, the Wall Street Journal reported that the US Securities and Exchange Commission is investigating Huang over concerns that the company misled investors.

Huang maneuvered Nvidia into a linchpin role for frontier AI labs and data center firms, not just by supplying hardware but also by providing equity, credit and financial backing and by mobilizing third-party capital to secure investment for the AI buildout of data centers. Nvidia’s circular financing schemes — in which the chipmaker helps fund a hyperscaler like Meta or Microsoft, which then uses the funds to buy its products — were allegedly a source of contention for Kress and other executives.

Critics have warned that this kind of vendor financing inflated the AI bubble by inflating growth projections and buoying the market valuations of chipmakers and hyperscalers.

According to Reuters, Kress was expected to pull back from some of the riskiest AI financial deals, including investments in OpenAI and AI infrastructure companies such as Lambda. Already, Nvidia has significantly scaled back financing for a $250 billion OpenAI data center deal. During Tuesday’s call, Kress failed to announce a sweeping unwind of Huang-era financing arrangements, focusing more on continuity than retreat.

Kress mentioned that chip producer TSMC, a key Nvidia partner, will continue working with Nvidia despite Huang’s departure. The company also said it was assuming no China data-center compute revenue in its next fourth-quarter outlook.

Nvidia supplies the lion’s share of the hardware necessary for data center construction and model training, and the company has long said that the demand for chips exceeds supply. Shortages of RAM and high-bandwidth memory slow down the production of AI servers and raise prices for cloud companies and other buyers. In an attempt to buy its way out of the bottleneck, Nvidia has committed large sums of money to secure future memory supply.

If Nvidia distances itself too much from its GPU commitments, that could be a problem for the entire tech industry. Semiconductor production is an incredibly specialized field dominated by very few companies, and any changes to the supply chain could have ripple effects on global markets and vendors, including in consumer electronics and cloud services.

Huang’s affair with a Chinese executive connected to the Singapore-based Megaspeed — whom US intelligence claimed was a Chinese intelligence asset — complicated an already tight supply chain. Megaspeed was under investigation for allegedly helping Chinese firms obtain high-value Nvidia chips through channels that violated US export controls.

According to the investment research firm Culper, up to one-fifth of Nvidia’s fiscal-year 2026 compute revenues were driven by movement of some of the company’s most advanced chips into China through nonrestricted intermediary countries in a process called diversion. When advanced Nvidia chips or servers are illegally routed to Chinese buyers, those units are no longer available to legal customers in the US and allied markets.

Huang was influential in shaping the Trump administration’s chip export policies, pushing back against export controls that limited Nvidia’s sales to China. But the recent scandal prompted new Commerce Department restrictions on chip shipments through third countries.

While Kress cited improving supply and sustained demand in raising Nvidia’s outlook, stricter export rules and tighter controls could further constrain capacity by creating more obstacles to compliance and delivery.

Nvidia’s better-than-expected earnings may steady investors in the short term, but any company that supplies essential hardware for the AI buildout is at risk if investor confidence in the industry falters. When Nvidia reports strong growth, AI-focused stocks and funds with large technology holdings are likely to go up. The mortgage industry was built to help Americans buy homes. An aging America needs more from it.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Survey of 2,000 homeowners 55 plus shows 88% prioritize staying home and 90% prioritize independence

For decades, the mortgage industry has helped Americans achieve one of their biggest financial goals: buying a home and building equity over time.

It’s a model we know well. Homeowners purchase properties, make mortgage payments, refinance when it makes financial sense and gradually accumulate wealth. Much of the mortgage industry is structured around supporting that journey.

But what happens after decades of building that wealth?

As homeowners approach retirement, the financial equation shifts. Instead of focusing primarily on acquiring a home and paying down debt, the question becomes how to use the wealth they’ve accumulated to support the years ahead. Housing presents a unique opportunity but also a challenge because, for many Americans, their largest financial asset is also the place they call home.

You can sell a stock without moving. Selling a home is another matter. Trading down and moving to a less expensive home to tap home equity is both expensive and difficult. This might be why our recent research shows that the desire to remain at home continues to grow stronger with age.

In a recent Longbridge Financial, LLC (NMLS #957935) study of more than 2,000 U.S. homeowners ages 55 and older, 90% said maintaining their independence was extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. Nearly as many (88%) said the same about remaining in their homes. Both priorities ranked above paying for health care, paying off debt, long-term care and providing financial support to family members.

The desire to age in place isn’t surprising. What’s particularly revealing is how strongly that sentiment differs across age groups.

Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 85% rated remaining in their homes as extremely or very important. That figure rose to 88% among those ages 65 to 74 and 93% among homeowners 75 and older. The importance of maintaining independence followed a similar pattern, climbing from 87% in the youngest group to 96% in the oldest.

These are differences between age groups at one point in time, rather than a measure of how individual preferences change over time. Still, the pattern points to an important finding in understanding how homeowners think about retirement.

Over decades, homeowners accumulate substantial equity through mortgage payments and increases in property values. Yet the very home that holds the wealth is also where many want to spend their later years. Its financial value tells only part of the story. Giving a homeowner a choice to move to a less expensive, and thus less attractive, home to use their home equity may not be much of a choice at all.

Consider two retired households with the same net worth. One holds most of its wealth in savings and investments. The other holds much of it in home equity.

Their balance sheets might look similar, but their financial flexibility is different. The first household may be able to draw from liquid accounts to cover an unexpected expense. The second has wealth, too, but accessing it may involve borrowing against the home or selling it altogether. Both options come with trade-offs, particularly when remaining in the home is a priority.

For an industry accustomed to measuring property values, loan balances and equity, this is an important distinction. These figures describe a homeowner’s financial position, but not necessarily how easily they can access that wealth — or what they might have to give up in the process.

The same survey reinforces why this distinction matters. Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 73% rated paying for health care as extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. That figure climbed to 85% among those 75 and older. While these findings don’t tell us how much homeowners will spend or whether they’ll tap into their home equity, they do highlight how financial priorities can shift across different stages of life.

Mortgage finance has traditionally centered on transactions: purchasing, refinancing, servicing and eventually paying off a loan. Those functions remain essential. But paying off a mortgage doesn’t end a homeowner’s financial relationship with their home.

For some older homeowners, selling or downsizing may be the right choice. Others may have sufficient income and savings to remain in place. Still others may consider home equity loans, lines of credit or reverse mortgages, depending on their financial circumstances, eligibility and the associated costs and risks.

The opportunity for our industry extends beyond any single product. It’s about understanding the role home equity can play in supporting homeowners through retirement, while recognizing the personal value they place on remaining in their homes.

It also requires recognizing that homeowners’ priorities differ. In our study, 93% of women rated independence as extremely or very important, compared with 88% of men. Similar five-point gaps appeared for remaining at home and paying for health care. While the research doesn’t explain why these differences exist, it underscores the importance of understanding homeowners as individuals rather than treating homeowners 55 and over as a single market with identical needs and priorities.

Housing has always been both an investment and a place to live. As homeowners age, the balance between those roles deserves greater attention from the industry that helped them achieve homeownership in the first place.

We’ve spent decades helping Americans build wealth through homeownership. The next opportunity is helping them understand how that wealth can support the years ahead, without losing sight of what they value most: independence, financial flexibility and, for many, the ability to remain at home.
