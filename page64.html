'Yellowstone' Killed Off One of Its Best Characters Too Early

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Writer and director Taylor Sheridan has, simply put, taken over our TV screens. The Jeremy Renner-led Mayor of Kingstown is gearing up for its fifth and final season, Lioness is still going strong, and Tulsa King, starring Sylvester Stallone, is currently airing its fourth installment with a spin-off on the way. None of these shows, however, would likely be in existence if it weren't for the massive success of Taylor Sheridan's Yellowstone. While the Western series, led by Kevin Costner, came to a close with Season 5, the Dutton dynasty is still being represented on television through CBS's Marshals and Paramount+'s Dutton Ranch.

While it can be hard for a multi-season series to remember every plotline from its early years — especially given how long Yellowstone's episodes can run — one forgotten character stands above the rest, awaiting real justice for his absence. With spin-off prequels such as 1883 and 1923 introducing so many other Duttons to add to the growing family tree, it's easy to forget about Dave Annable's Lee entirely. After all, the character isn't in the show all that much, and his siblings, Kayce (Luke Grimes), Beth (Kelly Reilly), and adoptive brother Jamie (Wes Bentley), often fought (usually each other) for Yellowstone's center stage. A humble cowboy like Lee wouldn't really fit into the cutthroat world Sheridan created, hence why he didn't stick around. But what happened to Lee, and why did Annable leave the show so abruptly?

If you forgot that Costner's John Dutton had a third son (well, fourth if you include Cole Hauser's Rip Wheeler, John's surrogate son), then you're not alone. Annable only played the firstborn Dutton in three episodes of Yellowstone, and in that time, his character never exactly made a real impact on anyone involved. First appearing in the extended pilot episode "Daybreak," Lee is the first in line for the Dutton throne. More than that, he's the only one of John's children who genuinely cares about the land and cattle, not just the power it buys. But Lee's tenure on the series wasn't meant to last, and neither was his memory.

Near the end of "Daybreak," Lee, himself a Livestock Agent, is murdered by Robert Long (Jeremiah Bitsui) — who just so happens to be Monica Dutton's (Kelsey Asbille) brother — during a cattle dispute on the border of the Dutton Ranch and the Broken Rock Indian Reservation. As a result, Kayce (Luke Grimes) retaliates by killing his brother-in-law in self-defense, which ends up causing more issues than Lee's death did. In many ways, Lee's demise serves as the catalyst for the events of the entire series, as the Duttons go to war with all the encroaching forces on their ancestral land. However, they sort of forget about him in the process.

The hidden meanings behind Beth's wounds might surprise you.

After his death, Annable was understandably written off the series. For years, his only reappearance was via a photo that cameoed in the Season 2 episode ironically titled "Resurrection Day." However, Lee did return via flashbacks to his sibling's childhood in the '90s (back when Costner's John Dutton looked more like Josh Lucas), where he was played by Kip Denton — who, aside from the episode "Blood the Boy," often went uncredited.

While Lee's occasional flashback appearances kept the character alive after his Season 1 demise (his pure moment with a young Kayce at the Dutton family Christmas of 1996 was a nice surprise), they're scarce and never focus on the character himself. In the present storylines, Lee's death is forgotten by the end of the first season, and rather than fight for the Dutton Ranch in honor of his son's memory, John Dutton does so out of some mysterious promise to his father that isn't revealed until later. By some miracle, Lee's the only Dutton child who's been murdered thus far, in spite of previous attempts on other family members' lives.

No doubt part of the reason that Lee is forgotten is that the death of a son and a brother is a hard thing to meditate on. No wonder John would rather put all his effort into preserving his land rather than grieve. But what starts as something understandable becomes something less so as time continues on. As Yellowstone continues to flashback to the Duttons' childhood, Lee is forgotten about entirely. Additionally, there are entire Dutton family photos that don't include their firstborn son at all, which we'd assume was a point of contention among the siblings if we didn't know it was clearly unintentional. More than anything, it just seems like Taylor Sheridan forgot about Lee's existence completely.

The show that claimed the most of your answers is the world you were built for. If two tied, both are shown — you're complicated enough to straddle two Sheridan universes.

You are a Dutton — or you might as well be. You understand that some things are worth protecting at any cost, and that the modern world's indifference to history, to land, to legacy, is not something you're willing to accept quietly. You lead from the front, you carry your family's weight without complaint, and when someone threatens what's yours, you don't escalate — you finish it. You're not cruel. But you are absolute. In Yellowstone's world, that combination of ferocity and loyalty doesn't make you a villain. It makes you the only thing standing between everything that matters and everyone who wants to take it.

You thrive in the chaos of high-stakes negotiation, where the money is enormous, the margins are thin, and the wrong word in the wrong room can cost everyone everything. You're a fixer — the person called when a situation is already on fire and needs someone with the nerve to walk into it. West Texas oil country rewards exactly what you are: sharp, adaptable, unsentimental, and absolutely clear-eyed about what people want and what they'll do to get it. You're not naive enough to think this world is fair. You're smart enough to be the one deciding who it's fair to.

That fluency makes you invaluable and it makes you a target. You've made your peace with both. Mayor of Kingstown belongs to people who understand that keeping the peace is not the same as being at peace — and who do the job regardless.

In many ways, Yellowstone developed Rip into Lee's replacement, especially in recent seasons. Not only did John gift his surrogate firstborn Lee's old cabin on the Dutton land, but John's love for his new son-in-law rivaled that of his love for his own children. Flashbacks to Rip's teenage years in Season 5 reveal that John helped hide the body of a murdered ranch hand whom Rip had killed by accident. In many ways, it's a scene that feels like a rite of passage between father and son, cementing the fact that Rip, not Lee, has become the rightful successor to John's cattle empire.

After nearly two seasons without hardly a passing mention of the lost Dutton, the final episode of Season 4, "Grass on the Streets and Weeds on the Rooftops," did what we never thought Yellowstone could do and brought Lee back from the grave. (Well, sort of.) As Kayce embarks on his vision quest ahead of Season 5, he sees a vision of his long-dead brother, one who wasn't particularly happy to see him. Not only did Lee confront Kayce about forgetting him, but his appearance in his brother's vision served more as a warning than a reunion. Annable's return was a welcome surprise for sure and brought the series full circle as Lee and Kayce looked eye-to-eye for the first time since the premiere.

Though it's unlikely we'll see Annable return again as Lee, it was nice to see that, after years of wondering why Sheridan and company seemed to have forgotten about him, Yellowstone was willing to elaborate further. After the death of John Dutton's latest grandson in Season 5, the patriarch recalls his own grief when Lee died back in the first season, explaining that he still blames himself to this day.

Despite being unceremoniously killed off on Yellowstone, Dave Annable has continued to play the cowboy on other neo-Westerns following his short tenure as Lee Dutton. In 2021, he landed the recurring role of Dan Miller on the CW's Walker, a modern reimagining of the original Chuck Norris-helmed Walker, Texas Ranger that took more than a few cues from Yellowstone. Appearing in 12 episodes as Miller in the midst of a Hatfield & McCoy-type family squabble, Annable has carved out a real Western legacy for himself.

Alternatively, Annable reunited with Taylor Sheridan in 2023 for Lioness. Slowly moving away from the Western genre, Sheridan's latest series is more akin to his work on the action thriller Without Remorse. After being killed off on Sheridan's flagship series, Annable was granted main cast status in the filmmaker's spy thriller. Playing Neal, the surgeon husband of Zoe Saldaña's lead character Joe, he's finally landed a role that's brought him full circle since Yellowstone. Here's hoping we'll see Annable play a cowboy again sometime soon.

Janus Electric secures battery supply deal for its battery-swap semi truck

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Australian conversion specialists Janus Electric have been celebrating a growing slate of orders for their Class 8 electric truck conversions, which ditch diesel in favor of advanced battery-swap electric power – but exactly how they’d deliver all those trucks was still a question. Now, we have an answer: Electrovaya will supply the batteries.

The concept of dropping an electric power unit into a Class 8 semi makes perfect sense, as most of these trucks are built as gliders. That is, rolling body and chassis without an engine and transmission that’s ready to receive power from Cummins, Detroit, International, or Volvo Penta. That’s the role that Janus Electric sees itself occupying, and it seems to be working.

Janus’ reservation book crept past 70 units this past summer, helped along in part by California’s HVIP Program and the US EPA’s Diesel Emission Reduction Act (DERA) Grants. When the HVIP voucher is combined with the Port of Los Angeles’ “Plus” support, customers can received up to $166,000 per truck conversion. Factor in the massive fuel savings of electric and the classic good looks of square-nose Western Star truck body, and it’s easy to see why they’d be a popular choice.

As Jerry Seinfeld taught us however, it’s one thing to take a reservation – it’s another thing to deliver. To help make it happen, Janus has inked a battery supply deal with Electrovaya that will see a $26 million initial purchase orders for Electrovaya battery packs to demand for Janus’ products in the US and Canada, along with the battery company purchasing approximately $1.4 million in Janus stock with (what seems to be) actual money.

“Electrovaya is an important partner to Janus Electric, and we’re particularly pleased it has decided to invest directly in our business,” explains Ben Hutt, Managing Director and CEO of Janus Electric. “The investment is a strong vote of confidence in our technology and approach to electrifying heavy transport.”

The Janus Electric-repowered trucks carry a stout, 480 hp electric motor drawing power from a standard 310 kWh battery pack, with up to two battery packs (one on each side) of the truck. In practice, that gives the Janus trucks up to 370 miles of loaded range. After that, the batteries can be DC fast charged in under 90 minutes, or swapped for fresh ones in about four minutes without too much in the way of specialized equipment. That’s not just Janus’ killer app, it means that a battery supply deal like this one with a strategic partner who’s invested in the fledgling company’s success is such a huge deal.

“Reliable battery supply is critical to our growth,” adds Hutt, “and Electrovaya’s expanding North American manufacturing capabilities will be important as we scale. Both companies see considerable opportunity in this market, and we look forward to building on our partnership.”

For their part, Electrovoya executives seem to know their role here, and seem excited to be a part of what Janus is doing. “Class 8 trucking is one of the most exciting and demanding frontiers in electrification, and Janus has demonstrated real commercial traction with fleets in the US, Canada and Australia,” said Dr. Raj Das Gupta, CEO of Electrovaya. “Janus has developed a practical solution to one of the major challenges facing heavy transport, enabling diesel-powered trucks to be converted to electric without compromising their commercial operations.”

Electrovaya will be expanding its American manufacturing capacity with lithium-ion cell, module, and battery pack production at its new, potentially BABA-compliant facility in Jamestown, New York, which is expected to come online in 2027.

Salon, Digital News Trailblazer, Folds After 31 Years

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The website said its "publishing model is no longer sustainable" given the "business realities of independent digital journalism."

Salon, a left-leaning publication known for its sharp political and cultural commentary that showed what online journalism could look like before most mainstream outlets entered the space, is closing up shop after 31 years.

In an unsigned farewell announcement on Thursday, Salon pointed to the profound changes in the economics of independent digital journalism and said its publishing model is no longer sustainable.

“Salon was never just a website,” the announcement said. “It was an idea about what journalism could be, born at a moment when the internet was still a largely unexplored frontier.”

The shuttering highlights the rapidly-shifting economics of digital media, where even established publications are contending with massive declines in traffic. Search and social media have upended how outlets attract readers. Artificial intelligence search overviews, which allow audiences to bypass clicking links, are now doing the same. In this landscape, longevity and editorial influence no longer guarantee financial survival.

Launched in 1995 by then-San Francisco Examiner editor David Talbot, Salon was an early experiment in digital publishing without a print offering. It went public four years later and went on to cultivate a sizable readership. Prominent journalists who got a foothold in media through the website include CNN anchor Jake Tapper, NBC News’ Steve Kornacki, The Intercept founder Glenn Greenwald, The Nation national affairs correspondent Joan Walsh, who served as editor-in-chief of Salon for five years.

The site also played a starring role in Nora Ephron’s 2009 feature Julie & Julia as the home blog of Amy Adams’ Julie, who pledged to cook all of Julia Child’s cookbook in a year.

Like many other media outlets, the site saw a massive decline in traffic in 2016 after then-Facebook changed the social media platform’s news feed algorithm to prioritize posts from family and friends over publishers. In 2023, Find.co acquired the website from its previous owners, Drew Schoentrup and Chris Richmond. The privately held media company bills itself as a marketing operation revolving around crypto brands. Its portfolio includes CCN, Webopedia and CryptoManiaks.

Salon’s Thursday post didn’t mention what will happen to its immense archive. On Bluesky, Salon chief content officer Erin Keane said it will “stay live for now.”

OpenAI makes new data center commitments, AI policy recommendations

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

OpenAI Group PBC today made a set of commitments designed to ensure that its data centers benefit the communities where they’re built.

It also proposed that lawmakers make some of the commitments mandatory for artificial intelligence companies.

The move comes two days after Democrats won both the House and the Senate on a campaign backing data center construction curbs. Additionally, voters approved dozens of ballot measures designed to scale back or limit AI infrastructure projects.

OpenAI today reiterated a commitment it made in January to fully pay for the electricity and grid upgrades that its data centers require. That covers items such as the construction of new overhead power lines. Additionally, the company pledged to provide “financial assurances” that will protect other grid users from potential project cancellations and delays.

OpenAI will also take steps to reduce grid strain. In particular, it will scale back its data centers’ power draw at times when energy demand is particularly high. That approach, which is known as a flexible data center model, has also drawn the interest of other industry players as Nvidia Corp. and Google LLC.

Some data center builders sign non-disclosure agreements with local officials to keep construction details under wraps. OpenAI today pledged not to use NDAs. It will instead only seek “narrow protections covering legitimate security information, trade secrets, or proprietary or competitively sensitive information.”

OpenAI will publicly share construction plans and noise projections before construction of a new facility begins. Additionally, it will provide information about the jobs that each data center is expected to create. The goal is to help local unions and training providers prepare residents for the positions.

OpenAI plans to tailor its commitments to each data center. It will publish the customized project terms in a document it calls a local compact. Once construction is underway, the company will provide annual updates and designate a local contact to field residents’ questions.

OpenAI hopes to codify some of the best practices that it published today into law. The company released a policy paper that calls on lawmakers to limit data center developers’ use of NDAs and require them to cover their electricity costs. Furthermore, OpenAI argues that AI companies should be offered incentives to lower their power usage at times of peak grid stress.

Some of the best practices that the ChatGPT developer is prompting are already being adopted by other industry players. Google, for example, today pledged to fully cover the electricity costs of a new $17 billion data center that it plans to build in Wyoming. It will also finance related grid upgrades. Anthropic PBC made a similar commitment in February.

Google, Meta, TikTok sue over social media law

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Three of the biggest social media companies want to block a California law designed to reduce youth social media addiction from going into law.

On Wednesday, Google, Meta, and TikTok each filed lawsuits against California's attorney general, Rob Bonta.

Beginning on Jan. 1, 2027, the bill (AB 1709) would prohibit platforms including Google, Meta, and TikTok from providing "an addictive feature" to users under 16. The law describes such features as "psychologically exploitative" and intended to maximize engagement in a way that leads to compulsive use. This includes infinite scroll, autoplay, and an "addictive" feed.

The law also requires platforms to "implement reasonable measures" so that young users aren't offered such features.

The companies argue that the law infringes on their free speech rights, as well as Section 230, a provision of a 1996 federal law designed to shield online platforms from liability related to user content. Critics of Section 230 say it wrongly provides online platforms with broad immunity.

Google claimed in its lawsuit that its subsidiary, YouTube, personalizes recommendations to ensure that young users receive content relevant to their interests and appropriate for their age and developmental needs. The company defended autoplay as helping users enjoy content uninterrupted and sequentially, like a playlist for educational videos.

"AB 1709's bans on personalized recommendations and autoplay would therefore frustrate YouTube's efforts to provide under-16 users with safe, responsible, and enriching experiences on the platform," the lawsuit said. Meta said that its services "collectively enable billions of people around the world to share ideas, offer support, discuss important topics." Prohibiting personalized feeds and autoplay, would "make it unlawful for Meta to disseminate speech to minors altogether," the company argued.

Lennon Torres, director of movement partnerships at the advocacy organization Heat Initiative, rejected the companies' arguments. Torres, a former child influencer, worked with the bill's author to address First Amendment concerns.

"They're framing this as a free speech fight, but what's really at stake is a business model that profits from keeping kids scrolling, even when it puts them at risk," Torres said. (Torres is a Mashable contributor.)

California's Attorney General's office told Reuters that it was reviewing the filings.

"Our office is committed to defending California's commonsense laws to protect kids online," the office said in a statement to Reuters.

In August, Meta settled a youth social media safety and addiction case for $17 billion, though the company did not admit guilt or liability. As part of that settlement, Meta agreed to limit teens' daily time spent on Instagram and Facebook to two hours; block access to both platforms overnight and during school hours; give teens the option to create a default non-algorithmic feed; and allow teens to turn off autoplay.

Meta also issued a "call to action" to YouTube and TikTok to adopt what the company describes as a new "industry standard." Neither company joined the settlement terms.
