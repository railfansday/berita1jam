The future of tech will not be inclusive by default

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ten years ago, QueerTech began with a feeling that too many 2SLGBTQI+ people in the technology sector know well: not belonging.

My co-founder Andy Saldaña and I were working at different companies in tech and kept running into the same frustration. We could see the possibility of the sector around us, had the skills to succeed, and believed technology, when stewarded responsibly, could change lives. Yet we could not see a place for ourselves, as queer professionals, within it.

I grew up in Morocco, where being queer was, and continues to be, illegal. In 2007, I left for New York City in pursuit of the "American Dream." There, I quickly realized this dream was based on a contradiction — opportunity without equal rights. Finally, I moved to Montreal, drawn to Canada’s reputation for respecting people across cultures, religions, and identities.

Across this journey, I expected geography to change my sense of belonging. It never did. So in 2016, Andy and I started small. QueerTech was a volunteer-run community group built around a simple need: to find one another, create connections, and make space for queer people in an industry where many of us felt invisible.

Today, QueerTech is a national nonprofit working across workforce development, entrepreneurship, research, responsible technology, and community. Our scale has changed, but the need has not. What has evolved is our understanding of what meaningful inclusion requires: not only creating spaces where queer people belong, but breaking down the systemic barriers that determine who gets access to opportunities, who advances into leadership, and who has the power to shape the future of technology.

The scope of our mission, spanning inclusive technology and professional wellbeing, demands ambitious goals and innovative programming grounded in collaboration and practical impact. Our community-first approach supports 2SLGBTQI+ professionals from industry entry through career advancement and entrepreneurship, while driving systemic change through partnerships with industry, investors, regulators, and community organizations.

QueerTech’s goals for the Canadian tech ecosystem are:

Increasing 2SLGBTQI+ representation to 20 percent across the sector.

Advancing 2SLGBTQI+ leadership representation to 5 percent.

Increasing funding for 2SLGBTQI+-owned ventures to 15 percent of total invested capital.

QT Access: Building pathways into tech through skills development, mentorship and career preparation.

QT Leaders: Advancing professionals into decision-making roles that shape workplace culture, policy and innovation.

QT Founders: Equipping queer entrepreneurs with tools, networks and access to capital.

QT Events: Creating safer spaces for connection, learning and opportunity, both in-person and online.

Our responsible technology priorities center on the QT Inclusive AI Initiative. Launched in 2026, it combines self-assessment tools, inclusive AI training, national research, community dialogue, practical governance frameworks and cross-sector partnerships to strengthen AI literacy, accountability, inclusive practices, and collaboration.

These efforts are grounded in a growing body of evidence about the barriers 2SLGBTQI+ professionals face, both within the technology sector and through the systems it creates. QueerTech’s 2026 research makes the scale of those challenges difficult to ignore.

QueerTech’s national Industry Census found 2SLGBTQI+ tech workers reported harassment and discrimination at roughly twice the rate of their non-2SLGBTQI+ peers: 44 percent reported harassment and 50 percent discrimination. Another 35 percent reported bias from AI hiring tools (versus 22 percent of peers). Inclusion in technology can no longer be measured only by who works inside the industry. We must also examine the systems the industry builds and deploys, including who participates in decisions, whose perspectives influence innovation, and who benefits from these advanced technologies.

This year, QueerTech studied both sides of that equation. Our research into AI development found 43 percent of Canadian technology companies reported limited to no formal processes for addressing bias, while 71 percent reported limited to no organizational support for equitable representation.

Our research into AI use in human resources also exposed a significant disconnect between institutional perceptions and queer professionals’ experiences. While 86 percent of HR professionals believed 2SLGBTQI+ employees were "comfortable being out," our 2026 Census found only 37 percent of queer professionals were "out" at work to a great extent — a disparity of nearly 50 percent.

The cost of that disconnect is higher than any one workplace. When queer professionals are overlooked, stalled or pushed out, companies lose skill, experience, and perspective. When people most likely to encounter bias are missing from product decisions, preventable blind spots can become features of automated systems and travel farther, faster and with less visibility.

That is not just an inclusion problem. It is a talent, product, and competitiveness problem for Canada’s innovation economy. As Canada invests in its technological future and digital sovereignty, we cannot afford to overlook talent, limit leadership access, or build technologies that fail to serve the people who depend on them.

The next question is what the sector does with that evidence.

On Nov. 18 and 19, more than 700 tech professionals will gather in Toronto for the 2026 QT Qonference (QTQ) under the theme Who Gets to Trust AI? This question is critical, given that trust in technology is shaped by various decentralized, and often competing, entities across the entire ecosystem. Developers build the systems. Employers decide how they are used. Executives set priorities. Policymakers create the rules. Workers and communities experience the results.

QTQ convenes these groups to address the same challenges from different perspectives. Better decisions happen when the people building, governing, and using technology are at the same table, but participation alone is not enough. Meaningful change requires institutions to translate conversation into action: removing barriers to opportunity, embedding accountability into technology development and deployment, and giving affected communities a meaningful role in shaping these technologies.

The future of tech will not be inclusive by default. It will be shaped by who is included in the decisions that define it. The responsibility belongs to all of us — industry leaders, investors, policymakers, and communities — to ensure that innovation expands opportunity rather than reinforces existing inequalities. Inclusion is not a concession to innovation; it’s a condition for responsible, sustainable innovation.

Your Favorite Coffee Shop Is Probably Streaming Music Illegally. It’s the Music Industry’s Fault, Too (Guest Column)

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A new study found 81% of U.S. businesses don't properly license music, costing creators and other rightsholders. The fix starts with education — not lawsuits.

Walk into almost any shop, gym, salon or restaurant in America, and you’ll hear music. Ask the owner whether it’s licensed and you will likely get a confident “yes.” But in most cases, that answer is, unfortunately, wrong.

Soundtrack recently surveyed 1,525 decision-makers responsible for nearly 15,000 business locations across 10 customer-facing industries to find out how U.S. businesses actually play music. Though the law is clear that businesses need specific licenses to play music in public, the results of the survey describe a market that loves music, depends on it, and, overwhelmingly, does not properly pay for it.

Ninety-seven percent of the businesses we surveyed play music for customers, and 77% do it every day. Adjusted for the size of the U.S., that means roughly 3.8 million U.S. business locations play music in their stores.

Nine in 10 of those surveyed said music boosts employee motivation and shapes the energy of their space, while more than eight in 10 called it an essential part of the business and said it has to fit their brand. Rather than merely being a background expense, businesses see music as part of the product.

Yet despite this overwhelming demand, the way music played in businesses is paid for has not evolved. A striking 79% of businesses illicitly use a consumer streaming service, such as a personal Spotify, Pandora or YouTube account, to play music for customers. Only 28% of businesses use a service built and licensed for business. This is one of the biggest failures, and also one of the biggest opportunities, for the music industry today.

Aside from the scale of misuse, it is significant how many businesses are sure they’re doing it right. Seventy-eight percent of respondents were confident the music in their business is legally compliant. Eighty-three percent said they’re familiar with the rules. Yet 81% are misusing music, and 58% believe it’s legal to stream a personal account in their business.

Even I struggle to distill the U.S. licensing rules into just a few sentences; such is the unnecessary complexity surrounding them. Put simply, businesses require both a music service licensed for commercial use and licenses with all four U.S. performing rights organizations (PROs) — ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR — that cover the music they play.

Some licensed music services bundle performance rights, while others require businesses to secure separate public performance licenses from U.S. PROs. For a business owner, working out exactly what’s included can be a job in itself.

Too often I speak to business owners who believe they’re fully licensed but are missing key PRO licenses or still playing music via their personal streaming account. The survey reflects this, finding only 5% of businesses hold licenses from all four U.S. PROs.

However, this isn’t a story about bad actors. Nine in 10 businesses said compliance matters to them, and most said it matters that artists are paid fairly. The businesses using personal streaming accounts are doing it because it’s what they know. It’s easy, familiar and often free. It’s not a problem of intent; it’s a lack of information and a non-user-friendly system.

The cost to music creators and other rightsholders is real. Combining what businesses told us they spend on music with the number of establishments recorded by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, we estimate that annual spending on music would rise by about $1.4 billion per year if every business was properly licensed through a compliant business music service. That money should flow to songwriters, artists, publishers, labels and the services that license them legally.

The music industry shares the blame. No one proactively educates the market or gives a business owner a clear answer about what they need to be fully covered. Consumer platforms offer free tiers, teaching a generation of shop owners that music costs nothing. And business music services, mine included, have not explained the difference loudly or simply enough.

Enforcement alone won’t close a gap this wide. You can’t sue three million locations into compliance, and you shouldn’t want to. These are the customers who say music is essential to them. The answer is a coordinated push across trade organisations, PROs, publishers, labels, consumer platforms and business services to create one plain-language message about what is legal, provide simpler routes to full licensing and build services businesses actually want to use.

That last part matters. Businesses told us what they look for in streaming services designed for businesses: ease-of-use, price and good playlists (the main complaint about business music services is that their music selections are limited and repetitive). The music offered by these services has to be at least as good as the consumer app on the owner’s smartphone — or the consumer app will keep winning.

Streaming rebuilt the recorded music industry by making the legal option easier for individuals than piracy. Now, the same needs to happen for businesses. The demand is already there, and most want to do it right. What’s missing is clarity, and a legal option that’s simple to use and offers better selections.

That $1.4 billion isn’t lost. It’s waiting for the industry to make doing the right thing the easy thing.

Ola Sars is the co-founder and CEO of Soundtrack, the music streaming service for businesses. The study was conducted by Soundtrack and the NMPA in 2025, surveying 1,525 U.S. music decision-makers via Sago’s opt-in B2B panel.

The U.S. Department of Labor proposed new policy this week that would end fast-track green cards for healthcare professionals like nurses and physical therapists—the latest in a string of policies from the Trump administration raising the bar for foreign-born workers and students across healthcare and other industries.

Currently, nurses, physical therapists and other workers “with exceptional ability in the sciences or arts” are granted an exemption from an administrative test in the permanent labor certification program (PERM), a months-long administrative process in which an employer must prove there are no able, willing, qualified and available U.S. workers for a permanent job ahead of sponsoring a foreign worker.

The exemption, referred to Schedule A, is given to occupations where the DOL recognizes a nationwide shortage of U.S. workers. Nurses and physical therapists have been listed under Schedule A for more than 30 years.

The Department of Labor, in its newly proposed rule, said it wants to eliminate Schedule A as it cannot “demonstrate that the occupations currently listed in Schedule A are still shortage occupations.”

It cited in the proposed rule a late 2023 request for information published under the Biden administration seeking input on potentially expanding Schedule A. Despite more than 2,000 responses, DOL now says it didn’t receive the information it would need to establish a methodology for regularly reviewing and updating Schedule A occupations, and questioned whether it has the statutory authority to make such occupational exemptions in the first place.

“The elimination of Schedule A would enable the Department to better ensure that U.S. workers have an opportunity to apply for available job opportunities, and adhere to the statutory requirements to protect U.S. workers assigned to it, as intended by Congress under the [Immigration and Nationality Act].”

The proposed rule, which is open to public comment for 60 days, also includes requirements for employers to advertise their open jobs more broadly and for a longer period of time to demonstrate a good-faith effort to recruit American workers before turning to a green card sponsorship. These and other proposed changes would be the first “comprehensive” reforms to the PERM program in almost two decades, the Department of Labor said.

If finalized as-is, employers who want to sponsor professional nurses, physical therapists and other affected roles “would be required to go through the lengthier and costlier PERM labor market testing process, adding time, cost and administrative work to a pathway that hospitals, nursing homes and rehabilitation providers have historically relied on to fill persistent shortages,” Caroline Tang, an Austin shareholder at labor and employment law firm Ogletree Deakins, told Fierce Healthcare.

Industry associations as well as other sections of the government have widely projected shortages in the healthcare workforce, including nurses, for the coming years. The Heart Resources and Services Association’s National Center for Health Workforce Analysis, for instance, projects (PDF) national shortages of registered nurses through at least 2038, with a projected 8% shortage in 2028 that is more severe in non-metro areas.

The proposed policy change is among several brought by the Trump administration in line with an order from the White House to prioritize domestic hiring—though many have been stymied in the courts.

Among the most prominent is a $100,000 supplemental fee the Department of Homeland Security attached to H-1B petitions that was vacated by two separate judged, and in August was succeeded by the somewhat altered proposal of an additional H-1B petition fee of $103,265. Another policy from the same department tightening how long nonimmigrant students may remain in the country (referred to as “duration of status”) was temporary blocked hours before implementation in September and fully vacated on Monday (PDF), though the former decision is already being appealed.

The Department of Labor has also proposed increasing the prevailing wage floors for several green cards that legal experts expect will be challenged by employers that would shoulder the resulting financial burden.

Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 per share on Wednesday, above the expected range, setting the stage for the Claude creator's eagerly awaited Nasdaq listing and a historic moment for the artificial intelligence industry.

The company, along with its seven co-founders, upsized the offering, selling about 980 million shares, according to a press release. That brings the amount raised to $81.3 billion, surpassing SpaceX, which held a record IPO in June. Elon Musk's company raised $75 billion initially and a total of $85.7 billion after underwriters exercised their overallotment.

Anthropic's fully diluted market cap sits at around $1.5 trillion. The stock is expected to begin trading on the Nasdaq on Thursday under the ticker symbol "ANT."

The listing is a major test for the AI industry, which has experienced a set of recent shocks, including a dramatic backlash that was felt at the polls in the midterm elections and last week's ouster of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. The market was already jittery on concerns that rising interest rates will make debt unaffordable for many data center developers and neoclouds.

After publicly unveiling its prospectus in October, Anthropic updated the document last week with an expected price range between $70 and $78 per share.  The company had been planning to sell 825 million shares.

Tech's biggest companies have significant exposure to Anthropic, as an investor, partner or customer. Amazon and Google both own stakes valued at over $100 billion, and a group of tech companies, including Google, Microsoft, Nvidia and Advanced Micro Devices agreed to collectively invest $26 billion at the IPO price. Anthropic, meanwhile, is a buyer of costly systems from Nvidia, Google, Amazon and AMD.

The federal government is ruled by inertia, but right now there’s some investigative momentum aimed directly at the major AI players. That will almost certainly ice out some investor hype. Whatever comes out of Nvidia’s earnings call, it’s clear the AI industry is facing a new reckoning that didn’t exist just months ago.

Click to expand Image Protesters attempt to march to the ASEAN Summit venue in Manila but are stopped by hundreds of police officers, November 17, 2026. © 2026 Lian Buan/Human Rights Watch

Police in the Philippines arrested and detained at least 245 people protesting the visit of US President Donald Trump to Manila for the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) summit. 

Protests in Manila started on November 15 opposing the Philippine government’s plan to build a technology manufacturing hub in Capas, Tarlac province, under the US government’s “Pax Silica” initiative. The construction will displace hundreds of Indigenous people and small farmers from their homes and farmlands.

An estimated 6,000 people joined the protests despite police efforts to contain demonstrations by banning them everywhere except in “freedom parks” – designated areas where rallies can be held without a permit.

Yet police arrested 236 people on the outskirts of Metro Manila who were traveling from the Southern Tagalog region to the main protest site. Among those detained were 29 farmers including members of the Indigenous Mangyan tribe, 19 journalists, 9 environmental defenders, and 6 human rights workers. Ephraim Cortez of the National Union of Peoples’ Lawyers said 19 children were detained. 

“This is the largest mass arrest we’ve seen under [President] Marcos,” protest leader Renato Reyes told Human Rights Watch. “Our whole delegation was arrested and taken to a police camp where lawyers were not given immediate access.”

Detainees are being held inside a police camp in a hall with poor ventilation, insufficient drinking water, and inadequate restroom facilities, according to activists and lawyers.

The protesters have been detained for three days without charge. Lawyers representing them expressed concern that they could face criminal charges of illegal assembly, alarms and scandals, and disobedience to authority. 

Police detained without charge another nine protesters at the main protest site on November 15 for attempting to march to the ASEAN summit venue. News footage shows police officers slapping and kicking a protester on the ground. 

International human rights law protects the right to peaceful assembly. Law enforcement may not carry out indiscriminate mass arrests even if a protest violates local laws or becomes disorderly. Arrests need to be based on individualized grounds and comply with the requirements of legality, necessity, and proportionality. Police can arrest people responsible for specific unlawful conduct but not just for taking part in a protest. All those arbitrarily detained for exercising their basic rights should be immediately released.

Binance Says It Won Court Order to Recover Frozen Hack Funds

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Binance says it has secured a court order from Abu Dhabi Global Market to help it recover crypto frozen after the record $6.3 billion hack last month, Reuters reported on Wednesday. The exchange has not said how much of the money the order covers.

Reuters described Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM) as Binance’s main financial regulator. Binance deputy general counsel Dugan Bliss confirmed the order in a statement but gave no details on its terms. Neither the company nor Reuters said which assets it applies to.

“We will continue using every lawful tool available to trace, freeze and recover stolen assets, support our partners in returning funds where possible, and keep those assets out of the hands of the attackers,” Bliss said.

Binance has said about $2.85 billion of the stolen tokens were frozen or intercepted after the Oct. 29 attack, according to its own incident report. That is roughly 45% of the $6.34 billion it says was affected.

Most of it sits with stablecoin issuers. Tether said it froze more than $1.5 billion in USDT within 30 minutes of the attack, and Circle said it froze about $1.1 billion in USDC in coordination with law enforcement. Circle added that it does not decide who owns frozen assets, a question it left to law enforcement and the courts.

The Justice Department has separately obtained court authority to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of the stolen crypto. It is part of more than $2.7 billion that Justice says government and private-sector efforts have frozen. Criminal Division head A. Tysen Duva said the department helped “immediately halt about half of this historic breach.” Reuters did not say how the ADGM order fits with the U.S. action.

Binance says the attack began months earlier, with a tampered software installer that compromised one engineer in June. In September, another engineer was approached by a fake recruiter and persuaded to run a bogus interview assignment that installed malware, the company said.

From there, the attackers reached an internal wallet-signing service and drained Binance hot wallets across multiple blockchains on Oct. 29. The thefts included 233.46 million XRP, about 970,500 SOL and 18,270 BTC, according to Binance.

The FBI has attributed the theft to North Korea’s TraderTraitor group, and Binance has said the same. Pyongyang denies involvement. Blockchain analysts said the attacker tried to outrun the freezes by swapping stablecoins into ether over about 20 minutes, but the issuers moved faster.

That split matters for bitcoin. Tether and Circle can block their own tokens, but bitcoin has no issuer. The stolen BTC is not covered by those freezes and can be stopped only if it reaches an exchange or service that blocks it.

Binance has already said it replaced the affected assets with company funds, using part of its SAFU emergency fund and its corporate treasury. Withdrawals resumed in phases earlier this month, and the exchange aims to reopen all remaining networks and assets by the end of this week, Reuters reported.

On Wednesday, Binance’s Asia-Pacific head SB Seker told Reuters at the Singapore FinTech Festival that the company plans to expand into five Asian markets in 2027 and does not need to raise new funds. He said talks are under way with five or six markets, three of them at a mature stage, and details are due in the first quarter.

Seker said the cost of replacing the stolen tokens was “statistically” not a large amount for the company. “In terms of the impact to us, we can take the impact and we can weather it without any change to the way we operate,” he said.
