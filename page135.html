Billed as a spiritual successor to Kojima's Metal Gear Solid franchise, Physint is an action-espionage game that features Skarsgård alongside several other actors, like Charlee Fraser, Don Lee, and Minami Hamabe. Skarsgård's love for the Metal Gear Solid franchise and a chance to work with Kojima proved to be the deciding factors in taking on the role of the unnamed Physint protagonist.

"I played every single one," Skarsgård said to Josh Horowitz on the Happy Sad Confused podcast. "Starting from the one on PlayStation One all the way to the last one. Skarsgård believes that Kojima's cinematic video games are a new medium that he's pioneering, and with Physint, the developer is getting closer to "something that's photorealistic and also interactive," the actor said. "I think he's a pioneer, and he keeps going out to untreaded waters."

What is EMDR therapy, and does it actually work?

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

Maybe a friend swears EMDR helped her move past something she’d carried for years. Or maybe you came across it online while searching for ways to cope with a memory that still feels heavy. Whether you’ve heard of EMDR but don’t know how it works, or you’re familiar with the approach but skeptical, we’re here to break it all down.

If you’re uncertain, that’s understandable, because EMDR can sound unconventional: you briefly recall a painful memory while following side-to-side stimulation, like tracking a moving target with your eyes. But the research tells a more reassuring story. It’s a well-studied therapy for trauma, and it’s increasingly explored for anxiety and depression, too.

Here’s what EMDR involves, what the research shows, and what a session is like, so you have a clearer picture of whether it might make sense for you.

EMDR stands for eye movement desensitization and reprocessing. It’s a structured therapy that helps the brain reprocess distressing memories so they carry less emotional weight. Psychologist Francine Shapiro, PhD, developed it in the late 1980s after she noticed that certain eye movements seemed to ease her own troubling thoughts.

EMDR rests on Shapiro’s adaptive information processing model. The idea is that your brain normally files an experience away, so you can recall it later without feeling like you’re living it again. But a frightening or painful event can interrupt that process, leaving the memory raw and unfiled, still carrying the fear and detail of that moment. That’s why it can resurface later, as vivid as the day it happened.

According to this model, “processing” a memory means helping your brain finish the filing. You don’t forget what happened, and the facts don’t change. What changes is the emotional charge it carries, so recalling it feels less like reliving it and more like remembering it. To help that happen, EMDR uses a technique called “bilateral stimulation.”

EMDR uses a technique called bilateral stimulation. While you hold a difficult memory in mind, a trained therapist guides you through side-to-side stimulation.

Bilateral stimulation can take a few forms:

eye movements that follow your therapist’s fingers

gentle taps that switch between your hands or knees

tones that play in one ear, then the other

There’s a theory behind the side-to-side part, though researchers are still working out exactly why it helps. The leading explanation is about mental bandwidth. Your working memory, the part of your mind that holds what you’re focused on right now, can only handle so much at once. When you track side-to-side movement while recalling a hard memory, the two tasks compete for that limited space. The hard memory tends to feel a little less vivid, and with repetition it can lose its sharp edges, so it feels less overwhelming to hold.

EMDR has eight phases, though they tend to blend together in practice. The early phases cover your history and teach you coping skills, so you feel prepared before any hard memories arise. In the middle phases, you focus on a specific memory while following bilateral stimulation, which is meant to help your brain reprocess it. The final phases check that the memory feels less distressing and help you close out the work safely.

The evidence is strong for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), a condition that can develop after a frightening or life-threatening event. A Cochrane review (a gold-standard summary of research on a topic) found that EMDR and trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) both reduce PTSD symptoms. They also outperformed other therapies when researchers checked in after treatment ended. A 2024 review in the Journal of Traumatic Stress identified more than 30 randomized controlled trials supporting EMDR for PTSD in adults and children. Major health organizations, including the World Health Organization, recommend EMDR for PTSD.

However, some guidelines, including those from the American Psychological Association (APA), rate the evidence for EMDR as less certain than for trauma-focused CBT. Researchers also debate exactly why EMDR works.

The picture is more mixed outside PTSD. Early research suggests EMDR may help with anxiety, as one meta-analysis found reduced symptoms of anxiety, panic, and phobia, though the studies were small and varied in quality.

Evidence for depression is emerging as well. Another meta-analysis showed reduced depression symptoms, with the strongest effects in more severe cases. These findings are promising, but they don’t carry the same weight as the PTSD research.

After you and a therapist finish the early phases, you bring a specific memory to mind and notice the thoughts and body sensations that come with it. Then your therapist guides you through the bilateral stimulation while you let your mind go where it goes. You can ask to pause or stop at any time. Unlike many talk therapies, EMDR doesn’t require you to describe the memory in detail.

Sessions usually run 60–90 minutes. Your therapist pauses often to check in on what you noticed. Over several rounds of bilateral stimulation, many people find the memory feels less sharp, even though the facts don’t change. Between sessions, a mindfulness practice, such as slow breathing or a body scan, can help you ease feelings that surface.

Think of it as a calming complement to therapy, not a replacement for a trained therapist.

More complex or long-standing trauma usually takes longer, sometimes many months. Therapists describe a typical course as 6–12 sessions, though it varies widely from person to person.

Along the way, some people feel worse before they feel better. Painful memories can stir up strong emotions in the short term, which is common and doesn’t mean EMDR isn’t working. Before reprocessing begins, your therapist teaches you coping skills, such as grounding exercises that anchor you in the present, so you have tools to use if those feelings get intense.

EMDR differs from talk therapy because it doesn’t require you to describe your experience in detail. You’ll still talk with your therapist, especially early on, but the reprocessing happens while you hold a memory in mind and follow bilateral stimulation. Talk therapies like cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) focus more on examining and reshaping your thoughts out loud.

The main benefit of EMDR is that it can ease the distress tied to painful memories. For many people with PTSD, that can mean fewer flashbacks and less of that constant, on-alert feeling.

Much of the debate is about why EMDR works, not whether it does. The evidence is strongest for PTSD, with early and more mixed findings for anxiety and depression.

Some studies suggest the eye movements themselves add little. The benefit may instead come from giving your mind a second task while you recall a memory, which can make it feel less vivid and emotionally intense. Other tasks that split your attention may have the same effect.

Certain guidelines, including the APA’s, consider the evidence for EMDR less established than for trauma-focused CBT. None of this takes away from the relief many people find with EMDR. It’s useful context as you weigh your options with a therapist.

EMDR is generally safe when a trained therapist delivers it, though you may experience some short-term effects. You might feel more emotional or tired between sessions, or have vivid dreams as your brain keeps processing the memory.

These reactions usually pass within a day or two. If they last longer or feel overwhelming, it’s a good idea to tell your therapist, who can adjust the pace with you. Serious side effects are uncommon when a therapist follows the full eight-phase process.

The number of sessions you need depends on the memory you’re addressing and how long it has affected you. Some people feel relief after a few sessions, while longer-standing experiences often take more time. A common range is 6–12 sessions, but your timeline may be shorter or longer. Your therapist can give you a better sense of what to expect once they know your history.

Full EMDR needs a trained therapist, so it’s not something to try on your own. A therapist helps you reprocess memories and stay grounded if one feels overwhelming, which matters most with intense trauma.

Quota o RNG? Due modi opposti di misurare il caso

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sabato sera, a San Siro, al derby sono bastati pochi minuti per prendere una direzione precisa. Pulisic al 4', Rabiot al 10', poi Bastoni ad accorciare e Diego Moreira a chiudere il primo tempo sul 3-1. Chi in quei minuti teneva d'occhio un'app di risultati ha visto i numeri delle quote muoversi quasi in tempo reale: ogni gol riscriveva le probabilità della serata nel giro di pochi secondi. Esiste però un altro tipo di numero legato al caso che non si sarebbe mosso di un millimetro, qualunque cosa fosse successa in campo. È quello dei giochi da casinò online, dove l’esito non dipende da ciò che accade nel mondo reale ma da un algoritmo certificato. Lì non ci sono formazioni da studiare né statistiche da aggiornare: le probabilità sono fissate in partenza e restano identiche dal primo all’ultimo giro. Su una slot machine, nessun gol, nessun infortunio e nessun cambio tattico può spostare di un decimale ciò che accadrà al giro successivo. Sono due modi opposti di trattare l’incertezza, e capire la differenza aiuta a leggere meglio entrambi.

Una quota decimale è, prima di tutto, una probabilità travestita. Il calcolo è semplice: dividendo 1 per la quota si ottiene la probabilità implicita che il bookmaker attribuisce a un evento. Una quota di 2,00 corrisponde al 50%, una di 4,00 al 25%. Se si sommano le probabilità implicite di vittoria, pareggio e sconfitta, però, il totale supera sempre il 100%: quella differenza è il margine dell'operatore, il prezzo del servizio incorporato nei numeri. Dietro quella cifra lavora un modello statistico alimentato da una quantità enorme di dati: risultati storici, rendimento in casa e in trasferta, assenze, calendario, condizioni della squadra. Negli ultimi anni a questi ingredienti si sono aggiunti gli expected goals, la metrica che assegna a ogni tiro una probabilità di diventare gol in base a distanza, angolo, parte del corpo usata e tipo di azione. Un rigore, per esempio, vale tra 0,76 e 0,79 xG: anche il tiro più «facile» del calcio, statisticamente, non è mai una certezza.

Perché la partita stessa è un flusso continuo di informazioni nuove. Prima del calcio d'inizio il modello ragiona su medie e tendenze; dal primo minuto in poi deve incorporare ciò che sta accadendo davvero. Un gol nei primi cinque minuti cambia radicalmente lo scenario, perché lascia a una squadra quasi tutta la gara per difendere un vantaggio e costringe l'altra a scoprirsi. Basta rileggere il tabellino del derby per capire quanto lavoro abbiano dovuto fare gli algoritmi sabato sera: quattro gol in 43 minuti significano quattro ricalcoli profondi delle probabilità, a cui si sommano cartellini, sostituzioni e ogni singola occasione. In questo senso una quota assomiglia più al prezzo di un titolo in Borsa che a un numero fisso: è una stima che si aggiorna man mano che arrivano notizie, e che può essere rivista anche più volte in pochi minuti.

Su una slot online non c'è nessun evento esterno da interpretare. L'esito di ogni giro è stabilito da un generatore di numeri casuali, l'RNG, un algoritmo che produce sequenze imprevedibili e che associa a ciascuna una combinazione di simboli. Ogni giro è indipendente dal precedente: il sistema non ha memoria, non conosce la «forma» e non tiene conto di quanto tempo sia passato dall'ultima combinazione vincente. Sul mercato regolamentato italiano questo meccanismo non è lasciato alla fiducia. Prima di arrivare nel catalogo di un concessionario come AdmiralBet, ogni titolo viene certificato da laboratori indipendenti che verificano la casualità del generatore e la percentuale teorica di ritorno al giocatore, il cosiddetto RTP. Per i giochi di sorte a quota fissa la normativa fissa una soglia minima del 90%, e il valore deve essere dichiarato in modo trasparente. È importante capire che cosa rappresenta quel numero. Un RTP del 96% non descrive una singola sessione, ma il comportamento statistico del gioco su milioni di giri simulati: è una proprietà del software, non una previsione su ciò che accadrà a chi gioca in una sera qualsiasi.

La sintesi più efficace è questa: la quota è una stima, l'RTP è una specifica. Nel calcio nessuno conosce in anticipo la probabilità «vera» che il Milan vinca un derby. I modelli la approssimano con i dati disponibili e possono sbagliare, perché in campo entrano variabili che nessun archivio storico può prevedere fino in fondo, dalla giornata di grazia di un singolo giocatore a un episodio arbitrale. In una slot, al contrario, la distribuzione delle probabilità non viene stimata ma progettata. Il game designer decide a tavolino con quale frequenza compariranno determinate combinazioni, i laboratori di certificazione verificano che il software si comporti esattamente così, e quel comportamento resta identico per tutta la vita del titolo. Il caso esiste in entrambi i mondi, ma in uno viene interpretato, nell'altro viene costruito.

L'errore di ragionamento più diffuso nasce proprio dal trasferire la logica del calcio ai giochi basati su RNG. Nel pallone la forma conta davvero: una squadra che vince da cinque partite ha buone ragioni statistiche per essere considerata favorita alla sesta. Su una slot, invece, una lunga serie di giri senza combinazioni vincenti non rende «più probabile» la prossima. Ogni giro riparte da zero, con le stesse identiche probabilità del primo. È lo stesso principio della moneta lanciata in aria: dopo dieci teste consecutive, la probabilità che esca croce al lancio successivo resta il 50%. I matematici lo sanno da secoli, eppure è una delle intuizioni più difficili da accettare, perché la nostra esperienza quotidiana, sport compreso, ci abitua a cercare tendenze anche dove non ce ne sono.

Forse proprio questo: che l'incertezza del calcio è irriducibile, e che è anche il motivo per cui lo guardiamo. Nessun modello, per quanto sofisticato, avrebbe potuto scrivere in anticipo il primo tempo di sabato sera, con tre gol rossoneri in meno di tre quarti d'ora. Le quote possono solo inseguire la partita, mai precederla. Il caso di una slot, invece, è un caso ingegnerizzato, misurato e certificato prima ancora che qualcuno prema il pulsante. Due numeri che a prima vista sembrano parenti, una quota su uno schermo e una percentuale in una scheda di gioco, raccontano in realtà due filosofie opposte: una prova a prevedere il mondo, l'altra lo simula. Ed è proprio nel distinguerle che si trova la chiave per leggerle entrambe con lucidità.

The AI boom’s missing balance-sheet question: What has actually been verified?

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

GUEST COLUMN by Neil P. Osnato

I spent 13 years on the floor of the New York Stock Exchange. One lesson stayed with me long after the trading jackets disappeared: A transaction could be executed, documented and perfectly valid while the assumptions that made it attractive proved less durable than anyone expected.

That distinction matters in the artificial intelligence infrastructure boom. We can believe AI will transform productivity and still ask whether each financing, data-center expansion or long-term capacity agreement is supported by evidence that can survive the investment’s life.

The industry is making commitments across different clocks. Compute contracts may run for years. Debt can mature decades from now.

Servers have much shorter technology cycles. Power infrastructure, transmission upgrades and buildings may outlast several generations of the equipment they were built to support. Meanwhile, enterprise customers are still learning what they will use, how intensively they will use it and what they will pay.

None of this proves the investments are unsound. It does create a question that belongs beside revenue forecasts and capital budgets: What exactly has been verified at the moment an institution decides to rely on a promise?

An agreement to purchase compute can be commercially meaningful. It may support financing and demonstrate customer commitment. But it does not by itself establish that the underlying workloads will remain economically productive at the contracted scale, or that the customer will renew on similar terms once technology and prices change.

A serious review must separate the existence of the contract from the economic assumptions behind its duration, usage profile, counterparty capacity and renewal expectations. Contract enforceability and economic durability are related; they are not interchangeable.

A data-center campus can be announced, financed and partially constructed before all of its necessary electricity, cooling, network and equipment dependencies have become operational. A power purchase agreement is not itself proof of deliverability at the exact commissioning date. GPU orders are not proof that a facility can run the ordered cluster at the planned duty cycle.

For infrastructure planning, I use a simple sequence: requested load, evidenced load, deliverable load, energized load, utilized load and sustained demand. These are not six fancy names for the same megawatt. They describe different claims, each requiring different supporting records.

That distinction is already relevant to public grid decisions. Persistence Analytics Group has submitted comments in MISO’s Large Load Working Group proceedings on load forecasts, application and study processes, paired-resource studies and cost transparency. The recurring question is how planners preserve alignment when a proposed project’s capacity, timing or other material conditions change after an earlier study or commitment.

Financial engineering can provide meaningful protection. Minimum payments, parent guarantees, collateral requirements, termination provisions and residual-value commitments can shift or mitigate losses. They should be evaluated on their actual terms and credit strength.

But a contractual remedy is not the same as the continued usefulness of the underlying asset. A guarantee may support recovery from a counterparty while leaving the investor dependent on that counterparty’s resources, enforceability, timing and the value remaining after a disruption. Those are separate assumptions that deserve separate evidence.

This is especially important when apparently distinct exposures rely on a small set of interconnected customers, infrastructure providers, funding sources or technology assumptions. Diversifying contracts does not necessarily diversify the dependency underneath them.

Initial diligence usually asks whether a project is supportable at approval. A more durable approach asks what must remain true afterward — and which changes require the original case to be revisited before additional money is committed.

Consider a campus whose customer delays its ramp, whose power availability moves to a later year or whose equipment changes materially. Each event may be manageable on its own. But if the original financing or infrastructure decision assumed the earlier configuration, someone must decide whether the updated evidence still supports the next commitment.

That is a decision gate, not a prediction about whether AI succeeds. It calls for a documented chain: the representation being relied upon; the assumption embedded in it; the evidence supporting that assumption; the dependencies that must hold; the party bearing downside exposure; and the material change that would require reassessment.

Different specialists own different pieces. Engineers assess technical deliverability. Lawyers assess contractual rights. Accountants and finance teams assess financial statements and cash flows. Independent decision assurance asks whether those records, taken together, actually establish the proposition the capital allocator is relying on — and whether known gaps have been made explicit.

The productive-utilization debate is essential. So is the question of who will pay for AI intelligence, and at what price. But before authorizing the next investment, a chief financial officer or investment committee can ask an even more immediate question: Which assumptions are verified facts, which are binding commitments, which are conditional forecasts and which need to be revalidated because something material has changed?

A sector can produce transformational technology and disappointing returns on some of its assets. We have seen that distinction in markets before. The lesson is not to stop investing. It is to stop treating execution, delivery, utilization and durable economic value as if they were the same event.

On the trading floor, the confirmation of a trade was the beginning of understanding the position, not the end. AI infrastructure deserves the same discipline.

If an AI visibility dashboard helped you win budget this year, read four datasets before your next quarterly review. Together, they show AI assistants send a sliver of the traffic Google sends. They also show that none of these datasets could isolate the hidden AI influence many of us assumed was turning up in Direct and branded search.

That doesn’t make AI visibility a sham. It means most of the ways we measure it wouldn’t pass a basic data quality test.

On Oct. 22, 2026, Rand Fishkin published a 5-Minute Whiteboard on the SparkToro Blog titled “Is AI Visibility a Marketing Boondoggle?” The next day, Amanda Natividad, SparkToro’s chief evangelist, sent her Audience Research Newsletter asking, “What if AI visibility is a sham?”

A long time ago, I learned to separate events from trends. Two warnings from co-authors of the same book, Zero Click Marketing, who work at the same company and publish a day apart, look more like one event than two.

But the data underneath their warnings came from other people. And it has been piling up.

Marie Haynes looked at 12 months of GA4 data across 100 websites. ChatGPT visitors converted at 13.8%, against 3.02% for Google organic. That’s more than 4.5 times better, and it’s the stat AI visibility vendors will quote.

But here’s the stat they won’t. Google still drove 96.5% of total conversions in her dataset, and Google organic drove 27 times more conversions than all LLMs combined. She also found that the exponential growth in ChatGPT sessions ended in August 2025, around the release of GPT-5.

SEO Works found a narrower edge. AI referrals converted at 7.18% versus 6.08% for organic, a pooled ratio of 1.18. Yet AI traffic converted worse on 23 of the 63 sites with enough volume to compare. For B2B product businesses, AI converted at 0.62 times the organic rate.

The line from that study I’d tape to every SEO manager’s monitor is this one. A 5% lift in organic search delivered 10.3 additional conversions per site per month. Doubling AI referral traffic delivered 6.6.

The usual rebuttal is that clicks were never the point. People see a brand in a ChatGPT answer, don’t click, and then search for it or type in the URL later.

Kevin Indig went looking for that. At first, eight of his 12 properties showed Direct traffic growing from August to September. Then he filtered for engaged sessions. Across the filtered January-September sample, total Direct rose 140.9%, while engaged Direct changed by −1.0%. On one site, GA4 recorded 409,000 Direct sessions, and only 198 were engaged.

He then ran 632 tests on four brands, comparing weekly AI visibility with Direct traffic and branded search at delays of zero to six weeks. When he shifted the data to deliberately wrong dates as a placebo check, several of those mismatched alignments correlated as strongly as the real ones. His conclusion: “An effect could still exist. I just couldn’t isolate it.”

Natividad tied this back to an Ahrefs study of 75,000 brands from May 2025. Branded web mentions correlated at 0.664 with AI Overview brand visibility, and branded anchors at 0.527. Branded search volume came in last at 0.392. If AI visibility reliably produced branded search, she argued, that number should be much higher.

Neither of them said AI doesn’t matter. Fishkin wrote that AI is “probably quite influential on some buyers in some sectors.” He credited Mike King with the better frame, that AI is “more like a brand channel, a digital billboard if you will, and it should be invested-in and measured as such.”

Natividad put it more sharply. “Familiarity itself is a marketing outcome.” Then she added the sentence that matters most for anyone building a dashboard. “If we can’t prove it, we shouldn’t trust the attribution just because it seems right.”

The SEO world is arriving late to a conversation the PR measurement world started months ago.

On May 20, 2026, AMEC, the International Association for the Measurement and Evaluation of Communication, launched its GEO Principles at its Global Summit in Dublin. Two days later, I admitted that I’d been treating AI citations as the new search rankings and that framing was wrong. Appearing in an AI answer is an output, whether it changes what people understand, believe, or do is an outcome.

AMEC has also gone a layer deeper with a companion white paper, “AMEC Data Quality Principles: Tackling the Unstructured Data Quality Challenges in the AI Era.” Its lead author is Rob Key, founder and CEO of Converseon, and it was produced with the Global Data Quality Initiative, a coalition that includes the Insights Association, ESOMAR, and the Market Research Society.

The paper was written for social listening and media analysts, not SEOs. Read it with an AI visibility dashboard open in another tab anyway. It proposes four questions any data set should pass before anyone acts on it.

The third question is the one that should keep SEO directors up at night. AMEC calls it predictive and construct validity. A metric can be “internally consistent but externally untrue,” the paper warns, and in an AI era that kind of data “does not stay quarantined; it propagates.”

That is a precise description of most AI visibility scores. They’re consistent from week to week. They go up when you publish more. Whether they predict anything a business cares about is, after Indig’s 632 tests, still unproven.

Two more lines from the paper belong in every GEO pitch deck. Principle four says, “Evaluations performed once and stored on a shelf are decorative, not protective.” And its crosswalk with AMEC’s GEO framework says outputs are directional and should be tested “across tools, prompts, and time.” A single screenshot of ChatGPT recommending your brand is an anecdote.

Principle five goes further. It tells practitioners to avoid manipulative GEO, meaning content engineered to game generative AI rather than inform it. AMEC’s phrase for it is “promotion over precision.”

One caution in the spirit of the paper itself. Some of its most striking figures, including the claim that off-the-shelf sentiment models hover at 60% to 65% precision, come from a Converseon-sponsored benchmark, and Converseon sells the cure. The paper calls itself non-promotional, and I think its principles stand on their own. But apply its Source question to the paper, too.

I don’t think AI visibility is a boondoggle. I think the AI visibility score is.

The datasets above don’t prove AI has no influence. They prove we’ve been grading a brand channel with traffic-channel math, then filling the gap with scores nobody has validated against behavior. That’s how a reasonable idea turns into a budget line you can’t defend.

Mike King’s billboard frame is the right one, and it fits what I’ve been arguing in my columns on YouGov’s brand research. AI brands are winning on consideration before they win on trust. If AI answers work like a billboard for your brand, measure them the way brand marketers have measured billboards for decades, with exposure, recall, and consideration, and stop asking them to behave like a search result.

Demote AI referral traffic to a diagnostic. Keep reporting it. Stop setting targets on it. Then run the SEO Works math on your own GA4 property. Calculate what a 5% lift in organic conversions is worth next to doubling your AI referrals. If organic wins, as it did across their sample, your roadmap should say so.

Run your dashboard through AMEC’s four questions. Before an AI visibility number goes into a deck, document which tools and prompts produced it, whether results were logged in or logged out, and how bot sessions were filtered. Define whether you’re counting a mention, a citation, or a recommendation, because they aren’t interchangeable. If you can’t answer those, the number fails AMEC’s Source and Accuracy questions before anyone asks about results.

Test your score for real-world validity, Indig style. Pull weekly AI visibility for your brand and line it up against engaged Direct sessions, branded search, and pipeline at lags of one to six weeks. Then shift the series to wrong dates and run it again. If the wrong dates correlate as well as the right ones, you’ve learned your score isn’t predicting anything yet. That’s worth knowing before your vendor renewal.

Build a governed prompt library and re-run it on a schedule. Pick prompts that map to real buyer questions, not prompts that flatter your brand. Run them across several AI tools, save the outputs, and repeat monthly or quarterly. AMEC’s principle is that accuracy isn’t a point in time, and the same holds for visibility.

Ask people, then triangulate. Indig’s recommendation is self-reported attribution. Add a free-text “How did you hear about us?” field to demo and signup forms, and add AI assistants to your brand lift surveys. Natividad warns that this data has its own flaws, so treat it as one input. Her framework of exposure, behavior, and business outcomes is a sensible way to weigh them together.

Put money where the evidence overlaps. SEO Works found the sites gaining AI referrals were the same sites improving in organic, with a correlation of +0.557. Ahrefs found branded web mentions tracked AI Overview visibility better than branded search did. Earned authority does double duty. That’s the least glamorous budget line on your plan and, right now, the best supported one.

Fishkin asked whether AI visibility is a marketing boondoggle. My answer is that it becomes one the moment we report a number we haven’t checked against what customers actually do.
