Alessandro Vocalelli, giornalista e opinionista de’ La Gazzetta dello Sport, ha presentato la sfida tra Inter e Como, in programma domenica alle 18:00 a San Siro.

“E così, in questa giornata che ci porterà alla pausa per gli impegni della Nazionale, si sfideranno al Meazza - in una partita che profuma di scudetto - un club molto potente ed un altro che rappresenta la classica favola e comunque un modello.

Ora, penserete voi, non c’è dubbio che il primo sia l’Inter e il secondo il Como, che sta facendo stropicciare gli occhi dalla meraviglia ed è reduce dal record dei 9 gol segnati in Champions League. La realtà - anche se può sembrare un paradosso e comunque rasenta la provocazione - va però rovesciata. Perché, lo dicono i numeri che spesso sono molto più obiettivi delle opinioni, il club che esprime una straordinaria potenza in questo momento è proprio il Como e - va preso come un formidabile complimento al lavoro di Marotta e del suo staff - la favola è quella interista.

In che senso? vi starete giustamente chiedendo. Nel senso che, basta prendere in esame il saldo di mercato delle ultime quattro stagioni, per scoprire che la differenza tra Inter e Como è veramente abissale. Pensate che l’Inter ha chiuso complessivamente con un saldo negativo riferito esclusivamente alle operazioni di compravendita, tra entrate e uscite, di 131 milioni. Naturalmente compensate con i vari ricavi, che hanno permesso di chiudere gli ultimi due esercizi in positivo. Comunque, tornando al mercato, nello stesso periodo il saldo del Como è stato di meno 325 milioni. Non solo: quello della Juve di meno 296, del Milan di 216 e della Roma di 187. Insomma, per farla breve e andare al nocciolo della questione, l’Inter ha “speso” in questi quattro anni addirittura 194 milioni meno del Como, ma anche 165 meno della Juve, 85 meno del Milan e 56 meno della Roma. Se non è una favola questa, o un modello, dite voi come si potrebbe diversamente raccontare una società che in questo quadriennio ha vinto due scudetti, conquistato due finali di Champions e vinto Coppe nazionali. E viene all’unanimità considerata quella che ha costruito negli anni la squadra più forte, più completa. Attraverso, e questo è un merito pazzesco, l’ingaggio di calciatori che sono stati rivenduti - pensate a Onana - con una plusvalenza di oltre 50 milioni. O come altri ingaggiati a parametro zero. E anche qui basta fare l'esempio, Marcos Thuram, che ha lasciato il Borussia Monchengladbach da svincolato, al pari di Zielinski, che con l’attuale gestione è diventato praticamente un insostituibile.

E se il Como - che naturalmente merita il massimo degli elogi per quello che ha costruito - è stato bravissimo a puntare su Fabregas, bisogna riconoscere che anche sulla scelta degli allenatori l’Inter è stata particolarmente illuminata. Perché dopo aver perso Antonio Conte, insofferente davanti alle cessioni di Lukaku e Hakimi, ha avuto l’intuizione di andare a prendere Simone Inzaghi alle prese con un complicato rinnovo di contratto con la Lazio. A carte viste, certo, la scelta si è rivelata azzeccata, ma in quel momento erano in molti ad essere diffidenti, magari credendo che per sostituire Conte sarebbe stato necessario puntare su un altro pilota di grande esperienza, magari attingendo al mercato straniero. E quando Inzaghi, un po’ come Conte, è andato via improvvisamente e per certi versi anche inaspettatamente, in pochi pensavano che - al di là della sua storia nerazzurra da calciatore - l’idea di affidarsi a Chivu (con appena sedici panchine di serie A a Parma) fosse quella giusta. La cronaca ha detto il contrario. La storia ci ricorda invece che, prima di dirottare su Chivu, l’Inter abbia pensato proprio a Fabregas. Perché a volte i destini, come appunto capiterà al Meazza, si incrociano. Ma questo è un altro discorso”.

Queen's Domain might be the trendy game of the moment but for my money, the classic grid-based action of Underkeep is where it's at. (And yes, there's a demo.)

Everyone's talking about Queen's Domain right now, but as someone who tried Queen's Domain and did not care for it, allow me to direct your attention to an alternative old-school dungeon crawler: Underkeep, which launched on Steam on November 5 and has been absolutely phenomenal through the roughly four hours I've put into it so far.

Like Queen's Domain, Underkeep is firmly rooted in the 1990s, but I'm talkin' the early '90s, and even before that: Classic grid-based RPGs like The Bard's Tale, Dungeon Master, Wizardry, and Eye of the Beholder. In fact, as someone who loves Eye of the Beholder (specifically the second one, The Legend of Darkmoon), I noticed a lot of nearly direct lifts from that series: primarily visual, but also a few audio cues, that I swear I've heard before.

But my concerns that Underkeep might be a low-effort knockoff evaporated pretty quickly. After four hours of play I feel like I've barely scratched the game's surface: I still haven't found the titular village of Underkeep, where I assume the real action will begin, but I've crashed (literally, in a few cases) through multiple dungeons, all interconnected horizontally or vertically, and explored an overworld that's a whole lot bigger than I expected. At the very least, Underkeep looks beefy.

Underkeep's gameplay preserves the vibe of those old-time RPGs while eliminating most of their more objectionable nonsense. Its shared inventory is divided into categories that make managing and swapping equipment between party members extremely easy, the automapping is detailed while still allowing freehand notes (I know, I know, get some paper and do your own maps, but who has time for that these days?), and tools are used automatically without needing to be equipped.

And unlike so many games in the genre, Underkeep is entirely turn-based. This eliminates the cheese strategy of hitting an enemy and moving a step away before they can hit back, which carried so many gamers through so many fights in so many games over the years. But it also encourages more thoughtfulness: When I see an enemy in the distance, I can take my time to think about what I want to do, move to a more advantageous position, or even just go take a break for a while.

I'm free to do whatever I want until melee combat actually begins. Once that happens, everyone in the party has to finish their turn, and then let the enemies finish theirs, at which point we're all free to run away (and probably be chased) or launch another round of fighting. I'll always come down on the real-time-with-pause side of the argument for isometric RPGs, but the turn-based approach is ideal for Underkeep.

Character classes are a fairly standard mix—fighter, paladin, wizard, thief, that sort of thing—but the races are a bit more interesting. Humans and dwarves are on the menu, and "elk" are kind of like elves, but then you get the stoneborn, raven, and slugman, all of them nicely weird. Stoneborn, for instance, are basically golems⁠—extremely strong and tough, but they have the agility of a Mack truck and can't wear body armor. They're basically a boulder that can hit you really hard, which is great in some ways but has some real drawbacks too. Ravens, meanwhile, are subject to random mutations that can take them off in unforeseeable directions: My own raven character recently mutated a healing buff called Roommate from the Void: "Something moved into your body at some point and never left. It pays rent in blood."

There's also no limit to combining races and classes: A stoneborn thief or slugman paladin may not be a great idea, but if you want to give it a shot, knock yourself out.

There's not a lot of reading to do in Underkeep, but the writing for those encounters is pleasingly evocative in an old-school tabletop way, and there are plenty of surprises to be had too. Depending on how you deal with the red-cloaked guy above, for instance, you may get a reward, you might get jumped, or you could find yourself facing some sort of bigger trouble down the road—that's not confirmed yet, but I definitely got some 'Be seein' you' stinkeye from him before he slunk off into the next life.

It makes me a little sad to say, but one of my first thoughts when I saw Underkeep was whether the assets were made with generative AI. I'm not the only one to have that worry: Multiple arguments about it are underway in the Steam forums right now. Unlike some people in those conversations who say they "can just tell" when AI is present, I cannot—but the Steam store page has no AI disclosure, and developer Rake in Grass has repeatedly insisted that no generative AI was used.

"All artwork in Underkeep was hand-drawn by our artists," an FAQ states. "Some variations in style are natural, as several artists contributed to the game. The occasional "mixels" are caused by dynamic scaling used to simulate 3D perspective and smooth animations."

It's unfortunate that debates like this are so commonplace now, because Underkeep is a joy: I haven't played a whole huge lot of them in recent years, but this is easily my favorite grid-based RPG since the great Legend of Grimrock 2, which will soon be 15 years old.

Whitney Museum selects a staff curator and a stage director to lead 2028 biennial

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Meg Onli and Zack Winokur Photo by Bryan Derballa

The Whitney Museum of American Art has selected Meg Onli, a curator at the museum, and Zack Winokur, a choreographer and director for the stage, to curate the 2028 edition of the Whitney Biennial. It is due to open in March 2028.

“Every Whitney Biennial springs from a distinct curatorial alchemy,” Scott Rothkopf, the Whitney’s director, said in a statement. “Meg is one of the most compelling curatorial voices working today, with an extraordinary ability to champion artists and bring fresh perspectives to the history of American art. Zack is an equally inventive artistic programmer who has become one of the leading voices of his generation through groundbreaking work across opera, theater, dance and the visual arts. Their partnership promises a radical new vision for the 2028 Biennial.”

Onli is one of the Whitney’s star curators, having joined the institution as a curator-at-large in 2023, then as a curator in 2024. She co-curated the 2024 Whitney Biennial, titled Even Better Than the Real Thing, with fellow Whitney curator Chrissie Iles. Most recently she co-curated the museum’s current Roy Lichtenstein retrospective (until 10 May 2027).

“From the Whitney’s early Annuals to its many subsequent iterations, the Biennial has a rich history of reinvention that is already giving us much to think about,” Onli said in a statement. “I couldn’t be more excited to explore what the Biennial can be alongside Zack, who is such a dynamic thinker, and to see where our conversations with artists lead.”

Winokur’s work spans dance, music, visual art, opera and theatre. He is the co-founder and artistic director of the performing arts collective AMOC* (American Modern Opera Company), which last year staged a five-week takeover of the Lincoln Center campus on Manhattan’s Upper West Side. He was previously the artistic director of Little Island, a park and cultural event space designed by Heatherwick Studio and MNLA that rises out of the Hudson River not far from the Whitney. His projects for the stage and screen have included directing productions at Opera Philadelphia and the Santa Fe Opera, and working on the expanded version of The Wizard of Oz currently showing at Sphere in Las Vegas.

“I’m honoured to be curating the Whitney Biennial with Meg,” Winokur said in a statement. “Our shared desire to centre objects and people and the collision between them already echoes our collaboration. It’s that energy that makes the dialogue with her so fun and inspiring. As an artist, what excites me most is being in service to other artists, and I’m delighted to have the opportunity to do so in the context of the Whitney.”

The 2026 Whitney Biennial, which was organised by staff curators Marcela Guerrero and Drew Sawyer, closed in October. It was seen by more than 550,000 visitors, according to the museum’s figures, around 75% of whom identified as either Millennials or Gen Z, making it the youngest audience since the Whitney started tracking visitors’ ages.

The exhibition’s 2026 edition was also remarkably controversy-free, though the exhibition is often a lightning rod for debates, protests and political statements. The 2019 edition was led by Whitney curators Jane Panetta and Rujeko Hockley, and came to be known colloquially as the “tear gas biennial” due to weekly protests and participating artists’ threatened withdrawals over the Whitney’s vice chairman at the time, Warren Kanders, who was the chief executive of Safariland, a defense and security contractor that manufactured tear gas canisters that had been deployed against asylum-seekers at the Mexico-US border, among other materials. Kanders eventually resigned from the museum’s board; he is currently the chairman and chief executive of Cadre Holdings, a Florida-based manufacturer of security and law-enforcement equipment.

The 2017 Whitney Biennial, which was co-curated by Christopher Y. Lew and Mia Locks, was largely overshadowed by the wide-ranging debate over a single painting in the exhibition: Dana Schutz’s Open Casket (2016), in which the artist portrayed the famous image from 1955 of 14-year-old Emmett Till lying in an open casket following his brutal abduction, mutilation and murder. The artist Hannah Black wrote an open letter criticising the work’s inclusion in the biennial and calling for its destruction, sparking protests inside and outside the museum and arguments about censorship, identity and cultural appropriation.

The 2028 Whitney Biennial will be the 83rd edition of the exhibition, which started out as a series of medium-specific exhibitions before becoming the Whitney Annual in 1937, then transitioning to its every-other-year format in 1973. It remains the most closely watched recurring exhibition of contemporary art in the Americas.

Where Claude Code, Codex and Cursor Keep Your Chat History on Disk, and How to Read It

Claude Code chat history is stored on your own machine as plaintext JSONL files under ~/.claude/projects/, one file per session, and deleted after 30 days by default. On Windows the same folder is %USERPROFILE%\.claude\projects\. Codex and Cursor keep their own records in their own places and formats. Paths and defaults below were last verified on 6 November 2026, and they do change, so check them against your installed version.

Most of us never open these files. We should, for two reasons. They record real work: why you dropped Redis for SQLite, the three-hour bug behind a one-line fix, the reason the repo always uses pnpm. Yet you may still re-explain your project every morning while all of that sits on disk.

They also hold more than you'd guess. Claude Code's own docs (the "Explore the .claude directory" page) say anything that passes through a tool lands in the transcript: file contents, command output, pasted text. That makes these folders worth knowing about whether you want to mine them or clean them out.

Each session is a file at ~/.claude/projects//.jsonl. The project folder name is your working directory with separators swapped for dashes, such as -Users-you-my-project. Each transcript holds messages, tool calls and tool results, plus the system prompt and tool definitions Claude Code sent the model.

Typed prompts: these live separately in ~/.claude/history.jsonl, which powers up-arrow recall. The 30-day sweep doesn't normally touch that file, so it stays until you remove it.

Retention: the cleanupPeriodDays setting controls the age limit. The default is 30 and the minimum is 1. Transcripts from sessions last used in Claude Desktop or Cowork follow a separate rule and are kept at any age by default.

Moving it: set CLAUDE_CONFIG_DIR and all of this moves with it.

Codex is less documented here. OpenAI's config reference covers settings, but community write-ups are the source for the layout: ~/.codex/sessions/YYYY/MM/DD/rollout--.jsonl. Documentary filmmaker Barry Avrich has turned his gaze on famed New York and Los Angeles art dealer Larry Gagosian for his next project.

But while some headlines have painted the unauthorized documentary, entitled Shadow Man: Inside the Secret World of Larry Gagosian, as a “cancellation” of Gagosian, Avrich said he aims to be balanced as he uncovers how Gagosian became arguably the most important art dealer for the past several decades. The 81-year-old owns several galleries across New York City and in Beverly Hills, London, Paris and more, and represents hundreds of the most-coveted artists, making him the go-to dealer for celebrities and Hollywood executives.

“Is it an exposé? Yes. Are there things in there, or comments and opinions that he won’t like? Sure. But it will be a very balanced biography of this person,” Avrich told The Hollywood Reporter. “There’ll be stuff he likes. There’ll be stuff that he detests. But that’s a documentary. I’m not doing a Michael Moore piece here.”

This will be Avrich’s third film about the art world after Made You Look, about an art forgery case and a Netflix film, Blurred Lines, which looked at the commercial forces in the art world, in addition to docs about producer Lew Wasserman and Vanity Fair columnist Dominick Dunne. Throughout the process of filming both art films, he was inspired to create this documentary.

“Larry’s name just kept coming up because you just can’t ignore it. When someone owns a lane, or invents a better mousetrap, whether it’s a Jeff Bezos or a Steve Jobs or an Elon Musk with Tesla, you just can’t ignore the sheer power and domination of an industry,” Avrich said. “And I’m fascinated by him. I’m fascinated that as the world changes constantly, that art continues to be this extraordinarily coveted asset for many different reasons, and Larry Gagosian has seemed to be somewhat Teflon and maintained this amazing control of the industry.”

While he again emphasizes that it is not meant to be a hit piece, Avrich said the documentary will examine how he’s been able to get a corner on the comparing it to “opening up a car dealership and having all of the greatest brands under one roof, but preventing those brands from being available to any other dealership. “

So far, Avrich said he has been fielding many emails and phone calls from people who want to participate in the documentary, and plans to include collectors, artists and former staff that have worked with him.

Avrich said he’s not looking for approval from Gagosian, who he has yet to speak with, but would welcome him to participate in the documentary.

A press representative for Gagosian did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Avrich and his team have completed their research and will soon be moving from pre-production into production, with plans to start filming in at the beginning of 2027, he said.
