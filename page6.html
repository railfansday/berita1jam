Dems' California redistricting mastermind flips script after helping party win 5 GOP seats: 'Kill'

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Outgoing Rep. Darrell Issa, R-Calif., says the GOP House delegation from his state is dropping to four or fewer seats out of 52 — less than 10% — despite 40-45% of the state’s voters being Republican, calling it pure gerrymandering.

The architect of California's Democratic-friendly redistricting plan, which helped the party flip five Republican-held House seats in November's midterm elections, is now calling on Democrats to put an end to partisan gerrymandering nationwide.

Paul Mitchell claimed in a San Francisco Chronicle op-ed published Wednesday that lawmakers should "kill partisan gerrymandering for good." His comments come after California voters approved Proposition 50 in November 2025, temporarily replacing the state's independently drawn congressional districts with a Democratic-friendly map for elections through 2030.

Mitchell acknowledged in his op-ed that Proposition 50 was designed to benefit Democrats, but defended it as a temporary response to Republican-led redistricting efforts before calling for an end to partisan mapmaking altogether.

"I wasn’t trying to do this [op-ed] to time with anyone’s efforts to do further gerrymandering," Mitchell told Fox News Digital on Thursday evening. "I always planned on doing this as soon as the election was over, and I even did a CNN interview with Elex Michaelson during the election coverage where I said that the answer needs to be to stop this redistricting war and actually fix the problem."

Mitchell argued that Republicans' midterm losses could make them more receptive to redistricting reform after years of opposing it.

"What makes now really important is that Republicans, who have opposed redistricting reform, should now realize that they lost in this war, so getting out of future wars is something maybe they would take more seriously now," he told Fox News Digital.

As for California, Mitchell believes the Proposition 50 maps will remain in place through 2030 and said the focus should be on restoring a fairer redistricting process in 2031.

He called for Congress to prohibit mid-decade redistricting but argued that such a restriction alone would not prevent partisan gerrymandering when states redraw their maps after each census.

"I think the best solution would be independent commissions in every state," he said.

The California Democratic Party touted Prop 50 as a "direct response to a Republican power grab orchestrated by President [Donald] Trump and state leaders in Texas, who redrew congressional district lines to gain five more seats in the U.S. House of Representatives."

Democratic California Gov. Gavin Newsom similarly claimed in a Nov. 4, 2025 social media post that Prop 50 "is about standing up to bullies like Donald Trump."

Mitchell's recent op-ed also claims that gerrymandering can erode voters’ ability "to elect the candidates they prefer."

A Menorca Farmhouse Finds Its Easy Elegance

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Isabel López-Quesada honored the historical essence of a 19th-century Menorcan retreat while introducing vibrant colors and an elevated ease.

Above: In the living room, López-Quesada replicated the original ceiling beams and finished the walls in traditional Menorcan lime plaster. A sofa of her design is grouped with a 1960s Tito Agnoli rattan daybed, a Mexican palm leaf stool, and a custom cocktail table upholstered with a Moroccan rug.

For Spanish designer Isabel López-Quesada, summers tend to revolve around Biarritz, in southwestern France, where she has a home. Still, she always carves out time for Menorca, regarded as the most laid-back of Spain’s Balearic Islands. That vibe suits the ELLE Decor A-list designer, whose signature style is luxurious but unfussy, harmonizing old and new with breezy refinement.

Typically, López-Quesada goes there to relax and spend time with friends, but around five years ago she received a call from a Bilbao family who asked her to refresh their vacation home outside Mahón, Menorca’s capital. An old farm property, or finca, the five-bedroom residence required extensive updates. López-Quesada was steadfast in preserving such historical elements as a well and donkey stables, which she transformed into an indoor-outdoor bathroom with a patio and marble tub. “It was important to respect the style of Menorca,” she says. “We were very careful with the materials, so it all goes together and conveys the sentiment I want the house to have.”

López-Quesada founded her namesake studio as a precocious 20-year-old Madrileña 40 years ago. Initially inspired by her mother’s yen for decorating and the work of celebrated Spanish designers such as Paco Muñoz and Fernando Benjumea, López-Quesada garnered wide attention in the early 2000s, when she converted an old Madrid wax factory into an atelier and a luxurious home for her family, filled with antiques and contemporary pieces. Soon she had interest from clients all over the world. Today López-Quesada’s 12-person team includes one of her three adult children, daughter Isabel Llanza. Five years ago the pair co-founded the lifestyle brand Isita Home, selling products they make themselves or discover on their travels.

The Menorca project began by gutting the house’s interior and rebuilding it “in an old style,” López-Quesada says. Central to it all was adding custom windows and doors to each room so the island’s golden light could flood the spaces. Vaulted and beamed ceilings were left intact or were recreated. Reconfiguring the floor plan, López-Quesada eschewed a proper dining room in favor of an eat-in open kitchen. “Most of the time you are eating outside,” she says.

With Menorca’s unrelenting summer sun, bougainvillea-draped pergolas were a must for leisurely alfresco lunches and dinners. Swaths of white lantana and lily of the Nile border the terraces, while “the pool reflects the sky like a mirror and provides a refreshing feel,” López-Quesada says.

Her collaborator on the gardens was Spanish landscape architect Luis González-Camino. “My vision was to design a water-efficient Mediterranean garden, divided into distinct yet connected ‘garden rooms’ for different seasons and times of day,” he says. González-Camino preserved the property as a natural Mediterranean woodland, with wild olive trees providing cover.

Indoors, López-Quesada installed French terra-cotta floor tiles chosen for their aged look. The three fireplaces were all handcrafted in traditional Menorcan style—a little imperfect, painted white—and decorated with vintage tiles the designer keeps in her Madrid warehouse.

She offset the white ceilings and lime plaster walls with vibrant textiles and tilework in blues and greens inspired by Menorca’s native palette. An upstairs bedroom is anchored by an iron four-poster bed with a blue-and-white-striped canopy; its bath has a shower lined with lapis-colored zellige tiles. Doors, which across Menorca are customarily painted in bold shades, here are finished in verdant hues.

Custom cabinets are designed with whimsically patterned cutouts for airflow, since humidity is a major factor. “On the islands, if you close the cupboards completely, mushrooms will grow inside,” López-Quesada says.

Step Inside This Renovated Menorca Retreat

She arrayed the spaces with a lively mix of vintage and new furnishings, emphasizing organic materials: rattan chairs, bamboo shades, Moroccan rugs, lots of linen and cotton fabrics in spirited florals or stripes. “If I use a heavy piece, then the rest is lighter,” she says. The tableware maintains the blue and green theme, including pieces from Isita Home and hand-blown glassware from neighboring Mallorca.

In the end, the owners asked López-Quesada to redo their home in Spain’s Basque region as well. It’s one of several projects she currently has across the country, including reimagining a hotel in Barcelona, and she’s also designing homes in Paris, Shanghai, and the Dominican Republic. Many of her projects are with repeat clients. “That’s how it works,” the designer says. “It’s like a chain.”

Warren Buffett Issued His Starkest Warning About the Stock Market in Years. Here's What History Says Comes Next.

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

More volatility could be coming, but history still has promising news for investors.

There's been no shortage of market volatility over the last week. After experiencing their worst week of 2026 in early November, the S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.40%), Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -0.91%), and Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI +0.10%) are rallying once again.

However, that doesn't mean that investors are in the clear. The tech industry is still struggling with volatility -- especially after Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's recent ousting -- and the market is the priciest it's been in decades.

Warren Buffett has also issued a direct warning to investors who may be underestimating market risk right now, and history has both good and bad news about what's coming next.

Warren Buffett. Image source: The Motley Fool.

During Berkshire Hathaway's 2026 meeting, Buffett discussed his thoughts on the current market in an interview with CNBC. He warned that in this historically expensive market, some investors are taking on too much risk.

"I've compared the markets to a church with a casino attached," he noted, with the church representing fundamentals-based long-term investing and the casino depicting short-term buying.

[T]he casino has gotten very attractive to people," Buffett warned, adding, "that's not investing, it's not speculating, it's gambling." He emphasized, however, that "that doesn't mean that investing is terrible. It does mean that prices for an awful lot of things will look very silly."

The S&P 500 has notched more than two dozen record highs so far in 2026, but the downside to all of that growth is that some stocks are at risk of becoming overvalued.

The S&P 500 Shiller CAPE Ratio measures the S&P 500's valuation over time based on its 10-year inflation-adjusted earnings. The higher the metric climbs, the more likely the index is overvalued and due for a pullback.

While no market indicator can predict when recessions or bear markets will begin, this particular metric has a long track record of preceding downturns. In 1929, for example, it spiked just before the Great Depression began. It surged again in 1999, during the dot-com bubble. Most recently, it peaked in late 2021, just months before stocks sank into a bear market.

In 2026, this metric reached its highest level since the dot-com bubble burst -- surpassing 40 for only the second time in stock market history. While this doesn't necessarily mean a crash is imminent, it does mean the market is incredibly richly valued right now.

The market may be pricey, but that doesn't mean it's a bad time to buy. It's crucial, however, to maintain a long-term outlook.

As Buffett mentioned, not all investing is bad. While some stocks may be overvalued right now, there are still plenty of fairly priced and even undervalued stocks with plenty of room for growth.

History also proves time and again that quality stocks will thrive over time. Since 2000, for example, the S&P 500 has faced the dot-com bubble, the Great Recession, the COVID-19 crash, the 2022 bear market, and countless smaller corrections along the way. It's also up by nearly 750% in that time.

It's impossible to say where the market is headed in the near term. But if history has just one lesson for investors, it's that healthy stocks and a long-term outlook are the keys to surviving whatever volatility may be looming.

Harvey Smith remembers the 10 months he spent as quality assurance lead on System Shock as a "brush with greatness".

"I had loved Ultima Underworld, and then I got to work with the very people that were rolling off of Underworld onto this new cyberpunk game," he says. "Looking Glass fought like cats and dogs, but man, they were passionate about what they were doing. And this was a mission for them."

Afterwards, when Smith joined System Shock producer Warren Spector at Ion Storm Austin, "we just applied that same hunger to Deus Ex," he says. "And then when I got together with Raphaël Colantonio, we were like, 'Why just live a normal life? Why fucking punch in and sit at a desk and run some numbers? We're just dying to do this, right?' And so we made Dishonored."

It's a simplified take on the story—one that skips the wilderness years spent fighting for funding before Arkane partnered with Bethesda. But the stubborn and single-minded outlook Smith describes is crucial to explaining his career in immersive sims. If he had been more sensible—less determined to funnel his days into spiritually satisfying work in the genre that first lit his creative fire—Arkane would never have made Dishonored at all.

"When Arkane was independent, very often people would come in and they would try to get us to work on some game that was not in our wheelhouse," he says. "People would say, 'You're going to go under by turning your nose up at doing this DLC for this third-person action shooter, this is money.' And we'd be like, 'Yeah, but we don't like those. We don't know how to do them well, and we think that game is dumb. So we're generally going to say no, and gamble the company on doing what we are known for.'"

Arkane very nearly did go under, pursuing its dogged goal of making immersive sims. But eventually, that doggedness was recognised by its peers in the games industry.

There are many ways to fail, many ways to succeed. But specialising in what you love is not a bad path.

"There was a moment we were pretty desperate, and a bunch of people working on BioShock 2 said, 'God, we're in a tight spot. We need somebody to take on a bunch of levels. You know who's out there? Arkane.' So we took Christophe Carrier, our level design director, who is really the wellspring from which a lot of that thinking at Arkane came from. By saying "no" enough times, Arkane defined itself clearly in the eyes of its fellows—as a team of consummate worldbuilders and determined architects of intricate first-person levels that rewarded deep exploration. In time, players came to recognise the studio for the same thing, and loved Arkane for it. "In a sense the purity was more valuable than the jack-of-all-trades," Smith says. "There are many ways to fail, many ways to succeed. But specialising in what you love is not a bad path."

Things only really went wrong a decade after Dishonored, with Redfall—the FPS that struggled to incorporate imsim values into co-op looter-shooting. "That was a game that did not come together," Smith says. "And we never should have been working on a games-as-a-service game, to be honest."

With its sleepy Massachusetts backdrop, leftfield movement abilities and a grounded theme of community resistance in the face of corporate greed, Redfall had some memorable and redeeming features. But it was a disappointment, and became a lightning rod for criticism of Xbox, who by that time was Arkane's owner.

"There were many things that seemed to converge all at once when Redfall was releasing," says Ben Horne, the production director of Redfall who co-founded Black Pony Immersive along with Smith. "I believe it was the first $70 price point game that Xbox had put out at the time. It was, I think, subject to some of the console wars."

To make matters worse, Arkane had developed a performance mode for Redfall on consoles, but held it back after noticing dips in its framerate. "That turned into a huge thing where people came and said, 'Oh, how come this isn't 60 fps at launch?,'" Horne says. "And so there were all of those things that were happening that we were trying to manage, but I think the bigger issue there is that we were working on something that was just outside of our expertise. Getting back to what we do best was the number one priority. For Harvey and myself, for the folks at Arkane and now at Black Pony, this is what our focus is."

Sometimes it blows up in our face, and sometimes it results in Deus Ex or Dishonored.

Smith and Horne co-founded Black Pony Immersive in the wake of Arkane Austin's closure, with the explicit goal of making immersive sims. Before Redfall, Horne had worked for years on annual NBA games in California. "You know occasionally you meet somebody who's on fire, they're very talented, and you can tell that they're hungry for something that is out there?," Smith says. "I've been in games 33 years now, and I would never want to weaponise this, but I can always tell when somebody needs something more substantial or more spiritual in their lives professionally. And that is, honestly, what we chase all the time. Sometimes it blows up in our face, and sometimes it results in Deus Ex or Dishonored."

Horne, for his part, still remembers his first day at Arkane. "Production director Susan Kath came up to me and was just like, 'Hey, why don't you play this build? We're about to release this DLC'. I think I left the office at midnight. I could not pull away from it."

The creative director of that DLC, Prey: Mooncrash, was Ricardo Bare, who also works at Black Pony today. "Ricardo is a deep thinker about the cosmos and what sentience, awareness and consciousness are," Smith says. "If you play Prey, you get to hear people asking questions about why some creatures are capable of empathy and some are not. Ricardo likes to think about those things, so while he's also a game designer and a writer and thinks about literary themes, he comes in thinking about that metaphysical layer as well."

The games Smith directs, meanwhile, have consistently explored systems of power and exploitation. "Dishonored is underpinned by that theme," he says. "The Outsider overtly and indirectly talks about themes related to the abuse of power. And you know, Deus Ex was a political awakening for me. I didn't know that much about politics, embarrassingly enough, when I was in my 20s, and I started working with Warren Spector."

Smith and Spector collaborated for nine years across two Deus Ex games. "Warren was well-versed in the way politics and power work across the world," Smith says. "The personal is the political because someone has power over your life."

Somewhere in the mix of these themes, perhaps, is the key to understanding the perspective of Black Pony's unannounced debut game. And mechanically speaking, we already know exactly what to expect. "First-person games that are a hybrid of shooter, stealth and RPG, where the player can be creative and explore," Smith says. "It's very player-paced, it's singleplayer, it's narrative-driven. There's a lot of atmospherics. That's what we burn to do."

During the years that Smith and Colantonio co-directed Dishonored, they shared an office and spent lots of time travelling together—discussing the precepts of the genre they'd devoted their professional lives to. "We realised that when it comes to immersive sims, some people are very dogmatic," Smith says. "They want to carry forward the sacred writings. You can't deviate from that, or you're a heretic. And other people are like, 'Yeah, sure, this is a cool base, but where can we go with it? What can we do that's new?' And that is always the challenge. That's the two wolves inside me. On one hand, I want to pay off those expectations. On the other hand, I want to exceed them."

Smith is a big believer in systems that flow from a game's setting. In his experience, even familiar gameplay ideas can be revitalised by new fiction. One example is Dishonored's possession power, which began as Arkane's riff on disguises in Hitman.

"I need to put on the disguise so the guard won't kill me and I can walk past the other guards," Smith says. "But then we were like, 'Well, if I'm in his body, then I can walk through the Wall of Light because it's attuned to the guards. Or if I possess a rat, I can get into the rat tunnels and come into the building. And if I walk past the guard, the guard literally tries to stomp me because I'm a rat.'"

Let's hope Black Pony's not the one trick pony, right?

Suddenly, level designers were creating routes that allowed players to possess a fish in a fountain, follow the pipes up the u-bend, and emerge in the toilet of an assassination target. "We got a lot of love for that," Smith says. "But it was magical to us too."

He hopes the same approach will see Black Pony Immersive join Arkane, Ion Storm and Looking Glass in the lineage of great immersive sim studios. "Even if we're doing the same type of game we've done for a long time, we can find a way to take a thing we've done before and recontextualise it because of the new subject matter," he says. "Let's hope Black Pony's not the one trick pony, right?"

Mads Mikkelsen Delivered His Best Performance In This Unforgettable 2012 Movie

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Few European actors have found the blend of native and international success that Mads Mikkelsen has. Born in Denmark, he became a star in his home country during the 1990s and 2000s, his career kicking off with Nicolas Winding Refn's 1996 crime thriller "Pusher." He's since become a favorite of Hollywood productions, too, starting with his role as the Bond villain Le Chiffre in "Casino Royale."

Since then, Mikkelsen has remained a go-to Hollywood bad guy, appearing in blockbusters like Marvel Studios' "Doctor Strange." He's also one of the privileged few to have played arguably the most famous screen villain ever, Hannibal Lecter. More than a few would argue Mikkelsen's Lecter in the 2013 TV series "Hannibal" is even better than Anthony Hopkins in "The Silence of the Lambs."

Mikkelsen's razor-sharp cheekbones, intense stare, and European accent instantly code him as "villain" in Hollywood language. When "Rogue One: A Star Wars Story" cast him as Galen Erso, a sympathetic scientist conscripted into building the Death Star, it read as casting against type to American audiences. But look at Mikkelsen's Danish resume and you'll find he's a much more diverse actor than the box that Hollywood has stuck him inside. Amusingly, one of those movies — Thomas Vinterberg's "The Hunt" — stars Mikkelsen as a good man mistakenly persecuted as a villain.

Lucas (Mikkelsen) is a Danish kindergarten teacher. One of his students, his best friend Theo's (Thomas Bo Larsen) daughter Klara (Annika Wedderkopp), has a puppy love crush on him. Obviously, he gently turns her down. Klara then vengefully suggests to the school principal that Lucas exposed himself to or molested her. (In reality, as the movie shows, her crude older brother shoved an iPad displaying porn in her face.) The ugly rumor grows out of control, and Lucas' reputation pays the price.

In "The Hunt," the town rushes to the worst possible conclusion about Lucas. All but a few loved ones like his son, Marcus (Lasse Fogelstrøm), believe the worst from hearsay. But they don't actually know it for a fact (indeed, what ultimately helps clear Lucas is the facts pointing in his favor). The movie is never ambiguous, though; we know from the very beginning that Lucas is innocent and this is all just a terrible tragedy of miscommunication. "The Hunt" also avoids reactionary narratives about scorned women peddling false rape allegations because Klara is a young child. She simply doesn't grasp the consequences of what she did, as is the case with the other preschoolers who echo her claims.

Compare a similar recent film, "Fjord," inspired by a real-life story about a Norwegian evangelical couple whose children were taken from them after suspicions of abuse. Like in "The Hunt," the allegations are implied to stem from leading questions and miscommunication from the kids. But "Fjord" counterintuitively stacks the deck in the parents' favor while trying to present objectivity, perhaps because it knows many left-leaning audiences are inclined to side against the conservative couple and takes perverse enjoyment in testing those sympathies.

A film like "The Hunt" could choose not to reveal Lucas' innocence either way or cast doubt, inviting you to sympathize with a possible monster as an endurance test. But the reality of "The Hunt," seeing an innocent man be branded with a scarlet letter, is also agonizing to watch in its own way. It's a modern iteration on the parable of the feathers in the wind, and how once spoken, even untruthful gossip can't be erased.

As Lucas, Mikkelsen sheds any of the power or menace he projects as a villain in those aforementioned Hollywood movies. "The Hunt" is also a Danish-language film, and Mikkelsen's comfort with his native tongue also makes for an even more seamless performance.

To this day, I've never forgotten the catharsis of a scene where Lucas stands up for himself. After being assaulted in a grocery store, he walks in again, headbutts the hostile clerk, and demands his groceries back. The first time I watched "The Hunt," I rewound the movie to see the scene a second time.

That relatively late-in-the-movie scene is a well-earned respite from Lucas' victimhood. Even in a story like this, too much passivity from a protagonist turns into a chore. Lucas similarly takes matters into his own hands at the movie's emotional climax. Again, Klara's father Theo is Lucas' best friend; while Theo takes Klara's side, he claims to know Lucas so well he can tell when he's lying or truthful by just looking into his eyes. Lucas goes to Christmas Mass, putting himself dead center in the community that has exiled him, and looks at Theo across the pews.

The power of this lynchpin scene is in Mikkelsen's silent, icy stare (Lucas pointedly ditches his glasses during this scene). But it's not the aloof stare of Le Chiffre or the predatory one of Hannibal Lecter. In his sad eyes and slight frown, Lucas is begging Theo to read the truth on his face. When that's not enough, he storms over to Theo, sobbing, and refuses to let him look away from his eyes.

Mads Mikkelsen is an extraordinary actor, and yet the greatest proof of that is him playing a normal man in "The Hunt."

The weird reasons why your ethernet cable might be slower than your Wi-Fi

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It could be hardware, software or something else entirely.

Ethernet cables, otherwise called network cables, are supposed to be two or three times faster than Wi-Fi. That's one of the primary benefits of going with a wired connection. However, that's not always true in a real world setting. Many users have noticed that ethernet cables can actually be slower than Wi-Fi.

How is that even possible? In rare cases, it's simply a matter of a fast wireless connection that outpaces Ethernet speeds. Wireless routers have come a long way in the past decade, particularly with the introduction of protocols like Wi-Fi 7.

It's not always greyhound-like wireless speeds. There are a host of reasons why an ethernet connection could be slower than its wireless counterpart. Some of this is hardware based, as in the wire itself, and some is connected to software issues. Let's get into the how and why of it all so you can continue browsing and streaming without any speed hiccups.

Ethernet cables have been around for decades. The technology keeps progressing, so not all cables are equal. Using old and out-of-date cables will absolutely cap the speed of any wired connection. A good rule of thumb? Make sure to get at least a Cat5 cable or better. If you don't know what kind of cables you're using, look along the outer plastic sheath. There should be some small text printed that explicitly lists the category.

Category isn't the only thing that matters. The quality is just as important, as some manufacturers cut corners. I'm not recommending splurging on the most expensive cables around, but there are some things to look out for. As previously mentioned, take a look at the category rating. Also, avoid copper-clad aluminum (CCA) cables. Go for pure copper cables instead. CCA cables tend to degrade quickly and break easily. You should also check for the certification, as reputable brands test and certify their cables to meet TIE/EIA standards. This should also be written somewhere on the sheath.

A lot of things can go wrong with ethernet cables, particularly if they're on the older side. For instance, these cables typically include four twisted pairs of wires. If one of the pairs is incorrectly twisted, it'll impact the overall speed. The same goes if the sheath is damaged, as this could eventually lead to damaged wires. A good troubleshooting step here is to simply try out different ethernet cables and conduct network speed tests.

The port itself can cause slow speeds, even if the ethernet cables are in tip-top shape. Any type of damage to the internal contacts could limit speed or render the port unusable. This can be tough to suss out, as swapping out ethernet ports isn't exactly feasible. Check the connection port on the router and look for a solid or blinking green light. If there are no lights at all, that likely indicates an issue with either the port or the cable. Swapping out the cables will narrow it down. If the router and computer have multiple ports, try using one of the available ones and run a speed test. A final option? Plug the cable into another device and check things out.

The speed could be impacted if you're using ancient drivers for the router, ethernet adapter or network card. This is really easy to miss, as updating random drivers is likely not on your list of most important tasks. Start with the router and move on from there, testing the speed at every step. This is especially important if you recently updated the operating system of the source device.

There are several settings that can impact ethernet speeds. Windows users can check out the aggregated link speed in the Network and Internet settings tab, and Apple users can check the Network page. Cross-reference this speed against what you're paying an ISP for. If you're paying for 1000Mbps and the link speed shows just 100Mbps, you know there's a problem somewhere.

There's some stuff to try, beyond checking the settings. Both Mac and Windows computers use something called Energy-Efficient Ethernet by default. Turn this off and see if it improves the speed. Also, Windows computers turn off the network adapter to save power on occasion. This can be changed in the Power Management tab.

Malware can slow just about everything on a computer down, and that includes wired internet. Spyware, trojans and worms tend to hog up the bandwidth with hidden background tasks, thus limiting ethernet speeds. Malware can also force a computer to mine cryptocurrency and some software infects the actual router, degrading traffic flow.

It can even impact routers. Perhaps the most famous incident of malware infecting actual routers, as opposed to computers, was something called VPNFilter. This malicious code got into more than 500,000 routers and network-attached storage (NAS) devices across the globe back in 2018. VPNFilter was really nasty and could steal data and brick the router.

Run a scan on everything with a reputable security scanner or built-in antivirus software. If the scan comes back positive, follow the next steps to eliminate any unwanted code floating around. You should make a habit out of running malware and virus scans at regular intervals, as the technology continues to advance.

It's possible that your slow ethernet speed is by design and has nothing to do with damaged cords or an abundance of malware. Check the fine print from your ISP to make sure there isn't a speed or service cap. These companies can be deceptive, particularly when running promotions. They like to offer higher speeds that degrade in a year or to to lure in new customers.

Run a speed test on the network when its plugged in via ethernet cable and when it's connected wirelessly, which should provide two data points. Give your ISP a call and ask what the deal is. They don't like to lose customers, so it's possible they could set things right (or at least find you a discount.)

However, a service cap is likely not the culprit if your Wi-Fi is chugging along at high speeds. If the wireless speeds are tip-top while the ethernet struggles, it likely indicates one of the aforementioned issues is at play.
