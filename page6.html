Coca-Cola CFO John Murphy to retire. Here are his 3 pieces of career advice after nearly 40 years at the company

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Good morning. CFO turnover continues across the Fortune 500, and one of the latest changes is at The Coca-Cola Company, where longtime finance chief John Murphy is preparing to retire after nearly four decades at the beverage giant.

Murphy, who has served as CFO since 2019 and president since 2022, will step down from his CFO role on April 2. He will remain president until his retirement later that year, although a specific date has not yet been announced.

To succeed Murphy, Coca-Cola has tapped another Fortune 500 finance chief: David Elkins, who has served as executive vice president and CFO of Bristol Myers Squibb since 2019. His previous roles include positions at Johnson & Johnson and AstraZeneca. At Coca-Cola, Elkins will oversee finance, global strategy and enterprise services.

Elkins has a “track record of success across complex, global companies,” Coca-Cola CEO Henrique Braun said in the company’s announcement Monday. Braun also highlighted Elkins’ ability to develop future leaders.

Murphy, meanwhile, has been a transformational leader during his 38 years at Coca-Cola, Braun said.

Although Coca-Cola selected an external candidate for its CFO role, Braun is a product of the company’s own leadership pipeline. He became CEO in March after spending 30 years at Coca-Cola, reflecting the company’s history of developing leaders from within.

Murphy, 64, joined the company in 1988 and held a variety of general management, finance and strategic planning positions around the world. His roles included vice president of business systems for Coca-Cola North America, executive vice president and CFO of Coca-Cola Japan, and vice president of strategic planning for the company.

I interviewed Murphy in September to discuss Coca-Cola’s planned $10 billion infrastructure investment from 2026 through 2030, which will span multiple projects across its system. Coca-Cola contributed $85 billion to U.S. GDP in 2025, or roughly $10 million every hour, according to an independent study commissioned by the company.

After our conversation about the investment, I asked Murphy what career advice he would share with people early in their careers, drawing from his own experience at Coca-Cola.

“The first thing I say to younger folks is to have the courage to get uncomfortable. I think you grow and learn more in positions where you are uncomfortable than when it’s easy, and sometimes it’s a tough decision for younger folks to make. Do that a few times, and you then start to differentiate yourself from those around you.”

“The second thing I would encourage people to do is focus on today—and tomorrow will take care of itself. In other words, do the very best that you can with what’s on your agenda for today, and don’t worry too much about what comes tomorrow or next week. And then, in a strange way, the better you do today, the better tomorrow and next week and next month will be.”

“The last thing I say to people is, from early on, build your network. You can never have too many people in your network. And the last time I checked—I’ve never yet been punished for being nice to people.”

As Murphy prepares to close a nearly four-decade career at Coca-Cola, his advice is a reminder that career growth isn’t necessarily about having a perfect plan. It’s about being willing to take on challenges, doing today’s work well and building relationships along the way.

AMD Stock Would Need to Rise About 57% to Get Back to Its Record. History Says How Long Past Comebacks Took.

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The chipmaker has come back from drops this big six times -- at very different paces.

By Daniel Sparks – Nov 25, 2026 at 8:37AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Advanced Micro Devices ›

Advanced Micro Devices (AMD -8.86%) stock would need to rise about 57% just to get back to its record. As of this writing, shares trade near $413, off about 36% from their Oct. 6 closing high of $649.42.

The fall came fast. Shares first closed more than 35% under the record on Nov. 5, just 30 days after the peak. They sank 17% that day alone.

A bounce to around $585 a week later didn't last. The stock fell again after a short-seller report on Nov. 19, and a broad sell-off in chip stocks on Tuesday pushed it back near its Nov. 5 close.

AMD shareholders have been here before. In daily price data since 1980, the chipmaker's stock has fallen 35% or more from a record six other times, though never this fast.

It eventually recovered every time. But the waits ran from around a year and a half to almost 20 years, and none of those drops ended at the 35% level.

Measured from each record close to the next one, the last comeback was the fastest. After peaking in March 2024, AMD shares hit a new record in October 2025, around 1.6 years later. The drops that started in 1980 and November 2021 both took around 2.1 years to erase, and the one that began in 1997 took about 3 years.

The other two took much longer. After peaking in August 1984, the shares didn't hit a new record until March 1997 -- a wait of around 12.5 years. And after its June 2000 peak, AMD shares needed over 19 years, finally passing that high in January 2020.

AMD's record here is mixed. Four of the six drops were made up within three years, but two took over a decade.

Buying when AMD shares first fell 35% under a record never meant buying near the bottom. From the day each of the six drops crossed that line, the stock fell another 41% to 95% before it bottomed.

Measured from the highs, the lows ranged from 63% under the record (in April 2025) to almost 97% below (in July 2015). The 1984 drop didn't bottom until 1990, around 91% below its peak.

The first year was usually tough, too. A year after each crossing, AMD stock was down four times out of six, with losses of 27% to 45%.

And a rise in that first year didn't mean a fast comeback. The stock was up 30% a year after its August 2024 crossing and hit a new record around two months later. But it was also up 16% a year after its 1984 crossing, early in what became the 12.5-year wait.

This time, AMD's first close under the 35% line was at $404.40. Another 41% drop from there would put the stock around $240. That isn't a forecast, but it's what the smallest of AMD's six past drops would look like from here.

The business, meanwhile, is still growing fast. AMD's revenue for the third quarter of 2026 climbed 45% year over year to a record $13.4 billion, up from $11.5 billion in the second quarter. Management expects roughly $17 billion in fourth-quarter revenue, implying growth of about 66% year over year. This forecast also implies sequential growth of about 27%, accelerating from 16% in Q3.

Data center segment sales, which include the company's AI chips and server processors, almost doubled from the year-ago quarter, hitting $8.5 billion.

The Nov. 19 short-seller report was from Hunterbrook, which disclosed a short position in AMD (a bet that the stock drops). It alleged that a blacklisted Chinese server maker kept getting newer AMD firmware, and Hunterbrook also argued that new U.S. export rules might tighten limits on AMD's foreign business. AMD said the claims misrepresent its business and that it follows U.S. export laws. None of the allegations has been proven.

Based on analysts' average estimate for 2027, the stock trades at a forward price-to-earnings ratio of around 23. For a company expecting growth this fast, that looks like a reasonable price to me.

Overall, I think AMD's business gives investors good reason to own this growth stock. But the stock's own past is a good reason to hold less of it than the business alone could justify.

I'd want a stake small enough that a drop to about $240, or a wait much longer than two years, wouldn't push me to sell.

Core inflation beat forecasts in October, reinforcing expectations of a December Fed rate hike

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The overall PCE price index rose 3.8% from a year earlier, matching forecasts, ahead of the Fed's Dec. 8-9 meeting

The Bureau of Economic Analysis reported on Wednesday that the core personal consumption expenditures price index, which excludes food and energy, rose 3.3% in October from a year earlier. That topped the Dow Jones consensus estimate by a tenth of a percentage point, according to CNBC.

The overall PCE price index rose 3.8% from a year earlier, up from 3.6% in September. That matched economists' expectations, according to CNBC.

From September, both the overall index and the core index rose 0.3%. Personal income rose 0.7% in October and consumer spending rose 0.3%. Real spending fell $5.8 billion, and the personal saving rate was 4.1%.

The Commerce Department also revised third-quarter GDP growth to a 4.3% annualized rate on Wednesday, according to CNBC.

The inflation data reinforced expectations of another rate hike in December, according to CNBC. The Federal Reserve meets Dec. 8-9. Market odds of a quarter-point increase at that meeting stand at about 97% on CME $CME Group's FedWatch Tool, according to ABC News. A December hike would be the Fed's second in four months.

Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan said earlier this month that the central bank needs at least two more quarter-point rate increases. She said core PCE inflation was 3.1% over the 12 months through September. The Fed held its target range at 3.75% to 4% at its October meeting.

Fed Governor Christopher Waller said in a speech before the October consumer price index release that the Fed should consider a half-point increase in December if core inflation printed at 0.3% or above. He said a quarter-point move in that case would put the Fed's credibility at risk. Core CPI rose 0.3% in October, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

US proposes new tariffs on 14 trading partners, including China and the EU

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The new tariffs would put a 20% rate on most Chinese goods, rising to 40% if the truce fails. They need a public hearing first and may not take effect until January.

President Donald Trump speaks to reporters before boarding Air Force One at Joint Base Andrews, Maryland, on 28 October 2026.

The US proposed new tariffs on 14 trading partners on Wednesday. China, the European Union, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan are on the list. The Trump administration says the duties target “structural excess capacity”, meaning factory output that subsidies keep high and that undercuts US manufacturers.

The US Trade Representative’s office announced the tariffs after an eight-month Section 301 investigation into 16 economies. David Lawder and Andrea Shalal reported the details for Reuters, citing a senior US official.

The other nine economies are Bangladesh, Cambodia, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Thailand and Vietnam. The US spared Mexico and Norway, the last two economies in the investigation, for now. USTR planned to publish the exact rates in the Federal Register on Wednesday.

China would face a 20% tariff on most of its imports, Courtenay Brown reported for Axios. That comes on top of the roughly 25% tariffs from Trump’s first term, according to Reuters. For some Chinese goods, the total rate would reach 45%, according to AP.

A senior US official told Axios the rate could rise to 40% if trade talks break down or China fails to meet its commitments. The 20% rate stays within the cap set by the trade truce with Beijing, which expires on 10 January, Reuters reported. About $30bn of Chinese imports under review for tariff cuts would be exempt.

“We know that they don’t like these actions, but we are seeking stability with the Chinese. China is seeking stability with us,” the senior US official said, according to Reuters.

The new tariffs will not surprise Beijing and are unlikely to undermine the truce, the official said, AP reported.

China’s foreign ministry rejected the idea of overcapacity but stopped short of threatening retaliation, Reuters reported.

“The so-called ‘overcapacity’ is a false issue, and China opposes using it as an excuse for political manipulation,” foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun said.

For the others, the proposals mostly target specific goods. They would raise existing tariffs by about 5 percentage points on average, the official told Axios. They would also stay within the caps in Trump’s trade deals: 15% for the EU, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, and 19% for Malaysia, according to Reuters.

Vietnam faces a rate of up to 35%, while others face 20% or 25%, the BBC reported. Caps in Trump’s trade deals will lower some of those rates, the BBC said.

The new duties will not stack on top of the 10% and 12.5% forced-labour tariffs the US put on 60 trading partners in July. Nor will they stack on the separate tariffs on cars, steel and aluminium, AP reported.

USTR’s notice pointed to unprofitable carmakers in China and Japan, according to Reuters’ live coverage. For the EU, it cited Germany’s and Ireland’s large goods surpluses with the US.

The duties are not final. They face a public comment period and a hearing, and may not take effect until January, the official told Reuters.

The US singled out Brazil after trade talks stalled, AP reported. It proposed higher tariffs on raw and refined sugar and a new 10% tariff on aircraft, which would hit Embraer, Brazil’s largest aircraft maker.

The plan replaces broader tariffs that the Supreme Court struck down in February. It also follows Republican losses in this month’s midterm elections. In a Reuters/Ipsos poll after the vote, 62% of respondents said they would support efforts to repeal Trump’s tariffs, Reuters reported.

Asian stocks fell sharply on Wednesday as reports of the plan added to worries about the tech sector, Semafor reported. MSCI’s global stock index fell 0.5%, according to Reuters.

The announcement follows Trump’s summit in Shenzhen with Xi Jinping last week. Trump plans to host Xi next month at the G20 summit in Florida, AP reported.

The new duties sit alongside the existing US semiconductor tariff. Earlier this month, Trump said he would tax US companies that build data centres abroad.

Stomach problems in autistic children raise new questions about gut bacteria

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A study found that autistic children with more severe digestive symptoms tended to have more difficulty picking up on social cues.

Children with autism often have stomach problems. What causes them, however, is still up for debate. Some scientists believe the lining of the gut becomes damaged or inflamed.

Little evidence for that explanation was found in a new study.

Autistic children with more severe digestive symptoms tended to have more difficulty picking up on social cues, according to parent questionnaires.

The researchers also found links between certain gut bacteria and digestive symptoms.

The study was led by J. Marc Rhoads. He is professor and chief of the Division of Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition at McGovern Medical School at UTHealth Houston.

Many children with autism are unable to explain where they feel pain. In such cases a change in behavior may be the first sign of a problem.

“Families may notice increased irritability, repetitive behaviors, or self-injurious behaviors when a child is experiencing gastrointestinal issues such as gas, constipation, or diarrhea,” Rhoads said.

“For children who are nonverbal, these behaviors may be one of the few ways they can communicate discomfort.”

About one in 31 American eight-year-olds had been identified with autism in 2022. This estimate was published by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Significant digestive problems have been reported in 31% to 84% of people with autism in earlier studies.

The researchers examined 72 children with autism who were between four and 16 years of age. All of the children had been enrolled in a probiotic trial. Their data were collected between 2016 and 2023.

Boys made up about 78% of the group and roughly a quarter of the children did not speak. Children with known digestive, liver or kidney diseases were excluded from the study. Those who had recently taken antibiotics or probiotics were also left out.

Several symptoms were assessed by the team. These included constipation, gas, stomach pain, unusually smelly stools and waking up at night.

A score of seven or more on a 17-point scale was taken to indicate significant digestive problems. About 63% of the children were placed in this group.

Parents were also asked to complete a questionnaire about the social behavior of their children. Higher scores on this questionnaire reflected more pronounced autism traits.

Higher scores were generally seen in children with worse stomach problems. The connection was not particularly strong but it reached statistical significance.

The clearest difference was observed in social awareness. This area covers abilities such as noticing the facial expressions, feelings or actions of another person.

Children with significant digestive problems scored about 7.5 points higher in this area. Their scores were compared with those of children who had milder symptoms.

The cells that line the intestines are packed closely together. The spaces between them are sealed by specialized proteins.

Substances from inside the gut may pass into the bloodstream if these seals are weakened. Inflammation could then be triggered in the body. This process is commonly described as a leaky gut.

Blood samples were tested for two proteins associated with these seals. Another blood protein linked to intestinal inflammation was measured as well. Stool samples were also analyzed for calprotectin, a protein routinely used by doctors to detect gut inflammation.

Most of the results fell within the normal range. Elevated calprotectin was detected in only two children and neither of them had significant digestive symptoms.

“Biomarkers of intestinal inflammation and gut permeability remained within normal ranges and were not associated with gastrointestinal symptom severity or autism symptom severity,” Rhoads said.

Children with worse stomach problems therefore did not show more signs of inflammation or a leaky gut on these tests.

Mixed results have been produced by earlier research. High calprotectin levels were found in up to a third of autistic children in some studies. No significant difference was found in a more recent review.

The bacterial findings proved to be more revealing than the blood tests.

Additional stool samples were obtained from Texas Children’s Microbiome Center. This allowed the analysis to be expanded to 135 children. The group included children with autism, their siblings and unrelated children without autism.

Fewer types of gut bacteria were present in children with autism than in their siblings. A similar difference was seen when they were compared with unrelated children. However, this second result may have arisen by chance.

The bacterial communities of children with autism also resembled one another more closely. Greater variety was observed among the other children.

Two groups of bacteria were more abundant in autistic children with significant digestive problems. One of these groups is known as Ruminococcus.

Children with higher levels of these bacteria reported more gas and stronger-smelling stools. Night waking was also more frequent in these children. Their overall digestive symptom scores were higher as well.

Ruminococcus feeds on the mucus that protects the intestinal wall. Higher levels of this bacterium in one-year-olds were linked to greater social difficulties at age three in an earlier study.

Another bacterial group called Sarcina was associated with gas, stomach pain, diarrhea and night waking. The researchers noted that Sarcina had not been linked to autism in previous studies.

The study captured a snapshot of digestive symptoms, gut bacteria and behavior.

The children were examined at a single point in time. It was therefore not possible to tell which change came first.

Gut bacteria may cause stomach problems, or digestive problems may alter the bacterial population.

Diet could also play a part in this relationship. Many children with autism prefer a limited range of foods. The bacteria living in the intestines are strongly influenced by what a person eats.

The study also had a few other limitations. Blood was not collected from healthy children for ethical reasons. Results were compared with established normal ranges instead.

Children with known digestive diseases were excluded by the team. Some of the analyses did not account for other factors that might have influenced the findings.

Children could be followed over several years in future studies. Such work may show whether changes in gut bacteria occur before digestive or behavioral changes.

Rhoads believes that everyday habits deserve more attention.

“This study highlights the importance of supporting a healthy gut microbiome in children with autism,” Rhoads said.

“A varied diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, along with healthy toileting habits, may help improve both gastrointestinal health and behavior.”

“Our team has also found promising results with probiotic treatment.”

The results of the probiotic trial have not yet been published. Scientists are still working to determine which probiotic supplements are helpful and under what conditions.

The most useful lesson for parents may concern the recognition of discomfort rather than bacteria.

A child may suddenly become irritable, sleep poorly or show more repetitive behaviors. Such changes may be caused by something as ordinary as constipation or stomach pain.

Many children are unable to put this discomfort into words. Behavior may be one of the ways in which they ask for help.

The study is published in the journal Pediatric Research.

AAA defines the 2026 Thanksgiving period as the seven days lasting from Tuesday, November 24 to Monday, November 30, 2026. But despite the expected 72.9 million road travelers, how congested the streets will be depends heavily on when you travel during the period.

“Grand Theft Auto VI” took less than 24 hours to generate $3.79 billion in global sales. That’s roughly 15 percent of the year’s worldwide box office — in under a day.

Still, that particular side of “GTA VI” discourse drives me up the wall. Games, movies, TV shows, and social media feeds do indeed battle for our attention, and the business of entertainment is ultimately all about influencing how strangers use their free time.

Rather, the issue lies in the assumption that one hour spent with one type of story is automatically equivalent to another hour spent with an entirely different medium — and that comparing those numbers tells us a thing about what either is really worth.

Last Thursday, I was at my favorite bar, catching up with a friend visiting from Seattle, when she got up to greet someone else. Scrolling on my phone, I opened a DM from my old coworker that said he was… already playing “GTA VI”? That couldn’t be!

Confused, I quickly searched “GTA 6 release date” and discovered I wasn’t just a few hours away from entering Vice City — but that I was already a full day behind the rest of the gaming world. Yes, Rockstar had spent more than a decade preparing its masterpiece, and I had somehow missed the actual drop like a tricky freeway exit.

I really should’ve known better, too. I’d preordered “GTA VI” and downloaded it on my PlayStation days earlier, but even $79.99 deep, I was caught off guard by the sudden arrival of Lucia and Jason, who, even welcome, had seriously complicated my night.

Never one to miss a groundbreaking moment in a fake place, I ran home to my apartment, determined to make up for lost time using whatever post-bar energy I had. (As it turns out, that wasn’t much.)

There’s something weirdly prudish about Hollywood’s anxiety over just how much time “GTA VI” fans might spend playing it — as though immersive and interactive narratives were an indulgence taking hours away from more legitimate entertainment. Games have been a major market and creative stage for decades, and still, the conversation around Rockstar’s new blockbuster brings to mind the sort of worried parents who would’ve been afraid of TV in the ’90s.

Those pearl-clutching concerns also miss a practical distinction. Playing a video game demands a different kind of physical and mental engagement than watching a movie or TV show. Admittedly, the first few minutes of “GTA VI” make that distinction remarkably easy to forget — opening on Lucia in prison for a sequence so beautifully lit and photographed that interacting with inmates feels at once totally real and like Rockstar showing off.

And yet, even in the “cinematic” wonder of that moment, rushing home left me in no state to steer a virtual boat. My first real “GTA VI” mission involved navigating choppy waters to pick up (and/or drop off, I honestly cannot remember) bricks of cocaine, but unfortunately, bro, I could not operate heavy machinery.

What felt like hours of circling checkpoints was probably closer to 15 minutes. Still, the technical wizardry bobbing across my screen clashed with my stomach contents, and I was desperate to stop. So, I turned off my PS5 and streamed “Knocked Up,” instead.

I woke up to the sound of Katherine Heigl crowning and went to bed at 1 a.m., truly grateful that Judd Apatow’s 2007 comedy had freed me from the obligation to captain anything. But that night, sober as a judge and eager to get behind the wheel again, I remembered what the decade-plus of “GTA VI” hype was all about.

My first in-game police pursuit was astounding in its intricacy, with Leonida (i.e., Rockstar’s Florida) providing a jaw-dropping menagerie of local characters, each living out a personal drama or comedy of errors against a backdrop of boundless scenery.

Weaving through traffic and cutting down alleys, I realized I wanted the full “Fast and Furious” feeling early and often. You know, tightly choreographed fever dreams complete with stunt drivers, cinematographers, and someone other than me to call cut. But here, all I had was myself: a shitty chauffeur, a worse debut director, and a leading man who kept mistakenly jumping onto the roof of her car before getting in.

To be fair, “GTA VI” offers a truly impressive range of camera options when you decide to properly educate yourself on the system’s customizable controls. But as a woman who couldn’t keep her wheels on the road, let alone capture Jason’s stubble from a flattering angle, I was practically begging for a union rep — and a body double. Worse still, I was getting more and more worried about my girl, Lucia.

Rockstar had sold me on Jason as a good boyfriend, and I wanted to prioritize that interpretation in my playthrough. But the longer I waited for Lucia to get out of prison, the more convinced I became that she might never be coming back. Had I missed a mission? Decapitated the wrong innocent bystander? Whatever had gone wrong in my game, uncertainty powered Jason’s decline.

I robbed a woman after she refused my offer to help with her broken-down car. I took surprising pleasure in dispatching adversarial gang members. After a seemingly infinite number of crashes, I cared less about playing the romantic lead than about combining my growing enthusiasm for scuba diving with organized crime.

In a movie, that sort of moral deterioration might suggest a carefully constructed character arc. In “GTA VI,” it mostly reflected my own impatience. Rockstar had given me the raw materials for a compelling love story, but the longer I waited for Lucia, the more willfully criminal my version of Jason became. By Friday night, I was having a good time — but I was also getting critically hardened, and increasingly convinced I’d done something wrong to derail the romance I craved.

Around 3 a.m. on Saturday, I was throwing up into my toilet with the word “WASTED” practically hovering above my head in enormous white letters. My plans to spend the weekend robbing old ladies and getting gradually pissier with the absent Lucia had been derailed by a profoundly gross bout of what seemed to be food poisoning.

Yeah, it was a real bummer, dog. But unable to muster the constitution for a car ride, scuba diving, or the next step in my budding drug empire, I settled for fixing up Jason’s rickety house and lifting weights on his back deck. Nauseous, I even briefly considered finishing Lucia’s bedroom, but gave up after getting stuck picking just one color from a trio of offscreen paint samples. (I didn’t know her well enough to know which one she’d like, and fighting a fever, that felt like an argument in the making.)

There was something genuinely lovely about occupying Rockstar’s beautifully realized environments without any particular objective in mind. Even under the weather, I understood why I’d seen so many people on social media comparing “GTA VI” to “slow cinema,” a style of filmmaking that finds meaning in naturalistic observation and drawn-out routines.

Its more intimate moments sometimes reminded me of Sean Baker’s “The Florida Project,” too, with its colorful vision of the Sunshine State smashing together the faded appeal of amusement parks and painful hallmarks of everyday desperation.

That’s a lot to contend with when you’re sick, though, so I turned on “The Hunting Wives” (another gloriously lurid piece of modern American satire!) and, while scrolling through social media, I was struck by a contrast.

Eight episodes of “The Hunting Wives” could simply wash over me. To get the same experience from “GTA VI,” I’d have to hunt through Vice City and its vast collection of in-universe media myself. Both had plenty to say about contemporary America. Only one required me to do much of anything to hear it.

So I was genuinely surprised when I began encountering livestreams on TikTok of people playing through later stages of the “GTA VI” story, including a scooter-stealing mission I’d just discovered but hadn’t tried yet. Then I caught a glimpse of someone playing as… Lucia?!

I scrolled away immediately. Having spent Friday convinced I’d sabotaged the romance through my own choices, I was relieved to see I was still on track, but simultaneously annoyed that someone else’s playthrough had spoiled part of my plot.

Still, I couldn’t stop laughing at the online debate over whether “GTA VI” was too woke, or whether Rockstar had successfully tricked millions of players into simulating healthy relationships. I’d bought the game partly because I wanted to participate in a massive cultural moment, and here I was, too sick to play but still in the thick of things — watching it unfold through Netflix, TikTok, and strangers whose versions of Vice City were already far ahead of mine.

It was exciting, irritating, occasionally informative, and even without my controller, I found myself making decisions about how I wanted to play later. (One person, for example, painted Lucia’s bedroom peach, a choice I disliked so much that I made both a mental and literal note to go with green in the end.)

By Sunday night, I was finally feeling well enough to enjoy the entertainment I’d been trying to consume all weekend. Lucia had come home in my playthrough, and exploring their relationship together made “GTA VI” click in a way no amount of aimless driving could.

At a lavish yacht party, interacting with other guests became an opportunity to figure out what sort of couple these two actually were. Were Jason and Lucia the kind of boyfriend and girlfriend who did drugs together? Would romantic jealousy be an issue, or were we cool with [wink] that?

Suddenly, I wasn’t waiting for Rockstar to tell me what their connection meant. I was actively deciding, and having thoroughly mastered the mechanics of walking around without looking like a complete lunatic by then, I was putting some swag into it.

A particularly sexy jet-skiing sequence made an impression, with Jason holding onto Lucia as she drove instead of the other way around. It was a sweet little reversal of gender norms, and the kind of affectionate character detail I’d been waiting to discover. Even better, it was happening in a game that seemed to understand exactly how much I wanted to drive its love story.

Then came the Carly Rae Jepsen car chase.

Glossy and expensive, our borrowed vehicle reflected the neon lights of Vice City like a scene from “Blade Runner.” With 2015 chart-topper “Run Away with Me” blasting from the speakers, Lucia and Jason were living out the spectacular criminal romance I’d been hoping for. Skyscrapers towered around our blossoming love, and I was riding high…

… Until I smashed us into a cement divider and Jason’s head exploded.

As the game reset and made my sudden brush with vehicular homicide a thing of the past, I found myself laughing like gunfire alone in my apartment. I was suddenly orchestrating a crime of continuity no movie could ever get away with — and it was one of my favorite “GTA VI” moments yet.

Great movies and TV shows often succeed by creating characters who sincerely feel like real people. Rockstar’s new release is exceptionally good at that. But an equally extraordinary connection between art and its audience can emerge from acknowledging that the people experiencing your story have their own needs, limitations, and personalities, too.

That’s the fundamental difference between this extraordinary simulator and a fantastic film. At its best, cinema invites you to inhabit a vision so singular it could only belong to someone else. A great video game gives you the tools to make someone else’s vision singularly your own. That takes a degree of commitment that doesn’t always accommodate the realities of being a person, like, oh say, racing to the bathroom with your digestive system in active, contaminant-born revolt.

Even so, when I think about the hours I spent with “GTA VI” this weekend, I’m not particularly interested in how much other “content” I could have been watching instead. No filmmaker could have anticipated the precise emotional satisfaction I’d get from that magenta-hued highway catastrophe, and, purely from a practical standpoint, no self-respecting artist should have to.

Rockstar built Vice City over the span of a decade, but that spectacularly stupid scene in all its accidental skull-smashing glory was mine — proof there’s a world of difference between commanding someone’s attention and handing them the keys.

Microsoft's 24-hour outage had no hacker behind it, and 10 lessons follow for business

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A dormant Windows flaw, not a hacker, caused Microsoft's 24-hour outage. Here are 10 risk lessons for businesses that rely on one cloud vendor.

Most corporate risk plans imagine a cloud outage that begins with an intruder, such as a stolen password, a ransomware gang or a hostile state. A global Microsoft $MSFT outage began with no intruder at all, and no hacker caused it. Even so, people around the world filed more than 7.4 million reports of trouble during the failure, according to ABC News. A fault inside one company was enough to disrupt work across the globe. The 2024 Microsoft IT outage was linked to CrowdStrike $CRWD, a cybersecurity firm, according to TIME. This time the fault sat inside Microsoft's own systems, in what CBC News reports was the company's second disruption of 2026. For a business, the question is less about who might break in and more about what happens when its provider breaks its own systems.

When a provider of this size fails, whole institutions stop working, not just their email. The Next Web reports that trading firms were delayed and airline communications were disrupted. Finance and air travel usually break down for different reasons, but this time they failed together because they rent the same software, and the weak point sat in a layer that no customer controls.

News18 laid out what went wrong behind Microsoft's "Code Red" and what enterprises should take from it. Its lessons cover a dormant flaw that no hacker planted, the backup channels a company needs when Teams and Outlook stop working, and continuity plans built for days rather than minutes. Together, they move the job of staying online from the vendor's promises to the customer's own preparations.

Microsoft $MSFT caused its own day-long outage with a change it had made months earlier. The root of the failure was a hidden flaw that Microsoft added to its gateway servers in July, not an attack by hackers, according to News18. The flaw was a scalability bug that nobody detected for months, The Next Web reports, and if Microsoft's own engineers missed it, no customer could have found it. A bug of this kind stays hidden until the load on a system grows large enough, so a clean record means only that nobody has triggered it yet.

The cost of a dormant flaw is nothing for months and then everything on one day. Once the flaw surfaced, users lost Exchange Online, Outlook, Teams and Copilot together, which left staff with no fallback app inside the same suite. The component that broke had seen "roughly ten years' worth of acceptable failures," Charles Lamanna, Microsoft's executive vice-president for Copilot, Agents and Platform, said in a Code Red review, as relayed in a memo by Amanda Silver and reported by The Next Web. Customers absorbed that cost during the 24-hour outage rather than in small amounts spread across years.

Vendor reviews built on uptime history and incident counts cannot detect this kind of risk, because nothing about a dormant flaw appears in the record until the day it fails. A company that rents its email and collaboration tools should assume that a flaw already exists somewhere in the stack and plan for the day it surfaces.

No customer could have reached the defect itself, but what triggers a hidden flaw and how fast Microsoft can fix it decide how much damage it does.

Microsoft $MSFT's day-long outage began on an ordinary day of operations, with no launch, attack or unusual event behind it. News18 reports that a routine traffic shift, combined with reduced capacity, set off the dormant flaw. The result was a full stop rather than a slowdown, with Microsoft's servers left "overwhelmed and unresponsive," according to ABC News.

An ordinary trigger makes this kind of failure harder to see coming, and it makes the failure no less serious. Engineers tend to flag rare events, such as a major product launch, for extra caution. A traffic shift belongs to the normal running of a service, and nothing about it marks it as a moment of risk. Walayat Hussain, an associate professor at Australian Catholic University, sees that ordinariness as the cause for concern. "This looks like Microsoft's own systems tripping over themselves, and that is the real warning," Hussain told ABC News.

For a customer, the trigger sits inside operations it does not run and cannot schedule around. A company gets no notice of routine traffic shifts inside its provider's systems and has no say in how much spare capacity the provider has on hand.

A company's defense has to begin the moment service stops, whatever the cause turns out to be.

A failure like this is only partly about the broken code. The rest depends on whether the people fixing it can agree on what is broken. Microsoft $MSFT's engineers exchanged more than 11,000 messages during the outage response, News18 reports, split across 11 different Teams chats rather than gathered in one. The weakness this exposed was coordination, not manpower.

Every one of those chats ran on Teams, Microsoft's own chat product. Teams was itself degraded during the outage, and the responders were coordinating through a tool damaged by the same failure they were trying to fix. A diagnosis scattered across many threads, on a tool that is failing along with everything else, is a risk any company could face in its own outage. For an enterprise, the lesson is to decide before a crisis where the diagnosis gets assembled. A company needs one named record that brings the separate threads together, with a person responsible for keeping it, and that record matters more than the number of people typing. It also helps to keep that record somewhere other than the chat tool the company depends on every day.

Responders can trade 11,000 messages and still lack a clear answer if nobody owns the full picture.

An outage ends when engineers have delivered a repair to every affected system, not when they find the bug. Microsoft $MSFT's services were out for 24 hours, according to Microsoft's internal memos reported by News18, and customers had no part in the repair for that whole time. No company can shorten a schedule that another company sets, and its staff could only wait for Microsoft to finish.

The obvious lesson is that Microsoft should push fixes out faster, but its chief executive points to the growing number of patches as a source of risk. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella sees patching speeding up rather than lagging, CNBC reports, and he credits artificial intelligence with finding more bugs for engineers to fix. "The thing that AI has done is it allowed us to find these bugs and fix these bugs. But that means you've got to then patch them, and that creates instability," Nadella said. Every fix is also a change to systems that are already running, and each change carries risk of its own. Patching is therefore a trade-off rather than a race. Leaving flaws unpatched keeps them open, while a steady stream of patches can unsettle the systems that receive them.

Neither faster nor slower patching addresses what made this failure global, which was the lack of a limit on how many systems one bad change could affect. Nadella acknowledges that Microsoft should have tested the change before releasing it and should have built barriers between its systems so that a single bug could not cause a worldwide outage, according to CNBC. For enterprises, the useful questions are whether the vendor releases updates in stages and keeps regions separate. They should also ask whether their own IT staff patch in-house systems in stages, so that nobody installs one update on every machine at once.

The measure that matters is not how fast a vendor delivers a good fix but how many systems it lets one bad change reach.

Microsoft $MSFT's promise to recover from an outage within an hour is a commitment about the next failure, not a record of this one. News18 reports that the company's Code Red plan sets a one-hour recovery target. Traffic management, the way Microsoft moves traffic inside its own systems, is one of the areas the company is overhauling to reach that target. Even if Microsoft meets it, the hour measures how quickly Microsoft resolves a disruption. It does not measure when a customer's courtroom, clinic or trading desk gets back to work.

The accounts of this outage's length do not agree. It lasted 24 hours across Nov. 12–13, 2026, yet The Associated Press reports that Microsoft did not announce full restoration until Saturday, Nov. 14. By either account, Microsoft's actual recovery took far longer than an hour, and a business that writes the hour into its own plans is counting on a performance Microsoft has not yet delivered.

Microsoft restored its services in stages, and customers lost work the whole time. Outlook problems piled up into Friday even as Microsoft kept restoring service, according to The Associated Press, and in Manchester, England, a trial was halted and the jurors were sent home. The recovery time a customer actually faces is Microsoft's gradual restoration plus whatever fallbacks the customer has built for itself.

Plan against the record, not the pledge, and test the hour the next time Microsoft's services go down.

Most companies picture a single point of failure as a target, a system that an attacker aims at and the security budget pays to defend. Microsoft $MSFT's systems stayed out of service for 24 hours without any attacker involved, according to News18. The company said it had found nothing to suggest that anyone acted with malicious intent, according to TIME. A routine update or a hidden design bug can knock out core systems as thoroughly as an attack, and defenses built to stop intruders are not designed to catch either one. The harm a company suffers depends on how much of its work rests on the failed component, not on whether anyone meant to break it.

While systems are down, nobody outside the vendor can tell an accident from an attack, and a dead inbox looks the same either way. In Australia, the outage cut off the Australian Federal Police and even ASIO, the country's domestic spy agency, according to TIME. No cyberattack was behind their losses. Their skill at spotting enemies gave them no protection against a fault with no enemy behind it.

Ruling out an attack became public work of its own. Australia's Home Affairs and Cyber Security Minister Tony Burke passed on Microsoft's early view of the cause. "They believe they are dealing in some ways similar to what we saw with respect to CrowdStrike $CRWD, where there is an update that has gone awry," Burke said, as reported by TIME. Microsoft gave that account while its recovery was still under way, so customers were weighing the failed company's own explanation at the moment they were least able to check it. A company's response therefore has to start from the damage, not wait for a verdict on the cause.

The more useful audit asks which single components, at the vendor or in-house, could stop the company's work for a day, whoever or whatever breaks them. Under that test, an accident at a vendor counts as a threat as serious as a break-in and should get the same contingency planning.

Most companies picture a backup channel as a list of phone numbers kept for emergencies, a way to keep talking rather than a way to keep working. Microsoft $MSFT had an outside channel of that kind during the outage, and it posted its public updates on X $TWTR, according to ABC News. Its engineers, though, ran the repair on Teams, the product that was failing. Microsoft had a separate line for speaking to the public and none for fixing the problem.

A fallback needs a trigger that the company controls as much as it needs a place to go. Microsoft's first update described a problem "in the Americas," ABC News reports, while Immigration New Zealand had stopped processing every visa application. A company that waits for the vendor to confirm the scope may lose hours it could have spent working. An SMS bridge runs entirely outside Microsoft's systems, and a company can switch it on by its own decision. The plan should state the trigger in plain terms: Staff move to the bridge when they lose mail or chat, not when the vendor's status update agrees. A bridge that nobody has practiced on is just a contact list again, and employees need to rehearse using it before a crisis.

Small companies have the least room to absorb a lost day. Exchange Online, Outlook, Teams and Copilot failed together, News18 reports, so a small firm that kept all of its mail and chat in that suite had to find a workaround somewhere else or go without. Some small businesses lost up to $30,000 in a single day, according to ABC News.

The time to set up and test a route outside the suite is now, while every channel still works.

Buying an AI assistant from the company that already runs a firm's email and meetings ties more of the working day to one provider and spreads none of the risk. Companies tend to budget for Copilot as a new capability with risks of its own. During the outage, it failed alongside the rest of the suite. Exchange Online, Outlook, Teams and Copilot all went down at the same time, according to News18. Microsoft $MSFT said AI agents had nothing to do with the cause, according to CBC News. Copilot went dark because it runs on the same foundation as the mail and the meetings, not because of a flaw in the assistant. Each product a company adds from the same provider rests on that foundation too, so it deepens a single dependency instead of adding a separate system.

The remedy is a second set of tools, but building one is a long project. "Ultimately, it has to be diversification. We have to make sure that when a system goes down, we have a backup," Francis Syms, associate dean of applied sciences and technology at Humber Polytechnic, told CBC News. Bill Laboon, vice-president of technical operations at the Web3 Foundation, says companies don't see the value of spreading their systems across providers until one of them fails.

The first step is knowing which work stops when the vendor does. A map of the processes that would halt in a vendor outage gives a company that knowledge before the next failure, and it can also show which critical work already sits outside the shared stack. Santé Québec does not handle patient communications by email, according to CBC News, so those exchanges did not depend on the email service that failed.

The useful question today is not which vendor to switch to, but which work stops when this one goes down.

A continuity plan prepares a company only for the outage it imagines, and a short outage is the easiest one to imagine. News18 reports that a company drafting a formal Continuity of Operations Plan should not count on a quick failover of 15 minutes. CBC News reports that Microsoft $MSFT's outage was in its second day on Nov. 13, so a plan built around a 15-minute switch would have been measuring the wrong scale. A plan that expects service back within minutes has no instructions for the second business morning, when staff, customers and deadlines are still waiting. Companies should size their plans to the failure that just happened, counted in days, rather than to the failure the plan was written for.

The organizations that held up did not recover sooner than anyone else. They had a way to keep working that did not depend on the failed service. Aviva kept its calls going on alternative numbers and moved recordings by Bluetooth, according to CBC News. Its staff could use those low-tech routes because they did not run through Microsoft's cloud. Where a company runs its software is also a continuity decision. Companies that hosted Microsoft software in-house came through somewhat better, and they had made that choice well before the failure, not in the middle of it.

Planning for a multi-day outage means deciding now which work must continue without the cloud, then building that route through manual processes or self-run systems before a company needs it for a second day.
