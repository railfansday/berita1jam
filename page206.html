Could Archer Aviation Stock Make You a Millionaire by 2036?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A multitrillion-dollar opportunity in aviation, an eVTOL that ferries passengers in the sky: Could Archer mint new millionaires?

The 2030s could become one of the most momentous decades of recorded history, for more reasons than one. Those years could be the first full decade in which we have artificial general intelligence (AGI), or something close to it. That would not only accelerate the pace of scientific and technological breakthroughs, but quite possibly shift the economic foundations on which society is based (e.g., robots in the workforce).

Another change in the next decade could come to transportation: Indeed, if Archer Aviation (ACHR -0.24%) has its way, by 2036, a large portion of travelers and commuters will be flying in air taxis instead of driving cars.

But can Archer make and certify eVTOLs at scale by 2036? And better yet, could its stock, currently trading almost 60% lower since last year, rebound and make investors millionaires?

In a nutshell, Archer is in the business of making eVTOL (electric vertical takeoff and landing) aircraft. Today, that business is mostly nonexistent (or rather, it exists in a pre-commercialization form). Indeed, no eVTOL is flying commercially right now in the U.S. But by 2036, this industry could be at a breakout point, one that transforms eVTOLs into a multibillion-dollar commercial market.

Morgan Stanley, for instance, projected in a 2021 report that the global total addressable market (TAM) for urban air mobility, a broader industry that includes eVTOLs, could grow from $55 billion in 2030 to $255 billion in 2035, before reaching a staggering $1 trillion by 2040. Morgan Stanley even went so far as to project a $9 trillion market by 2050.

Of course, this doesn't mean that Archer will grow into a $1 trillion company by 2040, or that it will even capture a large portion of that growing market. Indeed, Morgan Stanley's forecast encompasses several markets -- like cargo and military -- not just passenger transportation.

But since Archer is also developing military aircraft through its partnership with Anduril, and has plans to acquire a defense business (Insitu) from Boeing, its growth opportunity could well exceed ferrying passengers around cities.

Here's a thought: If Archer doesn't have a flying taxi business in 2036, can it survive as a company?

I think so. With its planned acquisitions from Boeing, it could lean more heavily on defense. It could also find opportunities selling autonomous aircraft for other purposes, like transporting cargo or carrying out military missions. But without a successful air taxi business, I doubt Archer will grow large enough to make millionaires out of investors buying shares in 2026.

Still, just because Archer doesn't mint millionaires doesn't mean it's off the table as a growth stock. But just know what you're buying -- a company with a lot to prove -- and understand the risks of investing in a speculative aviation company before you do.

The ‘Ocarina of Time’ Remake Is Finally Here, but Why Does Link Look so Yassified?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

After 28 years, the gamer’s game is getting a big remake.

A fully redone version of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time arrives Thursday exclusively on the Switch 2. Nintendo has been releasing trailers and screenshots in the run-up to the launch, and the imagery, while whetting appetites, has also left fans with an overwhelming question: Why does Link look like that?

Specifically, child Link. Ocarina of Time allows players to time-hop and explore levels as child and adult versions of the game’s main protagonist. Gameplay starts as child Link in the Kokiri Forest, which looks more lush and verdant than ever in the remake. But aesthetic upgrades have also been applied to child Link, resulting in a curled-eyelash, laminated-eyebrow, douyin-lipstick yassified elf boy.

Fans have been quick to compare the design to the human-vampire hybrid Renesmee from Twilight and the Gerber Baby. “He looks so microwaveable,” one Reddit user wrote.

The changes feel all the more stark when juxtaposed with the remake’s background nonplayer characters, who remain noticeably simpler and more reminiscent of the original game’s character designs.

The original Ocarina was limited to minimal polygonal shapes and textures because of the small storage capacity in N64 cartridges, and these technical limitations produced what became a recognizable and beloved visual style. Thanks to the Switch 2’s larger storage capacity and higher memory bus speed, designers have been able to add details to Link and the other main characters that wouldn’t have been possible in the original.

Beyond better technology, though, people have different expectations of video games from major publishers. Interconnecting level designs, intricate storylines, and highly realistic environment and character model details are table stakes for contemporary AAA games. Video games are also more mainstream, and once-regional media like anime and manga have become global phenomena over the past three decades, influencing taste in the larger culture.

Clayton Lundgren, a former quality assurance tester for the game developer Naughty Dog who currently streams Ocarina of Time along with other games on Twitch, sees nothing accidental about Link’s new look. “For a decision as important as the key art for the main character, this is the kind of thing that is gonna go past a lot of quality inspection higher up than the person that drew it,” he says. “This is 20-plus years of merchandising that you’re committing to.”

Since The Legend of Zelda launched the franchise in 1986, Link has existed in many forms. Though his long hat, pointy ears, blond hair, green tunic, and Master Sword are staples of his design, none of these characteristics have been stable across his 40 years. He’s had pink hair, his tunic has been blue and red. He’s even been a tree.

The remake takes the evolution a step further, though. Lundgren, who has at least 1,000 hours in Ocarina across speed runs, challenge runs, and community competitions, immediately realized that this week’s release is a ground-up remake when he watched the game’s new trailer. For many fans, the rich detail of the underbrush and sunbeams in Kokiri Forest visible in the teaser video were proof this isn’t the same Ocarina. But Lundgren saw something else: The undulation in the ground was different.

A 2023 version of Link from Tears of the Kingdom.

Unlike the 2011 Zelda remake for the 3DS, which uses the same underlying geometries as the original, the 2026 remake is a complete overhaul, from the superficial textures to the bones underneath. And if everything is different, how is this the same Link we’ve played and loved before?

Luke Cuddy, a philosophy professor at Southwestern College who has edited books about The Legend of Zelda, offers the “Ship of Theseus” thought experiment as a way to think through the conundrum. The famous philosophical riddle asks whether the true ship is the one Theseus is currently sailing or the original constituent parts that have been replaced over time. If one thinks the true ship is the one Theseus is currently sailing, they believe there is something beyond the physical body that makes something what it is.

“There are only a few minimal features that are continuous over time with Link,” says Cuddy. Aside from a few visual and narrative touchstones—and the occasional “Hyah!” or “Hyup!” voice line—there isn’t much else that Link is. “For me, that allows for a lot of malleability.”

That malleability is cushioned by other forms of continuity. Beyond aesthetic similarities and a shared title across Zelda games, contemporary games that were influenced by the original Ocarina are now shaping the remake in turn, and norms of play set by speedrunners and challenge runners of the original continue to shape engagement across gaming communities.

But another force is fueling the current scrutiny and anxiety over the remake, one that haunts every contemporary artistic endeavor: Was generative AI involved?

Artificial intelligence has long been part of video games designed to respond in real time to players’ actions. But these days, when the topic of AI use in video games comes up, it’s usually in reference to AI tools being used to make the visual parts of a game, the storyboards, the written scripts, or the spoken dialog.

The backlash to these tools is growing, rooted in concerns about the environment, artists’ intellectual property, and aesthetic taste. Players are increasingly worried that they’re engaging with media that betrays their values, and for a game like Ocarina, the possible juxtaposition of the original’s groundbreaking design with derivative and cannibalistic tools feels like a long way to fall.

Cuddy compares the “super-enhanced version of Link” in the remake to the output of an AI filter, and other fans are questioning why the designs look like they were run through DLSS 5, an animation upscaling tool that uses generative AI. Nintendo has confirmed its use of AMD FSR, a competing upscaling tool, though when WIRED asked if generative AI tools were also used in the character design process for the new game, the company declined to comment.

When one of the most beloved entries in a beloved game franchise gets a new coat of paint, fans of the game are of course going to scrutinize every aspect of the remake’s design. But Lundgren predicts that, based on Zelda fans’ history of embracing Link’s many incarnations in the past, “in two years we’ll think it looks great.”

Linehouse reimagines industrial aesthetics for Jen Hangzhou hotel in Kohn Pedersen Fox-designed building

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Photography by Jonathan Leijonhufvud and Wen Studio

International studio Linehouse has completed the interiors of the Jen hotel in Hangzhou, China, which features details that nod to local craft and the area’s industrial heritage.

The hotel, which is owned by the Shangri-La Hotel Group, is located along Hangzhou’s Dongxin River in an urban setting and flanked by two former industrial factory compounds.

The 18-floor hotel building was designed by US architecture firm Kohn Pedersen Fox, using steel, grey brick and glass to reference the site’s industrial past.

The same design approach was extended into the interior by Linehouse, which focuses on showcasing local craft and nods to the industrial elements of the former factories.

“Jen Hangzhou is an urban hotel that celebrates its neighbourhood context – one that layers history, craft, culture and rich materiality into a new perspective on Hangzhou,” Linehouse said.

The studio used earth-toned marble, oak panelling and relaxed furniture in the ground-floor lobby to create a calm and warm atmosphere.

At the entrance, guests are greeted by a textured artwork by artist Elsa Jeandedieu that depicts an abstract version of the bridges and canal close to the hotel.

Patchwork patterns on the marble floor and on a bespoke rug in the lobby were informed by the street layout and tile patterns of the surrounding neighbourhood.

All the hospitality spaces, including a restaurant, a bar and function rooms are located on the sixth floor.

The long, linear OpenHouse restaurant is wrapped by full-height glazing that invites natural light inside.

A timber design feature spans the ceiling and was informed by the craft of sunmao, a traditional timber joinery technique. It is highlighted by a thin black metal frame that supports suspended lighting.

At the centre of the restaurant, a coffee bar splits the space into a dining area on one side and an all-day lounge on the other.

In the private dining room, the timber canopy has an angled design and is decorated with burnt red and yellow hues. In the rest of the OpenHouse restaurant, rooms feature teal tiles and handmade ceramic wall lighting.

The adjacent Koan Bar is a triple-height venue characterised by an undulating black metal staircase that takes guests to a terrace on the upper floor, with incorporated platforms where guests can socialise.

The sinuous staircase is carved between walls lined with concrete panels, another nod to the site’s industrial heritage.

Beneath it, curved booths in walnut timber and tan leather provide intimate seating spaces.

Three interconnected spaces on the sixth floor, enveloped by undulating timber walls, will be used for events and intimate social gatherings. Like in the lobby, bespoke carpets feature patterns abstracted from urban details in the neighbourhood.

The seventh floor is dedicated to wellness and comprises a gym, a yoga room, a spa, a pool and changing rooms, made from tactile materials with muted tones to create a calm environment.

At the gym, a lava stone wall has engravings that illustrate movement, while the yoga room features hand-chiseled timber panels.

A sloped skylit annex accommodates the swimming pool.

The 176 guest rooms were conceived as “urban retreats”. Here, Linehouse used a restrained palette of gun metal, oak datum, textured render and tan leather to underline the industrial aesthetic.

“The public areas explore traditional craft techniques such as sunmao timber joinery, clay tiling and bricks, natural patterns, textures and renders, whilst the guestrooms explore a refined industrial aesthetic,” Linehouse said.

“Red brick, concrete, metal and bold forms reference the surrounding industrial environment whilst handcrafted tiles, vintage timber, colours and metals with different patinas introduce warmth, richness and texture.”

The guest rooms have curved ceilings that create indirect lighting and add softness to the space, while the bathrooms are illuminated by glass-block walls.

Founded by Alex Mok and Briar Hickling in 2013, Linehouse is an architecture and interior practice with studios in Shanghai, Hong Kong and Auckland.

It has recently designed Ink Ink Market in Shanghai with a translucent sloping facade, as well as the interiors for Bangkok’s Central Park shopping centre with three dramatic atriums, which was shortlisted of Retail Interior (large) by this year’s Dezeen Awards.

Improved PowerTRACE Technology Simplifies Bitmap-to-Vector Conversion

Bitmap images consist of individual pixels that determine how graphics appear at a specific resolution. Although suitable for photographs and digital content, pixel-based artwork can become blurred or visibly jagged when enlarged beyond its original dimensions.

Vector graphics use mathematically defined paths rather than fixed pixels. These paths remain sharp when resized, making vector artwork suitable for applications ranging from small product labels to large storefront signage.

For print-and-cut production, accurate vector geometry is particularly important. Cutting equipment relies on defined paths to determine where material should be separated. Excessive nodes, irregular curves, or poorly structured outlines can introduce complications during production.

How Yoga Brings You Closer to Yourself and the Life You Want

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

In Yoga Journal’s Archives series, we share a curated collection of articles originally published in past issues beginning in 1975. This article about yoga as a science and an art first appeared in the January-February 1977 issue of Yoga Journal. Find more of our Archives here.

Yoga is a science, an art, and a technique which spans the multilevel functioning of the individual. It is a science because of its empirical approach, presented most completely by the classic Yoga Sutras of Patanjali at least 1,000 years ago. Science is defined as a methodology which requires empirical proof of its theories; it requires that which proves a theory be easily repeatable by another experimenter if he/she follows the rules of the experiment.

Yoga, by this definition, is indeed a science. Its rules are given in sutra form and the presentation of Patanjali is a “how-to” book of the highest order. The practitioner need only follow the lead of Patanjali in order to have his own personal empirical results. (It is true that the directions are in parts esoteric and apparently illusive; this points out the necessity of having a teacher upon whom the practioner can rely for advice during certain intervals.) In addition to the work by Patanjali, the Hatha Yoga Pradipika and the Gheranda and Shiva Samhita are references which can explain the science of yoga.

But yoga is an art, too. Individuals have dedicated their lives to the perfection of this art. This does not necessarily imply the production of tangible output such as texts, although these have been produced in the history of traditional Indian thought. Rather, the use of the word “art” in this context implies the movement toward perfection within, the embodiment of beauty as it can be expressed through the human being. If one dedicates his/her life to the art of yoga, this implies a dedication which does not involve merely daily practice numbering a few hours. Instead, it implies a minute by minute practice which spans years, and some believe, lifetimes of existence.

The art of yoga is the practice of yoga on all levels of existence; this means every thought, action, dream, relationship, food intake, sexual activity, occupation, etc., are brought into the wider perspective of observation in light of the individual’s desire to understand the Self. This understanding does not imply intellectual understanding alone. It implies the “living” or the “being” in the Self rather than just mentally knowing about the Self. One can compare this to the experience of being in a hurricane with merely reading about the experience of a hurricane; the effect would be quite different.

One who lives in the Self has come into a wholeness of being, possibly for us all by virtue of our human existence, but available presently to only a handful due a complex interplay of events which, for whatever reasons, has brought them to a newer threshold of consciousness. Thus the art of yoga lies in bringing perfection into action through becoming one with oneself.

Part of the ability to do this lies with the powerful psychological technique of “acting as if.” If one wishes to meditate, one often begins by acting as if one were meditating. As the form of meditation is assumed, the content of meditation begins to be experienced. If one acts as if one loves another, and acts with sincerity and with honesty, often feelings of love toward that person do begin to flow. So with yoga. If one watches and practices as if yoga were an art and the Self were the artist, then this lends validity to the possibility of this being true.

This brings one to the question of technique. Yoga is also a concrete way of bringing about profound physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual changes within the individual. The student is instructed in yama and niyama (basic ethical precepts), in asanas (meditative postures), in pranayama (conscious voluntary control of the energy associated with breath), in pratyhara and dharana (watching and observing the mind), and finally, in dhyana, or meditation. The final experiencing of samadhi (union) cannot be taught; it is both the result of the foregoing disciplines and independent of them. The understanding of this apparent paradox is part of the process of learning about yoga.

Thus yoga can be seen from the angles of science, art or technique. It can be approached as a small practice of one’s life, or as the way that life is lived, a quality which is brought to live which liberates one from the strong bonds of ego.
