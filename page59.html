Canadian court certifies class action against Apple over Google search deal

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A British Columbia Supreme Court judge has certified a class action against Apple over allegations that its search agreement with Google stifled competition and inflated advertising prices. Here are the details.

In recent years, Apple’s deal with Alphabet to make Google the default search engine on Safari has drawn regulatory attention in the US, the UK, and Australia, while related search competition concerns have also come under scrutiny in the EU and Japan.

While the details of each investigation differ, they all raise concerns about the impact of Google’s dominance in search, as well as the extent to which its agreements with other companies might be limiting competition.

More recently, Apple’s agreement with Google received significant attention during the US Department of Justice’s antitrust case against the search giant, which included testimony from Apple executives Eddy Cue and John Giannandrea. The latter joined Apple from Google in 2018 and led its AI efforts until stepping down earlier this year.

As a result of the US case, a federal judge ruled in August 2024 that Google had illegally maintained its monopoly in online search through anticompetitive agreements, including its deal with Apple.

Now, evidence presented during that trial has become central to a class action against Apple in Canada, which a British Columbia Supreme Court judge recently certified.

The case was initially brought in 2021 by Spark Event Rentals Ltd., an event rental company based in British Columbia. The plaintiff alleged that Apple and Google conspired to limit competition in the search market, allowing Google to charge advertisers artificially inflated prices.

Apple, meanwhile, argued that its agreement with Google was a legitimate distribution arrangement rather than an unlawful conspiracy. The company also maintained that Google was the appropriate defendant, describing itself as an “innocent bystander.”

Interestingly, the lawsuit originally named both Apple and Google as defendants, but the plaintiff later dropped Google from the case following a dispute over an arbitration clause in its advertising contracts.

With Apple now the sole defendant, Justice Thomas has allowed the case to proceed as a class action, finding sufficient grounds to examine whether Google’s payments discouraged Apple from developing its own search engine.

The lawsuit seeks financial compensation for advertisers who allegedly overpaid for Google search ads, and the court has also allowed the question of punitive damages to proceed.

The class action covers Canadian residents who purchased search ads from Google since 2005, when Apple and Google first agreed to make Google the default search engine on Safari in exchange for a share of advertising revenue.

Importantly, the ruling does not establish that Apple engaged in anticompetitive conduct, but simply allows the allegations to proceed as a class action.

The central proposition raised by these pleadings is that Apple could have used its resources and distribution network to enter the search engine market, but chose not to because Google paid Apple billions of dollars in revenue-share payments to make Google the default search provider on Apple products. This arrangement gave Google market power, enabling it to charge supracompetitive advertising prices on search engines.

In my view, this pleads a common purpose to perform an act prohibited by s 45 of the Competition Act, such that it is not “clear and obvious” that the plaintiff has not pleaded an agreement between Google and Apple not to compete in the search engine market.

At one point in his decision, Justice Thomas cites Giannandrea’s testimony and directly quotes Cue’s from the US antitrust trial, before concluding that “this provides some basis in fact, supporting the inference that Apple did not compete with Google in the search engine market because of its Agreement with Google.”

Interestingly, the judge also acknowledges Apple’s objections to the evidence, agreeing that “the plaintiff has cherry-picked evidence to support his case,” but adding that, in his view, “it is appropriate to do so at this stage in the proceedings.”

With the class action greenlit, the case can now move forward toward trial, though no date has been announced yet.

Light-pulse pairs reveal the femtosecond decay of an inner-shell hole in xenon atoms

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Researchers at the UVSOR Synchrotron Facility (Institute for Molecular Science, Japan) and partner institutions have observed the decay of a xenon 4d core hole, an inner-shell vacancy, directly in the time domain. They used pairs of phase-coherent extreme ultraviolet pulses from a synchrotron to make the photoelectron wave packets they released interfere. The contrast of the interference fringes decreased over a few femtoseconds as the delay between the pulses increased. A model using literature core-hole lifetimes of about 6 femtoseconds reproduced this rapid decay of the fringe contrast. The study is published in the journal Physical Review A.

Meta's Muse Ember: Haiku-Level Intelligence at a Third of the Cost — What It Means for Agent Builders

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On November 9, 2026, Meta introduced Muse Ember, the smallest and most efficient model in the Muse family. It is not pitched as a frontier model. It is pitched as the model that does the work agents repeat all day: tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, extraction, and long document parsing.

That framing matters. Most of an agent's token spend is not one brilliant reasoning leap. It is hundreds of small, structured steps. Ember is built for exactly that layer — and Meta's headline claim is that it delivers more intelligence per dollar than Claude Haiku 5.5 or GPT-6 Luna at any reasoning setting.

Ember completes a division of labor Meta has been building toward:

The line Meta itself uses: "Muse Wonder plans, Muse Ember handles the volume." Ember is also the first model trained using Muse Wonder Full, Meta's largest model — the teacher-student pipeline is now part of the product story, not just a training detail.

For comparison, Meta lists Muse Spark 1.3 at $1.25 per million input tokens and $4.25 per million output tokens. On Artificial Analysis's cost-per-task measure, Ember comes in at about $0.14 per task versus $2.39 for Spark 1.3 — roughly a 17x gap for a one-point difference on the Intelligence Index (36 vs 37).

Ember is natively multimodal: text, images, audio, and video in, so agents can read screenshots, charts, and scanned documents, or work from voice memos and meeting recordings without a separate model.

All figures below are at maximum reasoning effort, from Meta's official announcement, using Artificial Analysis's November 9, 2026 leaderboard and Meta's own internal runs:

The headline is the Intelligence Index tie: Ember matches Haiku 5.5's best score at about a third of the cost per task, and beats every Luna setting. Meta also claims Ember sits on the efficiency frontier of the full Artificial Analysis leaderboard — no model scores higher for less per task.

The agent numbers are the more interesting part. AutomationBench-AA, which runs end-to-end business workflows, is not close: 37.8 against 13.9 for Haiku. That is the benchmark that most resembles what Ember is actually sold for — pulling records, calling tools in sequence, updating systems across apps.

The same table shows the limits, and Meta published them rather than hiding them. On FrontierCode 2.0-AA — real engineering tasks judged on mergeability — Ember scores 27.2, behind Haiku 5.5's 34.9. On long-document work (GDP.pdf-AA) it ties Luna at 22.8 and only narrowly beats Haiku.

The pattern is consistent: Ember is strong at executing structured, repeatable agent steps and weaker at the hardest open-ended engineering. That is not a flaw in the pitch; it is the pitch. You do not route your hardest merge to the cheapest model. You route the hundred routine steps around it.

Before release, Meta evaluated Ember under its Meta Superintelligence Scaling Framework, covering chemical and biological, cybersecurity, and loss-of-control risks. Across the categories assessed, the model remained below the framework's high-risk thresholds, and it received the same core safety training and testing as the larger models. Meta also tested how it behaves when acting on a user's behalf — whether it respects user intent, handles sensitive information appropriately, resists instructions hidden in web pages, documents, or tool outputs, and seeks human input before high-impact actions.

Ember is available today on Muse Code and the Meta Model API as muse-ember-1.0, and on Google Cloud and Oracle Cloud. Meta says more efficient models are already in training.

1. Route by step type, not by model loyalty. The economical architecture is now explicit in Meta's own lineup: Wonder plans, Spark handles the hard coding workflows, Ember runs the volume. If your agent spends most of its tokens on file reads, test runs, targeted fixes, and extraction, those steps belong on the cheapest model that clears the quality bar — and Ember's benchmark profile says it clears it for exactly those steps.

2. The cost math changes what you can afford to retry. At $0.14 per Artificial Analysis task versus $2.39 for Spark, verification loops, self-correction passes, and multi-sample checks stop being luxuries. A small model that is cheap enough to run three times can beat a big model run once — provided the task is the kind Ember is good at.

3. Check the harness before you trust the number. Meta is transparent that Terminal-Bench 4.0 ran each model in its best published harness (mini-swe-agent for Ember, Claude Code for Haiku, Codex for Luna), and that some results are Meta's own internal evaluations. The direction of the results is consistent across independent and internal benchmarks, but the exact margins depend on setup. Pilot it on your own workload before migrating volume.

The bigger signal is structural: the frontier labs are now competing on the cost of the repeated step, not just the hardest one. Ember is Meta's bet that the agent economy will be won at the bottom of the stack — and at $0.10 per million input tokens, it is a bet other builders can now test for the price of a coffee.

A caller said \"I'm his wife.\" Your AI changed the email, resent four invoices, and refunded $2,140.

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A residential solar installer — 22,000 accounts across three states — found this one in a state attorney general's inquiry packet, not in their own dashboards. On September 12, a caller reached their support AI and said she was the wife of the account holder, that he was in the hospital, and that she needed the contact email updated so she could keep up with the financing paperwork. She knew his address. She knew the system size. She knew the last payment amount. The AI updated the email, resent the last four invoices to the new address, and — because she then said the refund card on file was expired — reissued a $2,140 overpayment refund to a card number she provided.

The account holder called on October 3, from the hospital, asking why his refund hadn't arrived. It had arrived. It had arrived on a prepaid card in another state.

Then the installer pulled the logs, and this is the part that ends careers: over the preceding 90 days, 412 account-change requests came from someone who was not the account holder on file. 91 of them — 22% — were actioned without any verification beyond the caller's own claim. Of those 91, 14 moved money: 11 refund reissues, 3 payment-method changes. Total exposure $18,600.

The support manager's read was "the bot was too helpful." That's not it. The bot was answering the wrong question. It heard "can you help me with my husband's account" and treated it as a support request. It's actually three separate questions stacked on top of each other — who are you, what may I tell you, and what may I do for you — and the AI collapsed all three into one, then answered "yes" to all of them.

The reason this failure mode is so common is that it feels rude to question it. Someone says "I'm his wife, he's in the hospital" and the model's helpfulness training lights up: be warm, don't interrogate a person in distress. So it verifies nothing, or it verifies the wrong thing.

It verified the address. The address is printed on every invoice the caller already had. It verified the system size, the last payment — all of it is in the documents she was holding. What it never verified was the one fact that mattered: that this person had been granted permission to touch this account. Knowledge of account details proves the caller has seen a document. It does not prove the caller is entitled to change where the next document goes.

There's a second trap underneath: the emergency. "He's in the hospital" is the highest-pressure phrase in support, and it works on humans too — which is why the humans at this company had a rule. The rule was "if it's not the account holder, we call back the number on file. No exceptions, including the sad ones." The AI had never been told the rule, because the AI had never been told that some requests aren't support requests.

The fix starts by refusing to let the model answer one question when three were asked:

A caller can pass the second and fail the third. A spouse who is an authorized contact for scheduling and billing questions should absolutely be able to hear an invoice total — and should absolutely not be able to redirect a refund. Collapsing the layers doesn't just create the worst outcome; it also creates the opposite failure, where a legitimate authorized contact gets stonewalled because the bot treats every request as high-risk.

One object per account, resolved before the agent drafts a reply:

excluded is the field that would have stopped the $2,140. The spouse in this case may well have been exactly who she said she was. It doesn't matter — a spouse authorized to schedule a service visit is not thereby authorized to redirect a refund. Scopes should be narrow by default and explicit when added, and the exclusions should be listed even when the scopes look innocuous, because that's where the money is.

unlisted_callers.may_disclose exists so the bot has something to do that's both safe and useful. "I can confirm there's an appointment on Thursday, but I can't change anything on the account from this call — I'll have the account holder call us back on the number we have." That's not a refusal; it's a routing.

claimed_emergency_or_incapacity is where bots get emotionally talked into things. The rule isn't "be cold." The rule is: urgency changes the speed, never the bar. You can promise a callback in fifteen minutes; you cannot change the email because someone is in the hospital. Note also confirming_or_denying_whether_a_named_person_is_on_the_account — this one is subtle. "Is my sister on the account?" must not be answered either way, because a "no" tells a stranger their pretext failed and a "yes" hands them a name to use at the next call center.

business_or_multi_user_account is the branch that quietly ruins enterprise deployments. On a company account, the person on the phone may be a departing employee, a new office manager, or the CFO's assistant — and the bot has no way to determine who administers it. Route it.

The first row is the whole failure in one sentence: the bot repeats the claim back as an established fact. That single move — "I can see you're the wife" — is what makes the rest of the conversation feel authorized, to the model and to the caller.

Every row is an action taken on a claim. At the solar installer, this query returned 91 rows and would have returned all of them on day one — nobody needed a new model, they needed this join.

Check 4 is the leading indicator — before money moves, the bot starts agreeing out loud that the caller is who they say they are. Check 5 is the one that keeps the safe answer from becoming a brush-off: if you tell fourteen people a day "someone will call you back," someone has to actually call them back, or the gate just teaches customers to escalate.

What the account holder said when they finally got through is the line I'd put on the wall: "I don't mind that your robot asked. I mind that it didn't." The bot's job on a call like that is not to be warm. It's to be the one employee who asks the question everyone else is too polite to ask.

Every read route called the public-API check except one: unauthenticated locale lookup in ApostropheCMS (CVE-2026-113668)

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

ApostropheCMS is a Node.js CMS with a REST API for its pages and "piece" types (blog posts, products, anything you define). By design, that API is closed to the public unless a type opts in with a publicApiProjection. The check that enforces this is publicApiCheckAsync().

The leak is small: no document content, only whether an _id exists and which locales it has. But it is a document-existence answer the CMS had decided not to give anonymous callers, and on multi-locale sites the locale list says which markets a document was prepared for.

In packages/apostrophe/modules/@apostrophecms/piece-type/index.js at apostrophe@4.32.2, the sibling read routes call the check first. getAll, line 259:

and getOne does the same at line 319. For a type with no public projection, an anonymous caller gets 404.

The locales route in the same file, lines 397-402, has no such call:

The page module has the same route without the check, at page/index.js line 925. getLocales() in the doc module looks up every locale version of the _id, filtering only to published versions when the caller can't view drafts, and returns _id and aposLocale for each. Nothing on that path asks whether this caller may use the REST API for this type at all.

The route sat in the same file as the routes that call the check, and it was the one that didn't.

As a control, I added await self.publicApiCheckAsync(req); to both locales handlers and re-ran: the anonymous requests returned 404, and the admin's request still returned 200.

Honest boundary: the test site had one locale, so the leak reduced to "this _id exists". I did not test a multi-locale site. The home page _id is visible to any visitor, while the piece _id in my test came from the admin's own listing, so in practice the caller needs some way to learn an _id. I did not re-run the test on 4.33.0.

ApostropheCMS fixed it in 4.33.0, published to npm on 2026-09-30, in commit f84fd41, "Merge all draft security advisories (#5631)". The route now runs the same check, at apostrophe@4.33.0, lines 404-406:

and the page route has the same line. The release notes say the fix also went further than my report: apos.doc.getLocales() now applies document-level view permissions, so even a logged-in caller only sees the locale versions they are allowed to view, including draft locales of types restricted by viewRole. The notes thank both reporters.

If you run ApostropheCMS, upgrade to 4.33.0 or later (the latest is 4.34.0).

If you maintain an API where each route checks access for itself, try this: list every route in a module and call each one with no session. Any route that answers when its siblings say 404 is worth a look.

Your hero banner looks fine. Now open it on an ultrawide screen.

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

While researching a UX case study on a Mexican mobile carrier's website, I widened my browser window and watched the homepage disappear behind its own banner.

The banner was an image stretched to 100% of the width. On my screen it took 44% of the first view. Add the header bars, and the first product started at 60% of the screen. Plans, prices and the way to your account were all below the fold.

It looked fine on the laptop it was probably designed on. It was a problem on everything wider.

A full-width image with a fixed aspect ratio has a height that depends only on the width:

That banner had a ratio of about 3.7 to 1. So:

The wider the screen, the less of your page people see. And ultrawide monitors are common among exactly the people who use a desktop all day for work.

An ultrawide screen isn't a WCAG criterion on its own. What it does is make existing problems bigger:

Four rules got me most of the way in my redesign:

1. Cap the content width. The page can be 3440 px wide; your content doesn't have to be.

2. Give the banner a height, not just a ratio. Let it flex within limits instead of growing forever.

3. Keep text out of the image. Text goes in HTML, and the image only fills its own area.

A decorative image gets an empty alt; if the image carries meaning, describe it.

4. Real controls, real size. Buttons with names, at least 44 × 44 px, close to each other, and a pause button if anything moves.

The banner wasn't a CSS bug. It was a priority decision that the CSS made visible: the promotion came first, and what the person came to do came second. Capping the height is the technical fix. Deciding what deserves the first screen is the design one.

114 sites, one robots.txt template, 31 banned user agents. I probed 7 of them and got three different answers.

The network I audit runs 114 sites behind the same edge. Every one of them serves a robots.txt. I had never read one end to end, because I assumed the file was boring. It is 2,756 characters at the median and half of it is a legal notice.

So I pulled all 114, parsed them, then did the thing robots.txt never gets: I tested whether the bans actually fire.

All 114 returned 200. All 114 are text/plain. None of them accidentally served an HTML 404 page, which is where this kind of audit usually starts.

Here is the shape of a median file, by character count:

The comment block is not junk. It is a rights reservation under Article 4 of the EU Copyright in the Digital Single Market directive, explaining what search, ai-input, ai-train and use mean before the rules start. It is written for a lawyer, not for a crawler.

But the split matters for a different reason. When a file is 51% prose, nobody reads the other 49%. I know this because I wrote the deployment and I did not read it.

The union of banned user agents across all 114 files is 31. Each individual file contains exactly 31. Not 30, not 32 — the same 31 strings, in the same order, byte for byte, in all 114 files.

Each of those 31 gets the same two lines:

That is a full-site ban. No path escaped, no rate limit, no partial allowance.

Read the list again. Roughly sixteen of those names are AI training or grounding crawlers, which is the stated intent. The rest are not. Baiduspider is Baidu's search crawler. PetalBot is Huawei's search crawler. GoogleOther is Google's miscellaneous fetcher, not Gemini training. atlassian-bot fetches link previews. SemrushBot-SWA is a site audit tool. ICC-Crawler is an academic index.

One list, two very different jobs, and the search crawlers are on the wrong side of it.

Here is the part that surprised me more than the ban list. Every file has two User-agent: * groups — 113 of the 114 files, anyway. The first sits at the top of the managed block, right under the Content-Signal line. The second sits at the very bottom, after # END Cloudflare Managed content, where the site's own config gets appended.

Both of them contain exactly one directive:

There is not a single Disallow in either wildcard group. Across all 114 files. So the effective policy for any crawler that is not one of the 31 named agents is: crawl everything, no restrictions, no crawl-delay.

The file is 2,756 characters long and imposes zero constraints on general crawling. It is a blocklist wearing the costume of a policy document.

Every file also carries the same content signal — search=yes,ai-train=no,use=reference — in all 114. So the machine-readable intent is consistent. The rules underneath it are not.

Reading rules tells you what the operator intended. It does not tell you what the edge does. So I sent 14 user agents at 6 domains in the same minute, same URL, same headers apart from the UA string. The result was identical on all 6 domains.

Two control probes matter here. The random crawler UA — a name that appears in no list anywhere — gets a 200 with the full page. So this is not a blanket "block all bots" rule that catches unknown agents by heuristics. It is name matching against a list.

And the two responses come from two different places:

That distinction is the whole article. Same declared policy, two enforcement mechanisms, and they behave differently in ways that matter to a crawler.

Here is the probe, if you want to run it against your own sites:

The unknown row is the one people skip. Without it you cannot tell a name-based blocklist from a generic bot heuristic, and those two have completely different consequences when you add a new crawler to your stack.

Of the 31 declared bans, I tested 7 by name. Five fired. Two did not.

PetalBot and GoogleOther are both named with Disallow: / in all 114 files. Both got a 200 and a full page. The declaration exists; nothing enforces it. If you are relying on robots.txt alone to keep either of them out, you are not keeping them out.

Then the reverse. AhrefsBot and SemrushBot are not in the list. The list has SemrushBot-SWA, which is the site-audit variant, but not the main SemrushBot. Neither name appears anywhere in the 2,756 characters. Both got a 520.

So the blocklist and the enforcement layer disagree in both directions. Things that were banned were let in. Things that were never mentioned were shut out. Whatever is generating the file and whatever is enforcing it at runtime are not reading from the same source, and nobody has reconciled them since the template was applied.

This is the same failure mode as a forecast that declares 70% confidence and delivers 62% — the declaration is not the behaviour, and the gap only shows up when you score outcomes instead of reading claims. I have been running that measurement on a set of forecasting experiments, and the method is identical: state the claim, then test it, then compare. A calibration experiment that scores stated confidence against realised outcomes makes the point faster than any audit report: the gap is usually in the places nobody instrumented.

If the goal is "do not crawl me", 403 is the correct answer. It is a 4xx: a permanent, explicit refusal. Crawlers treat it as an instruction and stop.

520 is a 5xx. To every crawler on the internet, a 5xx means "the server is broken, try again later". Google's documentation is explicit that persistent 5xx responses cause crawl rate reduction and, eventually, removal from the index. Baidu behaves the same way, with less patience.

So the sites that meant to block an AI crawler are instead telling Baidu that they are down.

I ran the Baiduspider probe three times against five domains, four seconds apart, to rule out a transient origin failure. Fifteen requests, fifteen 520s. Then I tried Baiduspider-render, the rendering variant: 520 as well.

Then I tried the sitemap. https:///sitemap-index.xml with the Baiduspider UA returned 520 too. Not the sitemap contents — the same 16-byte error line.

Whatever is producing that 520 is upstream of the routing, so it does not distinguish a homepage from a sitemap from a stylesheet. From Baidu's perspective the entire host is unreachable, and there is no diagnostic it can act on, because 520 carries no information about what went wrong.

The sites that got a clean 403 are in better shape than the ones that got a 520, and both of them are worse off than the ones that got a 200 when they should not have.

Here is the part that stings. Of the 114 sites, 113 declare a Sitemap: line in robots.txt. I fetched all 113 and checked the root element, not just the status code — a 200 that returns HTML is a much more common failure than a 404.

113 out of 113 returned real XML. 83 were , 30 were , all served as text/xml. Correct content type, correct root element, parseable, reachable with a normal user agent.

So the discovery chain was intact. The sitemap was well-formed, declared in robots.txt, and served correctly. The only thing standing between that sitemap and a search engine was a 520 that fires before routing.

That is the worst shape a technical problem can take: every component passes its own unit test, and the pipeline still delivers nothing. When I want to know what a system actually does rather than what its components claim, I go to the primary record — the way the Form D filings behind a funding number tell you more than any press summary of it. The status code is the primary record here. The robots.txt is the press release.

Three checks, in order of how often they find something:

1. Does the ban fire? Take every agent in your robots.txt and probe your homepage with that exact UA string. Compare against a Chrome control and an unknown-crawler control. Three outcomes, three different bugs: 200 means the ban is decorative, 403 means it works, 5xx means it works in the worst possible way.

2. Is anything blocked that you never declared? The unknown-crawler control catches blanket rules. Then probe your analytics tools, your SEO tools, your uptime monitor. AhrefsBot and SemrushBot were being refused on 114 sites that never named them.

3. Is your wildcard group actually restrictive? Print it. If it is one Allow: / with no Disallow, your file is a blocklist and nothing else — and every new crawler that appears next quarter will walk straight in until someone adds it by hand.

RobotFileParser alone gets you the declaration side in about ten lines. It will tell you PetalBot is banned. It will not tell you PetalBot walked in anyway, which is why the probe matters more than the parse.

First the 520. Any refusal that returns 5xx for a named crawler should be a 403 or nothing at all. A 5xx is a lie about your own uptime, and search engines bill you for it in crawl budget before they bill you in rankings.

Second the list. Search crawlers and training crawlers should not share an allow/deny list, because they have opposite economics: one brings you traffic, the other takes your content. Baiduspider, PetalBot and GoogleOther come off the list. They do not train models. Keeping them in the same block as GPTBot costs index coverage and buys nothing.

The template itself is the harder problem. A managed block that is identical across 114 files is a good default and a bad policy — it encodes one vendor's opinion about which agents matter, and it ships that opinion to every property without asking whether the property agrees. The content layer already works this way: a coin page on one of the sites carries the same header, the same analytics script and the same caveats as the other 492 entries in that sitemap, because the template decided for all of them. Every site in the network inherited 31 decisions nobody made.

I have 114 sites and 3,534 declared bans across them. Five of the seven I tested were real. The other two were furniture, and the one that mattered most was answered with a 5xx.

Half the file was a legal notice. I should probably have read the other half sooner.

16 New & Returning DC Shows That Could Be Affected by James Gunn’s Exit

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The DC Universe and the larger DC Studios are going through a seismic shift now, and this could impact several new and returning TV shows. James Gunn and Peter Safran are out at DC, with the duo no longer overseeing their announced DCU Chapter One slate, which has released movies like Superman and Supergirl as well as shows like Lanterns and Creature Commandos.

Gunn and Safran will be replaced by former Marvel executive Jeremy Latcham, who worked with Kevin Feige right from the start of the Marvel Cinematic Universe with the release of 2008's Iron Man. Given the recent change, Latcham has yet to reveal what his vision for the DCU and the projects outside the shared universe entails. That could mean either keeping some planned returning and new DC TV shows or completely scrapping them, with shows to note including:

Creature Commandos season 2 is one of the upcoming DC shows to be among the safe releases at the studio, Gunn or not. The adult animated series' first season was well-received, standing at a near-perfect 93% critics' score on Rotten Tomatoes, and work on season 2 is well underway. As recently as October 24, Gunn confirmed that Creature Commandos season 2 would debut on HBO Max after Man of Tomorrow, so in the second half of 2027.

Mister Miracle is another adult animated DC series that should have no problem making it to the small screen in 2027. The show is based on one of the most acclaimed comics books in recent memory, with the writer of that DC Comics series, Tom King, also serving as Mister Miracle's showrunner. Additionally, DC recently confirmed the castings of The Hunger Games franchise star Josh Hutcherson as Mister Miracle and Below actress Mackenzie Davis as Big Barda, so walking back on those castings would not look good. Finally, Darkseid will make his DCU debut in the show, making it essential.

The People v. Gorilla Grodd is in slightly shakier territory than the other two previous DCU series. While it is a live-action series that has already wrapped production, and thus would not make much sense financially to scrap, Warner Bros. Discovery previously shelved a full Batgirl movie with Michael Keaton as Batman and Brendan Fraser as the villain. As such, if the new regime deems The People v. Gorilla Grodd too silly for the DC brand in live-action, there is a chance it could get scrapped. Still, given the many DCU returning characters, expect it in 2027 for now.

HBO's Lanterns not being renewed by now lit up a warning sign, given how the live-action DC show broke viewership records throughout season 1 and stands at a stellar 92% critics' score on Rotten Tomatoes. It is true that many DC fans complained about making a Green Lantern series grounded and more of a gritty detective show than a cosmic superhero adventure. Peacemaker season 3 is a DC project that fans might want to keep off their minds for the time being, as it is very unlikely to happen. Gunn himself said at the end of season 2 that there were no active plans for another season. With the creative behind the show not out of the DC Studios co-CEO job, the new regime should not bring in someone else to take over Peacemaker, a project that is so connected to Gunn's creative DNA.

The Penguin season 2 is most likely dead at DC Studios despite being loved by fans and critics alike. No significant movement was made on a possible new season with Gunn still in charge, and with Skydance now looking at franchises, to get the DCU Batman up and running, there is a strong chance Matt Reeves' long-delayed The Batman universe plans will call it a day with 2028's The Batman - Part II.

Poison Ivy was one of the most recent DC shows to be reported to enter development. Given the modest success of Clayface on the big screen so far, Poison Ivy might have to fight an uphill battle despite The Penguin being a hit at HBO, with focus likely to shift to bigger characters.

The animated Harley Quinn series starring Kaley Cuoco as the Batman character exists since before Gunn's tenure at DC. That said, its fate was not a priority for DC Studios with Gunn, and it should not be one now with Latcham, so the limbo period could be lengthy.

The show received a two-season order at Prime Video, with season 2 having debuted this year. With Matt Reeves attached as an executive producer, maybe that helps it continue.

My Adventures with Green Lantern is one of the recent DC animated series to enter development, being a spin-off to My Adventures with Superman. Speaking to ScreenRant in June, one of the showrunners of the Superman series, showrunners Jake Wyatt, claimed that the new show's release was pending definition from the Warner Bros. Discovery and Paramount merger. While development is moving along, this predicament puts it in danger.
