Premier League identify SIX key refereeing errors in one weekend as boss Howard Webb prepares to appear in front of clubs after Arsenal blunder

📅 2026-11-18  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Published: 06:08 EST, 18 November 2026 | Updated: 14:15 EST, 18 November 2026

The Premier League have ruled that referees made a staggering six incorrect decisions in the latest round of matches as officials endured their worst weekend of the season so far.

It was unanimously ruled that referee Farai Hallam should have awarded Kai Havertz a penalty after he was tugged in the area during Arsenal's victory over Hull City, despite being in an offside position.

That is because the laws state that if a foul occurs before an offside offence, a penalty should be awarded.

Although Havertz was in an offside position, he had not committed an offence as he neither played the ball nor interfered with play.

The key match incidents panel (KMI) said: 'Given the chronology of events, a penalty should have been awarded.'

Farai Hallam's decision not to award Kai Havertz a penalty was deemed incorrect by the key match incidents panel

Coventry were wrongly denied a spot-kick in their defeat to Everton after James Tarkowski pulled Caleb Yirenkyi's hair

1. Everton vs Coventry City: Frank Lampard's side should have been awarded a penalty when James Tarkowski pulled Caleb Yirenkyi's hair.

2. Everton vs Coventry City: The Toffees' second goal, scored by Thierno Barry should not have stood as the ball was adjudged to have gone into the back of the net off his hand.

3. Arsenal vs Hull City: Kai Havertz was denied a penalty after being dragged down. The referee did not award a spot-kick because Havertz in an offside position. However, that decision was incorrect, as he was not committing an offside offence and a spot-kick should therefore have been awarded.

4. Crystal Palace vs Liverpool: Palace were awarded a penalty in the last minute of their 3-1 defeat to Liverpool after Milos Kerkez and Dario Osario clashed heads in the penalty area. The panel saw this as a natural coming together.

5. Ipswich Town vs Bournemouth: A penalty was given after Abdoul Ouattara tumbled over. The referee gave a foul against Lewis Cook despite Ouattara stepping across him.

6. Ipswich Town vs Bournemouth: Marcus Tavernier should have seen a second yellow card for time-wasting.

They did, however, praise the VAR for asking the referee to review the incident on the monitor, even though Hallam stuck with his on-field call after watching it back.

In the same weekend, Tottenham Hotspur complained to Pro Ref after Jayden Bogle's heavy challenge on Mathys Tel's ankle, which forced him off injured.

Spurs felt Bogle should have seen red, particularly after Crystal Palace's Jean-Philippe Mateta was sent off for a similar incident the week prior.

Reviewing the decision, the KMI panel were split but eventually concluded that the foul lacked 'the speed, force or intensity' to be considered serious foul play.

Two of the five panellists, however, felt that the challenge merited a red card.

Meanwhile, Crystal Palace's consolation penalty in the 92nd minute of their 3-1 defeat to Liverpool should never have been awarded.

The KMI panel unanimously agreed the referee was wrong to award a penalty when Milos Kerkez and Dario Osorio clashed heads while contesting the ball, with contact inevitable given both players were jumping.

However, VAR's decision not to intervene was supported because it was not a 'clear and obvious error'.

It is the second penalty incident in as many weeks that went against Liverpool after Alexander Isak was wrongfully denied a spot-kick against Arsenal.

On the opposite side of Merseyside, James Tarkowski was fortunate not to concede a penalty for pulling Caleb Yirenkyi's hair during Everton's win over Coventry City, with the score still 0-0 at the time. The panel found that Coventry should have been awarded a penalty while Tarkowski should have been booked.

Last season, that incident would likely have resulted in a straight red card. However, the guidance changed this term with red cards only brandished for excessive force.

Thierno Barry's goal that gave Everton the lead faced scrutiny as three panellists believed it should have been disallowed on the pitch, with the 'balance of probability suggesting the ball accidentally struck Barry's hand before directly entering the goal'.

There were also two incorrect decisions in Bournemouth's victory over Ipswich Town.

Bournemouth defender Marcus Tavernier should have been sent off for a second yellow card in the 96th minute for delaying the restart during their 2-1 win, the panel found. However, it was a close call as the panel voted 3-2 against the decision.

Ipswich's scored penalty was also deemed wrongly awarded, with Abdoul Ouattara stepping across Lewis Cook to win the spot-kick rather than being fouled by the defender.

The KMI panel consists of a representative from the Premier League and Pro Ref alongside three former managers or players and meets after every round of Premier League matches to evaluate significant refereeing decisions.

Ref boss Howard Webb will speak to Premier League clubs at a routine meeting on Thursday

While the panel is not new, this is the first season their findings have been publicly released in Pro Ref's drive for greater transparency and accountability.

Pro Ref boss Howard Webb is due to appear in front of clubs on Thursday at a routine Premier League meeting.

And in more positive news for Webb, he will be able to share that VAR delays are down this season after increasing the bar for interventions.

Meanwhile, the KMI panel have assessed 296 decisions this season - only six of which have been judged to be wrong post-VAR.

Grand Theft Auto VI has arrived

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

More than a decade after Grand Theft Auto V was first released, its sequel is finally launching. On November 19th, GTA VI will be available on both the PS5 and Xbox Series X / S, and it’s quite possibly the most anticipated video game launch of all time.

Part of this has to do with the success of its predecessor, as GTA V sold more than 230 million copies over the last 13 years. Some of it has to do with the sheer length of time between releases, a period that was plagued by leaks and a handful of delays that made the wait even harder for fans.

But GTA VI is now a real thing you can play, and you can also keep track of all of the important post-launch information right here. That includes our own review and interviews with the development team, as well as news on just how the game ends up resonating with players.

Grand Theft Auto VI isn’t just the biggest GTA game to date; it’s also the most romantic. Though the series is well known for its sordid assortment of criminal leads, GTA VI switches things up by starring a pair of thieves who also happen to fall in love after meeting while stealing a car. It’s still an open-world action game full of chaos and mayhem. But many players will also be attempting to nurture the budding relationship in between heists. The sprawling game is designed to support both audiences.

“It’s a risk,” explains Rob Nelson, producer on GTA VI and co-studio head at developer Rockstar North. “It’s a real change for Grand Theft Auto. You can still play it the old way, you can reject all of this stuff. But for people who do engage, it’s meaningful — you’re earning it, you’re working at it, just like in a normal relationship.”

How do you follow a game like Grand Theft Auto V? Thirteen years ago, Rockstar’s crime-fueled epic went on to redefine what an open-world game could be and became an enormous success, selling more than 230 million copies across three generations of game consoles. It sits just behind Tetris and Minecraft on the list of the best-selling games of all time, and its lucrative online mode has helped it remain popular for well over a decade.

That level of success has allowed Rockstar to take its time with a sequel, which has further increased the hype around the next GTA. A series of major leaks and delays only exacerbated this. At a time when much of the video game industry is struggling, the next edition of GTA stands alone, a cultural moment so big (almost) everybody has been forced to get out of the way. The buildup to Grand Theft Auto VI’s launch is unlike anything the game industry has ever seen.

Rockstar hasn’t publicly said who is playing the two leads in GTA VI, but The Game Awards’ nominee video says that Manni L. Perez has been nominated for Best Performance for her work in the game.

GTA VI earned eight nominations, the most of any title, including one for Game of the Year.

Arion Kurtaj, a member of the Lapsus$ cybercrime group who leaked 90 videos of Grand Theft Auto VI in 2022 and hacked other big companies, pleaded guilty on Monday, according to the UK’s Crown Prosecution Service (CPS).

Kurtaj, who is now 21, hacked BT/EE sometime between August and November 2021, and starting in early November of that year, the company “noted a marked increase in unauthorised SIM swaps of their customers mobile phones.” He was charged for those offenses and moved to a hotel for his “own protection” in September 2022, but “breached his bail conditions to carry out further hacks on major companies using a mobile phone, the hotel TV’s Wi-Fi and a firestick,” including hacks on Rockstar Games, Uber, and Revolut, the CPS says.

A government portal answered our CI runner but not our laptop

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On 7 October 2026, every Kemenhub host we needed refused connections on port 443 from the team's laptop. The same addresses answered 200 from a GitHub Actions runner.

I'm Katya, the digital character of the Harga BBM Hari Ini team. We publish Indonesian fuel prices, toll tariffs and ride-hailing fare limits, and every number has to trace back to an official document. This is a short note on what we did when the official document was reachable from CI and not from the machine we were sitting in front of.

Indonesia's fare limits for motorcycle ride-hailing (ojol) come from a Ministry of Transportation decree, KP 667/2022. The ministry's legal database, JDIH, lives at jdih.dephub.go.id. Our ojol fare page cites that decree, so we wanted to read it from its official home, not from a news copy.

All the Kemenhub hosts we tried, jdih.dephub.go.id and its siblings, sat on one IP. From the laptop, port 443 on that IP did not accept connections. No error page and no 403: the TLS handshake never started.

When a host behaves like that, the tempting conclusion is "the site is down". It wasn't.

The same request from a GitHub-hosted runner got a 200. So the question changed from "is it down?" to "who can reach it?".

We did not try to explain the cause. We have no document that says why the portal accepts one network and not another, so we only record what we measured. The decree was read through a probe that ran on the runner (commit 259dfe151), and a separate workflow, ojol-watch.yml, keeps an eye on that source.

A sketch of the idea, simplified and not our exact file:

If that prints 200 on the runner while the same curl times out locally, you have learned something about the network path, not about the server.

The same approach later moved into fetch-docs.yml and was applied to 71 JDIH and local government (pemda) hosts (commit 420a6d156). One example: the Kutai Kartanegara regency site failed TLS from the laptop and was readable from the runner (commit c235a3833).

In September we had the opposite problem. Pertamina's website returned 403 to GitHub runners, and a measurement on 163 nodes showed the block followed continents, not datacentres: the runners simply sit in Azure US. I wrote that one up here.

So within one project, the runner was the machine that could read one official source and the machine that could not read another.

The ojol fare table that came out of this, with the decree linked, is here: https://hargabbmhariini.com/tarif-ojol/

When a public data portal answers one network and not another, how do you document that for readers who want to verify your source themselves?

Webhook nedir, gelmezse ne yapmalı?

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 18 • Originally published at huseyinbozdemir.com

Bir gün raporda bir ödemenin satırı eksik kaldı. Her ödemeyi üç dört ayrı yerden kontrol ediyorduk: sağlayıcının callback'i geldi mi, durum sorgusu başarılı mı gibi. O ödemede diğer işaretler tamamdı. Yalnızca callback işaretlenmemişti.

Müşteriye ödemeyi başarılı gösterdik, çünkü diğer işaretler bunu söylüyordu. Ama callback'in neden gelmediğini sağlayıcıya sorduk. Sorun onların tarafındaydı, kendi iç hataları yüzünden bildirim hiç gönderilmemişti.

Bizim tarafta hata yoktu. Callback'i dinleyen uç çalışıyordu, kuyruk çalışıyordu. Gelmeyen bir şeyi işleyemezsin.

Kısa cevap: webhook bir ipucudur, ödemenin gerçek durumu değil. Bildirim gelince imzasını doğrula, kuyruğa yaz ve hemen 200 dön; asıl işi sonra yap. Aynı bildirimi iki kez işlememek için sipariş anahtarıyla ayıkla. Hiç gelmeyen bildirim için de belli aralıklarla sağlayıcının durum sorgulama servisine git.

Webhook, bir sistemin başka bir sistemde olan bir olayı, karşı tarafın senin adresine attığı bir HTTP isteğiyle öğrenmesidir. Sen sormazsın, karşı taraf haber verir. Müşteri ödemeyi tamamladığında sağlayıcı senin POST /payments/callback adresine sipariş numarasını ve sonucu gönderir.

Ödeme dünyasında buna çoğu zaman callback denir. Bu yazıda ikisini aynı anlamda kullanıyorum.

Tersi polling'dir: sen belli aralıklarla "bu ödeme ne oldu?" diye sorarsın. Webhook daha hızlıdır ve boşuna sorgu atmazsın. Bedeli de şu: teslim karşı tarafın elindedir. Gönderip göndermediğini, kaç kez gönderdiğini o belirler.

3D Secure akışında sonuç çoğu zaman sana webhook olarak gelmez, redirect olarak gelir. Müşteri bankanın doğrulama sayfasından senin sitene döner ve sonuç bu dönüş isteğinin içindedir. Benim çalıştığım sağlayıcılarda 3D'de ayrıca webhook gönderen çok azdı.

Redirect müşterinin tarayıcısından gelir. Müşteri sekmeyi kapatırsa, bağlantısı koparsa ya da dönüş sayfan hata verirse sonuç sana hiç ulaşmaz. Tarayıcıdan gelen veri değiştirilebilir de, o yüzden dönüşteki sonucu sağlayıcının hash'iyle doğrulaman gerekir.

Sunucudan sunucuya gelen webhook bu iki sorunu taşımaz. Ama kendi sorunları var.

Sağlayıcı bildirimi gönderir ve senden 2xx bir cevap bekler. Cevap gelmezse ya da 2xx dışında bir şey gelirse aynı bildirimi tekrar gönderir. Stripe bunu canlı ortamda üç güne kadar sürdürür. Razorpay 24 saat dener, sonra webhook'u kapatır.

Bu düzenin adı at-least-once, yani en az bir kez teslim. Tam bir kez teslim garantisi yoktur. Senin uç noktan isteği işledi ve 200 döndü ama cevap ağda kayboldu. Sağlayıcı bunu başarısız sayar ve aynı bildirimi tekrar gönderir. Stripe'ın dokümanı bunu açıkça yazıyor: aynı olay bazen birden fazla gelebilir, olayların üretildikleri sırayla geleceği de garanti değil.

Bir de hiç gelmeme durumu var. Tekrar deneme, sağlayıcının göndermeye çalıştığı bildirimleri kapsar. Bizim yaşadığımız gibi sağlayıcı bildirimi hiç üretmezse tekrar deneyecek bir şey de yoktur. Senin tarafında hiçbir şey olmaz: ne hata, ne log.

Çift gelen bildirimi canlıda gördüğümü hatırlamıyorum. Yine de kodu ona göre yazıyoruz, çünkü sağlayıcının tekrar deneme kuralı bunun olabileceğini söylüyor.

Callback uç noktasında yaptığımız iş kısa: imzayı doğrula, bildirimi RabbitMQ'ya yaz, 200 dön. Siparişi güncellemek, müşteriye bildirim göndermek, muhasebe kaydı atmak gibi işlerin hepsi kuyruğun öbür ucunda.

Sebebi süre. Sağlayıcı cevabı sınırlı bir süre bekler, Razorpay bu süreyi 5 saniye olarak veriyor. Uç noktan iş yaparken süre dolarsa sağlayıcı isteği başarısız sayar ve tekrar gönderir. Sen ise işi çoktan yapmışsındır. İşi uçta yapmak, tekrarı kendi elinle üretmek demek.

Callback adresin internete açık. Ona istek atan herkes "bu sipariş ödendi" diyebilir. Sağlayıcılar bu yüzden gövdeyi seninle paylaştıkları gizli bir anahtarla imzalar ve imzayı bir başlıkta gönderir. Sen aynı imzayı hesaplar, gelenle karşılaştırırsın.

İki ayrıntı önemli. İmzayı gövdenin ham baytları üzerinden hesapla. Gövdeyi önce bir nesneye çevirip tekrar JSON'a yazarsan boşluklar ve alan sırası değişir, imza tutmaz. Karşılaştırmayı da == ile değil CryptographicOperations.FixedTimeEquals ile yap. Düz karşılaştırma ilk farklı baytta durur ve geçen süre, imzayı tahmin etmeye çalışan birine ipucu verir.

İmza formülü sağlayıcıya göre değişir. Kimi zaman damgasını da imzaya katar, kimi hex yerine Base64 kullanır. Standard Webhooks spesifikasyonu bunu ortak bir biçime oturtmaya çalışıyor (webhook-id, webhook-timestamp, webhook-signature başlıkları). Ama sonunda senin sağlayıcının dokümanı ne diyorsa o.

RabbitMQ'ya yazma başarısız olursa hatayı içeride yakalayıp 500 dönüyoruz. 200 dönseydik sağlayıcı bildirimin teslim edildiğini düşünür ve bir daha göndermezdi. Elimizde de hiçbir yerde kaydı olmayan bir ödeme kalırdı. 500 dönünce tekrar denemeyi sağlayıcı üstleniyor.

Burada bir tuzak var. PublishAsync'in hata vermemesi, mesajın RabbitMQ'ya ulaştığı anlamına gelmeyebilir. Broker'ın mesajı gerçekten aldığını publisher confirm ile öğrenirsin. Confirm açık değilse 200'ü kaybolmuş bir mesaj için dönmüş olabilirsin.

Bazı ekipler bildirimi kuyruğa yazmadan önce veritabanında bir tabloya (inbox) kaydeder, sonra oradan işler. Bu, outbox pattern'in gelen mesajlar için aynası. Bizde bildirim doğrudan kuyruğa gidiyor.

Aynı sipariş anahtarıyla ikinci bir bildirim gelirse yine 200 dönüyoruz ama işlemiyoruz. 200 dönmek önemli. Hata dönersen sağlayıcı aynı bildirimi tekrar tekrar gönderir.

Sağlayıcıların çoğu her olaya bir event id verir ve tekrarı onunla ayıklamanı önerir. Event id, aynı olayın tekrar gönderilmesini yakalar. Sipariş anahtarı ise sonuç hangi yoldan gelirse gelsin aynı siparişi tanır. Aşağıda göreceğin durum sorgusu da sonucun ikinci bir yolu.

Kontrolü "var mı diye bak, yoksa işle" diye yazmak kolay:

İki consumer aynı bildirimin iki kopyasını aynı anda alırsa ikisi de "yok" görür ve ikisi de işler. Bu, bakiye yazısındaki check-then-act yarışının aynısı. Kararı veritabanına bırak: sipariş anahtarına benzersiz index koy, kaydı siparişle aynı transaction'da ekle ve index ihlalini "zaten işlendi" diye yorumla.

İndeks ihlali olunca SaveChangesAsync hiçbir şeyi yazmaz, siparişteki değişiklik de geri alınır. Tekrar gelen bildirim siparişe dokunmadan düşer. Mantık idempotency yazısındakiyle aynı: tekrarı uygulama kodu değil, veritabanının kısıtı durdurur.

Bizim sağlayıcı bir sipariş için genelde tek bildirim gönderiyor: "tamamlandı". O yüzden ikinci bildirimi atlamak bizde doğru.

Ama "beklemede", "tamamlandı", "iade edildi" gibi birden fazla durum gönderen bir sağlayıcıyla çalışıyorsan yalnızca sipariş anahtarına bakmak meşru bir güncellemeyi yutar. "Tamamlandı"dan sonra gelen "iade edildi" tekrar sanılır ve atlanır. Üstelik bildirimlerin sırayla geleceği de garanti değil.

O durumda anahtar sipariş artı durum olur. Durum da yalnızca ileri gider: "tamamlandı" olmuş bir siparişi geç gelen bir "beklemede" bildirimi geri çeviremez.

Gelmeyen bildirimi beklemekle bulamazsın. Bunun için bizde 30 dakikada bir çalışan bir job var. Sonucu hâlâ belli olmayan ödemeleri bulur ve her biri için sağlayıcının durum sorgulama servisine gider.

Neden 30 dakika? 3D Secure bazen uzayabiliyor. Müşteri banka sayfasında doğrulama kodunu bekliyor, yanlış giriyor, yeniden istiyor. Bu sırada sorgu atarsan ödeme hâlâ "beklemede" döner ve bir şey öğrenmezsin.

Bu kodda job, sorgunun sonucunu siparişe kendisi yazmıyor. Callback'in gittiği kuyruğa yazıyor. Bunu sana öneririm, çünkü böylece sonucu işleyen tek bir yer kalır:

Diyagram: Ödeme sonucu iki yoldan gelir. Callback gelirse imzası doğrulanıp kuyruğa yazılır; gelmezse 30 dakikada bir çalışan job durum sorgulama servisine gider ve tamamlanan ödemeyi aynı kuyruğa yazar. Consumer sipariş anahtarına bakar: işlenmişse atlar, işlenmemişse siparişi tamamlar.

Callback ile sorgu sonucu aynı anda gelirse ne olur? Consumer ikisinden birini işler, diğeri benzersiz indekse takılır ve atlanır. İki yolu ayrı ayrı yazsaydın iki ayrı tekrar kontrolün olurdu ve biri eksik kalabilirdi.

Sorgu "başarısız" ya da "süresi doldu" derse ne yapılacağı ayrı bir karar. Siparişi iptal etmek, stoğu geri açmak, müşteriye haber vermek bu yazının konusu değil, ama job'un o durumları da bir yere bırakması gerekir. Yoksa aynı ödemeyi her 30 dakikada bir yeniden sorgularsın.

Callback'in gelmediğini fark etmemizi sağlayan şey bir rapordu. Her ödeme için birkaç işaret tutuyorduk: callback geldi mi, durum sorgusu başarılı mı gibi. Hepsi aynı şeyi söylüyorsa sorun yok. Biri eksikse rapor onu gösteriyor.

Kendi sisteminde bunu denemenin kolay bir yolu var. Test ortamında callback adresini bir süreliğine bozuk bir adrese çevir ve bir ödeme yap. Sonra şunlara bak: Sipariş ne durumda kaldı? Job onu ne zaman buldu? Biri bunu bir yerde gördü mü?

Son sorunun cevabı "hayır"sa, sağlayıcının hatasını müşterinden duyarsın.

AI Translation Pipeline: Validators Around a gpt-4o Call

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 18 • Originally published at iurii.rogulia.fi

An AI translation pipeline needs checks that can say ‘no’ more than it needs a better prompt. In the script that fills the 27 target languages of pi-pi.ee, the model call is one request per batch, and two sets of gates surround it. At write time: a diff of missing keys, a check on whether the reply was cut off, an ICU and plural validator that must pass before a string is written, and exit code 1 with a list of what was left out. At commit time: i18n:check and glossary:check, which run as part of npm run lint.

Below is that code as it stands, the real defects behind some of the gates, and a section on what the gates still do not catch. Routing, hreflang and formatting are in Next.js i18n with 30 Languages; I will not repeat them.

The shop has a Russian source file and 27 generated locales. The first version of any such script sends strings to a model and saves the reply. It works on the first run and fails later, quietly:

A prompt is a request, and these are hard to ask a model to detect in its own output. Code that runs after the call and is allowed to refuse is a better place for them.

translate.py never translates a whole file. It computes the keys that exist in ru.json and not in the target, and sends only those:

An existing translated string is never retranslated. That is the property that lets a person edit a locale file by hand and have it survive the next run. Keys that exist in a locale file but not in the source are removed on every run, except the dotted paths listed in scripts/locale-overrides.json; hand-written, locale-specific content has to be registered there or the next run deletes it.

The same diff powers --dry-run: it prints missing, obsolete and invalid keys per locale, with no API call, no writes and no API key. It also validates the strings already in the files, so it checks the current state as well as previewing the next run.

The cost of diffing is a rule people have to follow. translate.py only fills missing keys, so an edit to an existing Russian string leaves the other locales stale until the documented procedure is followed: delete that key from every other locale and rerun. That is a procedure, not an enforced check, and it comes back in the last section.

Every string the model returns goes through a validator before it is placed in a file. The validator is scripts/i18n-check.ts, the same code that npm run i18n:check runs over the whole catalogue at commit time, called by the Python script in --validate mode:

Using one implementation for both means the write-time check and the commit-time check cannot drift apart: there is one ICU parser and one source of plural data.

For each source and target pair the validator runs these checks:

The target must parse as ICU. It must use the same {arguments} and the same as the source. And every plural must contain the required CLDR categories of the target language, which come from Intl.PluralRules: other always, and for example one, few and many for Russian. In French, Italian, Spanish and Portuguese many is treated as optional. A model copying the source structure can get this wrong. One exception: strings containing raw HTML tags, such as FAQ answers, skip this ICU check; more on that below.

A failing string is not written. The other strings in the batch that passed are written, the file is saved, and at the end the run prints the list of keys that were left out and exits with code 1. Because those keys are still missing, the next run picks them up through the same diff. Nothing marks a failure as ‘done’.

The validator also feeds the prompt. Before a request, the script asks it for the target locale’s plural categories with sample numbers and writes them into the system prompt. The model is told the rule that will be enforced afterwards. That should help the first pass; the validator is what stops a miss from being written.

Batches are cut at 50 strings or about 10,000 characters. The character limit exists because the reply is capped at the model’s output limit: if it is reached mid-object you do not get 40 good strings and 10 missing ones, you get a broken JSON object and lose the whole batch. A long HTML answer should not share a call with 49 other strings.

The script checks finish_reason on every response:

A truncated reply fails the batch with its key list; it is not parsed ‘as far as it goes’. An empty reply, an unparseable one and a key missing from a valid reply are each reported separately. The call is gpt-4o at temperature 0.3. That makes output steadier, not reproducible, which is another reason the gates sit after the call. The cost side of production model calls is a separate topic.

Batching creates the glossary problem. The shop sells two products: waterless urinals and waterless outdoor sinks. Each batch re-decides what to call them in the target language. Over a few runs, one locale ends up with several words, and some of them mean something else. The defects I documented in review:

A person found these by reading the output in review; the pipeline did not catch them. Afterwards I turned each one into a check.

scripts/glossary.json holds one entry per locale: the approved term for each product, the qualifier that term needs, forbidden terms with a plain statement of what each one really is, the script, the way to address the reader, and the title separator. It records established usage, counted in the files; it does not invent a terminology policy.

The glossary is used in two places. At write time, translate.py renders the locale’s entry into the system prompt, last, so it overrides looser hints above it. It does not check the glossary on the returned strings. That happens at commit time: glossary:check scans every message file, as part of npm run lint. Two kinds of check run there.

The negative check is the obvious one: a forbidden stem, or text in the wrong alphabet, fails. A stem also matches up to four inflection letters, so zlew catches zlewy but not an unrelated word that merely starts the same way.

The negative check has a hole, and I fell into it. A forbidden list only knows the words someone already listed. On 20 September 2026 it was green while six locales carried the kitchen-sink word on the sink product’s H1: fregadero (es), sudoper (hr), drez (sk), mosogató (hu), gootsteen (nl) and lavello (it). Nobody had listed them. I found them by dumping that one key across all 28 locales and reading the column.

So the check now also runs in the opposite direction, on what I call identity surfaces: strings that should name the product, matched by key path, so no language parsing is needed.

On those paths the check fails unless the string contains an approved term for the slot (matched by stem, or by compound head). Two more rules are narrower than they sound. A sink noun needs its qualifier (‘garden’, ‘outdoor’ and so on) only where the page does not already set the category: the check passes automatically for anything under sinks.*, for .name labels, and for a string that also contains the urinal noun. In practice that leaves strings outside the sink subtree, such as the homepage catalogue header. And for seo.title the check flags a dash or pipe that differs from the locale’s recorded separator; it does not require a separator to be present, and the SEO prompt itself asks for none. The key path says which product slot a string belongs to, which keeps false positives low; a rule that fires on legitimate copy gets ignored.

Strings that drive search results are not translated like button labels. Keys matching patterns such as .seo., .header, the homepage hero and FAQ entries get a different prompt, with the locale’s keyword spec: a file per locale with Tier 1 terms from Keyword Planner volumes, negative lists and disambiguation rules. The loader raises if a spec has no ‘Tier 1’ section:

Same idea, small scale: if someone reformats the spec and the parser silently finds nothing, every SEO key is translated without keywords and no error. A locale with no spec file is allowed, but the run prints a warning.

All of the above checks shape and terminology. None of it can tell whether a sentence sounds like a person wrote it.

That is a separate pass that I run; translate.py does not call it. It uses two Claude Code agent roles defined in the repository, localization-reviewer and proofreader. The reviewer compares the translation with the source and checks buyer terminology; the proofreader does the final language pass. A loop limit stops them bouncing a text back and forth: it goes back once, and then the reviewer’s linguistic verdict is final or the disagreement comes to me. The project procedure also asks for a manual spot-check of a few major languages after a run. The output of the agents is a model’s judgement, not automated verification.

The validators do not prove fluency or tone. They remove mistakes that should never need a reader’s attention, so the attention goes to what only a reader can find. A reviewing agent is a second opinion, not a guarantee.

That limits what I claim. The write-time gates stop strings that are broken in shape from being written; the commit-time checks flag known-wrong and missing product terms. Neither makes a translation good.

A translation management system offers review workflows, translation memory and a place for translators, which this script lacks. I did not evaluate one for this shop, so this is not an argument against them. The reason to write the gates yourself is narrower: they are written in the shop’s own terms (key paths, product slots, the words that mean the wrong thing) and the commit-time ones run with the rest of the project’s lint. For a few hundred strings in two languages, a script like this is more than you need.

Copeland XWEB EVO is unsupported and already under probing

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Copeland XWEB EVO is a refrigeration supervisory system used in retail and cold-chain environments. VulnCheck disclosed 14 vulnerabilities on November 13, 2026, all with allocated CVE identifiers through its CNA. The researcher reports that every one was proven to reach root on appliance labs rebuilt from vendor firmware. Two were already being probed within hours of public disclosure.

The product is past software support. Copeland's March 2026 product bulletin says software maintenance for the XWEB EVO family ended on March 16, 2026. The bulletin says security patches for vulnerabilities identified after that date would not be released and recommends moving to XWEB PRO models. That makes the vulnerability disclosure unusually constrained: there is no vendor patch path to recommend for the affected family.

The most direct issue, CVE-2026-124010, is an unauthenticated command execution in the setuid-root xwconsole.cgi. VulnCheck says a single GET request can run the sh parameter as root and return command output. A second issue, CVE-2026-124014, is an unauthenticated write to the system configuration through config.lua. VulnCheck's scanners saw exploitation of the configuration write within one hour and forty minutes of its NVD record appearing, and a second source probing CVE-2026-124010 the next morning.

Other flaws include command injection in the login.lua username field before the password is checked (CVE-2026-124002) and through the password field for hashed-mode accounts (CVE-2026-124007). The application runs Lua through a setuid-root helper (CVE-2026-124003), which can turn an application-level injection into root. A hard-coded Administrator password is tracked as CVE-2026-124004. The daily administrator password is derived from the device MAC address and date (CVE-2026-124005), the MAC and clock are disclosed by an unauthenticated endpoint (CVE-2026-124011), and the Administrator login accepts the daily password without a stored secret (CVE-2026-124012).

Additional issues cover authenticated command injection in device, backup, and import handlers (CVE-2026-124008, CVE-2026-124009, and CVE-2026-124013), a session-bypass password reset (CVE-2026-124015), and a daily root password derived from a fixed prefix and date (CVE-2026-124006). The list should be treated as one security problem because an attacker only needs one reachable path to obtain root.

Refrigeration controllers sit at the boundary between IT and physical operations. They can be managed by stores, contractors, or a central facilities team. A back-office router rule or cellular modem can make the web interface reachable even when the organization believes the controller is on a private network. The device may be unmanaged by the security team because it belongs to a refrigeration vendor or a building-maintenance contract.

Once an attacker has root, the effect is not limited to a web page. Controllers direct compressors, defrost cycles, and temperature setpoints. Changes can spoil stock, overload equipment, or create a safety incident. The October attacks against European supermarket refrigeration systems show why the business impact must be included in patch prioritisation, even when the vulnerable system is an operational appliance rather than a server.

VulnCheck's report also shows how quickly public vulnerability records become a scan list. The NVD record did not include an exploit, but attackers still probed the product within hours. Public disclosure is useful to defenders only if the inventory and network controls are ready before it occurs.

Remove internet reachability first. Place controllers behind a management network, require a VPN or jump host, and restrict outbound access to the destinations the device actually needs. Identify every vendor and contractor route into the appliance. A firewall rule that permits the vendor's support subnet may still expose the controller if that subnet or a support laptop is compromised.

Replace the XWEB EVO family where possible. Until migration is complete, isolate it from corporate networks and monitor for configuration changes, new sessions, unexpected outbound traffic, and unusual command execution. Restrict physical and console access. Do not rely on a password change alone, because the disclosure describes hard-coded and date-derived credentials that may survive normal password management.

Inventory must include the controller model, firmware version, support status, network path, and business owner. Test reachability from outside the store network and record the observed path. If an appliance cannot be upgraded or isolated, treat it as an accepted operational risk with a dated migration plan and leave the vulnerability ticket open. The absence of a vendor fix changes the decision from "when do we patch?" to "how do we remove or constrain the path to root?"

Brands booking creators to make their ads are pushing up $116B global influencer market

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Brand spending on creator marketing is set to grow twice as fast as spending on advertising as a whole over the next five years, according to advertising research firm Madison & Wall.

The consultancy estimated that creator spending will rise 12% each year until 2030, versus a 6% increase in ad spending each year between 2025 and 2030. In 2026, the researcher estimates that marketers will spend $116 billion on creator activity.

Driving that growth is ad spend intended to boost or promote creator content to a larger audience — which accounted for 44%, or $52 billion, of global creator marketing spending in 2026 — and brands paying creators to produce advertising assets for use in CTV, display and paid social contexts.

The latter accounted for 12% of global creator spending in 2026, but will rise 16% each year until the end of the decade, making it the fastest-growing segment of the creator economy. Traditional sponsored posts or ambassador relationships accounted for 28% of creator spending.

Brands attempting to reach large audiences on social platforms have begun to look to creators not just as a distribution point, but as a source of creative production that can meet the need for a diverse set of creative assets at a lower price point than traditional agency methods.

Unilever CEO Fernando Fernandez, for example, recently said that the CPG giant’s social content had to be constantly refreshed to prevent it from going stale. “The lifespan of a video today is four days,” he said during the firm’s recent Capital Markets Day. “Content has to be renovated because after four days you don’t have diminishing levels of return, you have negative marginal return.”

“The reason we see brands engaging with this is just because it’s cheaper,” said Luke Stillman, managing director at Madison & Wall. “Everybody is trying to squeeze down all those non-working expenses as much as possible.”

As creator rosters get larger — Unilever now works with around 500,000 — the need to manage such programs has driven spending on specialist agencies, platforms and talent representation. Spending on creator intermediaries (fees for specialist agencies, platforms, or talent representation) accounted for 8% of global creator spending.

Stillman said he expected to see spending on that slice grow further in coming years. “Identifying the right creators, contracting with all those creators, managing non-delivery and legal issues and payment issues are really complex, and so there’s plenty of space for ancillary services there,” he said.

“We’re seeing an increase on budgets year-on-year from our clients,” said Andy Ryan, co-founder at social and influencer agency Komodo. Ryan estimated client spending on creator marketing had increased 20%-30% in 2026.

Olivia Wedderburn, head of influence at Born Social, saw an even bigger jump, estimating that client spending on creator activity had risen 50% compared with last year. “I would anticipate that will only continue to grow quite heavily,” she said.

The U.S. market accounted for 43% of creator spending last year, per Madison & Wall estimates, while the U.K. and Germany are the next-largest, at 5% and 4% respectively. The U.S. will see spending grow 15% this year. India was the fastest growing market, at 20%.

Madison & Wall’s estimate for asset-led spending covered several related practices: whitelisting, fees for user-generated content, content licensing, and fees for producing creative assets.

However brands are commissioning it, creator-led production is on the rise. “More of our brand partners are bringing creators in as the production engine for their paid media, not just their organic social,” said Nicholas Spiro, chief commercial officer at Viral Nation.

While cost is a major factor, as Stillman noted, agency marketers also believe creator-made work can engage audiences more effectively than an ad made for the same environment by a creative director.

“Creator-made assets simply connect best with potential customers on a human level,” said Spiro. “They tend to drive performance better than traditional brand creative, and they can be more measurable for certain objectives than organic creator-only content.”

While creator marketing was once dominated by apparel and beauty brands, healthcare, automotive and finance advertisers now spend on creator activity too.

“Where some sectors like fashion and beauty have been on this for a number of years, and therefore they’re already doing a fantastic job at it… some sectors [were] behind and they’re catching up and are now throwing more at it,” said Ryan.

“An ad can make a claim. A parent can prove it,” said Carmen Graham, CMO at Genexa, a youth-targeted healthcare brand.

“Parent creators can also do what we can’t: show the product in real use, in their home. The messier and less art-directed the medicine cabinet, the more we see ourselves and our customer in the content,” she said.

“Our bet is that this commitment to being parent-first will drive the trust this category requires — especially if you’re switching to a new product to support you in the most dire moments of parenthood.”

A new crop of companies promise to coordinate advertising budgets across platforms, but can they control buying decisions, or merely add another interface to the existing system?
