FAA official oversaw router work just before nationwide ground stop, report claims

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An official with the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) was on the ground in Arkansas when new telecommunications wires were replaced, an action that preceded Friday's national ground stop by eight minutes, according to the Washington Post.

The FAA official oversaw the work, tested it, and signed off on it, according to the Post's report. The work involved replacing a router and swapping old copper wires for new fiber-optic cables, part of a national effort by the FAA to upgrade the airline industry's communication infrastructure.

The fact that an FAA official greenlit the work complicates Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy's efforts to point fingers at L3Harris Technologies, a tech services provider and contractor tasked with updating the wires in Arkansas and leading the FAA's fiber-optic upgrades. Duffy said on Saturday that his agency is looking into whether "incompetence" by L3Harris led to the 4.5-hour ground stop.

Responding to the Post's request for comment, the FAA pointed to statements Duffy made on Tuesday in a news conference at the Capitol. During the conference, Duffy said that he wanted L3Harris to "hire better minds" for the wire replacement work and was considering bringing in new contractors to help.

An exact cause for the communications outage has not yet been confirmed, an anonymous source told the Post. Duffy told reporters on Saturday that the replaced router in Arkansas was faulty and flooded the network with over 1,000 connection attempts every few seconds, overwhelming it and making it difficult for planes in the air to communicate with air traffic controllers.

The FAA and L3Harris officials are currently studying the Arkansas router, according to the Post. That device was known as a Juniper router, the Post reported, and L3Harris has stopped installing them, though dozens have already been put in service.

Senate Republicans Seek to Again Raid States’ Coffers & Threaten EV Charging

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

FY 2027 Transportation Proposal Puts EV Charging Investments at Risk

WASHINGTON, D.C. — On Monday, Senate Republicans released an Appropriations package that proposes to take back nearly $2 billion in federal funding already allocated to states to build out electric vehicle (EV) charging infrastructure under the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act and repurpose it for general transportation uses, putting critical clean transportation investments at risk.

Republicans already clawed back roughly $500 million from the National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure (NEVI) Formula Program in the FY 2026 appropriations bill. Now, they are proposing to further divert these funds, even as all 50 states, Washington, D.C., and Puerto Rico have approved plans for their use and are working to deliver critical charging infrastructure to their communities. More than half of available NEVI funding has already been committed to projects, and the proposal threatens to upend additional awards that states continue to make to charging developers.

For example, Texas recently made an announcement of $226 million in awards that will support 321 stations. The Senate proposal would threaten Texas’s remaining NEVI funding, along with funding for states like Ohio, which has one of the largest numbers of operational NEVI-funded stations in the country.

In response, Clean Transportation for All Director Katherine García released the following statement:

“It is unacceptable for Senate Republicans to once again seek to claw back critical EV charging investments. Trump’s day-one freeze of NEVI set states back, and they are only now back on track after we successfully challenged that freeze in court. This latest proposal threatens affordable transportation for American families, clean air for communities, and states’ efforts to build the infrastructure of the future. We have successfully defended NEVI against the Trump administration’s attacks, and we will continue fighting to protect the program and ensure these investments reach communities across the country.”

The NEVI program, established through the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law in 2021, provides funding to states to build a nationwide network of EV fast-charging stations. Protecting that investment is essential to expanding charging access, supporting EV adoption, reducing dependence on volatile gasoline prices, and cutting transportation pollution.

Since the Trump administration’s unlawful freeze of the NEVI program was lifted following successful litigation by Sierra Club, its partners, and a coalition of states, hundreds of millions of dollars in NEVI funding have been committed to charging projects.

Congress must preserve funding for EV charging infrastructure while providing dedicated resources for passenger rail, public transit, and other clean transportation priorities.

Posted November 17, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Pope Leo’s Magnifica Humanitas (2026), subtitled “On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence,” has been heralded as one of the most significant and morally serious visions for how humanity ought to think about and navigate the advent of the AI era. Acknowledging “the ambiguity of tools,” which are neither morally good nor bad in and of themselves but “can cause harm when not oriented toward the good,” the Pope insists that the end toward which our aspirations aim is as important as the aspirations themselves.

For Leo, protecting and fostering the dignity of the human person must be the goal of everything we do. Contrasting two images taken from Scripture, the Pope calls us to consider how the desire that orients our ambitions changes the things we create.

In Leo’s first example, the construction of the Tower of Babel (Genesis 11:1–9) is motivated by fear and vainglory:

Fearing being scattered across the earth, they [the builders] sought to guarantee stability and power for themselves, and above all to ‘make a name’ for themselves.” Such motives cannot but end in ruin. Leo continues, “When a city is built on pride and the claim to self-sufficiency, communication breaks down, languages are confused and people no longer understand each other. The result is not unity, but dispersion. Babel thus reveals the limits of any effort that, however grandiose, arises from self-affirmation, sacrifices human dignity for efficiency and aspires to reach heaven without God’s blessing.

In his second, which comes from the Book of Nehemiah, Nehemiah returns to the city of Jerusalem after the Babylonian exile only to find it in ruins. Heartbroken, he takes in the destruction in silence. Then, before setting to work, he “convened the families, assigned each of them a section of the wall to rebuild, listened to their concerns, coordinated their efforts and addressed any opposition.”

Nehemiah, that is, kept the dignity of those around him at the center of his project rather than running headlong toward apparent solutions. In so doing, he was able to resurrect what had been lost while simultaneously creating something new. The Pope writes:

The narrative shows how the city is reborn, not through the initiative of one man, but through the shared responsibility of all: men, women, priests, artisans, heads of households and young people all play a part. It is an undertaking with God at the center, which rebuilds relationships before rebuilding with stones. Thus, ancient Jerusalem rediscovers a common language—not one of uniformity, but one of communion, namely the harmony that arises when all persons assume their own role and recognize that their strength comes from the Lord.

What makes these images apt for our current moment—and what many commentators have overlooked—is the role language plays in each. For, if the desire of those constructing the Tower of Babel was to create a single, unified language with which all could understand and be understood, so too is the aim of Artificial Intelligence to provide a kind of totalizing language capable of flattening ambiguity and rendering difference obsolete. And if Nehemiah’s success was rooted in the fact that he sought out the input of those he hoped to serve, and even (perhaps especially) those with whom he disagreed, so too must dialogue be at the foundation of technological advance.

Consider another example, this one taken from contemporary fiction, that draws out this tension inherent in language—the tension between the desire to communicate directly, if only superficially, and the desire to express how fraught and complex human experience actually is, even if that expression can only be accomplished indirectly and ambiguously.

It comes from Paul Auster’s great work of metareal fiction City of Glass (1987), in which the story’s chief antagonist Peter Stillman—is he still a man, still human, or has he been deformed by his mad pursuit of “perfectibility”?—obsesses over the duplicity of language, the way one word can mean two equal and opposite things. For instance, the word cleave “means both ‘to join together’ and ‘to break apart.’”

For Stillman, the Genesis narrative to which the Pope alluded “records not only the fall of man, but the fall of language” from clarity to opacity. Once, he conjectures, language was simple, direct, and stated exactly what it meant to say without confusion or ambiguity. There was a time when “words had not been merely appended to things . . . they revealed their essences . . . A thing and its name were interchangeable.” But after the destruction of the Tower of Babel, this was no longer true. Now words conceal as much as they reveal, and deception is built into the very nature of how we communicate.

Driven mad by the desire to use language to reunify what appears to him to be a fractured and fragmentary existence, Stillman sets out to realize a utopian future in which his model of a single, unified language makes absolute knowledge and mastery possible. (Sound familiar?)

If the fall of man also entailed a fall of language, was it not logical to assume that it would be possible to undo the fall, to reverse its effects by undoing the fall of language? . . . If man could learn to speak this original language of innocence, did it not follow that he would thereby recover a state of innocence within himself?

The trouble is that Stillman is not able to conjure up this mythical language model, and his attempts to do so inflict immense suffering on those around him.

In an ironic twist, Stillman ultimately resorts to deception himself in a vain attempt to accomplish his goal. Creating a character named Henry Dark, a fictional disciple of John Milton, he attributes his most controversial ideas to Dark and even falsifies the historical record to make it appear as though Dark had heralded his, Stillman’s, project over 300 years in advance.

What could offer a better image of the LLMs that seem so powerful and omniscient today—with their false citations and hallucinated facts—than a fictional character invented by a mad genius to dupe others into believing that his language model could solve the mysteries and riddles of the human condition? And yet, what could offer a better reason to hope? For, although Dark was invented by Stillman to serve darker ends, still his creation was—as Pope Leo recently called the craft of writing fiction—a profound “act of humanity,” one which points to the supreme dignity of its author even if he, like many of us today, is too consumed with his utopian ideas to appreciate it.

IDA Awards Nominations: ‘Concrete Land’ Lands 6, With ‘To Hold A Mountain’ Close Behind; Elizabeth Holmes Doc In The Mix

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Concrete Land, which has received little attention in the U.S. so far, surprised with a leading six nominations Tuesday as the 42nd IDA Documentary Awards announced its nominees. Sundance winner To Hold a Mountain came in close behind with five.

Concrete Land, directed by Asmahan Bkerat, centers on the the Najar family, “one of the last Afro-Palestinian Bedouin families near Amman,” who face eviction as luxury villas encroach on their land. It won the Grierson Award at the BFI London Film Festival last month but has otherwise largely gone under the radar. Along with Best Documentary Feature, it is nominated for Best Production, Best Documentary Not in the English Language, Best Editing, Best Sound Design, and Best Writing. Related Stories Documentary Oscar Contender 'To Hold A Mountain' To Open In Theaters In December Ahead Of HBO Debut Documentary IDA Documentary Awards Shortlists: 'You Can See Everything,' 'To Hold A Mountain,' 'Nuisance Bear' Make Features Cut, But What Got Snubbed?

Joining Concrete Land in the marquee Best Documentary Feature category is To Hold a Mountain, directed by Biljana Tutorov and Petar Glomazić. That film – Montenegro’s choice for the Oscar category of Best International Feature – tells the story of Gara, a remarkable woman who is determined to shield her daughter from harm while simultaneously attempting to prevent the Montenegrin government from turning beloved mountain pastures into a military training ground. Watch on Deadline

Also nominated for Best Documentary Feature: A Fox Under a Pink Moon, directed by Mehrdad Oskouei and Soraya Akhlaghi; Rehearsals for a Revolution, directed by Pegah Ahangarani, and You Can See Everything, directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim.

The latter film, the box office smash about imprisoned Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, earned three nominations overall, including Best Director and Best Original Music Score, recognizing composer Ari Balouzian. Rehearsals for a Revolution and A Fox Under a Pink Moon landed four nominations apiece.

Rehearsals for a Revolution (primarily in Farsi), A Fox Under a Pink Moon (in Farsi, Dari, and Turkish) and Concrete Land (primarily in Arabic) earned nominations for Best Documentary Not in the English Language. Curiously, To Hold a Mountain, in Montenegrin, didn’t earn a nomination in that category. Had it done so, it would have tied with Concrete Land for a leading six nominations.

Birds of War, directed by Janay Boulos and Abd Alkader Habak, scored three nominations – for Best Documentary Not in the English Language, Best Writing (for Jonathan Key, Janay Boulos, and Abd Alkader Habak), and Best Original Music Score, recognizing composers Harpal Mudhar and Darren Sng.

My Undesirable Friends: Part II – Exile, Julia Loktev’s follow up to her Oscar-shortlisted My Undesirable Friends: Part I – Last Air in Moscow, earned two nominations: for Best Director and Best Editing (recognizing Loktev and Michael Taylor). Closure, directed by Michał Marczak, and Nuisance Bear, directed by Gabriela Osio Vanden and Jack Weisman, both earned two nominations each, for Best Cinematography and Best Sound Design.

Also of note are nominations for Oscar winner Laura Poitras – for her short documentary They’re Here, co-directed with Rachel Lauren Mueller; Oscar winner Ahmir “Questlove” Thompson, nominated for Best Music Documentary for Earth, Wind & Fire (To Be Celestial vs. That’s the Weight of the World), and Oscar winner Morgan Neville, nominated for Best Music Documentary for Man on the Run. Prominent Hollywood hitmakers Baz Luhrmann and Paris Barclay earned nominations for their music documentaries – Luhrmann for EPiC: Elvis Presley in Concert, and Barclay for Billy Preston: That’s The Way God Planned It.

The 42nd IDA Documentary Awards will take place Friday, December 4, at The Ebell of Los Angeles, hosted by Tabitha Jackson, head of Film Forum and former director of the Sundance Film Festival.

In a statement, IDA board president Ina Fichman said, “I couldn’t be more excited to celebrate these meaningful and creative documentaries. It’s a challenging time for documentary makers, but also a really inspiring moment for IDA and for the field. This year we see nominees whose deeply personal stories share narratives of resistance and exile, and we see artful, innovative filmmaking that is capturing new and robust audiences. I hope many will join us on December 4th as we honor our incredible community.”

The IDA says it received a record 650 entries in all categories from 95 countries, “an increase over last year both in the total number of entries and the countries represented. This year, 32 countries are represented among the nominees.” IDA members from around the world vote to determine the winners in the Best Feature Documentary and Best Short Documentary categories (balloting began November 17 and extends to November 29). All other category winners are selected by juries representing the international documentary community.

For the First Time, Scientists Capture Molecular-Scale Images of the Poles of Bacteria

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists have captured the first molecular-resolution images of the ends of rod-shaped bacteria, revealing previously unseen structural features.

Scientists have captured the ends of living, rod-shaped bacteria at molecular resolution for the first time, exposing surface structures that were previously impossible to examine in such detail. The images reveal differences in bacterial architecture that could help researchers understand why some species respond to antibiotics differently from others.

The breakthrough, led by researchers at UCL (University College London) and published in Nature Communications, relied on an unexpected discovery: bacteria attached to a specially treated glass surface could remain standing upright, allowing scientists to examine their exposed tips, known as poles.

To keep bacterial cells from moving during imaging, the researchers coated glass with a chemical that binds to proteins on their surfaces. The technique held the bacteria securely without preventing them from growing or reproducing, overcoming a major obstacle to studying living cells at extremely high resolution.

After 20 minutes, the bacteria were generally immobilized horizontally. When left for longer (about 50 minutes), many unexpectedly stood upright, exposing the ends of their cells for direct examination.

The team used atomic force microscopy to image these surfaces. Unlike conventional light microscopes, this technology uses a probe just a few nanometers wide that gently taps across the cell, detecting its contours much like a finger reading Braille. The resulting measurements reveal structural details far smaller than those visible with ordinary light microscopy.

The images showed that bacterial poles change with cellular activity. Rapidly dividing bacteria (i.e., ones that were dividing rapidly) had fold-like defects at their ends, while less active cells generally displayed smoother, more rounded poles. These irregularities suggest that the bacterial surface may not have fully remodeled after cell division.

The researchers also examined E. coli (Escherichia coli) alongside Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii, species associated with difficult-to-treat infections. Unlike E. coli, the other two bacteria lacked the network of tiny openings (pores) observed on its surface. These structural differences may influence how the species interact with antibiotics, although their precise effects on drug susceptibility remain to be established.

Lead author Dr. Anna Scheeder, based at the London Centre for Nanotechnology at UCL, said: “Our immobilization method opens up exciting new possibilities. We can now study the armor of bacteria across different species more reliably and clearly observe nanoscale differences that were previously difficult to assess.”

Bacterial poles are closely connected to cell division, when new ends form as a bacterium splits into two daughter cells. Some antibiotics preferentially act on these regions, making their molecular structure a potential source of information for developing new treatments.

Senior author Professor Bart Hoogenboom (London Centre for Nanotechnology at UCL and UCL Physics and Astronomy) said: “Imaging the poles of bacteria is like opening a view of the back of the moon. We are looking at something that no one else has seen before. The poles are where cells divide and so contain critical information about this process. They may also be a particular target for antibiotics, with some bacteria-killing treatments preferring to attack this part of the cell.

“Our method of immobilizing the cells without affecting their ability to grow and reproduce also means we can image a much larger range of live bacteria than was previously possible. This is important as it may help us understand differences between species and why antibiotics work for some of them but not others.

“Our work is fundamental science that ultimately aims to find new leads to target drug-resistant bacteria.”

Reference: “Nanometer-resolution imaging of living bacteria across species and cellular orientations” by Anna Scheeder, Joanna Szczepaniak, Yulianna Koziy, Naomi Mara Claro De Oliveira, William Trewby, Renata Kaminska, Jeremy Brown, Colin Kleanthous and Bart W. Hoogenboom, 9 November 2026, Nature Communications. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-78975-y

The work was funded by the Wellcome Trust, the Biotechnology and Biological Sciences Research Council (BBSRC), the Rosetrees Trust, and the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (EPSRC).

Warren Buffett Just Gave Away $5.5 Billion of Berkshire Stock. He Says His Confidence in Berkshire Is "As Strong as Ever."

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The rest of his shares are set to leave his hands by 2034. Does this matter for the stock?

Warren Buffett gave away another big part of his wealth on Monday, Nov. 16. The chairman emeritus of Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA -1.02%)(BRKB -1.03%) converted 6,150 Class A shares into 9,225,000 Class B shares and donated them to four family foundations. At Monday's closing price, the gift was worth around $5.5 billion.

This gift is over three times the 2.7 million Class B shares he gave the same four foundations last Thanksgiving. Buffett also paired it with a short note to shareholders.

"You heard plenty from me in September about my confidence in Berkshire. That confidence is as strong as ever," Buffett wrote.

Berkshire's Class B shares finished at $592.85 on Monday, just under Friday's record close of $592.94. The stock is up around 18% in 2026 as I write.

Warren Buffett. Image source: Getty Images.

This year's note is much shorter than last year's, and most of it is about kindness, not business.

On Berkshire itself, Buffett just pointed back to September. That month, Berkshire announced that he had become chairman emeritus and that his son Howard Buffett had taken over as chairman.

In a letter put out with that news, Buffett wrote that he was more confident than ever in the future. In other words, Monday's gift isn't a vote against the stock.

After Monday's gift, Buffett holds 182,140 Class A shares and 1,162 Class B shares. His stake's worth around $162 billion at Monday's prices, or about 13% of Berkshire's market value.

It's all spoken for. In July, Buffett wrote that he hopes his children can "carry out the disposal of my shares" by Dec. 31, 2034. Either way, he said, the shares will reach the four foundations by then.

Showing how much the giving has picked up, Buffett's two gifts this year come to around 21.2 million Class B shares -- 12 million in July and about 9.2 million on Monday. The four family foundations got around 5.6 million Class B shares from him in two gifts in 2025. This year's total is almost four times as big.

Buffett explained the faster pace last year, saying that his children are all past normal retirement age and that he wants them to finish the work themselves. Of course, he added that the acceleration "in no way reflects any change in my views about Berkshire's prospects."

The foundations aren't built to sit on the stock. Buffett's 2006 pledge letter to Howard's foundation, for example, said there was no need to build large reserves, because new gifts would come every year. The Susan Thompson Buffett Foundation, the biggest recipient, paid around $1.5 billion in grants in 2025 alone.

It's fair to expect most of Buffett's remaining stake to be sold over time. Spread evenly through 2034, around $162 billion comes to about $20 billion of stock a year. That might seem like a lot. But Berkshire bought back around $14.4 billion of its own stock through September, including about $9.6 billion in the third quarter. Monday's 9.2 million shares also equal only about two days of normal trading in the Class B stock, based on its average volume over the last three months.

True, the buybacks might slow. Berkshire only repurchases shares when its CEO thinks the price is under the company's intrinsic value (an estimate of what the whole business is worth). And the stock is now near a record. But selling spread across gifts planned and announced years ahead arguably isn't likely to move the stock much by itself.

The price matters more. Berkshire's third-quarter operating earnings (which strip out the swings in its stock portfolio that hit net income) dropped about 6% year over year.

The largest drag was insurance underwriting, where profit slid about 43%.

But the stock now trades at about 27 times operating earnings over the past four quarters, up from about 22 times operating earnings at the end of September.

Ultimately, I don't see anything in Monday's gift or letter that should worry a Berkshire shareholder. For a new buyer, though, the record price is the bigger issue. I still think Berkshire is worth a look for the long run, but I'd be more comfortable paying under $593 for it.

7 Greatest Action Movies That Are Also Biopics, Ranked

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

It is not often thought of to associate action films with biographical dramas, but the two can intersect more often than not. There is a lineage of classic epics that are at least in part inspired by real stories, and many of them have been embellished over time. While many of these action epics are more based on religion than they are on history, which is the case with films like Ben-Hur or The Robe, they certainly kicked off an interest in using stories passed down by generations as a creative influence.

It can sometimes be hard to define a film if it does not entirely qualify for both categories. Lawrence of Arabia is a classic biopic that contains a lot of action, but might not have enough for it to be fully defined as an “action film.” Similarly, an action classic like Gladiator is loosely based on a true story, but contains too many factual inaccuracies for it to realistically be considered a biopic. Although the two definitions can be fungible, they crossover more times than some might expect.

Escape From Alcatraz was one of the greatest collaborations between Clint Eastwood and director Don Siegel, who had previously made the first Dirty Harry film together in addition to several other Westerns. The film is based on the true story of a 1962 prison escape in which three inmates on Alcatraz Island devised a scheme to break out of the notoriously secure facility. Eastwood stars as the ringleader of the group, and the supporting cast included Danny Glover in his first film role.

Escape from Alcatraz offered insights into the most mysterious prison in the United States, and attempted to depict it with more accuracy than the more sensationalist productions of films like The Rock. Although Eastwood had already built up a screen persona of someone who plays tough, morally flexible characters, Escape from Alcatraz saw him effectively mirroring a figure from the recent past, giving one of his best performances from a film that he did not also direct.

Bloodsport was widely marketed as being based on a true story, even if the facts regarding Frank Lux have been heavily debated, and in all likelihood somewhat fictionalized. There are nonetheless a number of great biopics that play fast and loose with the facts, and the inaccuracies involving Lux’s records do not prevent Bloodsport from being one of the most compelling martial-arts spectacles of all-time.

Jean-Claude Van Damme is not exactly an actor with a tremendous amount of range, but his skills as an actual martial artist made him perfect to star in this gritty thriller about a secretive fighting ring that accepts the best candidates in the world. Van Damme would go on to star in several other martial arts films, but the simplicity and unabashed brutality of Bloodsport made it a cult classic within the subgenre of late ‘80s fighting movies.

Braveheart is one of the breakaway successes of the ‘90s as it became a word-of-mouth hit in the summer of 1995 that turned into a true sensation, winning the Academy Award for Best Picture alongside a trophy for Best Director for Mel Gibson. Although the film has been heavily debated by historians for many of its perceived inaccuracies about the actual revolution by William Wallace, Braveheart certainly captures the sentiment of the Scottish rebels who were determined to be freed of English rule. Although Gibson’s only directorial credit up until that point had been the earnest drama The Man Without A Face, he certainly was able to transition into making a sweeping historical epic.

Braveheart captures the spirit of war in all of its ugliness in a successful way, as it offers maximum bloodshed without lionizing the act of violence itself; the story of the revolution begins as a simple act of revenge on the part of Wallace, and it blows up into a full-on political siege. While Gibson is by no means a subtle filmmaker and there are a number of rather obvious allusions to Christinaity, they felt less glaring back in 1995 when he had not yet reiterated his prior work.

Rescue Dawn is a curious project from Werner Herzog, who had previously made a documentary in 1997 about the same subject, Dieter Dengler, a German-American pilot who was shot down in the middle of the Vietnam War and forced to survive imprisonment and isolation. Although Herzog’s documentary had effectively captured the events of Dengler’s life and pointed to the context of why his rescue was so challenging, the narrative film is a more visceral experience that explores the minutia of being lost in the wilderness. The most important decision was casting Christian Bale to play Dengler, as at this point he had already attracted renown for his ability to physically transform.

Bale perfectly captures the spirit of a patriotic man who is hollowed-out by dispirited bleakness, and completely gives himself over to the part. Although Bale ended up getting the most attention, Rescue Dawn features an extraordinary supporting cast that includes brilliant supporting performances from Steve Zahn and Jeremy Davies.

The Revenant was based on the somewhat obscure story of Hugh Glass, a tracker in the early 19th century who was abandoned by his fellow men during a mission and left to die after his own son is killed by the deceitful con artist Fitzgerald (Tom Hardy). Given that it was an Alejandro Gonzalez Inarritu film, there is a tremendous amount of artistry relating to pain and suffering by means of the natural world; Leonardo DiCaprio completely transformed in an unrecognizable performance to play Glass, who is nearly killed by a bear before he grows up the strength to make the long journey in order to kill Fitzgerald.

The Revenant is an untraditional film for everyone involved, as it saw DiCaprio destroying his star persona to play a humbled man who calls upon ethereal strength in order to seek balance. It’s a revenge thriller that questions the very nature of vengeance, and it uses Glass’ violent odyssey as a means of exploring all the evil that exists within the American West, including the genocide of indigenious people and the destruction of nature. Rarely has a film of 156 minutes felt so relentlessly compelling, which is in large part due to the Oscar-winning cinematography of Emmanuel Lubezki, who used natural lighting to make the depiction of the Great Plains even more authentic.

Hacksaw Ridge was a comeback vehicle for Gibson after a period of Hollywood exile that briefly made it seem like he was going to be taken seriously again, at least until he started making more garbage like Flight Risk. Although it is unfortunate that Gibson seems intent on burning more bridges instead of actually seeking forgiveness, Hacksaw Ridge is a strong example of what he can do when he’s trained on telling an inherently compelling historical story that has strong moral takeaways. The film explores the plight of Desmond Doss, a drafted man in World War II who is played by Andrew Garfield. While Doss’ religious principles prevented him from touching a weapon, he agreed to serve the army as a medic, and saved countless lives in the process.

Hacksaw Ridge is well-drawn as a three-act hero’s journey arc that dedicates its first third to showing Doss’ upbringing, its second section examining the conflicts he came into with the most belligerent members of the army, and a stunning finale that includes some of the most gripping World War II combat footage ever brought to the screen. Although the film is stunningly violent in a way that does not shy away from the sheer brutality of wartime activity within the Pacific Theater, Garfield gives a deeply sensitive and moving performance that embodies the messianic themes at play. The entire cast is quite strong, with Hugo Weaving in particular giving an unforgettable performance as Doss’ father.

Napoleon is an interesting historical epic that was many years in the making, given that there have been many attempts to bring to life one of the most famous military leaders of all-time. Ridley Scott obviously has a vested interest in telling grandiose historical stories, and Napoleon allowed him to focus solely on the career of one man in a way that he wasn’t able to with more ensemble-based epics like Kingdom of Heaven and The Last Duel. The surprise of Napoleon is that Scott was more interested in the perception of a historical icon than nailing all of the facts; Joaquin Phoenix does not look or sound anything like the real Napoleon (and doesn’t even attempt to do a French accent), but the film still works as a harrowing character study.

Napoleon explores the extent of ego, as it uses the framework of a “great man” biopic to question why such vain men seek greatness. As is often the case, Scott made a much longer director’s cut that is superior.

We’re getting to the end of it, and people are starting to wrap, and we’re giving them their flowers, and then they’re heading off, and one by one, the family is dwindling down to just the final few. The First Original Movie of the Decade To Make $500 Million Is Officially on Netflix

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Hannah covers horror, sci-fi, and games at Collider, where she writes news and features, reviews new releases, and interviews the people who make them. She’s helping build out Collider’s gaming coverage, with Pokémon as one of her specialties. If she isn’t writing about a monster, she’s probably watching one on a tape from her VHS collection.

On its second weekend in theaters, one horror movie made $6.7 million more than it had on its first. That’s the kind of jump you might expect from a movie that opened in a few cities and then went nationwide, but Curry Barker’s horror movie was already in wide release. Its first weekend saw $17.2 million in earnings, followed by $23.9 million the following weekend, a 39% increase. The entire movie was filmed on a $750,000 budget, so even numbers from the first two weekends indicated an incredibly successful directorial debut for Barker.

What's most impressive is that Barker had a truly unique idea to sell with Obsession. A guy named Bear (Michael Johnston) breaks a trinket called the One Wish Willow and asks it to make his friend Nikki (Inde Navarrate) love him more than anything in the world. If you’ve ever spent five minutes trying to explain a horror movie to somebody without giving it away, you can probably see the advantage of having a setup that fits in a sentence.

As of November 17, Obsession is streaming on Netflix in the U.S. Its arrival on wide streaming comes with plenty of anticipation after it became Hollywood's first original movie of the 2020s to gross $500 million worldwide, a milestone the movie reached in August. Obsession became the fifth horror movie to gross $500 million worldwide, joining It (2017), The Sixth Sense, I Am Legend, and World War Z.

Barker told The Playlist that Netflix offered to buy the movie after its 2025 Toronto International Film Festival premiere and put it straight on streaming. However, Curry ultimately went with Focus Features, who acquired Obsession and released it theatrically in May. The movie eventually made more worldwide than any Conjuring movie, then went to Peacock for its first subscription streaming run. With Obsession finally reaching Netflix, it has already become the kind of hit a studio can spend years trying desperately to pull out of an established franchise.

The wish is what gets people in the door, but Inde Navarrette is the reason it works for a whole movie. Bear (Michael Johnston) already knows Nikki well enough to tell when she’s acting like herself, which makes him a lousy person to trust when he decides things are going fine. He wanted her to love him, so every time she gives him another reason to be scared, she’s also giving him a reason to stay. There’s an obvious question in the title about how far Wish Nikki will go once the wish takes hold, but it could also be said that the title actually represents Bear's obsession with Nikki and unwillingness to let Nikki go. After all, Bear is the true villain of the movie.

Obsession is available to stream on Netflix.
