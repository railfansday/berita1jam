Gloating Emails To A Newspaper Solved The Arson Spree That Hit 125 SUVs In One Night

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In the early morning hours of August 22, 2003, in West Covina, California, vandals damaged one building and 125 SUVs across four dealerships and a private neighborhood with paint nd destroyed others with firebombs. The vandals spray-painted messages like "I love POLLUTION" and "SUV = TERRORISM". Most of all, they repeatedly painted the acronym "ELF," linking them to the Earth Liberation Front, a leaderless, loosely organized activist group. ELF targeted property and managed to avoid causing serious physical harm to anyone in hundreds of credited attacks from the late 1990s through the 2000s. The government branded ELF a terrorist group, partly because ELF used destructive tactics like arson, which is a common tool of militant groups. And, in fact, multiple SUVs were set on fire that night in 2003.

Either way, the government knew they were looking for passionate environmentalists. Within just a couple of weeks, the feds arrested a suspect, Josh Connole. Connole was a peace activist, vegetarian, and solar panel sales rep. He drove an EV and lived at a commune. All of those things seemed to make him a perfect fit for a particular kind of profile.

But Connole wasn't the guy. After his arrest was announced, a reporter at the LA Times received the first of multiple emails from "Tony Marsden" that read, "The police have the wrong man here. I was amongst those responsible for the SUV attacks."

Was the sender concerned about an innocent man paying for their crimes? Maybe, but some gloating was involved, too. "We left plenty of clues, none of them pointing to Josh," the message said, then added smugly, "The FBI hasn't seemed to pick up on any of them, which makes the whole ordeal very boring for us, the true culprits." To prove his involvement, he noted that he had spray-painted an equation by Leonhard Euler, an 18th-century Swiss mathematician, on one of the SUVs. That was a detail that hadn't been released to the public.

His boredom wouldn't last long, as the emails would be traced to his college, Caltech. Apparently, he couldn't be bothered to send the emails from another location. Law enforcement seized multiple computers from the school and found a cache of the email messages on one of the machines, sent right after the sender checked his own messages. The actual sender of the email, as you might have guessed, was not named "Tony Marsden." His name was William "Billy" Cottrell, and he was going for his PhD in theoretical physics at Caltech.

If Billy thought that he could successfully deny sending the emails or his involvement in the spree, that wouldn't last long. Investigators soon matched his DNA to a headband found at the scene of the crime. For Billy, the jig was up.

At first, Billy told investigators he wouldn't implicate anyone else in the spree, but later blamed the arson part of it entirely on his friends, Tyler Johnson and Michie Oe. According to Billy, whose statements are a little self-serving and should probably be taken with a grain of salt, Tyler and Michie showed up that night and convinced him to go spray-paint some SUVs with them. Billy wouldn't have seen this as an odd request, since he and Tyler had recently planned to plaster a couple of hundred SUVs with bumper stickers that said, "My SUV supports terrorism," though that plan fell through.

The three hit one dealership, then another before moving on to a private neighborhood where they vandalized some more SUVs. It was in that neighborhood where Tyler, according to Billy, set the first SUV on fire. Billy claimed that Tyler had never told him about plans to set anything on fire, and he objected when Tyler used the first firebomb. Supposedly, Tyler said it wouldn't happen again. But Tyler didn't stop, and more SUVs were set ablaze at the last dealership hit that night. In fact, a Molotov cocktail accidentally hit Billy's clothes, setting them on fire. He quickly took off his shirt, which is how his headband wound up on the ground for investigators to find.

A few emails later, police had Billy in handcuffs. Johnson and Oe were later named as unindicted co-conspirators, but had fled the country before they could be arrested.

Billy's defense team had few options. They had to draw a line between spray painting, which wasn't a federal crime, and arson, which, in this context, it was. But Billy's attorney came up with an additional defense — Asperger syndrome. Asperger syndrome is a diagnostic term, since dropped by the World Health Organization, for a form of Autism that does not include intellectual or language impairment. His attorney hired a medical professional who later diagnosed Billy with the condition.

The defense claimed that his diagnosed condition kept Billy from picking up on social cues that would have told him that Tyler was lying about stopping at just one firebombing. When it came to figuring out other people's motives, as reported by Los Angeles Magazine, his lawyer asserted that Billy's brain was like "'a slow computer' with a dial-up Internet connection." That seems like an odd way to describe the brain of a doctoral candidate researching theoretical physics. Still, Billy did fit the common, if ill-informed, stereotypes of adults with Autism spectrum disorder with high intelligence. Why didn't he bother to drive to another location to send those emails? He had to get back to his theoretical physics work.

It could be considered a questionable defense strategy, given the estimated 5 million U.S. adults with Autism Spectrum Disorder and most of them, we assume, don't go around vandalizing SUVs. The judge would rule that Asperger's couldn't be used as a defense, but that ruling didn't come until the prosecution had almost rested its case.

A jury found Billy guilty of seven counts of arson and conspiracy to commit arson. The judge sentenced him to over eight years, which included three years added because of the supposed terrorism aspect of the crime. He was also ordered to pay $3.5 million in restitution.

Billy didn't do well in prison, even getting into a fight. It seems he tried to change the channel on the TV to the news when the other inmates wanted to watch "I Love Lucy". His ordeal prompted one of his former professors to write a letter detailing the emotional effects prison conditions were having on Billy. The professor got several prominent physicists, including Stephen Hawking, to sign the letter. It turns out that Billy's case had prompted an intense interest in the scientific community, with many expressing the view that imprisoning someone of his intelligence was depriving the world of the theoretical advancements he could be making. The letter worked, and Billy would be transferred to a different prison to serve out the rest of his sentence.

Eventually, the arson counts were thrown out, and Billy's entire sentence was vacated on appeal because his Asperger's diagnosis wasn't allowed to be used as a defense. The appellate court kept his conspiracy conviction, though, and sent the case back to the trial judge, who resentenced Billy to eight years for the remaining charge, meaning he would have to serve the same amount of time.

Billy was finally released in 2011. After he got out, he finished his PhD in 2017 at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, then held postdoctoral positions at the University of Amsterdam and Stanford University. He would go on to work at Jump Trading and teach a course on cryptocurrencies at the University of Chicago. Overall, things didn't turn out too bad for him. After all, the government has vowed to seek harsh penalties for this kind of crime, and the current administration has even threatened to send perpetrators of similar crimes to El Salvador.

What about his friends? Tyler Johnson fled to Europe and eventually wound up on the island of Corsica. He got by working odd jobs and even founded a nonprofit that supplied solar-powered ovens to households in impoverished communities. He also liked to hike the mountains, which would lead to his tragic death in 2010 in an avalanche. A documentary entitled "Finding Tyler" would be filmed about him after his death.

As far as we can tell, Michie Oe has never been caught. Her name doesn't appear in any law enforcement arrest databases we can search. Whether Billy actually participated in the arson that night or was only a willing participant in the spray painting is anyone's guess. But so far, he's the only one who has paid the price for what happened.

THR Visionary: 5 Janusz Kaminski Shots

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The cinematographer, this year’s THR Visionary Award honoree, always lets film follow function, choosing a visual style to match the story being told.

Try to find a signature Janusz Kaminski “look” and you’ll come up empty. Yes, he likes backlighting and has a penchant for smoke and film-noir style. But the Polish-born cameraman, winner of the 2026 THR Visionary Award for excellence in cinematography, has spent three decades making images that bend to the story rather than the other way around.

Kaminski’s guiding principle is that form follows function. With lenses, film stocks, color palettes and camera movement chosen to serve what each film needs to tell its story. The result is a body of work remarkable for its originality and visual diversity. Here are five of Kaminski’s most original visions. Steven Spielberg’s Holocaust drama was shot in stark black-and-white. The choice wasn’t nostalgic but a way of conjuring newsreels and documentary record, with a handheld immediacy that keeps the audience in the room with the horror. Kaminski’s hard, contrasty lighting, harsh shadows and drifting smoke owe a debt to German Expressionism and postwar European cinema. Against this monochrome, the lone splash of color, the little girl’s red coat, lands as one of the most devastating images in modern film. It won him his first Oscar.

For the Omaha Beach landing and the war that follows, Kaminski set out to make combat look like frontline footage rather than a Hollywood spectacle. Colors are leached out to a washed, metallic palette. He stripped protective coatings from his lenses to invite flare and a rawer, less polished image, and used a narrow shutter angle that makes explosions and bullet strikes snap with a jittery, staccato clarity. Handheld cameras and spattered lenses put the viewer on the sand with the soldiers. The look was so influential that it reshaped how war was filmed for a generation, and it earned him a second Oscar.

In Julian Schnabel’s adaptation of Jean-Dominique Bauby’s memoir, written by a man paralyzed but for one eyelid, the camera becomes that eye. Kaminski opens the film from Bauby’s first-person perspective: blurred focus, tilted frames, sudden blinks and a claustrophobic narrowness of vision that gradually widens. As Bauby’s imagination takes flight, so does the imagery. Soft, luminous pastels and sunlit memories of Paris and the seaside offer escape from the hospital room. It’s an exercise in subjective cinematography that earned Kaminski an Oscar nomination.

If Saving Private Ryan rejected glossy spectacle, War Horse embraced it on purpose. Spielberg and Kaminski drew on the sweeping, painterly look of classic Hollywood Westerns and epics, with John Ford and David Lean in mind. Anamorphic widescreen frames showcase burnished golds, glowing skies and silhouetted figures against vast landscapes, giving the First World War tale a storybook romanticism before the mud and horror close in. It’s lush, deliberately old-fashioned and entirely suited to a story told through the eyes of a horse. It was another Oscar-nominated turn.

For Spielberg’s tense thriller about the aftermath of the 1972 Olympics massacre, Kaminski looked to the gritty, paranoid cinema of the 1970s. The film has a grainy, slightly faded texture with muted, earthy colors, and frequently uses zoom lenses, a hallmark of the era’s thrillers, to create a sense of surveillance and unease. The images feel period-authentic without becoming pastiche, and their rough-edged restraint keeps the focus on the moral toll of the mission.

Almost seven years ago, while clutching a laptop, my phone, and a notebook in a sea of Tesla fans, I watched head designer Franz von Holzhausen throw a metal, baseball-sized ball at the driver’s-side window of the Cybertruck. The result was cracked glass — actually, two by the end of the presentation — and some awkward jokes and chuckling by CEO Elon Musk.

I have witnessed a number of stunning, odd, and fantastical moments at Tesla events. But the Cybertruck reveal was the most interesting — and not because of the postapocalyptic design. It was the crowd.

When the truck rolled out onto the stage, I watched attendees gasp, some in revulsion. There was mumbling (“This certainly can’t be the Cybertruck?” I heard Tesla superfans whisper). And yes, there were cheers. The momentary shock of Musk’s biggest fans was quickly replaced by support, acceptance, and finally, endorsement.

It moved like a wave over the crowd. By the time attendees were waiting in line for Cybertruck rides, the mood had shifted. Everyone was a fan.

That fandom carried over to Cybertruck reservations, which skyrocketed to 1 million, Musk said during a third-quarter earnings call in 2023. But it never made the leap to actual sales. Industry experts estimate about 71,000 Cybertrucks have been sold since the first deliveries began in November 2023.

And now the Cybertruck is headed to the scrap pile. Tesla will stop making the Cybertruck next year, Musk said Friday.

Was the vehicle too edgy? Maybe, but this was always going to be a niche market. I believe more practical reasons dampened sales. Musk promised the Cybertruck would have a 500-mile range and a price tag under $40,000. It achieved neither, senior reporter Sean O’Kane noted in his report.

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time Thankfully Resists FromSoftware’s Influence

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ocarina of Time sticks to the basics, and the remake is better for it

This year in video games, I’ve swung a digital sword more times than I can count. Thrilling action games like Onimusha: Way of the Sword and Phantom Blade Zero have gotten the feel of a sword fight down to a science, as steel clashes and sparks fly in spectacular fashion. But my favorite duels of the year aren’t in any of this year’s most razor-sharp action games; they’re all in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time.

The Switch 2 remake builds on the combat of Link’s 1998 adventure in careful but effective fashion. The Master Sword feels more powerful than ever thanks to killer sound design and a bit of visual pizazz on impact. What the remake doesn’t do, however, is change Link’s moves to give him the most widely used trick in modern action games: the parry. There’s no deflection to be found in Ocarina of Time’s remake, and that makes a seemingly simple combat system stand out in an unexpectedly refreshing way.

Compared to many modern games, Ocarina of Time’s swordplay doesn’t require much mastery from players. Aside from a charged spin attack and a leaping slash, Link’s offensive options are limited. Lock on, swing the sword, hit the enemy, and that’s basically all you need to know. Sure, you can block attacks with your shield or use one of your items to switch it up, but you’re always going to follow the same motions to take out a Deku Baba with consistent strikes.

One of my questions heading into the remake was about how much Nintendo would modernize that approach. After all, lots of remakes and remasters these days make reworked combat a priority; just this year, we’ve seen that in projects like The Witcher 3: Wild Hunt Remastered and Assassin’s Creed Black Flag Resynced. Rather than following that wave, Nintendo chose to keep Link’s offense relatively unchanged. That can take some getting used to if you play a lot of modern action games that all seem to have some version of a parry, or at least some kind of counterattack.

The quicker you acclimate to Ocarina of Time’s throwback action, though, the quicker you’ll come to appreciate its elegance. Fights aren’t meant to be long duels against evenly-matched opponents; Link is a master of his craft and can dispatch virtually any enemy in a few hits. Precision and timing are key as you patiently wait for your opening before lunging forward with a strike. The same goes for Link’s evasive maneuvers, which allow him to sidestep and backflip away from incoming attacks. It only takes a few Stalfos encounters to learn their attacks and lock down the right dodge and attack follow-up timing to take one out quickly.

In my review, I compared that to the experience of playing Punch-Out!!. The classic NES game’s staying power comes from the fact that every fight hinges on you learning each enemy’s tells and reacting to them accordingly. Every boxing match is a dance, and you have to learn your half of it. Ocarina of Time functions the same way, pushing you to hit all your marks rather than rush through a careful fight.

There are several fights in Ocarina of Time that test your ability to follow that flow, especially the late-game Iron Knuckles who hit like a truck if you fail to dodge their attacks. The best skill check, though, comes during a clash with Dark Link in the Water Temple. It’s the one time where you find yourself up against a fighter who is as efficient and precise as you, because it is you. Patience is your greatest skill in a battle that will taunt you for swinging wildly while your shadowy foe seemingly has an answer to every mistake you make. It plays out like a theatrically staged fencing match. Once you feel that, every other fight clicks into place, too.

Though it may sound a little simple, Nintendo finds the right way to make every slash feel powerful. Every sword hit is accompanied by an impactful thwack and an explosion of colorful particles. Even slashing the weakest enemies as Young Link feels spectacular, as if your blade has busted them open like a pinata. Those small touches make every hit feel important and satisfying to execute, rather than futile hits against a sponge. I can’t say the same for something like GameFreak’s summer not-quite-a-Soulslike Beast of Reincarnation, where bosses fall to death by a thousand cuts.

It may sound strange to call a combat system rooted in 1998 design fresh, but it really is in a world where so many action games I play feel like variations on a theme. Ocarina of Time may not impress modern players who look for expression in their combat, but you won’t hear anyone labeling it a Soulslike or dubbing it “parryslop.” The battles have an unmistakable texture to them, born out of a focused design philosophy that suits an equally focused swordsman. Who needs a parry when no blade can cross you?

Nintendo’s excellent Switch 2 remake recaptures the soul of a masterpiece
