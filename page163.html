Meta acquires AI coding startup Conductor, whose app runs several coding agents at once

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Meta Platforms Inc. has acquired artificial intelligence coding startup Conductor in a deal first reported today by Axios. Conductor confirmed it is joining Meta in a post on its website. Terms were not disclosed.

Conductor’s software lets a developer run several coding agents at once, each in an isolated copy of the codebase, and then review and merge what they produce. Supported agents include Anthropic PBC’s Claude Code, OpenAI Group PBC’s Codex and Space Exploration Technologies Corp.’s Cursor, and users pay for them through their own subscriptions and keys. The desktop app runs on Macs. A hosted version, Conductor Cloud, puts agents in sandboxed virtual machines where they can keep working for hours, and users can check on them from an iPhone app.

Engineers at Meta were using Conductor well before the deal. When the startup announced its Series A in March, it named Meta, Google LLC and Spotify Technology SA among the companies with dozens of engineers on the product. Conductor now says on its website that more than 100,000 people use it.

People familiar with the deal told Axios that Meta expects the Conductor team’s speed at shipping products to help it build its next generation of AI coding tools. The acquisition follows two Meta model releases last month that both pitched coding as a strength. On Oct. 15, Meta released Muse Spark Open, an open-weights version of its Muse Spark model aimed at coding and long-running agent work. Meta’s more capable Muse Wonder models stay closed inside its own Muse Code terminal and application programming interface.

The Conductor deal landed the same day OpenAI said it plans to acquire developer tools startup Pierre Computer. OpenAI had already announced plans in June to buy Ona, which runs coding agents in cloud sandboxes.

Local copies of the Mac app keep working until early 2027, when the team expects to release a new interface it has not yet described. New models and critical bug fixes stop arriving at that point, and Conductor will delete the app’s user data. The company said it has never sold Conductor user data or trained models on it, and does not plan to.

Chief Executive Charlie Holtz and Chief Technology Officer Jackson de Campos founded the company, formally Melty Inc., in 2024. Conductor raised a $22 million Series A in March from Spark Capital and Matrix. Y Combinator and the founders of Notion and Linear are also backers.

Why Opendoor Stock Jumped 13.8% Today

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Opendoor jumped as a Democratic sweep pushed oil and Treasury yields lower, but this is a rate-sensitive stock with a model I'm still skeptical of.

Opendoor Technologies (OPEN +13.79%) stock finished Wednesday, Nov. 4, 2026, up 13.8% after a Democratic sweep in the midterms set off a rally in housing stocks.

The S&P 500 (^GSPC +1.58%) and the Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC +1.44%) finished up 1.7% and 1.5%, respectively.

Democrats took both chambers of Congress on Tuesday night in a sweeping victory. The party has said that affordability is a major policy priority, including the cost of housing.

Beyond that, the results led oil prices lower as investors hoped for a quicker resolution to the Iran war now that Democrats control the legislature. That could help cool inflation, which in turn may mean long-term Treasury yields retreat -- exactly what the 10-year Treasury yield did today.

Opendoor is in the business of buying and selling homes, so the more active the housing market is, the better the company's sales. If yields move lower, mortgage rates follow suit, which typically means more buying and selling.

Opendoor also sits on a large, financed inventory. Higher rates eat into its bottom line as it is forced to pay more in interest.

The company reports Q3 results after the close on Thursday, with Wall Street looking for sales growth of about 17%. We'll see if they deliver.

Regardless, I'm still skeptical of the model long term. I'd be careful reading too much into today.

Money you invest in your 20s has about 40 years to grow, so small amounts go far. Contribute enough to your 401(k) to get the full employer match, open a Roth IRA while your tax rate is low, and invest both accounts mostly in stocks through a target-date or broad stock index fund.

Starting early can matter more than the amount you put in, since money you invest at 25 gets 10 more years to compound than money you invest at 35.

A Roth IRA suits your 20s. You pay income tax on what you put in at today’s rate, and in retirement the money and its growth come out tax-free. That trade pays off most when your tax rate is low, and on a first salary it’s likely the lowest it will be in your career.

With about 40 years until retirement, you can hold mostly stocks, which swing more than bonds but have historically grown more. If the market drops 30% when you’re 25, every contribution buys shares for 30% less while prices stay down, and you won’t need to sell for decades. Keep buying through it.

Take the steps in this order. First, get the full match, the money your employer adds when you contribute, as soon as your plan allows. Build a starter emergency fund, a small cash cushion for surprise bills, at the same time. Next, pay off any high-interest debt, such as a credit card balance. Then, if your income is under the IRS’s ceiling for Roth contributions, open a Roth IRA and contribute up to the yearly limit, $7,500. Anything past that goes back into your 401(k).

In both accounts, pick a low-cost target-date fund named for the year you plan to retire. It holds mostly stocks while that year is far off and shifts toward bonds as it nears. A broad stock index fund, which owns small slices of hundreds or thousands of companies, works too.

At a hypothetical 7% average annual return, raising your contributions from 6% to 10% of a $50,000 salary adds $2,000 a year that grows to about $400,000 over 40 years. So each time your pay goes up, save another 1% or 2% of it until you and your employer together put in 15% of your pay.

This Genius Trick Makes Perfectly Portioned Cinnamon Rolls—No Ruler or Guessing Required

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This clever little move turns your knife into a measuring guide for cinnamon rolls.

I have no objection to a shortcut in the kitchen. I’m happy to let boxed cake mix handle dessert or a great jar of pasta sauce get dinner moving. But I just can’t make peace with cinnamon rolls from a can. I want to love them. I appreciate the convenience, and I understand the appeal of having warm cinnamon rolls before the coffee is finished brewing. They just never quite satisfy that craving for a warm, deliciously gooey cinnamon roll.

Some viral upgrades make a compelling case—particularly the heavy cream bath and generous additions of butter and brown sugar. But my long-standing love for cinnamon rolls has much more to do with the soft, pillowy dough, so I’m willing to wait through a rise or two.

Having committed to that, though, I’m still interested in anything that makes the process easier. So when I saw a video of someone flipping a knife over a log of cinnamon roll dough to mark perfectly even portions, I wanted to try it. Cutting equal slices by eye can involve a surprising amount of second-guessing when you’d really just like to get them in the pan. I made our classic cinnamon roll recipe and put the trick to the test.

The clever part of this trick is that the knife becomes its own measurement. Rather than judging the thickness of each slice, you use the width of the blade to mark equal portions along the dough roll before slicing it into pieces.

Once your dough is filled and rolled into a log, gently lay the blade flat across the top at one end, with the knife running crosswise over the log. Then flip it onto its opposite flat side, moving along the dough one blade-width at a time. It looks a little like the knife is doing small somersaults, with its edge leaving a light indentation at each turn.

Continue down the log, using just enough pressure to leave visible marks. Then go back and slice through the dough at those marks.

This worked beautifully with our classic cinnamon rolls, but the same technique would work with just about any cinnamon roll recipe. The turning motion was simple, and the knife left an evenly spaced cutting guide along the dough. Once the marks were there, I could slice the rolls without measuring each one or estimating where the next cut should go.

I particularly liked being able to see all the portions before I started cutting. It made the job feel settled: The spacing was already worked out, and all I had to do was follow it. For something that takes so little effort, it’s a useful bit of precision.

This is the kind of cinnamon roll shortcut I can get behind. I still get the homemade rolls I wanted, but there’s one less thing to fuss over before the pan goes into the oven.

Crypto PACs eye 2028 “new map” as Democrats take Congress

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Crypto super PACs spent more than $176 million on the midterms, but the Democratic sweep raises the prospect of tougher industry scrutiny.

Two years after crypto money helped drive Sherrod Brown out of the Senate, roughly $64 million couldn’t keep him from returning.

Brown’s victory over Republican Jon Husted in Ohio delivered the industry its biggest electoral loss of the 2026 midterms as Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress. The sweep also puts Sen. Elizabeth Warren, one of crypto’s fiercest critics, in line to chair the Senate Banking Committee.

Crypto super PACs spent about $176 million across 100 seats during the election cycle, including primaries, according to Reuters, up from more than $130 million in 2024. Fairshake and its affiliated PACs accounted for $154.5 million across 89 races.

“The next Congress will bring a fundamentally different environment for crypto in Washington, and the industry should expect significantly more scrutiny,” Kyle Bligen, executive director at the Decentralization Research Center, told Cointelegraph.

Fairshake, meanwhile, is left with more than $67 million heading to the 2028 elections. “Newly elected and current members will have a choice: protect consumers and American jobs or fight progress,” spokesperson Geoff Vetter told Cointelegraph in an email. “We will be watching and ‘28 is a new map.”

Ohio was the industry’s biggest bet as crypto PACs put more than $64 million into keeping Senator Jon Husted in office. His opponent, former Sen. Sherrod Brown, won the special election to complete former Sen. JD Vance’s term, which ends in January 2029. He could seek a senior role on the Senate Agriculture Committee, which oversees the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC).

Brown’s comeback came two years after crypto’s biggest win in 2024, when Fairshake spent $40 million to help Bernie Moreno unseat him in Ohio’s other Senate seat. Some industry leaders treated that result as a warning to Congress. Gemini exchange co-founder Cameron Winklevoss declared on X: “This is what happens when you mess with the crypto army”.

This year, crypto spending was part of a much larger flood of outside money. Independent expenditures in the race topped $350 million, while Brown turned the crypto spending into a campaign theme, writing “I’ll be damned if crypto billionaires decide this race,” in a campaign message.

The loss in Ohio may weaken the perception that defying crypto PACs carries an electoral cost in Washington. Before the vote, a Republican crypto lobbyist told Politico that a Brown win would “neuter Fairshake’s perceived Sword of Damocles.”

Nebraska, meanwhile, offered crypto PACs a significant win, with Sen. Pete Ricketts retaining his seat after Fairshake affiliate Defend American Jobs spent $13.5 million opposing independent challenger Dan Osborn.

“As the biggest spender in the Nebraska Senate race, we’re proud to have helped Sen. Ricketts weather the waves of a hard-fought cycle,” wrote Vetter. “Fairshake played a meaningful role in winning competitive races across the country in 2026 and we will do it again in 2028.” The PAC’s other Senate wins came in Republican strongholds, including Barry Moore in Alabama and Andy Barr in Kentucky.

Other spending failed to deliver the intended results, both in primaries and in November. The Fairshake network’s roughly $10 million spent opposing Juliana Stratton in the Illinois Democratic primary failed to block her nomination. She later won the Senate seat. In Massachusetts, John Deaton lost despite roughly $700,000 in spending by crypto-backed Commonwealth Unity Fund to help elect him. Ken Paxton, who received crypto PAC backing in his successful Texas Republican runoff against John Cornyn, went on to lose to James Talarico.

The House brought another setback when digital assets subcommittee chair Bryan Steil lost to Democrat Mitchell Berman despite $1.9 million in crypto PAC funding.

The shift in congressional control could slow crypto legislation, but BitGo CEO and co-founder Mike Belshe said the election results would not alter the industry’s trajectory. “Crypto has continued to move forward under different administrations and different political majorities. The GENIUS Act is now law, stablecoin rules are moving ahead, and regulators are continuing to work on tokenization and digital asset markets.”Democratic victories could also bring crypto supporters into the Senate. Advocacy group Stand With Crypto counted 19 winning Senate candidates it classified as pro-crypto and eight as anti-crypto, with 33 of the 35 Senate races called as of 9:45 p.m. UTC on Nov. 4.

Despite the crypto PAC spending against him in Texas, Talarico voted for the Texas Bitcoin reserve as a state lawmaker and is rated by the group as strongly supportive of crypto.

For industry advocates, the next challenge is building relationships with incoming lawmakers. Jessica Martinez, US policy director at Fireblocks, told Cointelegraph that many newly elected members had limited records on crypto, making “educating members early” key to shaping the Democratic policy agenda.

10 Classic Rock Albums You Must Listen to Before You Die

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Classic rock is maybe definable as the kind of rock you're likely to hear on classic rock radio stations (unsurprisingly), but a better definition could be narrowing it down to rock released during a certain period. That period was when rock was at its most popular and culturally dominant, and arguably, during that time, rock music was also at its best, so it’s classic rock, and then it also happens to be rock that is classic (in terms of quality and historical importance).

There’s an attempt to single out some particularly great classic rock albums below, all of them worth listening to before you die. Or, less morbidly, listen to them all while you're still breathing; before the bucket’s kicked. That’s still pretty morbid. Sorry. But these albums are amazing, and enough so that you’ve probably heard most of them before, but if you haven’t, they're essential to give a spin at least once before… you know…

It feels uncool to call them Creedence Clearwater Revival instead of just “Creedence,” but either way, here's Cosmo’s Factory, which is probably the band’s best album. Creedence (yeah, let’s be cool for a bit; for fun) is entirely classic rock in sound, really being demonstrative of this particular era of rock, and so sure, Cosmo’s Factory is blunt, certainly straightforward, and pretty easy to listen to quite passively.

Sometimes it’s just what you need, though? Something loud, catchy, and very of its time, but all of those things in good ways. Cosmo’s Factory is strong enough that you might not love Creedence, or the particular kind of rock they made, but you're still likely going to, at the very least, tolerate this particular album. And if you are fond of classic rock and/or Creedence, then it’s safe to say you're going to go well beyond tolerating this, since you'll probably love it.

If there’s one flaw with Damn the Torpedoes, it’s that it starts in a way strong enough that you could argue the momentum isn't entirely made, yet that’s a nitpick, since the album after the first three tracks is still strong. It’s more just that the run from “Refugee” to “Here Comes My Girl” to “Even the Losers” is especially great, with three high-quality songs on their own all feeling perfectly sequenced, and thereby made even better.

But Damn the Torpedoes is not a long album, so once you're three tracks down, there are only six to go, with “Don't Do Me Like That” and “Louisiana Rain” among those remaining songs. Tom Petty and the Heartbreakers had a knack for pumping out punchy rock music early on, so the two albums before Damn the Torpedoes and the one following it are also great, yet it’s Damn the Torpedoes that’s the most consistent and memorable of the bunch.

Make no mistake: The Rolling Stones had a great run throughout a good chunk of the 1960s, but there’s an argument to be made that it was their 1970s run that demonstrated the band at their strongest. Sticky Fingers is especially strong, and the one being singled out here, but it wouldn’t be wise to overlook the 1960s, and then ‘80s-wise, the band was still going fairly strong there, even if their music in that decade wasn’t quite as “classic rock” in sound.

Sticky Fingers is great, and most of it holds up incredibly well over half a century later.

Anyway, Sticky Fingers was the album that helped demonstrate The Rolling Stones could well be the new kings of rock, making a great start for the whole decade, right around a time when another legendary British band had broken up, leaving the future of rock temporarily uncertain, to some extent. More on that other band later, by the way. But yes, Sticky Fingers is great, and most of it holds up incredibly well over half a century on from its initial release.

Easily one of the best double albums of all time, Electric Ladyland is probably The Jimi Hendrix Experience’s greatest album, which is a big take, even without too many albums by the band. It comes down to all those albums being phenomenal and influential, and it’s just the case that Electric Ladyland, with its size and also all the wild things it’s going for – and successfully trying to cram into – a single album.

Electric Ladyland is still such a wonderful and strange album to dive into, even today, with it coming up on nearly six decades since it first came out (safe to assume it would've been even more mind-blowing to hear back in 1968). There’s an undeniably interesting atmosphere to the album overall, and it’s surprisingly consistent, all the while saving two of the best songs until the very end: a cover of Bob Dylan’s “All Along the Watchtower” and then “Voodoo Child (Slight Return).”

This spot could’ve easily gone to Who’s Next, which has the singles and perhaps more historical/cultural importance, so consider that an honorable mention for that possibly easier pick (the one that’s slightly more straightforward, not to mention potentially simpler to praise). But Quadrophenia is even better, and maybe ever so slightly underrated, being like Electric Ladyland in the way it sustains itself for twice as long as a normal album, but then it’s also more of a concept album.

Broadly, Quadrophenia is about struggling to fit in while growing up and dealing with intense isolation, and lots of what it tackles lyrically still packs a punch. The story might not be as comprehensible as was the case with The Who’s Tommy, but it doesn’t really matter when there’s so much to feel throughout Quadrophenia, since it’s very emotional, cathartic at times, and then also a bit intense at times thanks to how full-on the introspection gets at other points.

Bruce Springsteen had a remarkable run throughout the 1970s and 1980s, since it’s that period of time that saw the release of the majority of his all-time great albums. There are albums after that era that are still pretty great, but then again, it’s also hard for anything to be close to Born to Run in terms of quality. And even compared to the other Springsteen albums of those two decades, Born to Run is special.

It does a lot to condense everything great about Springsteen into just eight largely phenomenal tracks, and while they'd all be worth mentioning, it’s especially worth mentioning the title track (an all-timer), “Thunder Road,” and “Jungleland,” the former kicking off Born to Run expertly, and the latter being one essentially perfect closing track. Everything here works, and it’s 40-ish perfect minutes of classic rock, in the end.

It says something about how great Led Zeppelin were, as a band, that you could probably put an album of theirs without “Stairway to Heaven” in a ranking like this and it would still feel right. The first half-dozen albums are all very hard to fault, but among greatness, it’s probably Led Zeppelin IV that well and truly is the greatest, and sure, “Stairway to Heaven” helps, but the other songs here are also fantastic.

Technically, this album’s untitled, but it’s easier to call it Led Zeppelin IV after the band put out three self-titled albums in the couple of years before Led Zeppelin IV. There’s also a great variety of tracks here, with some not being particularly rock-heavy, but then other parts do indeed get very energetic and loud. The slower songs make for a nice contrast, and Led Zeppelin IV transitions from song to song incredibly well, with the pacing and overall flow being impeccable.

There are arguments to be made for several other albums by The Beatles that aren’t Abbey Road to be worthy of being called the best, but it’s Abbey Road that feels like a culmination of the band’s entire run. No shade to those other albums, though, since the band was remarkably consistent and boundary-pushing, especially through the last half of the 1960s (see also Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band, the self-titled or "White" Album, Revolver, Rubber Soul, and Magical Mystery Tour, if you want to count that one).

Abbey Road has some all-time great tracks on it, and the whole thing flows incredibly well, too, especially all those short songs in the second half that make up a medley. It’s got almost everything great about The Beatles packed into one album, and is also significant for being the final one the band recorded, even if Let It Be, largely recorded earlier, was the last album of the band’s to be released (and the only one that didn’t come out in the 1960s, since it dropped in 1970).

Pink Floyd is known for making albums that you can really get lost in, and The Dark Side of the Moon is especially dense and immersive in a mostly good way. “Mostly” because this album is pretty heavy-going at times, so maybe it’s not for every mood, yet it’s for a lot of them, and it is overall about life quite broadly, helping it be a little easier to get into than some of the band’s other great (and also ambitious) rock albums.

So, it’s great within Pink Floyd’s discography, and then when judged on its own, The Dark Side of the Moon is still phenomenal and everything. It’s just such a quintessential album – rock or otherwise – that it’s hard to know how to even praise it or convince someone who’s yet to listen to it that they really need to get on it. Also, it’s probably the kind of thing almost everyone’s heard at this point, so maybe it’s an odd recommendation for present purposes. Still, uh, maybe re-listen to it before you die? Yeah, there we go. That works.

If you want to find great David Bowie songs, you can go as far back as the 1960s, since “Space Oddity” came out then and is an obvious classic, but if you want essential albums of his, it’s best to start in the 1970s. He kept going beyond that decade, of course, with his final album (Blackstar) even being a masterpiece, but the most masterful of his masterpieces is probably The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars.

And since no one has really ever done it better than Bowie, why not also call it the best classic rock album of all time? The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars is just too good, with everything being perfect to the extent that it’s hard to know what else to say. Why bother saying anything when you can just listen? Thankfully, The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars never really gets old, either.

ChatGPT Is Getting a GIF Maker Powered by OpenAI's Unannounced Images 2.6 Model

The internet may soon have an unlimited supply of AI-generated dancing cats, courtesy of ChatGPT.

OpenAI is rolling out a feature that lets users generate animated GIFs directly inside ChatGPT, according to The Verge. The feature reportedly comes powered by its new image-generation model called Images 2.6, which OpenAI has yet to formally introduce through its usual product announcements.

Users can describe an animation in plain English or upload an existing photo and ask ChatGPT to bring it to life. Once the GIF is generated, they can adjust its playback speed, inspect individual frames, make edits, and share the finished animation.

OpenAI demonstrated the capability with a simple request: “can you make a gif of a cat dancing?” The resulting interface reportedly includes controls to change animation speed and examine the GIF frame by frame.

We tried generating GIFs using ChatGPT, but the feature was not available on our accounts at the time of testing. In our interaction, the model generates a series of frames, but not the animation.

The rollout appears to be gradual, so users may have to keep asking for their dancing cats a little longer.

The GIF maker is reportedly part of a broader Images 2.6 update that promises faster image generation. The Verge reports that OpenAI is bringing the model to ChatGPT and Codex users across web, desktop, and mobile platforms.

Developers are also getting two new API models, GPT-Image-2.6 Flare and GPT-Image-2.6 Sunburst, designed for faster generation and higher-quality results, respectively. OpenAI has not yet published a standalone announcement detailing the new model's capabilities, and we could not independently test its performance.

The update follows the September release of ChatGPT Images 2.5, which introduced sharper details, more precise editing, and generation speeds up to 50% faster than its predecessor.

In our previous testing, Images 2.5 went head-to-head with Google's Nano Banana 2 across six categories, with each model winning three. OpenAI's model delivered particularly impressive illustration quality and improved on the oversharpening problems that affected earlier versions, although it still made mistakes when rendering text.

The move into GIF generation also comes months after OpenAI shut down Sora, its standalone AI video-generation app, in March. Animated GIFs are considerably simpler than the videos Sora generated, but the new feature brings a form of motion generation directly into ChatGPT without requiring a separate application.

OpenAI has not provided a specific date for when every ChatGPT account will receive GIF generation.
