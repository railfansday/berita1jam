Why Chris Pratt Was Recast In Taylor Sheridan's Sicario Meets The Terminal List Action Thriller

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Despite being the original lead of Taylor Sheridan's gritty Sicario meets The Terminal List action thriller, here's why Chris Pratt dropped out. While he initially broke through with comic roles on the likes of Parks and Recreation, Chris Pratt's movie and TV show career has seen him taking a hard right into action in recent years.

From The Tomorrow War to Mercy, it's increasingly common to see Pratt playing soldiers or cops. Pratt's Amazon franchise The Terminal List just scored a season three renewal too, alongside a prequel show. In short, the star would be perfect for a Taylor Sheridan military actioner, and that almost came to pass in 2018 when he signed on for Sheridan's thriller F.A.S.T.

This follows a former soldier recruited by the DEA to take down drug dealers in his local town, and the film was set to be Sheridan's next directorial outing following Wind River. That didn't come to pass, though, and while F.A.S.T. will finally arrive nearly a decade later in 2027, it won't be Pratt scowling on the poster whilst holding an assault rifle.

Sheridan was an actor before switching to screenwriting, after realizing his career had peaked with a supporting role on Sons of Anarchy. In a burst of creativity, Sheridan penned his Neo-Western American Frontier trilogy, consisting of Sicario, Hell or High Water and Wind River. F.A.S.T. was another script Sheridan wrote during this era, and one that Warner Bros. greenlit with a budget in the $75 million range (via Dark Horizons).

Chris Pratt soon signed on, but it soon hit a roadblock due to the surprise success of Sheridan's Yellowstone. Sheridan left the director's chair on F.A.S.T. to focus on the latter's second season, while Gavin O'Connor (The Accountant) stepped in as helmer. Ultimately, that version of the film was shelved, as Warners were concerned about making an R-rated film at that budget level.

With the movie dead (at least for the time being), Pratt moved on to other projects and Sheridan became one of the biggest names on television. After a long period away from the big screen, Sheridan resurrected F.A.S.T. as a producer, with his regular collaborator Ben Richardson (who helmed many episodes of 1883 and 1923) as the new director. The ever-busy Pratt did not return, however, and is likely a little too old to portray the role as written.

For a time, it looked like Sheridan would become the next big director of dark, R-rated thrillers and Neo-Westerns, like Walter Hill. Directing F.A.S.T. in 2019 would have been a level up for Sheridan, being a big-budget actioner with a major star. Had he actually made the film, and it proved a success, it's possible his career would have gone in a different direction.

Instead, Sheridan's Yellowstone universe proved a success, and the ever-prolific writer/producer was soon knocking out more hit shows like Lioness and Tulsa King. While he eventually directed a mid-budget movie called Those Who Wish Me Dead in 2021, his TV schedule has kept him away from the types of cinematic thrillers he made his name with.

Of course, Sheridan had no way of knowing that his decision to step away from F.A.S.T. would have such a profound impact on his career. At the time, it was a way to keep control of a TV series that meant a lot to him, but as a consequence, he became an in-demand showrunner. Who knows, had he chosen F.A.S.T. over Yellowstone, maybe Sheridan would be the one directing the upcoming Call of Duty adaptation instead of simply co-writing it; instead, Peter Berg is taking on that responsibility.

For somebody who had never written a script until his 40s, Sheridan learned fast. Sheridan quickly became known for his quotable dialogue and memorable characters, and F.A.S.T. itself won acclaim when the script did the rounds during the late 2010s. There is a glowing review of the thriller on the screenwriting blog ScriptShadow, which praises how Sheridan takes a familiar premise but approaches it from fresh angles.

The review specifically cites a scene where the main character is talking with a therapist about his wartime trauma. While F.A.S.T.'s protagonist keeps claiming he's fine, throughout his session he is flashing back to horrific combat experiences. It also sounds like the story has a lot in common with Lioness, and deals with the murkier side of warfare as the main character leads a misfit band of soldiers into a private war.

One of the downsides of Sheridan's move to television is that the lean writing style that made his films so great has fallen away. Outside of 1883, some of his shows - especially later seasons of Yellowstone - felt padded simply to fill an episode count. Part of what makes F.A.S.T. so exciting is that it will be a one-and-done action thriller, penned during the same creative run that led to Sicario and Wind River.

It's unknown if Pratt was approached about returning to F.A.S.T., but it's for the best that he moved on. The movie isn't a million miles from The Terminal List, which is also about a former soldier going beyond the law. As written, F.A.S.T.'s lead is a younger character, and the role has now gone to Brandon Sklenar.

Sklenar earned his big breakthrough playing manly hunter Spencer Dutton in 1923, and he has since appeared in hits like The Housemaid with Sydney Sweeney. F.A.S.T. will be his first major test as a leading man, and Sheridan clearly has faith in his 1923 star to pull it off. It helps that the 2027 thriller surrounds Sklenar with a supporting cast that includes screen vets like Jason Clarke, LaKeith Stanfield and Trevante Rhodes (Moonlight).

With F.A.S.T. and Call of Duty, it seems Sheridan is working his way back towards theaters. He hasn't directed a film since 2021, but if F.A.S.T. proves a hit, he might finally step back behind the viewfinder. The movie doesn't have much in the way of competition in May 2027 - though time will tell how audiences greet Mel Gibson's The Resurrection of the Christ: Part One when it arrives earlier that month.

Review: Secrets Playa Blanca Costa Mujeres (Hyatt Inclusive)

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Of note, I was in Cancun, Mexico for business meetings at another resort but wanted to evaluate both this experience for our readers as well as the overall position of Hyatt in its ever-growing Inclusives collection. My stay was almost exactly 24 hours and solo which is uncharacteristic at this destination resort. My experience will likely differ from other guests due to those factors.

Secrets Playa Blanca Costa Mujeres is an adults-only resort in the Hyatt Inclusive Collection, and I toured it on a site visit in November 2026. It sits on a stretch of white-sand beach in Costa Mujeres, 12 miles north of Cancun. Cancun International Airport is the arrival airport. I could not confirm a single official transfer time from the property page. Tour listings commonly put the drive in a 30 to 45 minute range.

Address: Carretera Punta Sam – Isla Blanca, Mz 2, Lt 10, Costa Mujeres, Zona Continental Isla Mujeres, Quintana Roo 77400, Mexico

Google Maps: Secrets Playa Blanca Costa Mujeres

Hyatt is the best in the business at high end resort arrivals. I had given my name at the security gate and by the time I reached the lobby, the door to the vehicle was opened for me, I was greeted by name and a “welcome home”, a constant refrain throughout my stay. It’s a small thing and maybe cliché but it was effective. I was given a Globalist upgrade even though rules at all-inclusives differ from the rest of the chain. As my upgrade was in a Preferred room, I was brought into a private room at the front of the lobby where check-in was done for me.

The lobby is an open-air room under an arched woven ceiling, with a direct view out to the ocean.

The resort is spread across 10 buildings with 505 keys. Of those, 221 are Preferred Club keys with an elevated experience. My building (and others like it) had just five rooms on each floor and just five floors which made it feel boutique rather than massive. Every oceanfront room is Preferred Club, and so are the first two buildings on either side.

The room views follow a simple rule by floor. Every first floor room is a swim-out, floors two through four are tropical view, and the fifth floor is ocean view or ocean front. That makes booking easy to reason through: if you want to step off your patio into the water, go low, and if you want the sea, go high.

The walkways give a sense of grandeur and space. All of the flora is local, and in a touch I loved, the service rooms are named after the plants.

There are four pools, and each one has a unique characteristic. The Jungle pool is drinks only and hidden in the middle of the resort by trees. The Social pool runs activities throughout the day at this adults-only resort for those who want to participate. There is also an infinity pool on the edge of the beach, and then the Preferred Club pool for those staying in that category. These are separate from the first floor swim-outs.

The spa (hydrotherapy is included for Preferred guests) featured an outdoor circuit that included both hot and cold plunge pools, amongst other therapeutic services.

The Preferred Club pool was the most frequented, though some images below look more vacant due to rain that rolled through during my opportunity to collect media on my short stay.

The gym is open to every guest from 6 AM to 8 PM, and it is outfitted with Technogym equipment.

The spa is open 8 AM to 8 PM with 17 treatment cabins and a beauty salon, and hydrotherapy is included. That last part is the headline. The hydrotherapy space is ridiculous, easily the standout of the entire property for me, and it is the kind of amenity that would make me pick this resort over a competitor down the beach. There are indoor and outdoor showers as well.

The men’s changing room featured private showers with an option for indoor or outdoor experiences.

The theater seats 264 people and hosts cabaret and Mayan shows. There are also pickleball courts, which I am starting to think should be required equipment at any resort that wants a younger crowd as well as padel for the European set (and growing American interest.)

Secrets is an adults-only brand, and every room here comes with what the company calls Unlimited-Luxury. For a standard guest that means unlimited dining and top-shelf drinks, Wi-Fi, daytime activities and nightly entertainment, with tips and taxes included. Add 24-hour room service, a mini-bar refreshed daily (you can ask for just about anything you want in there), and pool and beach wait service for all guests.

The Preferred Club is an upgrade, and at this resort it is a big one because it owns the oceanfront. Secrets lists personalized check-in and check-out, afternoon hot and cold hors d’oeuvres, desserts and premium liquors, access to the Preferred Club lounge and beach bar, an upgraded mini-bar, upgraded bath amenities, butler service, Bali beds at the pool and beach reserved for club guests, and exclusive dining at the Preferred Club beach restaurant. Preferred also gives club guests exclusive access to an infinity pool and puts an oversized soaking tub on the terrace of the upgraded suites (at least with the oceanfronts).

I book a lot of Hyatt’s all-inclusives through Hyatt Privé rates, and this resort is one of 388 in the collection. Privé is Hyatt’s invitation-only program for travel advisors, so you only get the benefits when an approved advisor makes the booking, and the stay still earns World of Hyatt points and elite nights.

The all-inclusive version of Privé looks a lot different from a Park Hyatt booking. As I wrote when Hyatt’s all-inclusive resorts joined Privé, there is no room upgrade and no breakfast benefit, since every meal is already included. At this property, Privé guests receive a $100 resort credit per stay, a welcome amenity and letter, hydrotherapy access, and early check-in and late check-out based on availability.

Elite status matters here more than at most all-inclusives. Under World of Hyatt’s terms, Globalists get the best room available at check-in, including rooms with club access, at the resort’s discretion. Explorists are eligible for upgrades too, but not to standard suites or club rooms. Travelers who have stayed at Secrets resorts as Globalists report being designated Preferred Club guests even when they did not land in a club building, which unlocks the lounge, pool and beach areas. This was my experience on all accounts at the resort. Late checkout is 2 PM for Explorists and 4 PM for Globalists, subject to availability at resorts. It was able to honor my request for a late check-out, I did not arrive early enough to request the early check-in. Hyatt waives resort fees on eligible paid rates (at least for now) and award nights for Globalists, and on award nights only for every other tier, and Globalists also get free parking on free nights though in 2028 Globalists will have to select this as a preference. One catch is that Suite Upgrade Awards cannot be used at Secrets resorts, so you cannot lock in a suite with one here.

Weddings are big business here. The courtyard outside the theater is the setting, with the sunset behind it, and the resort hosts lots of them. The resort disclosed its occupancy to me but it wasn’t clear if it was for public consumption. It was shockingly high especially for a Thursday in mid-November. Over the holidays or spring break all the hotels are full but this is a period where I would have expected a lull. Still, despite being extremely full, it never felt crowded.

For meetings and groups, the convention space measures 9,800 square feet and splits into five sections. The boardroom was occupied at the time so I couldn’t include images of that.

Restaurants can be booked in advance through the Hyatt Inclusive Collection app, which I wish more all-inclusives offered. Nobody wants to find out at 7 PM that the restaurant they wanted is full and through the app this can be done well in advance of a guest’s arrival. The dress code in the restaurants is resort casual, though the patio does not require slacks and closed toed shoes for men, shorts are acceptable outside.

The restaurants include: Portofino (Italian), Bordeaux (French), Himitsu (pan-Asian, with sushi and teppanyaki), Oceana (seafood in an open-air setting), Barefoot Grill (light lunch), Market Café (the international buffet, which I noted on site as the Marketplace), Seaside Grill (a poolside grill), and Coco Café. The bars on that list include Rendezvous in the lobby, Barracuda on the beach, a swim-up bar, a beach bar, and the Preferred Club beach bar and lounge bar. There’s also a Sports Bar and Lounge next to the theater.

Oceana is the seafood restaurant, set in an open-air dining room with woven pendant lamps, macrame hangings and a pebble mosaic floor. I chose this for my one dinner on property during my brief stay.

I started with the lamb croquettes, which the menu serves with yogurt and sweet chili sauce, cashews, watercress and a pickled shallot salad, and the Iberian salad with fresh mozzarella, prosciutto, rucula, toasted pistachios, balsamic and roasted peach. A crusty loaf came with the meal.

For a main I had the coconut shrimp, served with parsley orzo pasta, coconut sauce and parmesan crispy, and finished with the mango cheesecake, which the menu plates with vanilla cream and a mango compote with honey and basil.

Market Café, the international buffet I noted as the Marketplace, serves breakfast until 11:30 AM. [TO CONFIRM: lunch and dinner service at Market Café]

Seaside Grill hosts the Preferred Club à la carte breakfast. The official dining page says breakfast there is served for Preferred Club guests only. I tried a number of dishes for lunch to report to our fine audience.

Rendezvous sits at the heart of the property with 360-degree access, so you can walk up to it from any direction.

Preferred Club guests get their own bar at the beach, and the Preferred Club lounge also has an indoor lounge for when the sun gets to be too much.

My room was a Preferred Oceanfront King, a single open room with a king bed against a macrame headboard wall, rattan pendant lights, a ceiling fan, and a daybed sofa facing a marble bistro table. The balcony with jacuzzi looks straight over the beach. In the bathroom, a freestanding tub sits under a wood-slat ceiling beside a walk-in rain shower with a pebble floor and a bench. The double vanity has vessel sinks, round mirrors and a wave-textured wall, and the shower is stocked with Le Labo Bergamote 22 shampoo, conditioner and shower gel. Le Labo is a confirmed winner for me.

I also had the chance to tour a master suites, all of which are oceanfront, and they are also available as swim-outs.

Every first floor room is a swim-out, opening onto the pool.

Secrets Playa Blanca Costa Mujeres is an incredible resort that somehow felt small when you were in your building but big enough to have everything. For my stay, I used 45,000 points on a no-notice booking to offset a $1,300/night stay. I have seen rates from $560 for off-season which is without question worth it. I loved the property and I look forward to returning with more time and my wife, but the adults-only nature of the property will make it a harder stay for us to achieve.

‘Hunger Games: Sunrise On The Reaping’ $120M U.S. Opening Is Second Best Start For A Prequel After ‘Rogue One’; Lionsgate Franchise Returns To Fortune – Sunday Update

SUNDAY AM: Refresh for more…Lionsgate and Suzanne Collins accomplished what no other Harry Potter movie has pulled off at the box office: Seeing a preview/spinoff opening to north of $100M at the domestic B.O., which is exactly what their Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping did with a North American start of $120M.

In addition, Sunrise on the Reaping ranks as the second best U.S./Canada opening for a prequel after 2016’s Rogue One: A Star Wars Story which debuted to $155M.

As rich as Harry Potter was in its sequels, Warners wasn’t able to get Fantastic Beasts to shine greater than a $74.4M opening, which is what the first prequel/spinoff title opened to in 2016. Hence, the Fantastic Beast movies became industry comps (along with 2019’s Hobbs & Shaw with a $60M start) in regards to the ceilings of what a spinoff/prequel franchise could open to at the box office.

The reason why we’re banging this drum is because when Lionsgate opened The Hunger Games: Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes to a ho-hum $44.6M three years ago, it felt like the popularity of the Collins franchise had hit the bottom of the well, destined to live in a middling range for the rest of its days. Nope.

Thumbs up to Collins for getting the franchise back on track in the backstory of Woody Harrelson character Haymitch Abernathy, making the sixth novel in the 14-year old series feel like the biggest Games yet, and to Lionsgate Motion Picture Chair Adam Fogelson in the continued belief that Hunger Games could see a renaissance among its fanship. It also helped that Collins’ latest novel Sunrise on the Reaping sold way more, and was received with more warmth than Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes which debuted during Covid.

How Lionsgate got Sunrise on the Reaping to shine brighter than the sun: That teaser, which launched a year ago, was the second most-watched in the studio’s history after Michael with 109M global views in its first 24-hours. If you want to bridge the gap in a legacy franchise between old fans and a new generation, you have to honor the old with the new. As such, to make fans go bananas, it was leaked back in December that OG Hunger Games stars Jennifer Lawrence and Josh Hutcherson were reprising their characters in the prequel — a scene that fans weren’t treated to in the book. Lionsgate leaned into them and Harrelson in the marketing with appearances on The Tonight Show and Watch What Happens Live.

When Lionsgate announced the 16 individual castings, those yielded 541 million views and 35.2 million engagements, an early driver and indicator of fans excitement.

Overall, the marketing campaign for Sunrise on the Reaping generated 1 billion owned social views and 74 billion views of fan-generated content across relevant Hunger Games TikTok hashtags and 1.5 million new followers across official social channels. The 8 Grittiest Hard Sci-Fi Movies, Ranked

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

What, exactly, makes a movie "gritty" is a relatively subjective matter. For the most part, though, these are films defined by a harsh, unpolished, and realistic tone. They prioritize raw emotional potency over any sort of elegant escapism, and are often visually desaturated, grounded in their storytelling, and full of flawed characters and sharp social commentary. One genre that gets along particularly well with gritty filmmaking is science fiction, as has been demonstrated by many an incredible masterpiece over the years.

But where "soft" sci-fi (a branch of the genre that focuses on human emotions and soft sciences like sociology and political science) is typically more polished, "hard" sci-fi films (which adhere as strictly as possible to real-world physics and natural laws) are often far better-suited for gritty tones. From underappreciated modern gems like Sunshine to foundational classics like THX 1138, plenty of the greatest hard science fiction films ever made can very reasonably be described as gritty in their stories, tone, or aesthetics.

Danny Boyle has time and again proven himself to be one of the most versatile filmmakers currently working in Hollywood and in the UK, and some of his most unique works come from his collaborations with screenwriter Alex Garland. Case in point: Sunshine, which Garland was inspired to write after learning of scientific ideas regarding the heat death of the universe. Boyle and Garland worked closely with scientific advisers like particle physicist Brian Cox in order to ensure the film was as scientifically plausible as possible.

Whenever the creatives behind a hard sci-fi movie hire scientific advisers, that's how you know that you're in for a hell of a ride—and that is, indeed, the best way to describe Sunshine. But even though the movie pivots softly into the realm of far softer science fiction in its third act, it's still one of the most perfect R-rated hard sci-fi movies ever, with a uniquely gritty atmosphere coming largely from the pure, almost surreal existential dread generated by its premise.

While it's almost inarguably among the most underappreciated sci-fi movies of the 21st century so far, Duncan Jones' Moon (his directorial debut) has nevertheless only continued to grow into more and more of a cult classic as the years have passed. It was made on a modest budget and made an equally modest amount of money at the box office, but with the brilliance of Nathan Parker's script and of Sam Rockwell's lead performance, it may well be one of the best hard sci-fi films of the last 26 years.

Moon is not gritty in that it's action-packed or filled with complicated conflicts, but rather in how visceral its story about corporate exploitation and how corporations see humans as disposable really is. Add to that a grimy, desperately industrial aesthetic and the strong sense of psychological realism of Rockwell's performance, and you get one of the most psychologically intense science fiction thrillers that the big screen has ever seen.

It's not perfect, but Sebastián Cordero's Europa Report is nonetheless one of the most intense hard sci-fi movies ever made, as well as one of the most unique found footage movies of all time. Grounded in scientific realism and made to feel all the more matter-of-fact by its found-footage approach to the story, it's a low-budget masterpiece whose non-linear style truly creates a sense of immersion much akin to watching a documentary about space exploration.

To ensure realistic depictions of gravity, radiation physics, and the surface geography of the eponymous Jupiter moon, the people behind the film actually consulted NASA scientists, and it paid off in spades. But on top of feeling scientifically plausible from beginning to end, Europa Report feels unique thanks to how much of an anti-Hollywood depiction of survival in space it presents. Stripping away any traditional form of sci-fi heroism, the movie instead depicts space as a completely indifferent vacuum that kills indiscriminately and punishes any kind of error. It's a cold, unceremonious movie, and hard sci-fi fans wouldn't have it any other way.

Many of history's greatest science fiction movies—hard sci-fi certainly included—have been substantially divisive, and Claire Denis' High Life is no exception. Starring Robert Pattinson at the top of his game, it's one of the most perfect hard sci-fi movies of the past 10 years, and it was developed with the help of physicist and black hole expert Aurélien Barrau as a consultant. Visually stunning and intellectually challenging in ways that not just everyone might appreciate, it's nevertheless one of the most potently thought-provoking sci-fi films in recent memory.

It's a boldly arthouse-coded approach to the genre, one that subverts traditional science fiction tropes by turning its focus inward to the decaying bodies and psyches of its crew, rather than the cold void of space. This makes it a masterpiece that feels far more psychologically gritty than it does physically or aesthetically gritty, and that works surprisingly well under Denis' magnificent direction. It's a movie full of blood, sweat, tears, and all other sorts of bodily fluids, making it irresistibly yet unceremoniously realistic.

Before he revolutionized not only the science fiction genre, but cinema as a whole, with 1977's Star Wars, George Lucas made his feature directorial debut with the social science fiction masterpiece THX 1138. Based on Lucas' 1967 student film Electronic Labyrinth: THX 1138 4EB, the movie received mixed reviews from critics and underperformed at the box office upon release, but in the years since, it has become one of the biggest hard sci-fi cult classics of the entire 20th century.

THX 1138 crafts a totalitarian dystopian society which feels so real that it's nothing short of terrifying.

It's an alienating, deeply alienating kind of sci-fi movie, with a gritty tone that feels as soul-crushingly visceral as it does terrifyingly sterile. Without any horror elements of any sort, THX 1138 crafts a totalitarian dystopian society which feels so real that it's nothing short of terrifying. Many dystopian movies that are described as "gritty" use rusty metal, rain-slicked urban hellscapes, and literal grit in order to achieve that kind of tone, but not THX. Instead, Lucas achieves that feeling of detachment from blindingly white and overwhelmingly empty sterility.

With its having made nearly $800 million dollars at the box office worldwide, Gravity is Alfonso Cuarón's second-highest-grossing movie to date by a pretty decent margin. It's science fiction that leans far more toward the "science" part than the "fiction" part, and that's exactly what makes it one of the greatest hard science fiction movies that the world has seen in years. Led by Sandra Bullock at her very best, it's a must-see for fans of the genre.

There's a wide variety of reasons why Gravity is one of the most masterfully gritty films of its kind, not least of which is the fact that it's among the most perfectly-written hard sci-fi movies ever made. Add to that some of the most groundbreaking CGI that the film industry has ever seen, and it should be perfectly understandable how Cuarón managed to make a film that's so exhausting, so powerfully claustrophobic, and so sensorially punishing.

Shane Carruth's Primer is a cult classic that's absolutely legendary among fans of sci-fi movies made on shoestring budgets, and for good reason. Made for less than $10,000 dollars, it's nevertheless one of the best—and most fascinatingly realistic—time travel movies ever made. Carruth studied physics in order to help him make the plot plausible and the characters' technical dialogue sound as authentic as possible, and the research definitely paid off.

Indeed, Primer is one of those hard sci-fi movies that are perfect from the first scene to the last, a love letter to science fiction filmmaking that's as dedicated as possible to scientific and technological realism. That commitment to realism inevitably ends up translating in the form of pure grittiness as well, which is further boosted by the visceral aesthetics of the movie, taking full advantage of its admirably low budget.

Right after he established himself as a true Hollywood titan with 2004's Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban, Alfonso Cuarón was given a creative blank check to adapt P. D. James' 1992 novel The Children of Men. The result? 2006's Children of Men, which bombed at the box office at the time of its release, but soon started gaining more and more of a cult following. Today, it's so universally acclaimed that it's just about as mainstream of a modern sci-fi classic as they come.

Calling Children of Men the single grittiest hard sci-fi movie of all time is a take as cold as Antarctica. It's grit that you can practically feel deep in your body, present across virtually all aspects of the movie: Its dark story, its dystopian world-building, its damp and dirty visuals, and its gray cinematography. From top to bottom and from beginning to end, Children of Men serves as a masterclass in how to make an apocalyptic sci-fi film that, while undeniably gritty, also has a refreshingly hopeful and humanist core.
