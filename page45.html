Data centers are learning to be flexible

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Data centers are driving up electricity demand, but flexible power use could ease grid congestion and speed up connections

Jim West/UCG/Universal Images Group via Getty Images

A version of this article originally appeared in Quartz’s AI & Tech newsletter. Sign up here to get the latest AI & tech news, analysis and insights straight to your inbox.

Picture a soccer club that bought a passenger van for its tournaments a few times a year. The van sits in a driveway the rest of the year. Buying it was the right call, because a van that wasn’t there on tournament weekend would have stranded the team.

The American power grid works the same way, and now we have numbers to show just how little of it gets used. This month, under a new state law, Virginia’s utilities reported theirs. Over the year ending in July, the distribution grid run by Dominion $D Energy, the utility behind the northern Virginia data center boom, was being used at 41% of its capacity on average.

Even in its busiest hours, the system averaged 80%, with some pockets running higher and others lower. In the state’s rural southwest, where demand has been falling, Appalachian Power reported 14% for the year and 54% at peak.

Grids are sized for the few hours a year when everyone runs the air conditioner at once, and building for those few hours is expensive. A data center that pulls full power around the clock raises that peak, and raising the peak means buying another van that mostly sits in the driveway, costing money while it gathers dust.

Researchers argued there might be a cheaper route than building, and it required something we prize in people: Flexibility. Duke $DUK researchers calculated that the existing grid could absorb nearly 100 gigawatts of new demand if the newcomers agreed to cut back its power use for roughly half a percent of the year.

The study caused a stir in energy circles, and Google $GOOGL hired its lead author. The idea came down to a trade. A customer accepts being throttled in the few hours when the local grid is stretched, and in return connects in months instead of years.

The clearest track record of this working belongs to California’s PG&E. EV charging companies, which draw heavily on the grid, saw the wait for power fall to four to eight months from one to three years before its Flex Connect pilot.

In the pilot’s first two years, the power participants asked for was there nine hours out of ten. In the hours it wasn’t, the limits cut into operations less than 1% of the time.

Batteries offered another way to ease the pressure at peak. In 2025, a data center company in Oregon secured a faster hookup from Portland General Electric $GE by agreeing to add a 31-megawatt battery, enough to run about 25,000 homes for two hours, that it could switch to if needed.

Others flexed the computing itself. Google started by putting off work that could wait whenever grids in Belgium and Taiwan ran tight. In 2024, it dialed back machine learning jobs three times when the grid around Omaha was under strain. This year it promised five utilities it would hold back as much as a gigawatt of data center demand when the grid was stretched.

Startups are selling software that slow, pause or relocate AI jobs when the grid is tight. There were plenty of ways to do it, and more kept coming.

Flexibility is already becoming required in some regions. A 2025 Texas law requires new large data centers to install equipment that lets the grid operator cut their power remotely in emergencies. Late last year, FERC, the federal agency that regulates the grid, directed the operator PJM to create interruptible transmission options for data centers built next to power plants. The Southwest Power Pool, which runs the grid across much of the Great Plains, made a similar move.

Of course, waiting is expensive, and data center queues in popular markets can run for years. Take an Nvidia $NVDA chip that costs $30,000 and is depreciated over three to five years, or $6,000 to $10,000 a year. Picture 100,000 of them in one cluster, waiting for power the way Microsoft $MSFT’s chief executive, Satya Nadella, said some of his company’s chips were last year. A year of waiting represents $600 million to $1 billion in potential computing value that goes unused.

The trade cost something on the other side too, although it was much less. For companies racing each other to build more powerful AI, even a few curtailed hours counted. If those 100,000 chips sat idle for the equivalent of 44 hours a year, the lost computing time would be worth $3 million to $5 million.

Flexibility will not fully replace the building the aging grid needs, because old equipment has to go regardless. But utilities planned to spend at least $1.4 trillion through 2030, with data centers and load growth among their top reasons, and a large transformer could take more than two years to arrive. Flexibility could shrink that bill, and that wait.

Cost per task: Using cheap classifiers to route across Gemini models and thinking levels

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Per-token API prices keep falling, yet engineering bills for autonomous coding agents keep climbing. Price per million tokens isn't the metric that actually governs production economics anymore. The dominating metric is now cost per successful task: total spend across every loop iteration, tool call, retry and internal thinking pass divided by the number of tasks that pass verification.

When an agent calls a high-thinking reasoning configuration for every micro-decision in its harness, you're paying deep-reasoning prices for questions a lightweight configuration already answers accurately. To prevent this unnecessary overhead, consider adding a lightweight routing layer that can intercept routine decisions before they ever reach an expensive reasoning model.That is the premise behind wiring TypeSafe's Jev classifier in front of Google Antigravity's model calls. Rather than generating text token by token, Jev—a specialized structured-output classifier designed for high-throughput decision pipelines—evaluates structured classification primitives in a single forward pass: a Choice from a list, a numeric Score or a Noul, which returns a 0 to 1 probability for a binary statement.

For full disclosure, Google has no partnership with TypeSafe, and this article is not an endorsement of TypeSafe or its products. Jev is used here as one example of a lightweight classifier, and the same routing pattern can be built with other classifiers, including Google-native ones. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab, for example, runs the same kind of classifier on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, in your own Google Cloud project.

We ran a 50-question, auto-graded, coding-agent benchmark against live Gemini and Jev APIs to test whether a near-free classifier can route each task to the cheapest model that still gets it right. Here's what we learned:

The naive way to ask Jev to route a coding task is a single broad Choice question: simple or complex. When we tested that first on 16 coding-agent tasks, Jev routed nearly everything to simple, even tasks requiring an LRU cache trace or modular exponentiation. TypeSafe's documentation warns against this exact anti-pattern. If a question weighs multiple factors or requires a meta-judgment, decompose it into an atomic check.

When we replaced the broad Choice question with one atomic Noul question, it fixed the separation immediately. The question was: does answering this require performing more than one sequential calculation, simulation step or logical deduction, rather than a single direct lookup, format check or one-step classification?

On those initial 16 tasks, Jev routed 13 of 16 in agreement with our hand-labeled difficulty on the first run and 14 of 16 on the second pass. Many engineering teams ask whether they even need a classifier API when they can write a zero-cost regex and prompt-length heuristic in Python, matching words like sequence, modulo and race condition or escalating any prompt over 35 words.

On the 16 development questions used to write those exact rules, the local heuristic scored 15 of 16, or 93.8%. To test whether that heuristic survived contact with realistic coding-agent traffic, we froze its code without changing a character and added 34 unseen held-out tasks across seven coding-agent buckets: fact extraction, schema validation, short classification, single-hop code checks, state simulation traces, math or logic chains and subtle language-semantics bugs.

On the 34 held-out questions, the frozen heuristic dropped 23.2 percentage points to 70.6%. Realistic simple tasks such as extracting a port from a Kubernetes YAML manifest, a file path from a unified git diff header or a line number from a Python traceback exceeded 35 words and triggered false escalations. Conversely, concise multi-step code traces such as mutable default arguments across calls, Python dictionary key hashing on {1: "a", True: "b", 1.0: "c"} and C short-circuit evaluation lacked the heuristic's hardcoded puzzle words and were falsely routed to the simple path. Jev classified all 34 unseen tasks with 100.0% agreement, 34 of 34, reaching 48 of 50, or 96.0%, across the full suite.

A common mistake when building a fast-path router is assuming that setting a low maxOutputTokens cap on a reasoning model will make it spend less on easy tasks. Our 50-question data shows why that assumption fails.

When we compared gemini-3.8-flash with maxOutputTokens=300 against gemini-3.8-flash with maxOutputTokens=4096 while leaving thinkingConfig at the API default, both configurations spent almost identical thinking tokens on the 27 tasks Jev labeled SIMPLE:

Note: Figures reflect single-run experimental measurements on a sample test suite; actual latencies, token counts, and billed costs will vary based on prompt length, context caching, and production load.

Capping the model's output at 300 tokens didn't save any money on easy tasks. The cap is only a ceiling, and easy answers never came close to it. On the easy tasks, the capped version actually cost slightly more than the uncapped one. All the cap did was break 7 of the 25 hard tasks. On six of them, the model ran out of room while still reasoning and never gave an answer. On the seventh, it squeezed its reasoning to fit, finished on time and got the answer wrong.

So a Jev router that sends easy tasks to the capped model and hard ones to normal Flash got all 50 right. It still cost slightly more than skipping the router and using normal Flash for everything, and it was slower. If your cheap option doesn't actually make the model think less on easy tasks, no router can save you money. Not even a perfect one that costs nothing.

The obvious way to spend less on thinking is to ask the model to think less. So we ran a second round of tests with two lighter options: Flash-Lite, which doesn't think at all by default, and Gemini 3.8 Flash with its thinking level set to low. We added those results to the same results file, matched question by question.

A note on timing. Most of the router's time per task is spent in Flash-Lite and Flash calls, which we measured in the same run and time window as the plain Flash baseline, so the speed comparison between them is fair. Jev's own decision is a small share of the total and comes from the first round. Speed comparisons against default Flash or Pro mix the two rounds, so read those as approximate.

Note: Figures reflect single-run experimental measurements on a sample test suite; actual latencies, token counts, and billed costs will vary based on prompt length, context caching, and production load.

The 7-bucket breakdown shows where gemini-3.5-flash-lite and the heuristic router missed tasks and how Router Jev: Flash-Lite to 3.8 LOW recovered 100% accuracy:

The strongest setup without a router was Gemini 3.8 Flash on low thinking. It solved all 50 tasks. That's the number a router had to beat.

The Jev router beat it. It sends every task to Flash-Lite first and escalates only what Jev flags as complex. It also solved all 50 tasks, and it can help reduce both cost and response time compared with running low thinking on everything. On prompts this short the cost difference per task is small, so for an interactive agent the shorter wait is the bigger benefit.

Why it works. On its own, Flash-Lite answered every simple task in half a second but missed 6 of the 25 hard ones. Jev flagged all 6 as complex with high confidence and escalated them. That brought its cost close to what a perfect router would achieve.

The keyword heuristic was cheaper, but only because it got things wrong. It sent 5 hard tasks to Flash-Lite, and 2 of them failed, for 48 of 50.

Escalating to a heavier setting didn't pay here. Routing hard tasks to Flash on default thinking was cheaper than running default thinking everywhere. It still cost more than running low thinking on everything, which already solved all 50. A heavier escalation target only makes sense when the low setting starts failing.

Limits: one run per setup, 50 prompts with hand-assigned difficulty and simple automatic grading.

Two things in our results only show up once you look past it.

Routing and caching pull in opposite directions. Our test prompts were short, standalone tasks, and on those the Jev router helped reduce both cost and response time. Real coding agents often look different. They send the same big block of context on every turn, such as a map of the repository and the tool definitions. Agent Platform Context Caching stores that block so you pay 75% to 90% less to resend it. The catch is that a cache belongs to one model. If your agent switches between Flash-Lite and Flash from turn to turn, it either keeps two caches or loses the discount. The practical split: route short, self-contained tasks like log parsing or schema checks, and keep long, context-heavy turns on one model.

Match the model to the workload. Gemini 3.1 Pro is built for deep, multi-step reasoning, and it brings that same depth to every request, including short lookups that don't need it. For a workload made mostly of quick checks and extractions, a Flash configuration is the better fit, and Pro is best saved for the tasks that need its reasoning depth. Model configurations can also apply different safety settings to security-related prompts. In our run, the Pro configuration declined two short code-security review prompts that Flash answered. If your agent performs security reviews, test those prompts on the configuration you plan to use.

Other ways to build the router. Jev is one way to get a fast classifier, but not the only one. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab runs the same Choice, Score and Noul questions on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, hosted on a Compute Engine GPU VM or on Cloud Run in your own Google Cloud project. An open-source server exposes the same API in front of it, so the routing code in this article only needs a different endpoint. Our results come from Jev, so measure accuracy and latency on your own tasks before switching, and keep in mind that a self-hosted model is billed by the GPU-hour rather than by the token.

Taken together, the results give three practical rules.

Start with Flash on low thinking before you build a router. It solved all 50 tasks and cost considerably less than the default setting. Lowering the output token limit is not a substitute, because it doesn't reduce how much the model thinks on easy tasks. Set the thinking level directly, with thinkingLevel: "LOW".

Use Jev to route short, standalone tasks. Send simple tasks to Flash-Lite and hard ones to Flash on low thinking. Ask Jev one simple yes-or-no question per factor. In our test, this helped reduce cost and response time compared with running Flash on low thinking for everything, and came close to what a perfect router would achieve. Keep long, context-heavy turns on a single model so caching keeps working. For high-volume warehouse workloads, Jeff Nelson's BigQuery and Cloud Run cascade pattern applies the same idea.

Measure what your agent actually spends. Send your agent's traces and thinking-token counts to Cloud Logging and BigQuery, so you can see which tasks drive your spend and catch truncation or retries that inflate it. The benchmark code will be published after open-source review.

In summary, the most cost-effective way to run an agent is to match each task to the model that fits it. Start with a fast model on a light setting. You'll often find it already gets the job done. Then add a small, almost free classifier like Jev to send only the hard tasks to a stronger model, and ask simple yes-or-no questions rather than one broad one. Judge every option by what it costs per task done right, not per token.

Now, dessert lovers nationwide can get one of these famous pies delivered through Goldbelly. However, a single pie costs a whopping $70, including shipping. But is the Apple Pie in a Paper Bag worth the hype—and the sticker shock? I got my hands on one to find out.

The bag isn’t just a marketing schtick. 26 years ago, The Elegant Farmer’s former owners, Dan and Karen Scheel, wanted to sell a new dessert using the apples grown in their orchard. Karen found a recipe in a cookbook for a pie baked in a paper bag, so she enlisted their team of bakers to develop their own version.

The paper bag offers two benefits. The sealed environment traps moisture as it bakes to keep the Ida Red apples juicy, but it also prevents the crust from burning. Partway through baking, The Elegant Farmer cuts a hole in the center of the bag so that the top crust is perfectly golden by the end.

When you order this pie, it arrives pre-baked and frozen in a shrink-wrapped cardboard box. All you need to do is warm it straight from frozen in a 325°F oven for about an hour. If you thaw it in the refrigerator first, you can reheat it at 425°F for just 8 to 10 minutes. Yes, it’s that easy.

The instructions direct you to place the pie on a baking tray, but I’d recommend going one step further and laying down a protective layer of parchment first. A considerable amount of caramelized sugar collects at the bottom of the bag and can leak into the oven. Adding the extra barrier is a simple step that spares you from scrubbing it off later.

An hour later, the pie was warm and ready to serve. The torn paper and cracked crust admittedly don’t make this the most visually stunning pie in the world. But once our editors took a bite, any aesthetic concerns went out the window.

The crust got equally rave reviews. “I would buy this just for the crust,” said Senior Editor Samantha MacAvoy. The pronounced butter flavor, golden caramelization, and crunchy texture perfectly offset the softness of the fruit. Even Digital Community Editor Taylor Worden—a self-proclaimed pie hater—fell in love with it. And Test Kitchen Manager Alejandro Valdes Lora kept his opinion short and sweet: “I’m obsessed with this.”

If our office’s unanimous praise is any indication, The Elegant Farmer’s Apple Pie in a Paper Bag is absolutely worth the hype. We may hesitate to spend $70 on this pie on any ordinary day. But for special occasions like Thanksgiving, it’s a convenient showstopper that’ll easily upstage everything else on your holiday menu.

The three Americans currently living off Earth just made sure their electoral voices were heard.

Anil Menon told us today (Nov. 3) that he and fellow NASA astronauts Luke Delaney and Jessica Watkins voted in the U.S. midterm elections from the International Space Station (ISS).

"Yes, you can even vote from space! All three U.S. crew members had the privilege of voting from space station. We filled out an electronic ballot and sent it securely to the election officials," Menon wrote in an X post this morning.

Yes, you can even vote from space! All three US crew members had the privilege of voting from space station. We filled out an electronic ballot and sent it securely to the election officials. https://t.co/hJCExQ2nKrNovember 3, 2026

That post included a 29-second video, in which Menon — wearing, like many of us today, an "I voted" sticker — shows us his "voting booth."

It's actually his room on the ISS, Menon explained. "Inside of it is my computer, where I was able to fill out an electronic form and send it to Harris County so I could vote today," he said.

Most NASA astronauts register to vote in Texas' Harris County; it's home to Johnson Space Center in Houston, the agency's human spaceflight hub.

Americans have been able to vote from space since 1997, when Texas passed a law allowing this to happen. The first person to take advantage of the legislation was NASA astronaut David Wolf, who cast his ballot from Russia's Mir space station that same year. (The ISS didn't welcome its first crew until November 2000.)

"Just like any other American away from home, astronauts may fill out a Federal Post Card Application to request an absentee ballot," NASA officials wrote in an astronaut-voting explainer.

The process is somewhat involved, however.

"After an astronaut fills out an electronic ballot aboard the orbiting laboratory, the document flows through NASA's Tracking and Data Relay Satellite System to a ground antenna at the agency's White Sands Test Facility in Las Cruces, New Mexico," they added. "From New Mexico, NASA transfers the ballot to the Mission Control Center at NASA Johnson and then on to the county clerk responsible for casting the ballot. To preserve the vote's integrity, the ballot is encrypted and accessible only by the astronaut and the clerk."

Delaney and Watkins launched to the ISS on Oct. 1 on SpaceX's Crew-13 mission. Menon has been there since mid-July, arriving aboard a Russian Soyuz spacecraft.

The trio currently share the orbiting lab with four other people — Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk and cosmonauts Anna Kikina, Pyotr Dubrov and Sergey Teteryatnikov. Kutryk and Teteryatnikov are members of Crew-13, whereas Dubrov and Kikina shared the Soyuz with Menon.

From corsets to bustles to floor-length skirts, the womenswear of the Gilded Age was known for many things. But Louisa Jacobson’s frayed raw hems and buckled collars were certainly not in the ladies’ 1880s style guides.

While doing press in New York City for the fourth season of her smash HBO historical drama The Gilded Age, Jacobson showed us a bit of her own street style... and it was a far cry from the looks that her character, Marian Brook, has donned on the very same streets.

Leaning instead into a bit of menswear, rather than the pastel gowns we’ve come to expect from her on-screen alter-ego, the actor experimented with suiting and modernized tailoring, thanks to the help of her stylist, Edward Bowleg III.

Last Monday, Jacobson turned to Mel Usine for a light-gray set from the label’s Spring/Summer 2027 collection. Composed of a frayed, collarless jacket and matching inside-out skirt, the ensemble offered an unfinished, deconstructed look you’d have never seen during the 1883 Metropolitan Opera House opening.

It was layered over a black-trimmed white tank with a skinny red tie wrapped around her waist, and paired with peep-toe heels.

The next day, the actor pushed things even further, wearing a black-on-black monochrome look from Commission’s Spring/Summer 2027 collection. This one was all about the buckle accents, with a zip-up blazer topped with a belted collar and little capri leggings trimmed with the same detail. In between was a pleated skirt, and for shoes, Jacobson wore pointed-toe pumps over calf-high socks.

Later in the day, Jacobson was a proper Calvin Klein girl—seen in Hudson Square wearing a dark-clay satin blazer and matching trousers with a heather-gray jacket over both. Her little nude ballet flats featured a contrasting reflective strap, and she grabbed a shoulder bag before heading out for an afternoon of press.

In an age of constant method dressing, it appears Jacobson is doing things her way.
