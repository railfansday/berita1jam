Syfy’s 5-Season Zombie Series Breaks All The Storytelling Rules

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Even the most diehard fans of zombie stories will struggle to deny that the genre can get repetitive. Across zombie TV shows, movies, books, games, and graphic novels, survivors tend to face the same exhausting routine: find safety and shelter, watch it eventually fall to the undead, then hit the road to find another refuge and begin the cycle anew. The specifics may change from story to story, but the genre still over relies on this tried-and-tested narrative loop.

From George A. Romero’s Night of the Living Dead in 1968 to modern hits like The Walking Dead, zombie entertainment has repeatedly built suspense around supposedly secure shelters becoming death traps. It's an effective setup, but one that can soon feel stale to experienced genre fans. That’s why one Syfy series should be celebrated for finding so many new ways to keep its undead apocalypse moving. Z Nation, the five-season saga that made zombies fun again, throws storytelling tradition out the window and gives its survivors much bigger problems than finding their next temporary sanctuary.

Following survivors escorting Murphy (Keith Allan), a potential source of a zombie cure, across America, Z Nation turns saving humanity from the undead into a spectacularly messy road trip. The cult classic series offers a refreshing alternative to the likes of The Walking Dead and The Last of Us, especially for viewers exhausted by the relentless misery that tends to define the genre’s small-screen entries. While its campy performances and B-movie sensibilities might initially discourage viewers more used to prestige TV, beneath the cheap thrills sits one of the most subversive zombie shows ever made.

Initially, Z Nation looks ready to follow The Walking Dead’s familiar blueprint. After an opening involving Murphy receiving an experimental vaccine before getting bitten by zombies overrunning a military facility, the Syfy show drops viewers into a familiar-feeling survivor camp in upstate New York that feels plucked out of literally any zombie TV show. Murphy’s arrival and the reveal of his apparent immunity creates a central mystery from the same trope The Last of Us is built on, and the immediate setting suggest a The Walking Dead-like lengthy struggle to protect a precarious home. That’s when Z Nation pulls out the rug from under smug viewers schooled in zombie stories.

The camp falls almost immediately, and its survivors inherit a military mission to transport humanity’s potential salvation to California. Despite its size, Plural’s fundraise isn’t (only) for a growth fund: two thirds will go to its third early-stage fund, and one third to a new “opportunities” fund making select follow-on investments, TechCrunch learned from Plural partners Khaled Helioui and Carina Namih.

The two buckets are fixed, but the check size is not. Plural is “still very committed to being an early stage fund,” Namih said. But Helioui added that some of the early-stage “moonshots” it plans to back may require “raising a couple hundred million” to get off the ground.

“Moonshot” is an appropriate term to describe some of Plural’s portfolio highlights, such as Proxima Fusion and The Exploration Company — and its new fund will be looking for equally promising opportunities in energy, AI infrastructure, defense, health, and more.

Many of these are capital-intensive, but this is no longer stopping European entrepreneurs, especially second-time founders, and companies like Daniel Ek’s Helsing are “not isolated cases,” said Helioui, who backed the startup’s seed round alongside two Plural partners.

“If you’ve built and scaled a company, chances are, you’re more likely to be driven and passionate about having an impact on Europe’s ability to defend itself, procure its own energy, or own its operations and manufacturing, rather than just another app,” he said.

With bigger pockets, Plural will be able to keep up longer with the larger and larger rounds its portfolio companies have gone on to raise. It also made an effort to raise more of this capital from Europe, Namih said.

While Europe’s institutional LPs were, for a long time, more likely to back banker types than founders hoping to displace those bankers, the current shift towards Europe’s sovereignty and resilience creates tailwinds for Plural, whose office in Tallinn played a key role in its decision to invest in defense tech — including in a missile company, Hypersonica.

“The reality is today still, you have a different level of conversation and a different sense of appreciation of the urgency and the criticality of these topics depending on how far from the border to Russia you are,” said Helioui.

Two of Plural’s six partners are Estonians, and Skype alumni: Wise cofounder Taavet Hinrikus and Teleport’s Sten Tamkivi. However, there is more to Plural than Tallinn; the fund also has offices in London, where Helioui, Namih and Ian Hogarth are based; and in Paris, where it hired former Uber executive Pierre-Dimitri Gore-Coty as a full-time partner in October 2025.

According to a press release, each partner will lead a small number of new investments a year. These companies will then get access to Plural’s growing portfolio support, which already ranges from hiring and communications to finance, Helioui said. That’s another indicator that VC firms in Europe are increasingly starting to look like their U.S. peers.
