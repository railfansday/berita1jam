I Know This Much Is True, adapted from Wally Lamb’s 1998 bestselling novel, follows the fractured yet mirrored lives of identical twin brothers. Starring Mark Ruffalo as both Dominik and Thomas Birdsey, the story centers on the aftermath of crisis and the relentless passage of time and age. Their bond, despite its strain, somehow captures the inescapable weight of generational trauma and the confusing yet undying devotion found between brothers. Director Derek Cianfrance paints a beautiful, tragic story, showing with immense care and depth the tenderness of the human experience.

In short, the audience meets the Birdsey brothers at a sore spot in their lives. Thomas, a lifelong paranoid schizophrenic, in a very public and violent fashion, protests the Gulf War by sacrificing his own hand. Consequently, he’s thrust into the legal system with only his brother Dominik on the outside to vouch for him. All at once, he must go from being a brother to the sole advocate and protector, all the while unearthing his own trauma as his story unfolds.

Ruffalo's claim to fame is, of course, tied to Marvel. Because of this, it can be easy to forget what a truly stellar performer he is. Critics and fans alike have pointed to his performance of Thomas and Dominik Birdsey as the most emotionally raw and resonant of his entire career. Each character demands something entirely different. Each is incredibly complex, nuanced, and requires an incredible level of adaptability and grace to pull off. Ruffalo appears to balance both Thomas's schizophrenia and Dominik’s grief effortlessly.

While scenes with himself are incredible, so are scenes with his cast mates. Alongside Kathryn Hahn as Dessa, Dominik's ex-wife, or Rosie O'Donnell as Thomas's social worker, sometimes clashing with them in these big, loud, cross-cutting conflicts or sharing these quiet, near-whispered intimate moments, with such a high level of skill captured on screen, there is so much room for play and for depth. Much of the series is heavy, some have said, exhaustively so. However, the performances tugging it along are most certainly worth a watch.

As is often the case when a series comes from a book, the plot of this show is truly incredible. The care and poignancy are evident from the very beginning. The Bridsey brothers were born into a family with many, many dark secrets. Each of them bears the weight. Their mother is a submissive, exploited woman, controlled by their abusive stepfather. He had a particular affinity for abusing Thomas as well, claiming he was soft and often asking for punishment. Notably, for the sake of their protection, she never revealed their birth father’s identity.

Present day, decades later, Dominic is a bit shifty but relatively stable in comparison to Thomas, who developed schizophrenia and fell in and out of institutions for years. Through creating a past so tight and intricately woven, their present becomes all the more deep. Each of their motives becomes entirely intuitive to the audience, and thus one not only feels for the Birdsey boys, but feels deeply connected to them. As the story progresses, the desperation grows thicker. Things unravel rather quickly. Dominik is unrelenting in his pursuit to vindicate his brother, and Thomas is entirely reliant on his persistence. The flashbacks and winding plots within the series are both truly sickening and incredibly loving. That combination makes for a truly thrilling experience.

The series achieved both commercial and critical success. Ruffalo won an Emmy, as well as a Golden Globe, and a Screen Actors Guild Award. It holds a highly decorated 74% on Rotten Tomatoes, and by all accounts is an incredible must-watch. There are very few series that result in unanimous approval, and This Much I Know Is True earned exactly that. From the plot to the performances to the original novel itself, each piece of the puzzle created a highly decorated, well-loved masterwork.

There's often a tragic backstory to any great game in which developers were forced to work way beyond their scheduled hours, often late into nights and even on weekends. Usually, the perception is that everyone's working their tail off to get a project out the door in time for whatever deadline is set by the publisher, but in the case of BioWare's seminal 2003 RPG Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic, a lot of people showed up at the studio just because they felt bad for people who actually had stuff to do.

John Winski, a former employee of BioWare going all the way back to 1996, reflects on Knights of the Old Republic's development in a recent conversation with Pembridge TV. "I think we worked five months of crunch time," he says. "We started with Tuesdays and Thursdays, we'd work 12-hour days. Then it became five days a week working 12-hour days. Saturdays, I don't know how long we were doing seven days a week, 12-hour days."

That's a lot of crunch. Without official numbers to go by, it's hard to know how that stacks up against notoriously crunch-heavy development cycles like The Last of Us 2, Red Dead Redemption 2, and Cyberpunk 2077, but if you're doing the math, that equates to 84-hour work weeks, more than double the average in most places. According to Winski, "a lot of times it was sympathy crunch," meaning workers who weren't required to come into the studio were doing so anyway because they felt bad for those dealing with mandated crunch.

"You don't have any work, but a bunch of other people have to be here, so you're gonna be here too. Which wasn't great, but also, if you look back, it's like, 'I am supporting all those people that have work to do," adds Winski. "Why wouldn't I be? And when you're young, you're kind of stupid and going, 'well, let's just do it', and it worked."

Can't argue with that. I mean, I'll argue all day long against crunch culture, but there's no denying that Knights of the Old Republic was a huge success, becoming an immediate hit and the best-selling Star Wars game of all time when it released. It's also not just one of the best Star Wars games to this day, it's also one of the most beloved RPGs of all time, proving that, unfortunately, great games can come from problematic working environments.

On the other hand, I'm reminded of comments from earlier this year made by Fallout co-creator Tim Cain, who offered a different perspective on crunch in the games industry. While he said he's "glad things have changed" and called Fallout's development "unsustainable," Cain said the developers working unreasonable hours on Fallout were doing so because "they loved what [they] were doing." I'd like to think the Knights of the Old Republic team felt the same.

A lot can go wrong. The fast but now vulnerable wheels of the AI trade can get wobbly. Nvidia's record margins can contract. The floor might seem a lot lower than the ceiling may seem higher, but can you honestly feel that way given today's valuation? I believe Nvidia will beat the market from here, making it a rising name on my list of potential stocks to buy in December.

Mike McCarthy just 'focused on winning' after Aaron Rodgers saga

📅 Nov 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Mike McCarthy helped guide the Pittsburgh Steelers to a resounding 27-5 victory over the Denver Broncos on "Black Friday" after one of the franchise's most tumultuous weeks in recent memory.

The trouble began after the Steelers announced veteran quarterback Aaron Rodgers was being benched in favor of Mason Rudolph at 11:10 p.m. ET on Tuesday, Nov. 24. The following day, Rodgers did not report to the Steelers' facility and instead aired his grievances with McCarthy in an appearance on "The Pat McAfee Show" before Pittsburgh released the 42-year-old quarterback.

The accusations claimed McCarthy had fallen asleep in team meetings and had performed a "1, 2, 3, Packers!" during a halftime huddle breakdown with his team, among other issues. Naturally, it garnered significant attention in the NFL world, with many decrying Rodgers for breaking the sanctity of his team's locker-room bond.

And one of the most notable reactions came from McCarthy's predecessor, Mike Tomlin, who referred to the organization as having a "dumpster fire" on its hands following Rodgers' release.

Following the Steelers' Week 12 win, McCarthy was asked for his reaction to those comments.

"I really don't have one," he replied, stoically, during his postgame news conference. "Let's just be real here. There's only so much time and energy in the day. I think you have to, when you go through things like that, I have to put all my energy into winning."

"I get that this is part of the responsibility and the opinions and comments of others is why I'm in here to answer questions," McCarthy added. "But I'm focused on winning."

McCarthy's approach paid off, as the Steelers were able to improve to 7-4 with their victory over the Broncos. That has put them a half-a-game behind the Cincinnati Bengals in the AFC North race while giving them more breathing room in the wild-card race, as they now are a game-and-a-half ahead of the No. 8 seed New England Patriots.

McCarthy appreciated earning that victory after what he acknowledged was a difficult week for his team.

"It was definitely a hard travel week for us as a football team," he said. "I'm proud of our players, I'm proud of our coaching staff, I'm proud of our support staff and I really appreciate all the other support."

The Steelers were playing at home at Acrisure Stadium in Pittsburgh in Week 12, so it wasn't immediately clear whether McCarthy was using the phrase "hard travel week" metaphorically as a reference to a series of events the team endured amid the Rodgers saga.

Either way, McCarthy came out of the win pleased with his team. He praised Rudolph for being able to effectively guide the team without Rodgers and also praised his veteran-laden defense for its stalwart performance against Bo Nix.

But above all else, McCarthy was grateful for the team's veteran leadership for establishing "urgency" after a meeting they held earlier in the week.

"I think it says a tremendous amount about the team," McCarthy said, referencing the win. "I really probably want to pivot off the leadership council meeting. Everything that was discussed was healthy. A lot of things in there, but what came out of it was urgency. And I thought our leaders did a tremendous job of bringing the urgency to their rooms and it definitely showed up."

"You definitely saw the urgency today," McCarthy later added. "That's what really stood out for me as far as the vibe in the locker room. Our guys do a good job of that. From the get-go, our leaders really had the guys going. And our defense got us started. We played the game that we needed to play. Just very, very impressed and very proud of the way we handled this."

10 Spies More Lethal Than James Bond

📅 Nov 28, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Few literary spies are as synonymous with danger and lethality as James Bond. Ian Fleming’s 007 literally has a license to kill, proving that a capacity for violence isn’t just part of his job description, it’s one of his greatest professional strengths. Across Fleming’s 14 James Bond books and the dozens of continuations from other authors that followed, M16’s most famous spy permanently dispatched countless foes in a variety of painful and creative ways.

James Bond may be the poster child for dangerous fictional spies, but in reality there are plenty who make him look relatively tame. The Bond of the novels is far less trigger-happy than his movie counterpart, but the same can’t be said for the generations of thriller spies he inspired. Plenty are far more lethal than 007, with the most dangerous completing missions through means Fleming’s vision of Her Majesty’s Secret Service would never allow.

Surviving the massacre of a presidential protection detail is an unusually brutal introduction to a thriller protagonist. That’s precisely how readers encounter Scot Harvath in Brad Thor’s The Lions of Lucerne, which establishes that his character has much more depth than his Secret Service credentials. Before protecting presidents, Harvath served as a Navy SEAL. His subsequent career in covert counterterrorism after the death of his security team combines both backgrounds, creating an operative who understands how protection works and possesses the military skills to dismantle it.

Throughout his series, Scot Harvath fights multiple sustained campaigns of violence under conditions that should finish him. For example, 2019’s Backlash strips away his support and leaves him fighting through hostile Russian territory, turning his survival skills and appetite for revenge into an especially vicious combination. It’s not uncommon for Harvath to kill dozens of people in a single novel. This is especially true when his friends and family are at risk and things get personal. Bond is dangerous when cornered, but Harvath’s special operations background makes pursuing him an exceptionally costly mistake.

An art restorer hardly sounds like the obvious choice to outclass 007. The civilian profession of author Daniel Silva’s Gabriel Allon, however, suits a killer whose defining qualities include patience, precision, and extraordinary attention to detail. Recruited into Israeli intelligence after the 1972 Munich Olympics massacre, Allon participated in the campaign to eliminate those responsible. His background establishes him as a specialist in targeted assassination long before the first novel in his series, 2000’s The Kill Artist, begins, with the consequences of that work following him throughout Daniel Silva’s novels.

Gabriel Allon is a notably lethal spy when he’s dealing with his targets up close. Daniel Silva describes his early assassinations as close-range shootings with a .22-caliber Beretta, emphasizing nerve and preparation over overwhelming firepower. He’s not a literary spy that only has violence to rely on, however. Allon’s linguistic abilities and talent for assuming convincing identities make reaching the target part of the same skill set as killing them. Whereas meeting James Bond isn’t an encounter those in his orbit forget in a hurry, Gabriel Allon knows how to become completely unremarkable until he makes his move. The fact that various organizations try to bring him back to his career as an assassin despite him wanting to leave that life behind is further testament to just how lethal he truly is.

You can purchase The Kill Artist, the first Gabriel Allon novel by Daniel Silva, here.

Losing his memory should make Jason Bourne an easier target. Instead, 1980’s The Bourne Identity introduced readers to a covert operative whose survival instincts and skills remain terrifyingly effective even after he’s forgotten honing them. Author Robert Ludlum builds much of the suspense of the Jason Bourne novels around that contradiction: the titular spy knows how to survive a world full of professional killers, but can’t remember how he acquired his lethal skillset. Bourne’s level of combat conditioning is so deep and thorough that his training kicks in on a subconscious level.

The novels also give Jason Bourne a considerably wider skillset than a straightforward assassin. Robert Ludlum’s original trilogy places Bourne in an elaborate operation that requires deception and infiltration just as much as armed and unarmed combat. Later authors expand the violence, placing Bourne against rival assassins and intelligence organizations equipped to anticipate his methods. Across those different incarnations, Bourne proves himself much more dangerous than 007 thanks to his incredible adaptability. Removing his support network, compromising his identity, or sending trained operatives after him never seems to make him any less of a threat.

You can purchase The Bourne Identity, the first Jason Bourne novel by Robert Ludlum, here.

The distinction between merely investigating terrorists and actively eliminating them is central to the appeal of the Mitch Rapp novels. Author Vince Flynn’s CIA operative specializes in the latter, approaching counterterrorism with a lethal urgency that repeatedly puts him at odds with his superiors. 2010’s American Assassin, which serves as a prequel, traces Rapp’s trigger-happy mentality back to personal loss and his recruitment into a covert assassination program. From there, Rapp honed himself into one of the most proficient killers at the CIA’s disposal.

What makes Mitch Rapp especially frightening is his cold and calculated approach to even the most violent operations. His training encompasses all manner of firearms and close combat, but it’s his willingness to use his skills without a second’s hesitation that makes them so deadly. This is where his operations differ significantly to 007’s, and this difference reveals everything about which is the more dangerous. James Bond’s assignments frequently require him to uncover a larger conspiracy before confronting its architect. Rapp is often dispatched because the U.S. Government wants an enemy to stop breathing.

You can purchase Transfer of Power, the first Mitch Rapp novel by Vince Flynn, here.

A body count only reveals so much about an assassin’s skills. Often how targets are killed matters just as much as how many have been dealt with. This makes John Rain an especially competent literary spy, since his victims often aren’t recognizable as murder victims at all. The Japanese American freelancer from Barry Eisler’s novels specializes in deaths that appear natural, making him particularly valuable to employers who need a person removed without attracting attention. Beginning with 2002’s Rain Fall, also published as A Clean Kill in Tokyo, Barry Eisler turns Rain’s ability to conceal violence the basis for one of the most lethal spies to grace the pages of a thriller novel.

As expected, the ability to make a murder look like an accident is far from the only trick up John Rain’s sleeve. Serving in the Special Forces during Vietnam and extensive martial arts experience mean he’s just as capable in an open fight as he is setting up an elaborate assassination. Still, it’s his penchant for creative and untraceable kills that define Rain. Surviving a gunfight is one thing. Arranging matters so that nobody realizes a killing occurred requires an entirely different level of control.

You can purchase A Clean Kill In Tokyo, the first John Rain novel by Barry Eisler, here.

Pike Logan belongs to an organization designed to operate beyond the normal limits of American law. In Brad Taylor’s novels, a black book group codenamed The Taskforce recruits from elite military and intelligence backgrounds. Even among a roster of highly experienced specialists, however, 2011’s One Rough Man establishes Logan as an especially lethal operative. His notably military approach makes him especially dangerous when a mission develops into a sustained confrontation.

Logan thinks about factors like surveillance, positioning, coordination, and escape as connected problems, and has no qualms about using lethal force whenever the situation demands it. Author Brad Taylor’s own Special Forces experience adds a notable level of detail that makes Logan’s professional competence feel legitimate. James Bond is an exceptional lone operative, but Pike Logan brings the tactical lethality and group-coordination of an elite military unit into the world of espionage. His lethality comes just as much from knowing how to use the people around him as it does any weapon.

You can purchase One Rough Man, the first Pike Logan novel by Brad Taylor, here.

Most fictional spies have a life before whatever intelligence service they work for recruits them. Even the enigmatic James Bond went to Eton and served in the Navy before joining Her Majesty’s Secret Service. Evan Smoak’s preparation begins at twelve, when the Orphan Program takes him from a group home and starts conditioning him to be a highly covert government assassin on a team that doesn’t officially exist. By adulthood, Smoak’s entire world has been defined by violence, surveillance, and operational discipline. Gregg Hurwitz’s Orphan X, the first novel in the series of the same name, introduces the result: a man whose formidable resources and specialized skills make his disappearance from the organization that made him a matter of national security.

Working as the Nowhere Man, Smoak redirects his abilities toward helping people, but that doesn’t make him any less dangerous to those who earn his attention. His lethality comes from obsessive preparation just as much as shooting or fighting. He assesses his surroundings, anticipates complications, and treats small mistakes as potential disasters, an approach that leads to watertight operation planning. James Bond is a man who has been trained for danger. Evan Smoak feels like he’s been psychologically conditioned into a living weapon.

You can purchase Orphan X, the first Evan Smoak novel by Gregg Hurwitz, here.

Being hunted by the CIA would be a serious problem for any literary spy, including James Bond. For Court Gentry, aka The Gray Man, it becomes an extended demonstration of why the agency couldn’t simply let him walk free after his years of service. Author Mark Greaney’s former covert assassin survives long after his own employers mark him for death. He manages to build a successful career as a freelancer despite being hunted by one of the most far-reaching intelligence organizations in the world.

As for why Court Gentry is more dangerous than James Bond, the central spy of The Gray Man novels has an advantage when it comes to sheer concentration of expertise. Unlike 007, who carries out a wide variety of types of missions, Gentry’s career is built on surviving encounters with other highly trained killers. Bond often comes face-to-face with dangerous foes, but this is rarely part of the plan. Gentry exists precisely to take out these kinds of threats. Bond may be the more well-rounded spy, but Gentry is inarguably the more lethal of the two.

You can purchase The Gray Man, the first Court Gentry novel by Mark Greaney, here.

Before adopting the name John Clark, John Kelly from the world of the Jack Ryan novels was a former Navy SEAL and Vietnam veteran whose personal revenge campaign develops alongside a military prisoner rescue operation. Tom Clancy gives Clark the skills for both undertakings when he made his debut in 1988’s The Cardinal of the Kremlin, and then spends multiple books showing just how ruthlessly he can apply them. Clark’s professional effectiveness comes from versatility accumulated over a long career. A potent combination of maritime, military, and clandestine fieldwork experience allows him to function across situations that demand very different kinds of expertise.

John Clark’s later leadership of the multinational counterterrorism unit Rainbow showcases the full extent of those capabilities, as he turns a team of elite but disconnected specialists into a lethally coherent unit. James Bond may have formidable instincts when violence becomes the only option, but Clark’s professional capacity for it ensures that he rarely, if ever, needs to rely on anything but carefully applied brute force. He understands how to plan a mission, select the necessary people, and deliver operational success even when up against overwhelming firepower.

You can purchase The Cardinal of the Kremlin, the first novel to feature John Clark by Tom Clancy, here.

Espionage missions that heavily feature luxury, global travel, and impeccable presentation make Villanelle, aka Oxana Vorontsova, an obvious spiritual successor to James Bond. However, she doesn’t just build on his legacy when it comes to making the spy lifestyle look lavish. She also embodies the cold-blooded professional killer archetype that Ian Fleming’s 007 is often mistaken as being. Author Luke Jennings introduces Vorontsova in the Killing Eve novels as a Russian killer recruited by the secretive Twelve, whose intervention transforms an already violent young woman into an international assassin. Her handlers supply new identities and intensive training, turning her capacity for murder into a highly marketable professional skill.

Like 007, Villanelle is an extremely adaptable and capable spy. Where they differ is that she has an alarming absence of remorse. Bond’s conscience often causes him to hesitate before making a kill in the novels, or even change his mind and lower his weapon. This isn’t the case for Villanelle. What’s more, she appears to take a disturbing pleasure in this aspect of her work. Bond is only ever violent when he feels it’s necessary to complete his mission. Villanelle seems to go out of her way to find solutions that require bloodshed. This means that, naturally, she ends up killing much more frequently than James Bond ever has, and ensures she’s one of the most dangerous spies ever to appear in a thriller novel.

The New Airbus A220 Coming In 2027

The Airbus A220 is currently preparing for a major upgrade, although the manufacturer is falling short of launching a new variant altogether. This year, new Airbus A220 deliveries will be outfitted with the manufacturer's Airspace interior, aligning the A220 with the interior feel of the A350, A330neo, and A320neo. Highlights include new upgraded extra-large overhead lockers, which will be around 20% lighter while offering more than 15% additional baggage capacity.

These aircraft will also feature 16-million-color LED lighting, allowing airlines to tailor their brand or flight phases. Airbus also plans on offering this unique interior as a retrofit option for existing Airbus A220 customers. Meanwhile, starting in 2027, a higher-density Airbus A220-300, one that features a 160-seat layout, will be offered, which provides operators with additional revenue and unit-cost gains without introducing a larger model to any given route.

However, the much-discussed Airbus A220-500 variant remains unannounced, and it does not look like Airbus has any plans to make any quick moves there as well. The manufacturer has looked to continue improving the profitability of the Airbus A220 family while also improving production rates, offering even more flexibility for operators. Let's analyze all the upcoming changes to the Airbus A220 family.

To contextualize the upcoming upgrades to this extremely popular aircraft program, it is important to begin by breaking down the Airbus A220's origins and discussing the aircraft's industrial footprint. The Airbus A220 began its life as a member of the Bombardier CSeries, a five-abreast narrowbody jet which was launched on July 13, 2008, with the intent of bridging the gap between regional jets and larger single-aisle aircraft.

It was first introduced to the market as the CS100, and it first took to the skies on September 16, 2013, before being certified by Transport Canada on December 18, 2015. The jet entered service with SWISS on July 15, 2016. The Bombardier CS300, which flew on February 27, 2015, would be certified on July 11, 2016, and it entered service on December 14 of that year with launch customer airBaltic.\

In July 2018, after Airbus took a majority stake in the company, it was quickly rebranded as the Airbus A220 and later organized as Airbus Canada Limited Partnership in June 2019. The aircraft program was now fully in the hands of manufacturing giant Airbus. The manufacturer's industrial capacity was expanded beyond Mirabel, Quebec, with a second final assembly line opened in Mobile, Alabama, in August 2019.

This allowed the manufacturer to avoid hefty import duties from US regulators. The manufacturer's ownership stake was increased to 75% in February 2020, with Bombardier electing to exit the program. The Airbus A220 remains the only commercial program managed by the manufacturer entirely outside of Europe. After entering service, the aircraft quickly established itself as offering strong dispatch reliability and improved passenger satisfaction over other models.

The Airbus A220 is powered by Pratt & Whitney PW1500G geared turbofan engines, and the aircraft offers a carbon-composite lightweight wing and an aluminum-lithium fuselage. Fly-by-wire controls and refined aerodynamics help deliver long legs and low-cost operations. Family weights range from 63 to 71 tonnes (139,000-156,000 lbs) of maximum takeoff weight (MTOW).

The aircraft family offers a range of 6,390-6,670 km (3,450-3,600 nautical miles), making it capable of serving almost all transcontinental niches in the United States and even longer-range lower-gauge routes within Europe. The 35 m (115 ft) Airbus A220-100 seats around 108-133 passengers, while the longer A220-300 seats a larger 130-160 passengers, depending on the specific configuration.

Airbus also launched the ACJ TwoTwenty business-jet variant, which was launched in late 2020. Delta remains the largest customer for the aircraft, and, globally, more than 1,100 Airbus A220s have been ordered, with over 500 delivered to date.

Globally, the fleet has surpassed 1.5 million flights and 2.7 million block hours, with the aircraft carrying more than 100 million passengers. The aircraft has also managed to maintain an exceptional safety reputation, with only one non-fatal smoke-related accident showing up on its rap sheet. It mainly competes with the Embraer E-Jet E2 family, holding a 55% share in this small-airliner niche, demonstrating the success and competitive value this program offers.

Airbus already operates several variants in its A220 series.

The A220 has slowly matured from being a promising upstart in the industry to becoming a core single-aisle workhorse for most carriers, especially with airlines wanting more revenue, lower unit costs, and a cabin that matches the rest of the manufacturer's portfolio. The five-abreast Airbus A220 already offers exceptional passenger comfort and impressive fuel burn, but bin space, lighting, and brand cohesion lag behind the newest Airbus models. Bringing the Airspace cabin to the aircraft helps close this gap.

Airlines today are extremely focused on premium and brand perception, and improving interiors is a key element of this overall strategy. From a commercial perspective, airlines need a low-risk way to add seats without adding a new aircraft type. The 160-seat Airbus A220-300 adds about ten more seats while providing more attractive costs per available seat mile, which ultimately proved useful for capacity-constrained layouts where upgauging to an A320neo would negatively impact profitability.

From a strategic perspective, Airbus needs to widen the A220's overall addressable market and strengthen the plane's business case before choosing to green-light a stretch to the Airbus A220-500. After all, the manufacturer wants to continue improving cabin appeal for legacy operators first.

Throughout this retrofitting and upgrade process, Airbus is mainly looking to pull two key levers. This year, new Airbus A220 deliveries will adopt the new Airbus Airspace cabin, a similar design that has been seen on Airbus A350, Airbus A330neo, and Airbus A320neo jets. Headlines are quick to discuss the 16-million-color LED lighting, which brand-matches the ambiance and circadian cues across different flight phases.

However, the Airspace kit also standardizes modern trim, surfaces, and touchpoints so that the aircraft will resemble any other Airbus model as closely as possible. Airbus is also quick to point out that it will be offering flexibility to operators, specifically when it comes to high-density Airbus A220-300 models. This ensures that its upgrades are not only providing optionality to future customers, but also letting existing operators improve their fleets.

The idea of stretching to the Airbus A220-500 also brings a lot to the table, as this model would upgauge the family and could potentially fill a capacity gap (albeit a small one) that exists in the Airbus commercial lineup. These upgrades are an extremely low-risk way to improve its product offering quickly.

From a financial perspective, the A220 upgrades represent a relatively low-risk lever being pulled by Airbus. For operators, these upgraded cabins will lift premium appeal, especially when it comes to corporate travelers who are interested in flying more modern aircraft. This will also result in more loyalty captured across the board.

Larger overhead bins are beneficial for operators, as they enable them to have lower gate-check-related costs and faster aircraft turns. Higher-capacity A220 variants can lower unit costs by spreading overall expenses out over more seats and adding capacity at slot-constrained airports and during peak leisure travel seasons.

Investing in these upgrades comes with low risks as aircraft downtimes are relatively low. This refresh supports overall pricing and will hopefully accelerate Airbus' order momentum: Delta, for example, is in the process of replacing its aging Boeing 717 fleet with advanced Airbus A220 models. This will improve learning-curve economics all along the way, ultimately pushing the program toward profitability before the A220-500 stretch.

At the end of the day, all of this means relatively little for passengers. The upgraded A220 cabins will likely provide a marginally more comfortable passenger experience, thanks to the cabin lighting being a little nicer, and it is also less likely with these new cabins that passengers will have to place their bags far away from where they currently are.

“Outlander” was never a series to hold back on a brainy twist that took a minute to digest. But its prequel series, “Outlander: Blood of My Blood,” may have outdone its predecessor with a twist that rewrites at least one family tree.

Since the prequel premiered last year, it has always operated on its own narrative timeline, separate from author Diana Gabaldon’s book series. In those books, Julia (Hermoine Corfield) and Henry (Jeremy Irvine), the parents of original series heroine Claire (Caitriona Balfe), died when she was 5 years old in a car crash. But with “Blood of My Blood,” executive producers Matthew B. Roberts and Maril Davis ponder what might have happened if they actually time-traveled through the same magic stones their daughter would decades later, and were left to survive in 1710s Scotland.

It has been one tragedy after another for the lovebirds, and that’s saying something, given that they weathered World War I. But one question has loomed large over the Beauchamps: What will happen to their son, William, with whom Julia was pregnant when they went through the stones? He’s not in Gabaldon’s books, so Roberts and Davis have a bit of freedom to play with his fate. And they seemingly gave fans their answer in the Season 2 finale.

Julia and Henry were dealt their biggest setback yet earlier this season, when a seer informed her she isn’t a time traveler, she was just pregnant with one. William has his father’s mystical gene, and now that Julia is postpartum, she won’t be able to go back to her own time unless they get pregnant again, which itself is a moral quandary. The news doesn’t stop them from trying to run away together again. But in the process, Henry is shot by Lord Lovat (Tony Curran) in a case of mistaken identity, and Julia’s attempts to flee Lovat’s henchman leave her lifeless at the bottom of a waterfall. Fearful for wee William’s life without his parents, Julia’s confidante Davina (Sarah Vickers) takes him to the safest place she can think of –– to her son Brian (Jamie Roy) and his new wife Ellen (Harriet Slater). Mere moments after Brian and Ellen’s long-awaited wedding, the Frasers have a new addition to their future home at Lallybroch. This is where the series goes for broke, and makes a frankly brazen claim. Brian and Ellen are, of course, the parents of future “Outlander” hero Jamie (Sam Heughan). According to Gabaldon’s original books, he had an older brother named William (aka Willie), whose death when Jamie was young forever affected how he saw the world and cared for those he loved. (Not for nothing, Willie was so formative for Jamie that fans latched onto the wooden snake he carved before he died, and the onscreen prop sold for more than $5,000 at auction earlier this year.)

If “Blood of My Blood” is to be believed, Brian and Ellen being given an infant named William could mean that Claire’s heretofore unknown younger brother was actually raised as a Fraser 200 years in the past. It’s a twist that completely alters how fans should look –– at least in the TV series’ timeline –– at Claire and Jamie’s relationship. Not only does the prequel depict their parents as unexpected allies, but it is now suggesting they may have even shared a sibling, not by blood thankfully.

Now before anyone gets too excited (or angry!), Roberts and Davis aren’t exactly making this twist official on a birth certificate just yet: Even when pressed multiple times for confirmation, they dodged answering whether this really is the Willie of the Fraser family tree. But with Season 2 coming to a close without news about a Season 3 renewal, it’s hard not to feel the impact of this twist, given it could be the revelation the prequel leaves fans as a parting gift. For now, Davis is tying it all back to the original.

“With Willie/William, even if they’re not the same person, I think it’s so interesting to think how these two stories are interwoven now,” she tells Variety. “It binds Jamie and Claire’s parents, and how will that affect them in the future? We always loved the idea that Jamie and Claire were meant to be, and we’re seeing how that fate all comes together.”

Roberts has a bit more fun with his response about the mystery of it all. “I feel like this is the natural place to go, and I like to manipulate people,” he says with a grin. “That’s my job.”

“I like to do some alt endings to throw people off in case scripts get leaked,” he says. “I like to keep actors on their toes, too. I have something in my head that I want to put on screen, but we did multiple endings with the finale of ‘Outlander’ as well.”

But rewriting lore to this degree is risky business, especially given the series isn’t yet guaranteed to come back. Still, Roberts and Davis have already planned out what would happen next should they get the go ahead, and there is one thing audiences won’t ever see in this prequel: young Jamie.

While diehard fans have undoubtedly done the math to figure out just how soon their favorite Highlander could make his debut, Roberts says he is hesitant to bring the action too close to the stories told in Gabaldon’s books and the original series.

“It’s hard to think about the stories with young Jamie in them, because that feels like you’re starting to tread on ‘Outlander,’ and I don’t want to do that,” he says. “It feels like you want to get maybe as close to the birth of Jamie as we can, but that still gives us quite a bit of time.”

If “Blood of My Blood” is at least in step with Gabaldon’s book series’ timeline, Willie is born in 1716 –– the same year of Brian and Ellen’s wedding, as seen in the Season 2 finale. Jamie will be born in 1721, so five years could be what’s left of the narrative runway for the series.

With those parameters in place, Roberts and Davis also continue to look beyond “Blood of My Blood” for future “Outlander” franchise opportunities. Even with the wealth of Gabaldon’s world building at their disposal (her 10th and final book has yet to be released), they continue to be vocal about a particular contender.

Lord John, a fan-favorite character played by David Berry in the original series, is the subject of Gabaldon’s companion series of novels, and Roberts and Davis previously tried to spin off those stories while the original “Outlander” was still airing. The series didn’t move forward then, but they seem to be actively hoping the franchise’s continued success will reignite interest.

“There are certainly other things out there for us to explore, and Lord John remains one of them,” Davis says.
