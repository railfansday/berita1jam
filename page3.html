Singapore Airlines Unveils Posh New First Class Suites With Double Beds For 777-9 And A350ULR

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Singapore Airlines has unveiled its next-generation first class seat, with sliding doors, companion dining, and center seats that combine into a double bed measuring five feet across. The new product will appear on the Airbus A350-900ULR and Boeing 777-9, bringing first class to the airline’s ultra-long-range A350 fleet for the first time.

Singapore Airlines’ new first class specifications reveal a small cabin with substantial personal space. The A350-900ULR will have four seats, while the Boeing 777-9 will have six. Both use a 1-2-1 arrangement with direct aisle access and a curved cabin layout.

PriestmanGoode designed the product, with Collins Aerospace manufacturing the seats. The interiors feature Poltrona Frau leather and padded walls incorporating Singapore Airlines’ batik motif.

The centerpiece is the pair of center seats. A fully retractable divider allows passengers to share the space, with the seats moving together to create either a two-person sofa or a double bed. That bed measures 80 inches long and 60 inches wide. Singapore Airlines describes it as the largest double bed in commercial aviation.

Solo passengers receive an 80-inch-long, 30-inch-wide bed. The seat itself measures 23 inches across with the armrests raised, expanding to 30 inches when they are lowered.

Every seat also supports face-to-face dining with a companion, a feature I appreciate in first class: traveling together should include the option to sit across from one another for dinner, even when you prefer separate window seats.

The seat includes electronically adjustable side wings, upper and lower lumbar support, adjustable armrests, a leg rest, and an ottoman. Integrated myKrisWorld controls can save preferred seat positions and lighting settings for future flights.

For sleeping, the crew will prepare the bed with cotton linens, a duvet, a choice of pillows, and a new mattress topper from German bedding company Paradies. Singapore Airlines says the topper uses breathable spacer fabric to improve cushioning and temperature regulation.

Dedicated storage includes a personal wardrobe, a leather-lined vanity drawer, and space for cabin baggage beneath the ottoman.

Both aircraft receive a 32-inch 4K entertainment screen, but the A350ULR version is OLED. The 777-9 specification lists a conventional 4K display.

Passengers will be offered Bang & Olufsen wireless headphones or can pair Bluetooth headphones with the system. A separate touch panel displays flight information, connectivity status, and lavatory availability without interrupting the main screen.

The new cabins are scheduled to begin entering service in the second half of 2027. Unlike with ANA, the unveiling does not signal an imminent onboard debut. First class is planned for the A350-900ULR and 777-9. The standard long-haul A350-900 will receive the new business class, but no first class cabin.

Singapore Airlines’ new first class looks lovely, particularly for couples who can use the center seats as a shared living and sleeping space. Companion dining at every seat and substantial bedding are welcome details too.

Meteorites that may have delivered carbon to early Earth carry an oxygen signature older than their asteroids

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Organic specks in primitive meteorites carry an oxygen signature set before their asteroids formed, likely in the cold outer solar system.

Some of the carbon on the early Earth may have arrived inside meteorites, carried as dark specks of organic matter far too small to see.

Where those specks were first made is still an open question. A new study suggests their oxygen dates from before the asteroids that carried them had formed.

In seven primitive meteorites that were never strongly heated, the organic matter carries nearly the same oxygen fingerprint.

Water on the asteroids barely changed it. That makes the oxygen a record of where the material formed, not of what happened to it later.

Where the organics formed is the team’s interpretation, drawn from a small set of meteorites and two lab tests.

Daniel Crocker and David Johnston of Harvard’s Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences led the work. Their co-authors were Conel Alexander of the Carnegie Institution for Science and Mark Thiemens of the University of California, San Diego.

Oxygen atoms come in three stable forms: oxygen-16, oxygen-17 and oxygen-18. They differ only in weight. Scientists call these forms isotopes, and the mix of the three in a sample works like a fingerprint.

On Earth, almost any process that sorts oxygen by weight changes the share of oxygen-17 about half as much as the share of oxygen-18.

The oldest minerals in some carbon-rich meteorites break that rule. In them, the two heavier forms change by equal amounts.

That odd pattern is usually explained as a blend of two ancient pools of oxygen, one rich in oxygen-16 and one poor in it. Researchers drew a reference line for it in 1977 from minerals in the Allende meteorite, but what produced it is still debated.

The meteorites in the study belong to a group called carbonaceous chondrites. The most primitive ones hold up to 4 percent carbon by weight in their organic matter. Between 30 and 80 percent of that organic matter won’t dissolve in acids or solvents.

Alexander’s lab separated that material from the rock. Crocker then measured its oxygen with a method the Harvard group had recently developed.

Water had altered the seven primitive meteorites on their home asteroids, always below about 302°F (150°C). Their organic matter came out almost identical.

The oxygen-18 readings varied by only about 2 parts per thousand, and every sample was within the margin of error of the reference line.

“What surprised me was how similar the oxygen isotope compositions were across meteorites that experienced very different alteration histories after they formed,” Crocker told Earth.com.

These asteroids once held liquid water, and water can swap oxygen atoms with the material around it.

The team calculated what would happen if the organic matter had traded much of its oxygen with that water. Its oxygen-18 reading would be 27 to 58 parts per thousand above a standard.

The actual readings were 10 to 12. So the organic matter most likely swapped very little oxygen with the water. If it had, samples from several separate asteroids would have needed nearly identical conditions to end up with the same values.

The water still changed the material’s chemistry, and some oxygen-bearing parts of the molecules were lost. But it left the oxygen’s mix of weights mostly alone.

“That preservation gives us an opportunity to look past what happened to the organic matter on the asteroids and investigate the environment in which it originally formed,” Crocker said.

Organic matter from six meteorites heated on their asteroids, or by impacts, looked different. It held less of both heavy oxygen forms, and the readings formed a trend away from the unheated group.

To test whether heat alone could do that, the team warmed organic matter from the Mighei meteorite in a vacuum for an hour. One run reached 932°F (500°C) and the other 1,112°F (600°C).

The samples lost about 59 and 67 percent of their oxygen respectively, and what remained shifted along the same trend as the heated meteorites. Undo the heating, and they would move back toward the primitive group, which fits one shared starting fingerprint.

Water didn’t set the fingerprint, and heat changes it in a different direction. So the authors concluded the oxygen most likely went in when the organic matter itself formed, before it was gathered into any asteroid.

The organic matter holds less oxygen-16 than almost every mineral in these meteorites. In the early solar system, water was the main ingredient low in oxygen-16. So the authors think the organics took their oxygen from ancient water as they formed.

Extra heavy hydrogen and nitrogen in the organics already hinted at a cold place with strong radiation. The team favors a birthplace in the outer solar system, with the same well-mixed supply ending up in several asteroids.

“Rather than starting with the chemistry that occurred on the early Earth, we can ask what organic material Earth and other young planets inherited from the Solar System environment in which they formed,” Crocker and Johnston told Earth.com.

The researchers said they “cannot yet identify a single location or oxygen-bearing molecule as its source.” They also checked for lab effects, such as stray mineral grains and the chemicals used to free the organics. They concluded neither changed the readings in any meaningful way.

The unusual pattern in the ancient minerals, first spotted more than 50 years ago, still lacks a full explanation. “That doesn’t solve the mystery, but it provides a new constraint,” the two researchers added.

Next, the team sees the oxygen as a way to narrow down where, and under what conditions, the material formed. That could show how widely the raw ingredients for life’s chemistry were spread among the young planets.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

At least 10 local governments have moved to drop Flock’s surveillance cameras since summer

At least 10 local governments and police departments have moved since this summer to curb, restrict, or end their use of Flock Safety technology as concerns about mass surveillance and data privacy continue to grow. The backlash is part of a broader pushback against the rapid adoption of advanced AI technology, including the data center buildout, facial recognition, and other uses of AI.

Flock, founded in 2017, sells surveillance software — like license plate readers, drones, and microphone-based edge-computing devices to pick up gunshots and other sounds — that can help solve crimes, such as finding missing people or stolen cars. More than 100,000 Flock cameras are in use nationwide.

But in the past three months, Monroe, Ohio; Newburgh, New York; Richmond, Virginia; Hopkinton, Massachusetts; New Bedford, Massachusetts; and Lansing, Michigan, have all moved to end their contracts with Flock. Meanwhile, police in East Bridgewater and Grafton, Massachusetts, also announced plans to stop using Flock cameras. Just this week, residents of League City, Texas, voted against their police department’s continued use of Flock’s license plate readers, and the cameras are now set to be removed.

The concerns range from privacy to how data is shared (and with whom) to procurement concerns.

In Asheville, North Carolina, police lost access to Flock after the city voted to terminate its contract in August, and the El Paso County Sheriff’s Office in Colorado announced in September that it would stop using Flock cameras. When asked for comment, the El Paso County Sheriff’s Office redirected TechCrunch to its press release, where Sheriff Joseph Roybal said that discontinuing the use of Flock was the right decision for the county.

“We have the tools, training, and experience to locate and apprehend criminals without relying on Flock cameras,” he said in the press release.

Tatiana Rice, a senior director for U.S. legislation at The Future of Privacy Forum, told TechCrunch that the backlash to Flock isn’t surprising and only further represents the “growing public skepticism toward technology.” Flock is a target because people can see the sensors and cameras as they drive along roads; data centers have also become physical embodiments of the resentment growing against emerging technology. “A camera at the end of your street is tangible in a way that everyday sharing and selling of personal data online is not,” Rice said.

At the state level this summer, for example, Florida barred license plate readers from state highways, while Texas paused state funding for Flock. The issues again range from data privacy concerns to misuse of the technology.

Misuse has been a real problem. In August, for example, an officer in Brentwood, Missouri was accused of using Flock cameras to stalk his ex-wife during their divorce. In September, an officer from East Texas was sentenced to prison for using Flock to stalk his ex-girlfriend and her family.

Another growing concern is how immigration officials have been able to access the technology. One case that captures the issue came in March, when Ithaca, New York, passed a resolution to terminate its contract with the Flock Group.

In the resolution, the city said there were “proven instances of Flock Safety infrastructure being used by federal agents for information purposes even without specific authorizations from the contracting locality,” and that the city had reviewed reports that “either intentionally or through its negligence,” Flock had allowed U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) and the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) to “use query results from its database of state and local ALPR [automatic license plate reader] data to investigate and persecute immigrants throughout the United States.”

“Every day, police departments across the country are using Flock technology to solve serious crimes, locate suspects, find missing people, and make a measurable impact on public safety,” Flock told TechCrunch in a statement. “While we respect every community’s decision, removing a tool that is actively helping solve violent crimes and protect communities moves public safety backward. Every Flock customer owns its data and controls who it is shared with and how it can be used. Flock does not sell data and does not have a contract with ICE.”

This August, Flock announced it would shorten the default period it stores data for new customers, from 30 days to seven. It also said agencies now need a case code before they can search the database.

The conversation around Flock echoes, in many ways, the fight over data centers. Many of these cities adopted Flock just a few years ago, and residents likely had no idea it was being installed, Adam Schwartz of the Electronic Frontier Foundation, a digital privacy nonprofit that has investigated Flock before, told TechCrunch.

That lack of awareness is similar to how data centers are popping up across the country, often before residents understand what is happening. Schwartz said police departments often decide to use Flock on their own, with little oversight from city councils. In South Windsor, Connecticut, according to local reports, Flock cameras went live last year without public warning or a vote. In October, the town voted to remove its Flock cameras.

“Grassroots activists across the country have been demanding that city councils exercise their procurement power to refuse to pay for ALPRs,” Schwartz said, referring to councils’ power to decide what the city buys. He added that activists have been fighting for years and that “many city councils are listening.”

The main argument for technology like Flock is that it helps combat crime and keep neighborhoods safe. And many local governments are not dropping Flock or similar technology; instead, they are adding restrictions on its use or looking for alternatives.

Cleveland, Ohio, for example, voted this summer to extend its contract with Flock, but has started restricting searches related to immigration. It has also limited access to the database to the Cleveland Police alone, cutting off the Northeast Ohio Regional Fusion Center, essentially a federal intelligence agency, which had initially been given access.

“They are used and needed for investigative purposes,” the city said when reached for comment. “The Department of Public Safety is currently reviewing responses to an RFP [request for proposals] that was sent out to other vendors that provide the same technology as Flock.”

But as with data centers, communities want more say in the trade-offs. “These tools can offer real public safety benefits, but communities are clearly interested in ensuring that those benefits are not at the cost of their civil liberties,” Rice said. Surveillance technology will likely remain a political hot topic, especially since Democrats now control Congress.

In September, President Donald Trump said he liked Flock’s technology “because of law enforcement,” even though a Reuters poll that same month found that more Americans dislike the technology than like it.

Also that month, Rep. Thomas Massie, a Republican from Kentucky, introduced a bill called the Flock-Off Act to bar the use of federal money to buy “Flock-style surveillance” and put other safeguards in place. In October, Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.), Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-N.Y.), and Sen. Jeff Merkley (D-Ore.) introduced the Ban Flock Act to end “the rise of AI-powered surveillance cameras.”

Juventus won 2-1 at AZ Alkmaar on a night when Teun Koopmeiners faced his old club, even though the hosts dominated the expected-goals count. The result strengthens their league-phase position ahead of Sunday's Serie A trip to Fiorentina.

The trip to Alkmaar returned Teun Koopmeiners to his former club. His brother Peer was in the AZ side that Juventus then held off. UEFA picked out the family meeting in its matchday 4 round-up.

Kolo Muani supplied the pass for the opener. Nick Woltemade was credited with the assist for the second goal. Kolo Muani earned a rating of 8.61 from the data provider, with Koopmeiners on 8.2.

AZ pulled one back in the 88th minute. Mexx Meerdink scored from an Elijah Dijkstra assist. The hosts could not find an equaliser in the remaining minutes.

The home side had 56% of possession. They completed 523 passes to Juventus’s 378. Eleven of AZ’s attempts were blocked, against three of Juventus’s.

Both teams put three shots on target. Vicario made two saves for Juventus. AZ’s expected goals total points to the chances the hosts created without converting them.

The figures are consistent with a Juventus side defending a first-half lead. Spalletti’s team committed 20 fouls to AZ’s 13. Andrea Cambiaso, Jeff Ekhator and Juan Cabal were booked for Juventus. Wesley Hoedt was the only AZ player shown a yellow card. Damian Sylwestrzak refereed the match.
