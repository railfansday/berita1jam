If the Browns are serious about staying in the playoff race, there is no excuse to lose this game. The Saints are bad and have nothing to play for. Cleveland's defense should be good enough to limit whatever New Orleans wants to do on offense.

There is already talk that if Sanders looks even remotely competent, head coach Todd Monken will bench Watson and turn Cleveland's playoff hopes over to Sanders in an attempt to "see what the Browns have." Never mind that Monken has never once even hinted that he would consider such lunacy.

Watson has not been playing good, but in Cleveland's wins he has been good enough. And there is no evidence to support the idea that the Browns would be in the playoff picture in November if Sanders had been the starter from Week 1.

It's the Browns and quarterbacks, so people tend to get irrational. But there isn't a single team in the league that would bench its starter and go with a backup in this situation, so there is no reason for the Browns to be trendsetters.

Sanders gets the start today, because that is what the backup quarterback does when the starter is out. Then, as soon as the starter is cleared to play, things go back to the way they were.

The Browns need to win this game, and it doesn't matter how they do it as long as they accomplish that goal. But no matter what happens, the quarterback pecking order is established and is not changing.

3 Millionaire-Maker Quantum Computing Stocks

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The quantum computing market is full of exciting investments, but not all of them will pan out.

Quantum computing is an emerging industry that could have several huge winners in it. There are countless early-stage quantum computing stocks that I think could provide massive returns, as this technology is set to provide massive economic value once viability is reached. While that may seem like a far-fetched technology, the reality is many in the industry believe 2029 could be the year when viable quantum computing shows up.

That's right around the corner, so what stocks have huge potential? Let's take a look.

IonQ (IONQ +23.42%) is one of the first stocks investors think of when they hear about quantum computing. It was the first quantum computing stock to go public, and it has performed exceptionally well as a company, even if the stock is currently well off its all-time highs.

IonQ currently has one of the most accurate quantum computing technologies, with a 99.99% two-qubit gate fidelity score. IonQ has achieved this thanks to its trapped-ion approach to quantum computing, which yields excellent accuracy but at the cost of processing speed.

Next year, IonQ has plans to release its 256-qubit system, which could drive huge growth for the company as clients adopt early-stage quantum computing technology. That could be the event that is needed to turn IonQ's stock around and could kick-start huge returns.

Quantinuum (QNT +25.99%) is one of the newer quantum computing stocks on the public market. However, it's quickly emerging as a top option. Quantinuum just published results of 99.9995% logical fidelity, which isn't the same measure as IonQ's, but is still a very impressive result.

Quantinuum also uses trapped-ion technology like IonQ, which gives it an advantage in the accuracy measure of quantum computing. It believes it can achieve fault-tolerant quantum computing by 2029, and if it could do that, it would achieve impressive shareholder returns along the way.

Rigetti Computing (RGTI +19.29%) is taking a different approach than these two. It's focusing on superconducting computing, which hasn't yielded the greatest accuracy results. However, superconducting computing can operate at far greater speeds than trapped-ion computing, which will be a major advantage if Rigetti can get its accuracy where it needs to be.

Rigetti's two-qubit fidelity for its largest 108-qubit system is 99.1% (comparable to IonQ's figure) or 99.9% for a single qubit (comparable to Quantinuum's figure). As Rigetti's qubit size decreases, its accuracy goes up, but those systems are so small that doing anything useful on them isn't really viable.

Rigetti's goal is to produce a 1,000-qubit system with 99.9% two-qubit gate fidelity over the next three years, but that will be significantly smaller and less accurate than anything IonQ or Quantinuum is producing. However, because of Rigetti's speed advantage, it may be a longer-term quantum computing play that could make a comeback over the next few years.

We'll see how it pans out, but it's still a viable investment option.

One thing investors need to keep in mind with this trio is that there is no guarantee any of them will reach profitability. These are very high-risk stocks that could provide a high reward, but there's no guarantee either. There are countless impressive competitors in this space, many of whom have massive cash flows to fund quantum computing operations. These three are all upstarts and must continue to get funding from outside sources until they have a commercially viable product and business in place.

If they can make it to that stage, then they will be huge winners and a successful investment. But the reality is there will be a lot of failure in this industry too. Investors need to choose the best of the best, and these three represent that.

‘Rogue One’s Best Scene Was Actually the Worst Thing to Ever Happen To Star Wars

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Julio is a Senior Author for Collider. He studied History and International Relations at university, but found his calling in cultural journalism. When he isn't writing, Julio also teaches English at a nearby school. He has lived in São Paulo most of his life, where he covers CCXP and other big events. Having loved movies, music, and TV from an early age, he prides himself in knowing every minute detail about the things he loves. When he is older, he dreams of owning a movie theater in a small countryside town.

It was hard to contain the excitement viewers felt after walking out of Rogue One: A Star Wars Story when it first premiered in 2016 — especially after that ending. The movie was an undeniable game changer for Star Wars, and Darth Vader's hallway carnage scene felt like the crowning achievement. It's incredible, indeed, and it belongs in Rogue One right at that moment. Ten years later, it remains one of the franchise's finest hours, but it's also impossible to ignore how that hallway scene impacted not just the galaxy far, far away, but the fandom, too. From how stories are conceived to fan expectations, the overall effect of Vader's hallway scene has unfortunately not been as positive as one might expect.

It's undeniable that the hallway scene is one of the coolest things about Rogue One, and a top Darth Vader moment. The way he casually walks into that hallway, lets his mechanical breath fill the room, and ignites his lightsaber with perfect timing highlights how much of a drama queen he really is, and it's all for the benefit of the scene, really. It even started a franchise trend. However, all that goes so hard, some fans forget (or ignore?) that the scene's not really about him; it's about the rebels.

The purpose of the movie is to tell the story of how the rebels got the Death Star plans, using the Rogue One team to illustrate how much the Rebellion had to sacrifice to achieve that small, preliminary victory. Vader's role is to embody the abysmal difference between the Empire and the Rebellion's capabilities, in that sense, and that's what the hallway carnage is all about: even after all that, the Empire still has a literal Sith Lord to do what its whole army and navy couldn't.

The moment really was vital to Rogue One's story. It's supposed to highlight the constant sense of urgency inherent to being a rebel, because they are always much closer to defeat than to victory, regardless of how much they sacrifice and how righteous their cause is. It's also something that we all knew Vader could do, but never actually saw; such a moment could never fit within the Original Trilogy, for example. Unfortunately, what some viewers took away from the scene was a distinctly opposite impression.

It didn't take long for Vader's hallway scene to have its meaning twisted, however, with certain parts of the fandom seeing in it as something completely different than intended. The argument that it symbolizes what Star Wars could and should be has been repeated ever since: grittier and more adult (really meaning "violent").

Pretty much every franchise release after that has been divisive, regardless of purpose or whether they fit within these expectations. Vader was brought back in Obi-Wan Kenobi, just as vicious and even more important, but it was overlooked. The Acolyte appealed to Prequel Trilogy apologists and featured an equally awesome villain with The Stranger (Manny Jacinto), but, then, it fell to discourse. Even Andor, a direct prequel to Rogue One and the grittiest and most realistic work in Star Wars, wasn't enough — there was no Vader.

None of those are perfect, of course, but the fact remains that, after that hallway scene, fanboy discourse robbed Star Wars of its right to simply be fun. Some people saw it as an outlet for their worst opinions while ignoring what the scene really means, and with their sheer volume were loud enough to influence other parts of the fandom to agree with them. None of that is the scene's own fault, of course, but it's time these things were properly faced.

As Mon Mothma (Genevieve O'Reilly) brilliantly says in Andor Season 2, ignoring the truth means becoming "vulnerable to the appetite of whatever monster screams the loudest." The truth here is that Star Wars is in worse shape now than it was before Rogue One — not because of the quality of its products, but because it let itself be affected by those screaming loudly. Instead of taking risks and showing off personality, it now feels like the franchise is trying to draw as little attention as possible, to avoid this continuous backlash.

It's a pity, because Star Wars is such a big sandbox, it makes no sense to focus only on what you want to see and disregard the rest. What has become clear in the ten years since Rogue One is that people don't actually want "gritty, realistic, and adult" stories, they want killing, one-liners and gratuitous demonstrations of strength for its own sake. They saw the form of Vader's hallway scene and loved it, while ignoring all the meaning and substance behind it.

Again, that scene belongs in Rogue One, and it fits with Vader's overall character, but, even through that lens, it's still misunderstood. People see that and think Vader is a badass, but he really isn't; he's just super strong and easily manipulated. Considering all we know about him from the Prequels and the Original Trilogy, he is actually quite pitiful. His hallway scene doesn't exist in a vacuum, so it shouldn't be viewed that way, either. Before we get more of that, we should really understand everything it really means.

After a wonderful breakfast of eggs, great bacon, and hot coffee, our tramily members Robert and Jill caught up with us. By 9:30 AM, we were all back on the trail together.

Shortly after starting, we spotted a large group of hikers boarding a tour bus. They were likely being shuttled to a spot further down the trail to collect another passport stamp before heading on to Santiago. While the popular trail moto is “Hike Your Own Hike” (HYOH), this felt more like bus your own hike (BYOH)!

It was a gorgeous morning featuring blue bird skies, warm sun, and a crisp, cool breeze. Leaving Palas de Rei, the Camino entered a beautiful patch of woods along an ancient road. As we emerged from the forest, rolling hills and towering mountains opened up in the distance. I certainly didn’t expect this much dramatic typography on this Camino.

Our next milestone was the tiny medieval hamlet of San Xulian where we stepped inside a charming chapel to collect our first stamp for the day.

Leaving the village, we stepped onto a deeply sunken path with 15-foot-high earthen banks on either side—the result of centuries of footsteps wearing down the soil. These ancient roads are easily some of my favorite sections of the entire trek.

The trail then guided us through a magnificent eucalyptus forest filled with massive, towering trees. As we exited the canopy, we ran right back into the weekend crowds. Having developed our solid “trail legs” over hundreds of miles, Knockerz and I were moving at a much faster pace than the casual walkers. Passing them proved tricky, however, as large groups frequently blocked the narrow paths without realizing it.

As we approached Melide, we noticed numerous tour buses parked along the road. Apparently, this was where the “BYOH” crowd rejoined the path after skipping the 9 miles of walking in between! Melide is also a major trail milestone: it’s the official junction where the Camino Primitivo merges into the Camino Francés.

We arrived at 1:00 PM to a town bustling with energy. Knowing that siesta was fast approaching and the streets would soon turn into a ghost town, we set out to grab lunch immediately.

Knockerz was craving a hamburger, though she was disappointed to find the beef patty was blended with pork. Meanwhile, I couldn’t pass up Melide’s most famous specialty: Pulpo a la Gallega (Galician-style octopus). Traditionally boiled in large copper kettles, sliced into thick pieces, seasoned with paprika, and served on round wooden plates, it was delicious! Knockerz got a bit squeamish watching me eat the tentacles with their large suckers attached, but it was a highlight for me.

After lunch, we searched for Melide’s famous local pastry—a traditional anise-flavored donut (melindres)—but siesta had already begun and the bakeries were locked up tight. Fortunately, we found a tiny grocery store that remained open and picked up a bottle of Crianza Rioja wine.

It turned out to be a perfect afternoon with warm sun and a cool, refreshing breeze. We spent the rest of the day relaxing on the top terrace of our albergue, sipping Rioja while soaking in sweeping views of the distant Galician hills.

Gold is still 25% below its peak even as demand reaches $188B in Q3

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

October's losses show why knowing who's buying gold is only part of understanding its price.

Central banks bought more gold in the third quarter of 2026 than consumers worldwide bought for jewelry.

According to the World Gold Council's latest demand report, central banks and other official institutions acquired an estimated net 321 metric tons, while jewelry consumption came to 313 tons.

In the same quarter a year earlier, jewelry buyers had consumed about 150 tons more than central banks acquired.

By September, the institutions buying gold for their reserves had overtaken the people buying it to wear, although roughly 63% of estimated official demand couldn't be traced through publicly reported country-level purchases.

The price barely kept pace with Bitcoin, which gained 42.7% during the quarter versus gold's 3.84%. Both attracted money through exchange-traded funds, so this wasn't a straightforward case of investors ditching one for the other. They bought both and got different results.

However, Bitcoin's lead came crashing down in October: gold fell 6.3%, while Bitcoin lost 7.3%. Governments kept accumulating bullion, and investors kept believing in scarce assets, but neither belief did much for that month's returns.

The buyers behind those beliefs, and their reasons for holding on, explain rather more than the safe-haven label does.

Global gold demand reached $188 billion in the third quarter, up 22% from a year earlier. That sounds like an impressive shopping spree until you weigh the purchases.

Buyers took home 1% less metal, at 1,369 tons, while the average quarterly price was 23% higher. Much of the extra spending covered the price increase rather than buying more gold.

That was especially apparent in jewelry, where consumption fell 17% to 313 tons, the weakest third quarter in the council's records since 2000. Buyers in China and India cut purchases or chose lighter and lower-carat pieces, adjusting what they bought to what they could afford.

Physical investment wasn't immune either: demand for bars and coins fell 19% to 268 tons. Paying more per ounce didn't make every buyer more eager to own one.

Central banks' net purchases rose 42% to 321 tons, while gold-backed ETFs added 211 tons, reversing the previous quarter's withdrawals.

Those purchases helped offset weaker demand from jewelry buyers and physical investors, giving the market support from buyers who wanted gold as a financial holding even as others balked at the cost.

The categories don't divide neatly into institutions buying and households retreating. Individuals own gold through ETFs, and institutions can own bars and coins.

But the numbers do show that demand weakened in traditional consumer and physical-investment markets, while official reserves and listed funds absorbed more metal. Gold was becoming a harder purchase to justify at the counter and an easier allocation to defend in a portfolio.

Of the estimated 321 tons in net purchases, only about 120 tons appeared in publicly reported country-level activity.

China disclosed additions of 63 tons and Poland 40 tons, making them the largest reported buyers, but the country disclosures leave approximately 201 tons of the council's estimate unaccounted for in the public figures.

Reporting delays and purchases governments don't disclose can explain activity missing from the country data, and the broader estimate may be revised. The gap alone also doesn't establish secret, coordinated purchases.

We have a much better estimate of the total than a complete account of who bought it, which limits how confidently we can interpret the buying.

Central banks acquired an estimated 675 tons in the first nine months of 2026, exceeding their purchases over the same period of 2025.

Their appetite is large enough to offset weakness elsewhere, but incomplete disclosures make it harder to attribute that appetite to particular governments or motives. The survey evidence offers more detail on the latter, with some important limits of its own.

In the World Gold Council's 2026 central-bank survey, 74% of respondents expected the US dollar's share of global reserves to be lower in five years. That gives some basis for the argument that reserve managers want less dependence on the dollar.

The IMF's second-quarter foreign-exchange reserve data put the dollar's share at 56.7%, down from 57.18% in the preceding quarter. It remained the dominant reserve currency, with dollar holdings largely steady as holdings in other currencies expanded.

This series also excludes monetary gold, so it can't tell us how much gold replaced dollars.

Even the funding of gold purchases is less straightforward than the familiar sell-Treasuries-buy-bullion argument allows. Half the survey respondents said they'd finance new gold purchases through domestic programs using local currency, while 38% said they'd sell existing reserve assets.

Buying locally produced gold with local currency doesn't require a corresponding sale of US government debt, and the quarterly demand totals can't identify what financed every purchase.

Reserve managers can want more gold without wanting out of everything else. Bullion gives them an asset whose value doesn't depend on an issuer honoring a financial obligation, making it a way to reduce reliance on other governments' currencies and debt.

However, the bargain still includes a market price, and that price can fall enough to make the word “protection” feel rather generous over shorter periods.

Gold finished October at $3,913 an ounce, down 6.3% for the month and roughly 25% below its January peak, according to the World Gold Council's November market commentary.

Central bank demand hadn't spared holders that loss, because even a persistent buyer can't dictate the price everyone else trades at. Futures positions and the need to raise cash can overwhelm the reassurance of a government adding bullion to its reserves.

Global gold-backed ETFs attracted $2.7 billion in net inflows during October, adding 19 tons and taking holdings to a record 4,275 tons. Their combined assets under management still fell 6% to $542 billion, as the lower gold price more than offset the new money.

The funds held more metal and were worth less, an entirely possible outcome that gets lost when inflows are treated as a verdict on returns.

The broader point is consistent with a July 2026 IMF study, which found that gold's ability to hedge market risk depends on the shock and the economic conditions around it. Gold can help diversify a reserve portfolio over time without reliably sheltering it during extreme stress.

Someone reducing exposure to sovereign credit over several years and someone trying to avoid a loss this month may buy the same asset, but they're asking it to do different jobs.

Bitcoin gave investors a much better third quarter, gaining 42.7% to end September around $83,570, according to 21Shares' quarterly analysis.

US spot Bitcoin ETFs added about 79,800 BTC, worth about $6.2 billion, reversing roughly $4.8 billion in second-quarter withdrawals.

Their purchases were nearly twice the estimated 41,300 BTC newly mined over the period, giving the rally a measurable source of demand through funds available to professional and individual investors alike.

That comparison with new supply is striking, although newly mined coins are only one source of Bitcoin available to buy. Existing holders own a far larger stock, and some were reducing their positions while ETFs accumulated.

21Shares estimated that balances in the long-term-holder cohort fell by 248,000 BTC during the quarter. The figure isn't a direct count of exchange sales, since coins can leave the cohort through transfers or other classification effects, but it prevents ETF buying from being mistaken for a market in which everyone was adding to their holdings.

Global gold-backed funds attracted a record $31 billion in third-quarter inflows and added 211 tons of bullion, even as the metal's price gain fell well short of Bitcoin's.

The dollar totals cover different markets: the gold figure is global, while the Bitcoin figure covers US spot ETFs. Their respective market sizes and investor bases differ too, so treating the totals as a head-to-head contest would tell us little.

Investors added exposure to both assets, but their quarterly returns differed by nearly 39%, which offers little support for a simple account of Bitcoin rallying because money was leaving gold. It also limits what ETF data alone can explain.

Listed funds supplied demand in both markets, while trading elsewhere, including derivatives positions and borrowed money, helped determine how far each price moved.

October narrowed that enormous performance gap in an unwelcome way. Bitcoin fell 7.3% to about $77,500, while gold lost 6.3% over the same month.

Gold ETFs continued to attract net investment globally, led by Europe and Asia, while North American funds recorded modest withdrawals. On Nov. 4, Reuters reported that the largest Bitcoin-backed funds tracked by LSEG/Lipper lost $215 million in October.

While the totals don't establish that investors moved money from Bitcoin to gold, they show that net buying persisted across global bullion funds while the tracked Bitcoin funds lost money to withdrawals.

The World Gold Council pointed to reductions in speculative positions, revised expectations for Federal Reserve policy, and rotation between assets as possible contributors to gold's decline.

Bitcoin contended with weaker tech stocks, tighter credit conditions, the Binance hack, and the US midterm elections. Different sellers had different reasons to act, even though holders of both assets ended the month with losses.

Scarcity offers little defense against someone who needs cash or wants less exposure to volatile markets. Central-bank purchases can absorb gold without guaranteeing its price, just as Bitcoin's supply limit can constrain issuance without guaranteeing buyers at the price its holders would prefer.

October put both propositions on display, even while some investors continued to add bullion to their portfolios.

The sovereign comparison needs a careful approach, because a government owning Bitcoin doesn't necessarily mean it has entered the market to buy it.

The US established a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve in March 2025, formally giving Bitcoin a place among assets the federal government can retain and manage.

However, the executive order creating the reserve directed that it be funded primarily with Bitcoin already held through criminal or civil asset forfeitures.

Officials were also authorized to devise budget-neutral ways to acquire additional Bitcoin without incremental taxpayer costs. That permission and the decision to retain existing coins have policy importance, but neither establishes a recurring purchase program comparable with central banks adding gold to their reserves.

That's why putting seized Bitcoin into a reserve can happen without buying a single additional coin on the market.

The World Gold Council's quarterly figures estimate net additions to official gold holdings. The US reserve order governs, in large part, what happens to assets the government already owns.

Gold already has an established role in central bank balance sheets and reserve management. Bitcoin's newer sovereign role could evolve, but ETF access, government recognition, and official purchases each affect demand through a different route.

Investors can't assume progress on one will produce the buying associated with another, however attractive the reserve announcement sounds.

Interest-rate expectations and access to cash will continue to test both markets. Stronger US employment or inflation readings can encourage expectations of tighter Federal Reserve policy, increasing the appeal of interest-bearing assets relative to gold and potentially making financial conditions less accommodating for Bitcoin.

Weaker readings can support expectations of easier policy, although a deterioration severe enough to prompt fears of an economic slowdown can also leave investors wanting cash.

Neither the gold-demand report nor Bitcoin's ETF inflows can settle how investors will react to the next release. They can tell us more about who has been buying and how readily those buyers might leave.

Central banks can accumulate gold over several years, while ETF investors can cut exposure much faster. Consumers can buy lighter jewelry, and traders using borrowed money can face demands to reduce positions precisely when they'd prefer to wait.

That mix helps explain why persistent belief in an asset can coexist with a sharp fall in its price.

The reserve manager diversifying away from another government's obligations may have little reason to sell bullion during a correction, while an investor facing losses elsewhere may sell the same metal because it's readily tradable.

Bitcoin's fixed supply doesn't remove that need for cash, and official recognition doesn't give its holders the resources or mandate to ride out every downturn.

Central banks overtaking jewelry buyers shows how gold demand is being redistributed, and October's losses show the limits of what those purchases can promise other holders.

Someone buying for protection needs the asset's behavior to fit the risk they're trying to reduce and the period they can afford to hold it.

Gold can serve a reserve manager well over years and disappoint an investor over weeks, while Bitcoin can deliver a spectacular quarter without becoming a dependable place to wait out the next sell-off.
