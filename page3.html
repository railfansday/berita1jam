Embedded banking partnerships and cross-border compliance capabilities are quietly building structural advantages that could sustain growth for years.

On its first-quarter fiscal 2027 earnings call, held on Oct. 28, 2026, ADP (ADP -0.43%) raised its full-year fiscal 2027 revenue outlook to about 6% growth. Steady payroll demand and operational productivity gains from artificial intelligence (AI) continue to expand operating margins. For long-term investors, the company's ability to lower its cost to serve while expanding its software footprint strengthens its competitive position. Three things management said stand out.

ADP is rolling out The Zone, its proprietary AI platform, across client service functions to automate workflows and reduce contact volume. Operational productivity expands operating margins while lowering the cost to serve millions of workers.

By embedding payroll and human capital management, or HCM, software directly into digital banking platforms like PNC Bank, ADP opens new distribution channels. Integrating software into bank portals lowers client acquisition costs and deepens small business customer retention.

Navigating localized labor laws, tax regulations, and data privacy requirements across international borders creates steep switching costs for multinational clients. Expanding Lyric HCM, ADP's enterprise human capital management platform, across Europe deepens its structural moat.

Client hiring remains steady across core verticals, and operational efficiency continues to climb. ADP relies on global scale, deep regulatory expertise, and high switching costs to defend its position as it tracks client retention.

Maintaining customer trust and strict regulatory compliance remains vital as automation reshapes workforce management. Strategic investments in embedded channels position the business for durable multiyear compounding.

16 New & Returning DC Shows That Could Be Affected by James Gunn’s Exit

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The DC Universe and the larger DC Studios are going through a seismic shift now, and this could impact several new and returning TV shows. James Gunn and Peter Safran are out at DC, with the duo no longer overseeing their announced DCU Chapter One slate, which has released movies like Superman and Supergirl as well as shows like Lanterns and Creature Commandos.

Gunn and Safran will be replaced by former Marvel executive Jeremy Latcham, who worked with Kevin Feige right from the start of the Marvel Cinematic Universe with the release of 2008's Iron Man. Given the recent change, Latcham has yet to reveal what his vision for the DCU and the projects outside the shared universe entails. That could mean either keeping some planned returning and new DC TV shows or completely scrapping them, with shows to note including:

Creature Commandos season 2 is one of the upcoming DC shows to be among the safe releases at the studio, Gunn or not. The adult animated series' first season was well-received, standing at a near-perfect 93% critics' score on Rotten Tomatoes, and work on season 2 is well underway. As recently as October 24, Gunn confirmed that Creature Commandos season 2 would debut on HBO Max after Man of Tomorrow, so in the second half of 2027.

Mister Miracle is another adult animated DC series that should have no problem making it to the small screen in 2027. The show is based on one of the most acclaimed comics books in recent memory, with the writer of that DC Comics series, Tom King, also serving as Mister Miracle's showrunner. Additionally, DC recently confirmed the castings of The Hunger Games franchise star Josh Hutcherson as Mister Miracle and Below actress Mackenzie Davis as Big Barda, so walking back on those castings would not look good. Finally, Darkseid will make his DCU debut in the show, making it essential.

The People v. Gorilla Grodd is in slightly shakier territory than the other two previous DCU series. While it is a live-action series that has already wrapped production, and thus would not make much sense financially to scrap, Warner Bros. Discovery previously shelved a full Batgirl movie with Michael Keaton as Batman and Brendan Fraser as the villain. As such, if the new regime deems The People v. Gorilla Grodd too silly for the DC brand in live-action, there is a chance it could get scrapped. Still, given the many DCU returning characters, expect it in 2027 for now.

HBO's Lanterns not being renewed by now lit up a warning sign, given how the live-action DC show broke viewership records throughout season 1 and stands at a stellar 92% critics' score on Rotten Tomatoes. It is true that many DC fans complained about making a Green Lantern series grounded and more of a gritty detective show than a cosmic superhero adventure. Additionally, Lanterns season 1 wrapped up its story with a couple of loose ends, setting up John Stewart for Gunn's Man of Tomorrow. Lanterns season 2 feels like it could go either way.

Peacemaker season 3 is a DC project that fans might want to keep off their minds for the time being, as it is very unlikely to happen. Gunn himself said at the end of season 2 that there were no active plans for another season. With the creative behind the show not out of the DC Studios co-CEO job, the new regime should not bring in someone else to take over Peacemaker, a project that is so connected to Gunn's creative DNA.

The Penguin season 2 is most likely dead at DC Studios despite being loved by fans and critics alike. No significant movement was made on a possible new season with Gunn still in charge, and with Skydance now looking at franchises, to get the DCU Batman up and running, there is a strong chance Matt Reeves' long-delayed The Batman universe plans will call it a day with 2028's The Batman - Part II.

Poison Ivy was one of the most recent DC shows to be reported to enter development. In late October, The Penguin creator Lauren LeFranc was revealed to be developing a live-action Poison Ivy series set outside The Batman universe, thus likely in Gunn's DCU. Given the modest success of Clayface on the big screen so far, Poison Ivy might have to fight an uphill battle despite The Penguin being a hit at HBO, with focus likely to shift to bigger characters.

The animated Harley Quinn series starring Kaley Cuoco as the Batman character exists since before Gunn's tenure at DC. The show's long-running success on HBO Max and the fact that it is both animated and set within its own universe with spin-off Kite Man: Hell Yeah!, means a renewal might not conflict with the new DCU plans. That said, its fate was not a priority for DC Studios with Gunn, and it should not be one now with Latcham, so the limbo period could be lengthy.

Batman: Caped Crusader was dropped by HBO Max during development, with the DC series spearheaded by Batman: The Animated Series' Bruce Timm later moving to Prime Video. The show received a two-season order at Prime Video, with season 2 having debuted this year. It remains to be seen what Prime Video thinks about another season, but as far as the new DC goes, the show is unlikely to be a priority. With Matt Reeves attached as an executive producer, maybe that helps it continue.

My Adventures with Green Lantern is one of the recent DC animated series to enter development, being a spin-off to My Adventures with Superman. Speaking to ScreenRant in June, one of the showrunners of the Superman series, showrunners Jake Wyatt, claimed that the new show's release was pending definition from the Warner Bros. Discovery and Paramount merger. While development is moving along, this predicament puts it in danger.

The acquisition is expected to add a new financing layer to Securitize, allowing the platform to offer asset-backed lending on top of its existing tokenized asset offerings.

Asset tokenization firm Securitize's NYSE-listed stock Securitize Corp. (SECZ) rose significantly after markets closed on Monday following the announcement of its third-quarter results and its acquisition arrangement with River One.

Securitize reported 13% year-over-year growth in Q3 revenue to $18.6 million, compared with a 5% year-over-year decline to $14.4 million in the second quarter.

The company posted a net loss of $66.3 million, which translates into a net loss per diluted share of $0.41. Around 90% of its net loss primarily came from non-cash expenses and accounting changes associated with its business combination and public listing, Securitize said.

Average tokenized assets rose 39% from a year ago to $5 billion, hitting an internal record. The firm's board of directors also authorized a $50 million share repurchase program.

Securitize's shares, which closed Monday at $6.97, surged 12.20% in after-hours trading to $7.82.

Meanwhile, Securitize announced on the same day that it has entered into a definitive agreement to acquire the credit infrastructure company, which is developing technology to connect stablecoin capital with traditional lending markets.

The acquisition is expected to add a new layer of financing to Securitize, enabling the platform to offer asset-backed lending alongside its existing tokenized asset offerings. The transaction is expected to close in the fourth quarter.

"By combining Securitize's tokenization infrastructure with River One's warehouse financing technology, the companies aim to enable institutions to bring financial assets onchain and access financing against those assets through an integrated platform," Securitize wrote in a press release, further explaining that the transaction allows the firm to enter the $6 trillion global private asset-backed finance market.

Founded in 2025, River One was incubated by Framework Ventures and is backed by both Framework Ventures and Maple Finance. It specializes in warehouse financing backed by portfolios of income-generating assets, including loans, receivables, and other collateral.

River One is establishing ties with capital providers in the stablecoin ecosystem to finance high-quality, income-generating assets. In practice, it uses stablecoin capital to fund the facilities on the back end while borrowers draw and repay in ordinary dollars, so the crypto layer stays mostly invisible to them. This is to maintain traditional credit underwriting standards and legal protections while using blockchain to improve financing efficiency.

"River One brings a talented team, deep expertise in asset-backed financing and blockchain technology that will allow us to participate in asset origination and lending markets," said Carlos Domingo, Chairman and CEO of Securitize. "This opens up a significant new addressable market for Securitize, with opportunities for higher-value services, additional revenue streams and greater monetization across our platform."

How the American French Film Festival Is Bringing French Cinema to 3,000 Los Angeles High School Students With Packed Screenings of ‘In Waves’

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The American French Film Festival, which celebrated its 30th anniversary this year, has been on a journey to cultivate a new generation of French movie lovers for two decades with its educational program for Los Angeles high school students.

This milestone edition brought around 3,000 students to the DGA theater for five packed screenings of “In Waves,” Phuong Mai Nguyen’s heart-wrenching animated feature which was bought Netflix at Cannes. Adapted from AJ Dungo’s graphic novel at set in Southern California, “In Waves” is a love story between a shy teenage boy and the girl who teaches him how to surf and how to live, before she succumbs to cancer.

The screenings, which drew applause and tears throughout, also gave students the opportunity to engage in the post-screening Q&A with the film’s producer, Priscilla Bertin, and screenwriter Fanny Burdino.

Spearheaded by Anouchka Van Riel, the festival deputy director, the program brings together more than 60 schools over five days, with roughly half coming from the public school system and half from private institutions. It also reaches beyond French-language classes to students studying performing arts and other creative disciplines.

In an interview with Variety, Van Riel discussed the importance of the programme to allow younger audiences to discover international cinema at a time when arthouse moviegoers are aging and foreign-language films face growing challenges in U.S. theaters. She also talked about the program’s rapid growth, why subtitles are no longer the barrier they once were, and the powerful response to “In Waves” which Netflix is positioning in the Awards race.

How many students and schools participate in the program?

We have more than 60 schools participating over five days, so that’s more than 12 schools a day. We organize five screenings of the same film, each with around 600 students. That’s approximately 3,000 students every year. I’ve built a database of around 300 schools, and we start reaching out in May. Within a week of sending the first email, half the seats — around 1,500 — are already booked. Then I spend the summer filling the remaining places. There’s enormous demand. We also make a point of having a mix of public and private schools, roughly 50/50. That’s very important to us. For public schools, transportation can be a challenge. Some have to apply for grants just to pay for the buses. There’s an entire economy around organizing these trips, and a lot depends on the teachers.

How does the festival work with teachers to prepare students for the screenings?

We work with the American Association of Teachers of French, which prepares two educational guides, one in French and one in English. The French-language guide is around 100 pages long. It’s incredibly detailed and allows teachers to explore the film from multiple perspectives, including its themes and cinematic language. The guides are designed to help students study the film before or after the screening. And we don’t just work with French-language classes. Around half of the participating classes are English-speaking. Many Los Angeles high schools have performing arts programs, and those students come because they’re interested in cinema and want to meet the filmmakers. The majority of these kids have never attended a film screening followed by a Q&A. That’s something we want to change.

That’s surprising in Los Angeles, where the film industry is everywhere.

It’s extraordinary. I’m a member of the Producers Guild, and I receive invitations to screenings and conversations with filmmakers almost every day. There are incredible events happening constantly, but these kids don’t have access to them. Of course, if their parents work in the industry, that’s different. But otherwise, most students aren’t exposed to that world. I grew up in a small town in Alsace, and I remember my French teacher taking us to the movies all the time when I was in middle school. Some of those screenings even had Q&As. There’s a tradition of that in France that doesn’t really exist here in the same way. We used to organize masterclasses for university students, including at USC, but we realized it was difficult to attract them. They’re already surrounded by so many opportunities on campus. With high school students, we can work directly through the teachers. That’s how we’ve developed this program.

Is the goal ultimately to cultivate a younger audience for French cinema?

Absolutely. It’s about renewing the audience. A couple of years ago, we screened “The Marching Band,” and when the film was eventually released theatrically in the U.S., I was invited to moderate a Q&A. I loved the film, so I was very happy to do it. But the theater was only half full, and I don’t think there was a single person under 65. That’s the problem. Foreign-language films have traditionally been considered niche in the U.S. Streaming platforms have helped change that perception somewhat, but we still need to familiarize younger audiences with these films. That’s why this program is so important.

Are subtitles still an obstacle for younger American audiences?

Not nearly as much as people think. We’ve conducted surveys with students after the screenings, and more than 80% say subtitles aren’t a barrier to watching foreign-language films. It’s not a completely scientific study, but it gives us a good indication of how attitudes are changing. Between streaming platforms, social media and series like “Squid Game,” young people have become accustomed to watching subtitled content. Sometimes there’s still a little hesitation before the screening. But once they’re sitting in the theater, they realize it’s easy. I really don’t think subtitles are the problem for this generation anymore. Distribution is a major part of the problem. That’s why streaming platforms have become so important for the circulation of foreign-language films. But there’s also the question of exposure. If young people aren’t given opportunities to discover these films, they’re not necessarily going to seek them out themselves.

This year’s selection was “In Waves.” What made it particularly suitable for the program?

There’s a wonderful connection between this film and the festival. The producer, Priscilla Bertin, had already come to the festival two years ago with “Wild Diamond,” and at the time she was in post-production on “In Waves.” We became familiar with the project very early, and it made complete sense for us to bring it back this year. What’s interesting is that the film is based on an American graphic novel that was discovered by a French producer in a secondhand bookstore in France and developed into a movie. We explain that background to the students before the screenings, including why it’s considered a French film and the response has been extraordinary.

The screenings were packed, and the students seemed particularly moved by the film. What was the atmosphere like?

It was very striking. At the first screening, I went straight into introducing the filmmakers for the Q&A, and I realized something wasn’t right because the students were still processing what they’d just watched. Usually, when a film ends, the students immediately line up to ask questions. But with “In Waves,” it took time. Every day, there was this moment of silence. The film had affected them so deeply that they needed a little time before they could speak. There’s something cathartic about sitting in a dark theater with other people, crying together and sharing the same emotions. You feel safe because everyone is experiencing that grief together. I think young people have fewer opportunities to experience cinema that way now. That’s what made these screenings so special.

You've got half a minute and one thumb to win

When we reviewed Major Mayhem, the iPhone 4S was brand new. Rocket Jump, the studio behind it, is back, this time with Rocket Rally, which is a physics-driven arcade rally racer that’s up for pre-registration on iOS and Android.

If it predates your phone, Major Mayhem was a one-finger cover shooter. Your soldier ran between cover by himself, and you held a finger down to fire. It was brash and silly and very easy to pick up for a minute, and it also earned a Silver Award from us.

Rocket Rally takes that one-input approach to rally. Races last thirty seconds, you play it one-handed in portrait, and the cars run on a custom arcade physics system the studio built itself. Time Attack and Head to Head sit alongside Rally X4 and Rally X6.

Around that is a solo career set in locations around the world, where you buy cars, upgrade them and build up a garage, plus daily online competition. There are over 100 stages across four environments at launch, with more promised every month, and hundreds of cosmetic options for your liveries.

Thirty seconds doesn’t feel like a lot, but I think it’s the right length for something like this. I don’t see myself completing a three-lap race on my phone while I’m en route somewhere, but a rally stage before my bus reaches its stop? I can see myself getting behind that. Of course, that means it leaves no time to learn handling that doesn’t make sense straight away.

Rocket Rally is expected to release on December 15th, although that could change. It will be free-to-play with ads. Pre-registration is currently open on the App Store and Google Play.
