Excuse me if I’m reading between the lines too much, but it seems like you’re trying to weigh the benefits of running out the clock by continuing to work vs. having a concrete plan that you can track your real retirement against. Most people I know definitely choose the former option, but I don’t think you need to overcomplicate the latter.

TPAW planner is a fairly good tool if you’re trying to figure out whether you can support temporary periods of higher spend. It gives you a month-by-month figure out what you could spend in the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentile of return outcomes. You could look at the range and see if it seems realistic to you. You will be projecting assumptions just like all the other tools, but I think it is clearer mental model to consider “could I live on $x” than it is to see it as a Y% of success.
Thanks for the TPAW rec! I've glanced it a few times but never concluded that it offered something more useful than I was getting from Pralana Online. To be totally transparent, I'm just iterating through possible scenarios in Pralana, but none of them rely on coming to any conclusion based on any year's withdrawal rate percentage. I mean, I do have numbers in the 4-5% range in mind when I look at what Pralana's deterministic projections show me needing to withdraw based on what I've told it about in expense assumptions but that's only casually useful as a sort of qualitative observation that most years are nicely below that 4-5% range. But I don't conclude anything from that. I much more heavily rely on Monte Carlo runs and testing sensitivity to things like my asset class return assumptions over time, AA, retirement date, LTC costs, how big a backstop the paid-off house would be, etc.

I think I was just wanting to validate that I'm not fundamentally missing some aspect of SWRs that people are leaning into. Your last sentence kind of sums up what's seemed off to me about SWR-based analyses:
“could I live on $x”
It's just too simple a question to try to answer in that form (unless you try to compensate like delamer suggested by modeling lifetime lumpy expenses as a fixed annual expense).

On OnlyFans, one-time transactions are winning out over subscriptions

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Back in August, OnlyFans announced that 5,076 creators have brought in at least $1 million each since the NSFW hub kicked off in 2016, with total creator payouts adding up to $30 billion. About 20% of that $30 billion—roughly $6.2 billion—was delivered to creators during the year that ended on November 30, 2025.

That last figure represents a solid increase in how much creators are making on OnlyFans—but the way they’re making money on the platform has evolved in recent years, too.

According to a recent report from Giggster, the vast majority of the $6.2 billion paid to OnlyFans creators in 2025 stemmed from non-subscription-based transactions. To compile its report (titled “Behind the OnlyFans Paycheck: Creator Payments, Platform Fees and U.S. Rankings”), the online marketplace combined “illustrative payment calculations with third-party estimates of OnlyFans creator accounts across all 50 states and Washington, D.C.” and examined “company-level revenue by payment type and revenue attributed to the U.S.”

Overall, the report estimated that 700,000 OnlyFans creator accounts are active in the U.S., a number that makes up roughly 28% of the platform’s global total. And despite representing less than 30% of OnlyFans accounts, Giggster found that ~62% of OnlyFans’ platform revenue ($965.4 million) was attributed to the U.S. in the 2025 fiscal year.

One of the report’s most significant findings, however, relates to the rising importance of non-subscription-based revenue—aka payments from one-time transactions like tips, paywalled posts, paid direct messages, and custom content requests. According to Giggster, those payments generated 73% of OnlyFans’ platform revenue in the 2025 fiscal year (up from roughly 40% in 2020 and 60% in 2024).

OnlyFans isn’t the only creator-driven platform leaning into one-time payments, either. In a 2024 end-of-year post, Patreon noted that creators on its platform “increased monthly revenue of one-time purchases by 4x” year-over-year. More recently, a study from Ipsos commissioned by TikTok predicted that over 60,000 U.S.-based TikTok LIVE creators would make “more than the median part-time monthly income” from gift earnings in 2025.

YouTube has devoted significant resources to one-time transactions, too. In a January blog post, CEO Neal Mohan reassured creators that the video platform would “continue to invest” in “fan funding features like Jewels and gifts,” adding that “we know these are important revenue streams, especially outside the U.S., for creators like Santiago Matias, who uses Super Chat as a way for fans to influence the outcome of his hit reality series.”

At the end of the day, the rising importance of one-time transactions demonstrates an important truth for creators: if you want to earn more revenue, give fans more ways to pay you.

S&P considers Nvidia credit downgrade over governance and ‘key man’ risks

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

S&P Global Ratings, one of the three major credit rating agencies, is considering downgrading Nvidia’s rating, citing concerns about the company’s management and governance practices.

Despite Nvidia’s strong financial performance, the ouster and reinstatement of CEO Jensen Huang has raised concerns about “weak executive oversight and lack of succession planning” and “key man risk,” meaning heavy reliance on Huang for its future success, S&P said in a statement.

The agency expects to finish its review within 90 days and could cut Nvidia’s rating from AA to AA-, the fourth-highest on its scale.

Although Nvidia is certain to keep its investment-grade rating, a downgrade could raised its borrowing costs. Because Nvidia backstops neoclouds and other infrastructure companies that lack strong credit on their own, a lower rating could make their debt costlier or force them to post more collateral.

In August, Nvidia announced plans to arrange $500 billion in financing for AI infrastructure with six financial institutions, including Goldman Sachs, Apollo, and Blackstone. Under the original terms, Nvidia would guarantee 25% of the loans. But even before Huang’s ouster and return, lenders had been demanding that Nvidia guarantee more, Bloomberg reported.

A downgrade would likely prompt lenders to demand even larger guarantees.
