Retirees: Is Now the Time to Ditch S&P 500 Funds?

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The recent Nvidia scandal underscores just how vulnerable the broad index can be to tech stocks.

By David Jagielski, CPA – Nov 23, 2026 at 6:13PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Vanguard S&P 500 ETF ›

The stock market has been volatile in recent weeks, with the scandal involving Nvidia and its CEO, Jensen Huang, resigning and then coming back within a few weeks, highlighting just how erratic tracking the S&P 500 can be. Last week, amid the Nvidia sell-off, the broad index fell to below 7,000 -- its lowest point since April. Now, with Nvidia's stock jumping on the news of Huang being reinstated as CEO, the index added more than 300 points in a single day.

The wild swings due to tech are something investors have to come to terms with if they track the index via funds such as the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO +4.46%). While over the long run it can be a good way to invest in the markets, the exposure the index has to tech these days makes it a riskier-than-normal option.

For risk-averse investors and retirees, it begs the question: is investing in S&P 500 index funds still a good move right now?

Through the S&P 500, investors gain access to the largest, most valuable companies on U.S. markets. But these days, that means a lot of tech exposure, as it's been tech stocks that have been rising feverishly due to artificial intelligence (AI) in recent years.

Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, and Alphabet are the largest holdings in the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF. Just those five holdings alone account for close to 30% of the Vanguard S&P 500's portfolio. And that's just among the largest holdings. There are also many companies that indirectly have significant exposure to AI, including energy companies involved in data center build-outs.

Tracking the S&P 500 makes sense for the long haul. But during a period of five years or less, a lot can happen. If a crash occurs, it can take a long time for the market to recover, time that retirees can't spare.

For most long-term investors, tracking the S&P 500 still makes a lot of sense, even if there might be a potential crash or correction on the horizon.

Retirees, however, may be better off considering dividend-oriented funds to track instead, which don't have nearly as much exposure to tech. Some funds don't have any exposure at all. During shorter periods, those types of investments can offer much less volatility and be safer options to hang on to.

One of the world’s biggest zinc mines is getting Alaska’s largest solar farm

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Red Dog Mine in Alaska burns about 40,000 gallons of diesel a day to keep one of the world’s largest zinc mines running, and now, an 8.8-megawatt (MW) solar farm and battery storage will help cut that fuel use.

The Alaska Energy Authority’s board unanimously approved a nearly $5 million loan for the project through its Power Project Fund. Tugliq Red Dog1 will build, own, and operate the roughly $35 million solar project and sell the electricity to mine operator Teck Alaska. The state’s loan review says site work began in June and expects the solar and storage to come online in October 2028.

Red Dog sits about 105 miles north of the Arctic Circle, on land owned by NANA, an Iñupiat-owned Alaska Native corporation. The array would be larger than any solar farm operating in Alaska today, as KOTZ’s Desiree Hagen reported for Alaska Public Media. Hagen reported that the plans call for more than 15,000 panels across around 30 acres.

The mine gets all its power from on-site diesel generators. In summer, it can draw so much electricity that Teck has to cut power elsewhere at the site. The solar array and the 8.8 MW, 15 megawatt-hour (MWh) battery will give the operator another source of power during those crunches.

The state of Alaska expects the solar and storage project to cover about 5% of the zinc mine’s electricity needs under its current operating plan. The project could do even more after mining ends, covering about 90% of the site’s annual electricity needs.

Teck expects the mine’s existing deposits to support operations through 2031, and it’s studying whether to develop two nearby deposits. If that expansion goes ahead, the state projects the solar and battery project would cover about 3% of the larger operation’s electricity needs. If mining ends, demand will drop sharply, but the site will still need electricity for cleanup and long-term water treatment.

Researchers Just Sent an Unblockable Message Through a Solid Wall Using Gravity

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Researchers have demonstrated an unusual way to transmit information through solid barriers using gravity rather than electromagnetic signals.

Physicists at the University of Queensland have transmitted a message through a brick wall using gravity instead of electromagnetic signals. Their unconventional communication system relied on a moving elevator counterweight and an extremely sensitive instrument capable of detecting tiny changes in the gravitational field.

The experiment took place in UQ’s Parnell Building, where researchers moved a 1.8-metric-ton (about 4,000 pounds) elevator counterweight between two positions representing binary 0 and 1. A gravimeter positioned in a nearby bathroom detected the gravitational changes and decoded a single word: “Gravity.”

Unlike radio waves and other electromagnetic signals, gravitational fields cannot be blocked by ordinary building materials. This means information carried by changes in gravity could potentially reach places where conventional wireless communication struggles.

Dr. Charles Woffinden from UQ’s School of Mathematics and Physics explained the advantage. “Unlike the electromagnetic signals used in modern wireless communication, a gravitational signal cannot simply be screened out by placing ordinary material in its path,” he said. “Anyone who has lost phone reception in a tunnel or struggled with Wi-Fi through a solid wall will understand the appeal.”

“While the present system is far from practical, it demonstrates the possibility of communicating using a method that ordinary physical barriers cannot block.”

The communication experiment grew out of an unrelated effort to measure vehicle weights without physical contact. Dr. Andrew Groszek and his colleagues were developing a system that could estimate the mass of trucks and other heavy vehicles by detecting their gravitational attraction.

“We were aiming to determine the mass of trucks and other large vehicles by measuring their tiny gravitational pull,” Dr. Groszek said. “Once we had shown that the idea worked, we realized the same physics could be used to encode and transmit information.”

The researchers applied that principle to communication by moving a large mass between fixed positions. Each movement altered the gravitational field measured by the detector, allowing the team to encode binary information without transmitting electromagnetic signals.

Although the experiment successfully transmitted information, its data rate was exceptionally low. Professor Andrew White identified the aging elevator as a major constraint.

“Our lift was snail-paced – it’s from the 1950s – so our experiment transmitted just 1 bit per minute, meaning it took an hour to send a single word,” Professor White said.

“The speed of gravity communication would come down to how big the mass is, how fast you can move it, and the sensitivity of the detector.”

“It took more than 100 years to go from the first telegraph system to the mobile phone, so while gravity communication is not practical today, it could be in the future.”

Professor Matthew Davis said the experiment established that information can be deliberately encoded in a gravitational field and recovered by a detector. The achievement demonstrates the underlying physics, although substantial improvements would be needed before the technique could serve as a useful communication system.

“It’s not often that you get to demonstrate an entirely new way of communicating,” Professor Davis said. “We have shown experimentally that information can be encoded in the gravitational field and recovered at the other end.”

The researchers also modeled how more sensitive quantum sensors might improve the technique. Unlike the classical gravimeter used in the experiment, such sensors could potentially detect weaker gravitational changes at greater distances.

“Our experiment used a classical gravity sensor, but our modeling indicates that quantum sensors could, with rather ambitious assumptions, support ranges of up to about 500 meters (about 1,640 feet) and data rates of around 15 bits per minute.”

Those figures are theoretical projections, not experimentally demonstrated capabilities. Gravity-based communication remains far too slow for practical applications, but more advanced detectors could eventually make it possible to exchange information in environments that radio waves, light, and other conventional signals cannot penetrate.

Reference: “Unblockable communication with gravity” by Andrew J. Groszek, Charles W. Woffinden, Michael D. Harvey, Andrew G. White and Matthew J. Davis, 18 November 2026, Science Advances. DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.aee0802

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A Figma engineer is suing the design software company and its co-founder and CEO, Dylan Field, over allegations that she faced sexual harassment and retaliation while managers and HR repeatedly told her to confront the very people she accused of misconduct.

The lawsuit, filed Monday in U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, alleges that Figma’s culture of “direct feedback” led managers and HR to make engineer Serry Park address harassment herself rather than investigate her complaints. Park, who joined Figma in 2022, alleges the company’s response damaged her career, compensation, and mental health. The lawsuit also alleges that Park, who is Asian, faced racial and gender discrimination, including performance criticism based on stereotypes that Asian women are meek, timid, and conflict-averse.

“Figma was my dream company for a long time,” Park told TechCrunch in an interview before the lawsuit was filed. “It was a place where I felt that I could level up in my career, working on a lot of really cool, interesting things at scale.”

Park is seeking damages, a jury trial, and a court order prohibiting Figma from requiring employees who report harassment to confront their alleged harassers. She also wants the company to adopt and enforce a process for investigating harassment complaints promptly and confidentially.

When reached for comment, a spokesperson for Figma said, “Figma does not tolerate discrimination or harassment. Figma also does not tolerate extortion. These claims have no merit, and we will present the facts in court.”

Park told TechCrunch she initially had faith in Figma’s complaint process, expecting that if she documented the alleged harassment and reported it to the right people, the company would act. But as an engineer in a male-dominated field, Park told TechCrunch, “I don’t feel that they took me seriously throughout this process.”

Park, who is still employed at Figma, joined its San Francisco office in October 2022. Within weeks, her onboarding mentor — a fellow engineer — asked her on a date and continued pursuing her after she declined, Park alleges. She initially feared reporting him, worrying that her manager would “dismiss her reports or view her as the problem,” according to the lawsuit.

Park says she tried to avoid her onboarding mentor. In early 2023, for example, she delayed choosing a work project until he had picked one. When her manager criticized her for not being a “team player,” Park explained that she was trying to avoid the colleague she accused of harassment, according to the suit.

Her manager allegedly dismissed her concerns, saying he had “met his wife at work too” and treating the behavior as “harmless flirtation.” When Park asked to be kept off projects with the onboarding mentor, HR allegedly instructed her to confront him directly about his behavior.

The lawsuit ties that response to Figma’s culture of direct feedback, which Park alleges Field established and enforced. It says the company’s People organization trains managers to direct employees, including those reporting harassment, to give feedback directly to the person involved.

In March 2023, her manager assigned him to a major project she was leading, despite her request to be separated from him. Park told her manager that she would “figure it out,” but alleges that having to work with him affected her performance. Her manager gave her a 2 out of 5 on her performance review, criticizing her for not speaking up enough or engaging with others and for lacking presence. Park was put on a coaching plan, which she completed in late 2023.

Changing managers didn’t help, according to the lawsuit. Her second manager also refused to separate them, allegedly telling Park that someone at her level should be able to resolve conflicts with teammates and that separating them would be “avoiding the problem.” Like her previous manager, he allegedly pointed out that he had met his partner at work.

Park alleges the harassment continued throughout 2023.

In April 2024, she transferred to another team, a move she claims set back her career by resetting her performance and promotion timeline.

On her new team, which had more women, Park said she felt better and no longer had to work with her alleged harasser. Her performance reviews improved, but in late 2025, she alleges another incident of sexual harassment, this time by her new manager.

The incident followed a night out for drinks with her wife and two teammates, including her manager. The next day, during a team lunch, the manager allegedly said Park’s sex life had been discussed the previous evening. Park, who said no such conversation had occurred in her or her wife’s presence, felt “horrified and humiliated” by comments she believed fetishized queer relationships.

A colleague reported the comments to HR as sexual harassment, according to the suit. Weeks later, Park’s manager apologized in a Slack message, saying she didn’t recall what she had said. When Park sought HR’s guidance, she was again told to address the matter directly with her manager.

During their conversation, the manager allegedly became defensive, and the encounter left Park feeling “crushed, anxious, and vulnerable.” The manager also allegedly said she would never make such a comment because she had “a lot of gay friends.”

The manager told HR that they had agreed it was a misunderstanding. HR closed its investigation without interviewing witnesses, according to the suit, even though Park disputed her manager’s account.

“Requiring an employee to confront her harasser as the price of complaining — and then closing her complaint — would dissuade a reasonable employee from complaining at all,” the suit said.

Park alleges her manager retaliated by publicly berating her, sometimes so aggressively that colleagues intervened or checked on her afterward, according to the lawsuit. The suit also alleges that other teammates left the team over concerns about the manager’s behavior.

Park again complained to HR about “escalating retaliation” and expressed concern the manager would “derail her career.” HR allegedly defended the manager’s conduct, saying the manager was “within her reasonable authority” and again instructed Park to address her concerns directly with the manager.

In early 2026, Park received another low performance review and a reduced bonus. The lawsuit alleges that similarly similarly situated men continued to receive promotions, raises, and bonuses. When Park reported the negative performance review to HR in March, she was allegedly told her sexual harassment complaint against her manager could not be “substantiated.”

Park took medical leave in March to receive treatment for PTSD symptoms and generalized anxiety. Cleared to return to work in June, she was prescribed workplace accommodations, including a graduated work schedule and mandatory remote workdays.

Park told TechCrunch that Figma approved the accommodations but had not implemented them at the time of the interview.  In July, Park and her lawyers notified Figma of their intent to pursue legal action.

“It’s really scary for me,” she said about taking legal action. “I don’t know what’s going to happen next with this, I don’t know what the outcome is going to be, but at least it feels like this is my chance to do the right thing.”

The president shared an image featuring the philandering businessman in Trump’s own pose after the attempted assassination in Butler, Pennsylvania.

The news that Jensen Huang has been reinstated as the CEO of the world’s fourth most valuable company has been met with both applause from investors and derision and confusion from those who thought the leather-jacket-clad adulterer was being dragged to the trash bin. His return to Nvidia was facilitated by Vice President JD Vance and celebrated by the White House’s social media influencer-in-chief, President Donald J. Trump, with a post on Truth Social, which is hardly truthful, quite anti-social, and rife with the AI slop that has come to characterize the current lame-duck à l'orange administration.

As news of the Huang turn by the Nvidia board caused gasps the world over, including among Congressional Republicans who decried Trump’s involvement in the decision to bring the philanderer back into the fold, Trump shared a meme watermarked by a well-known right-wing meme brand, @GRANDOLDMEMES.
