Anthropic Priced Its IPO at $83. Here's What I'd Pay for the Stock.

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic's explosive growth has made its IPO one of the biggest events in AI. But its enormous valuation could limit investors' future returns.

By Matt DiLallo – Nov 18, 2026 at 8:30PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Anthropic, PBC ›

Anthropic's (ANT +0.00%) initial public offering (IPO) is one of the biggest events in the AI industry. The developer of Claude has grown its revenue at an extraordinary pace as businesses adopt its AI tools. The AI giant just priced its IPO at $83 per share, above its expected range of $70 to $78 per share.

However, that price already assumes considerable success. Even if Anthropic becomes one of the world's most successful AI companies, its stock could still deliver disappointing returns from here.

I ran the numbers on what Anthropic's stock could be worth over the next five and 10 years. Here's what they suggest, and the price I'd be willing to pay for the AI stock.

Anthropic recorded $33.2 billion in revenue in just the first nine months of 2026, up a blistering 1,200% compared to the same period last year. Its third-quarter revenue alone was around $17 billion, and its annualized revenue run rate reached $81 billion by Oct. 31, up $10 billion from the prior month.

Enterprise adoption of Claude, its coding tools, API usage, and integrations give Anthropic several avenues for continued growth. However, AI infrastructure and model training are extraordinarily expensive. Anthropic committed to spending a staggering $895.4 billion on compute and infrastructure via purchase commitments and lease agreements. Meanwhile, Anthropic's revenue growth will also almost certainly slow as its business scales.

At $83 per share, Anthropic has a valuation of about $1.5 trillion. That's around 18 times its $81 billion run rate.

That run rate isn't the same as reported annual sales. It extrapolates recent results, which inflates a company growing this quickly. Meanwhile, compute costs, research spending, and future capital needs make it difficult to turn that revenue into sustainable profits. Additionally, stock-based compensation adds dilution, which will be a headwind to per-share earnings growth when the company finally becomes profitable.

The crucial question isn't whether Anthropic is a great company. It's whether its growth can justify a valuation this high.

My base case assumes Anthropic grows its revenue to $400 billion by 2031 with an 18% net margin and a 25x earnings multiple. That would be a remarkable business, yet it would only lift the stock to $91, about 10% above the IPO price, or roughly 2% annually. Meanwhile, my bear case assumes slower growth, thinner margins, and a lower multiple, which would send the shares down roughly 73%. It would take my bull case, with a 24% net margin and a 30x earnings multiple, to deliver a strong return of about 19% annually.

The base case return isn't compelling. Anthropic could execute extremely well and still deliver underwhelming returns because the IPO price already reflects so much success. That's the key takeaway: explosive business growth doesn't guarantee an attractive investment return.

I want to earn at least 10% annually from a stock like this using conservative assumptions. For Anthropic's base-case price of $91 in 2031 to deliver that return, I'd need to buy at around $56 per share. That's roughly 30% below the IPO price.

That's why I'd rather wait for a better entry point than chase an elevated first-day price. I'd prefer to buy closer to $55-$60 per share. Even then, I'd want to see that Anthropic can scale its revenue, produce sustainable margins, and defend its position against rival AI platforms.

Anthropic could become one of the most important technology companies of the next decade, and its revenue growth and enterprise adoption are extraordinary. However, the IPO price already reflects that potential, and my base case points to modest returns over the next five years.

I'd rather risk missing the initial excitement than overpay for an exceptional business. The stock gets much more interesting to me at $55 to $60 per share, as long as the underlying growth outlook remains intact.

If you drive to work, price each commuting mile at the IRS business mileage rate, which counts what a car costs to own, not just gas. Pay for transit, vanpools, and parking with pretax money through a commuter benefit at work. Then drive fewer days with a carpool or a remote day or two a week.

Gas is the commuting cost you notice. Insurance, repairs, and the value your car loses with each mile never show up at the pump.

The IRS business mileage rate, 76 cents a mile, counts them along with the gas. The IRS sets it from a yearly study of what cars cost to own and drive. It’s an average, and the full cost of owning a car varies by model.

Say you drive 20 miles each way, 230 days a year, and count each mile at 76 cents. That’s 9,200 miles and almost $7,000 a year. If you ride transit, add up a year of fares and parking instead.

Next, pay what you can with pretax money. Under the IRS’s fringe benefit rules, a commuter benefit at work lets you buy transit passes and vanpool rides with up to $340 a month of pretax pay. A separate $340 a month can go to parking at or near work, or where you catch transit, a vanpool, or a carpool. That pay skips federal income tax and the 7.65% Social Security and Medicare tax, so in the 22% federal bracket each dollar of fares or parking costs about 70 cents.

Then cut the trips. Working from home two of your five days takes 40% off those 9,200 miles and brings the cost at 76 cents a mile down from almost $7,000 to about $4,200. Gas and upkeep on the skipped miles stop at once, and a car driven less lasts more years.

On the days you still go in, each of these cuts your driving.

Look in your benefits portal for a commuter benefit, along with other workplace perks you may be missing, then ask your manager about working from home a day or two a week.

For their deal, 36-year-old L.A.-based King will make 10 original short-form videos, which Disney will have exclusive rights to distribute. One of the King projects coming to Disney+ is Cardboard Wars, his 52-minute intentionally-low-budget tribute to Star Wars: A New Hope. King uploaded the film to YouTube in August, and soon will cross-post it to Disney’s streaming service.

But there’s still more: King will pop up on Disney’s social accounts as well, hosting behind-the-scenes content about the VFX and filmmaking techniques he uses in his content.

“I fell in love with filmmaking and storytelling as a young kid, grabbing my parents’ home video camera and teaching myself to edit,” King wrote in an Instagram post. “A lot of my early homemade films were inspired by the Disney movies and the behind-the-scenes footage they would show in the DVD featurettes. There was always a sense of magic I would see on the screen, and so I’m thrilled to be working with Disney to continue that magic of filmmaking for the next generation of storytellers.”

King has worked with Disney regularly in recent months: He was at its glitzy Created in L.A. event in September, and earlier this month played a game of viral format Cake or Fake? with Hexed (and Arcane, and Sinners, and Spider-Verse, and Hawkeye) star Hailee Steinfeld. But this is his first official, expansive content deal.

“Zach makes the impossible look real, bringing the kind of imagination and originality that has always defined Disney,” Asad Ayaz, The Walt Disney Company’s CMO and Brand Officer, said in a statement. “He brings both a filmmaker’s ingenuity and a fan’s passion to our stories, along with a unique connection with audiences. We want to build lasting relationships with creators like Zach, giving their ideas room to grow across Disney. That’s what makes this next chapter of our work together so full of possibility.”

As we mentioned above, everyone–including YouTube–has had their eye on Netflix’s obvious poaching efforts…leaving room for Disney to sneak in the back.

In the last couple months, Disney has announced deals with major creators Dhar Mann, Dude Perfect, and Like Nastya. Crucially, these are all development deals–the exact kind of agreement that has YouTube on guard with Netflix.

And Disney’s not quite showing its full hand just yet. During its earnings call Nov. 17, executives only mentioned YouTubers once, in passing and in reference to Disney+’s growing crop of approved creator content.

We suspect more is in the pipeline–and that YouTube may have to start paying creators to turn down Disney deals, too.

American Airlines’ Airbus A321neo Fleet Grows To 90, Expects 2 More & 1 A321XLR By Year’s End

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

American Airlines has expanded its Airbus A321neo fleet to 90 aircraft, with two more standard A321neos and one A321XLR expected before the end of 2026. The latest arrival continues the carrier's fleet modernization program, in which Airbus narrowbodies play an increasingly important role in its domestic and international networks.

The latest delivery follows an unusually busy period at the end of last month, when American received four A321-family aircraft in just seven days. Those deliveries included three standard A321neos and one A321XLR, registered N311NY. With no further Boeing deliveries expected this year, Airbus remains central to American's near-term fleet renewal strategy.

American's standard A321neo fleet has reached 90 aircraft, with two more expected before the end of the year. The airline is also anticipating one additional A321XLR, extending its investment in long-range, single-aisle aircraft. If all three outstanding deliveries arrive as planned, American will finish 2026 with 92 standard A321neos, alongside its growing fleet of 12 A321XLRs.

American's third-quarter financial disclosures showed that it had 165 Airbus A320-family aircraft on order as of September 30, with seven scheduled for delivery during the final quarter. The subsequent acceleration in October, when four A321-family aircraft arrived within a week, highlighted the program's pace. American also expects another 27 A321-family deliveries in 2027, although the precise split between the standard A321neo and A321XLR has not been disclosed.

The A321XLR is particularly important to American's international expansion, allowing it to serve longer routes with fewer passengers than a widebody aircraft would typically accommodate.

Brian Znotins, American's former Senior Vice President of Network Planning, highlighted the aircraft's importance when announcing the carrier's 2027 international expansion.

"The A321XLR is transforming our international network, expanding it to offer destinations to satisfy every travel palate. Combined with our investments enhancing our onboard product and in-flight experience, travelers have more reasons to choose American when they fly around the world."

American received its first A321XLR in October 2025, becoming the first US operator of the type. Its original 50-aircraft commitment was subsequently reduced to 40, including five delivered by the end of 2025. Each aircraft features 20 Flagship Suite business class seats, 12 premium economy seats, and 123 economy seats, emphasizing premium revenue over maximum capacity.

The XLR restores some of the long-range narrowbody capability American lost when it retired its 757s in 2020. Its economics make thinner transatlantic routes viable without deploying a much larger 787 Dreamliner. The aircraft initially entered service on premium transcontinental flights, before expanding into international markets as more aircraft became available.

American's 2027 schedule demonstrates its ambitions, with four additional European routes assigned to the XLR. Philadelphia-Vienna, measuring approximately 4,321 miles (6,955 km), is particularly notable as it will become the world's longest narrowbody flight. The route will operate daily from May 6 through January 2028, while New York gains new services to Amsterdam and Nice.

13 new routes were planned but now only seven are scheduled.

The standard A321neo remains central to American's high-capacity narrowbody strategy. With its fleet now at 90 aircraft, it still has firm orders for 126 more airframes as the airline continues investing in the type to support busy domestic routes and replace older aircraft.

Configured with 196 seats, including 20 First Class and 47 Main Cabin Extra seats, American's legacy A321neos accommodate 41 more passengers than the XLR. It is particularly valuable on busy domestic routes connecting American's major hubs and supports the gradual replacement of older narrowbodies. Its versatility extends internationally, with Philadelphia-Reykjavík scheduled to launch in May 2027.

American's Boeing 737 MAX 8s complement the A321neos, offering 172 seats on routes requiring less capacity. The airline also has substantial outstanding Boeing narrowbody orders, including the larger 737 MAX 10, although deliveries of that variant are not expected until 2029 or later. With an eventual planned fleet size of more than 250 A321-family aircraft, Airbus narrowbodies will continue to play a leading role in American's domestic modernization and expanding transatlantic network.

Six days, six outages: Why reliability isn’t resilience

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

GUEST COLUMN by Zeus Kerravala

A rash of major outages over the past week all seem to follow a similar script. Services go dark, Downdetector lights up, the provider posts a string of status updates, a root cause emerges a few days later, and everyone goes back to work, assuming it won’t happen again. This past week, that script was on overdrive.

None of these was a cyberattack, and as far as anyone can tell, none was related. A dormant bug, overloaded legacy routers, a bad internal update and an on-premises hardware glitch are about as different as root causes get. Some of these systems run in the public cloud, some in a company’s own data center, and some on air traffic control gear that’s decades old. That’s precisely the point. The common thread isn’t the technology. It’s that each organization had a critical dependency fail, and the business stopped with it.

That’s the lesson I want business leaders to take away. Outages aren’t rare events to be explained away after the fact. They’re a certainty to plan for, and the plan must address what happens when an entire service fails, not just a server or a data center.

I wanted to dig into what real resilience looks like, and Zscaler Inc. is an interesting case study for a specific reason. When a company deploys Zscaler, every user, branch and device connects to applications through its Zero Trust Exchange. That places Zscaler squarely in the critical path. If it goes down, users can’t reach Microsoft 365, Salesforce, internal apps or anything else, no matter how healthy those applications are. That all-or-nothing exposure has forced Zscaler to think harder than most about what happens when its service fails.

At the last Zenith Live, Zscaler’s user event, I asked Chief Executive Jay Chaudhry about this, and he explained that because of the critical nature of their offering, it can’t afford any downtime, so the company built resiliency into its service design. After all these outages, I asked to speak with Chaudhry and Chief Reliability Officer Misha Kuperman to gather lessons learned for the industry.

To be clear, Zscaler couldn’t have kept a single Microsoft mailbox online or restarted the FAA’s routers, and no one at the company claimed otherwise. But its approach to continuity for its own platform is a useful model for how any service on a company’s critical path, including software-as-a-service, should be architected. Here are my thoughts on what this means for boards and C-suite leaders:

I came from financial services, where the disaster recovery site was non-negotiable and everyone knew which services had to survive a failure. Chaudhry, who spent years at IBM Corp., made the same point.

“Most of my large customers had a production data center and a DR data center,” he told me. “If the production data center went down, they’d fail over to DR. That was standard practice. When the world moved to the cloud, they forgot about it, thinking disaster recovery was the cloud provider’s responsibility, even though it is actually a shared responsibility.”

He’s right, and the record proves it. Availability zones and multiregion designs protect against hardware failures and localized events, but they share software, control planes, configuration pipelines and often DNS. When a bad configuration or a dormant bug propagates, it spreads everywhere. That’s exactly what happened to Microsoft.

Availability architecture and resilience architecture aren’t the same thing. One keeps a service up when a component fails. The other keeps the business running when the entire service fails. Most companies have the first and assume they have the second, but as we have seen recently, that assumption is incorrect.

During the call, Chaudhry used an analogy that everyone can understand. You can own the world’s most reliable car and service it religiously, yet you still buy insurance for other risks not covered by the warranty, such as theft or a tree falling on it. Having insurance and knowing when and how to use it helps you get back on the road quickly. Zscaler has built the cyber equivalent.

In the real world, people understand it. In cyber and digital, we seem to forget and rely on service-level agreements. A 99.999% uptime commitment sounds good until you translate it into business terms. Chaudhry said some of Zscaler’s largest banking customers told the company that five nines wasn’t enough, “because we could lose hundreds of millions of dollars if you went down even for five minutes.”

Going back to my IT days, I’ve never liked SLAs because they are financial instruments, not operational ones. They tell you what credit you’ll receive after the fact, not how you’ll keep processing payments, as Chase customers learned, or how you’ll keep planes in the air, as United learned. Regulators have recognized this. The EU’s Digital Operational Resilience Act and Australia’s CPS 230 both require regulated firms to assess, test, and mitigate the risk posed by their technology providers. “Our cloud provider went down” is no longer an acceptable defense.

IT executives should stop asking, “What’s our provider’s SLA?” and start asking, “What must our business continue to do when a critical dependency fails, and have we proven we can do so?”

The instinctive response to an outage like Microsoft’s is to buy from a second provider. Chaudhry shared a story about a chief information officer whose CEO had flagged Office 365 as a business risk and told him to find an alternative. The CIO signed up for Google Workspace. When asked whether documents, links and role-based access controls would work after a switch, even in half the cases, he said he didn’t think so.

That’s the reality. Email alone is hard enough because users lose their history the moment they switch. Modern productivity suites aren’t storage anymore. Every email includes links to shared documents, permissions are tied to identity, and workflows span multiple apps. Kuperman framed the problem in terms of scale: “I can switch between Gmail and Outlook, but can 10,000 people? Will they be as productive?”

The same problem applies in security and networking, where the stakes are arguably higher. The vendor A/vendor B model requires two policy sets kept in perfect sync, two client software sets and a way to move tens of thousands of users and devices from one to the other in the middle of a crisis. Kuperman described one customer for whom it “used to take seven days to switch over and 30 days to switch back. You can’t run a business like that.”

A backup that’s never tested at full scale isn’t a backup. It’s a hope. And a backup that changes the user experience will fail exactly when it’s needed.

Another instinct is to turn off security entirely; however, when a crisis occurs, threat actors will rally and launch campaigns. During the CrowdStrike outage, phishing campaigns were mobilized almost immediately, and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency had to issue warnings not to take the bait. Threat actors are present both internally and externally, so security must remain on guard during critical failures, especially when they are publicly known.

This brings me to what Zscaler has done with its Business Continuity Cloud and why I think it’s the right template for the industry. Rather than asking customers to bolt on a second vendor, Zscaler built a dedicated, per-customer environment that’s architecturally separate from its primary Zero Trust Exchange.

The design choices are important to understand. The Business Continuity Cloud runs in third-party data centers, not Zscaler’s own, on separate networks, autonomous system numbers, DNS, and cloud domains. Software and threat-intelligence updates are deliberately staggered so a bad production release can’t land in the backup at the same time. Policy and identity data sync one way into a read-only, last-known-good state. It’s offered across nine regions, enough to keep operations in-country if a nation were cut off.

“No linkage — no network, no shared routers, no switches, no shared network operations,” Chaudhry said. That’s the old production/DR discipline, rebuilt for the cloud. After watching overloaded routers ground the nation’s air traffic, “no shared routers” doesn’t sound like overkill.

There were two operational details I felt were worth noting. First, Zscaler encourages customers to continuously route synthetic or trusted-user traffic through the backup environment in peacetime, so the failover path is validated daily rather than in an annual tabletop exercise. Second, the failover is automated to optimize for a low recovery time objective or RTO, or it can be triggered by the customer, ensuring it works even if Zscaler itself is unreachable.

Zscaler says nearly 100 customers now use its service, including a global bank with more than 300,000 users, a global pharmaceutical company with more than 100,000, and a U.S. telco with more than 75,000. The company charges what Chaudhry called a nominal fee to cover the dedicated infrastructure, network and always-on capacity.

The lesson from the Microsoft outage, in particular, isn’t that customers need a second productivity suite. It’s that the primary provider should offer a fate-separated continuity environment as part of the service. Zscaler has shown it can be done for a global security cloud. I’d like to see every SaaS provider that sits in a company’s critical path, and Microsoft in particular, be pressed by large customers to offer the same.

The Microsoft outage also exposed a concentration risk that many companies have faced through enterprise licensing agreements. Microsoft said the incident affected not only Exchange and Teams but also Defender XDR, Defender for Office 365 and Intune. For many organizations, Microsoft is the productivity suite, the identity provider and the security stack. When the shared infrastructure beneath them fails, all three can fail together.

Kuperman described the minimum set of capabilities a business needs to keep operating during a disaster: people, identity, network and security, and business-critical applications. Each must work, and any one can fail. “You have to think about where your eggs are and which baskets they’re in,” he said.

I’m not arguing against platforms. But boards should understand where consolidation has created a single point of failure across identity, applications and security, and ensure those layers don’t share the same fate.

This won’t be the last major outage, and the next one may be a cyberattack, a war, or a configuration push rather than a dormant bug. Here’s what I recommend IT and business leaders do now:

The cloud delivered on its promise of agility and scale, but in the process, the industry let a hard-won discipline lapse. This past week reminded us that everything fails eventually, whether in the cloud, a data center or an air traffic control tower. When it does, an SLA credit won’t keep the business running. A real continuity plan will.

Keep a log of one normal workweek, written every half hour, to see where your hours go before you try to fit more in. Then hand off or drop the work that doesn’t serve your top priorities, and book calendar time for the work that does, while your week still has open hours.

When long days still leave you behind on your top priorities, staying later only adds hours of the same mix of work.

You win time back by dropping tasks, not by doing all of them faster, and memory is a poor guide to which tasks to drop. Looking back on a week, you’ll remember the long meetings and the big deadline, not the five-minute favors, status emails, and file searches that filled the time in between.

So log the week as it happens. Pick a week without a holiday or a deadline crunch, and every 30 minutes write down what you did in the half hour before, interruptions included. A notebook works, or use Clockify, a time tracker whose free plan times each task and breaks the week down in a report.

On Friday, sort every entry into three groups: work only you can do, work someone else could do, and work nobody needs. Hand the second group to a teammate or a direct report, or automate it with an email template or a scheduled report. Stop doing the third, after you check with your manager that no one relies on it.

Take a manager whose log shows nine hours a week in status meetings, including two hour-long weekly check-ins where people only share updates. She replaces those two hours of meetings with a shared written update that takes each person 15 minutes a week to post and read, so she and every attendee get an hour and 45 minutes back each week.

Before you leave on Friday, book time on next week’s calendar for your top two or three priorities, so new meetings get scheduled around those blocks.
