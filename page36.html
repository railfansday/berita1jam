Here is all the latest Arsenal injury news and when the three players are expected to return.

Konsa started Arsenal’s 2-1 victory over Slavia Prague on Wednesday but came off in the second half after experiencing discomfort.

He appeared to limp during the first half, so Arsenal will monitor his fitness closely ahead of Saturday’s match.

Arteta said the change was not a tactical one, as Konsa had been playing a lot of games recently and had some problems during the match.

Potential return date: November 7 vs Hull City (3pm kick-off).

A positive from the Gunners’ trip to Prague was the inclusion of Saliba in the squad.

The defender has not played this season after picking up a long-term back injury. The issue got worse during his time with France at the World Cup finals, but he managed to avoid surgery.

Saliba has steadily built up his workload and played for 45 minutes in a behind-closed-moors game. He returned to full training on Tuesday and travelled with the squad to take on Slavia Prague.

Arteta said that Saliba was feeling confident, but it will depend on the needs of the squad.

Potential return date: November 7 vs Hull City (3pm kick-off).

The winger suffered the injury approximately 10 minutes into the Arsenal Champions League clash against Bayern Munich last month.

This means it is unlikely he plays against Hull. Arsenal are not expected to bring him back too quickly ahead of the international break.

Arteta will miss one of his most influential attackers. Hopefully the next scan will give a clearer indication of how long he is likely to be out for.

Expected return date: Unknown. Saka is not expected to face Hull City on November 7.

Arsenal host Hull City at the Emirates Stadium on Saturday, November 7th.

The game comes ahead of a tough sequence of games which sees Manchester City, Real Madrid and Tottenham on the horizon. Arteta will want as many defensive and attacking threats on hand.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/regulatory/cms-final-2027-outpatient-pay-rule-brings-hospitals-little-relief-340b-site-neutral" hreflang="en">CMS’ final 2027 outpatient pay rule brings hospitals little relief on 340B, site-neutral changes</a>

📅 2026-11-05  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
CMS’ final 2027 outpatient pay rule brings hospitals little relief on 340B, site-neutral changes" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services on Tuesday finalized its annual outpatient hospital payments rule with a lower-than-proposed rate bump and its major, industry-opposed policy shifts moderated, but largely intact.

The 2027 Hospital Outpatient Prospective Payment (OPPS) and Ambulatory Surgical Center (ASC) Payment Systems rule outlines a 2.3% reimbursement increase for those meeting applicable quality reporting requirements. That’s below the proposed 2.4% increase, and reflects a 3.2% projected hospital market basket increase and a 0.9 percentage point productivity adjustment.

The agency is also moving forward with a substantial and controversial cut in 340B drug reimbursements, though not quite to the extent that it initially proposed.

The final rule adjusts these drug payments down from the current bar of average sales price (ASP) plus 6% to ASP minus 28%, as opposed to the proposed 33.4% below ASP. To make the reimbursement change budget neutral as required by statute, OPPS payments for non-drug services will be boosted by 7.5%.

CMS said it tweaked the proposed 340B reimbursement cut based on 340B ceiling price data from the Health Resources and Services Administration, with the baseline reductions informed by a drug acquisition cost survey it conducted earlier this year. Those changes will reduce Original Medicare drug payments by $4.35 billion and the drug payments from beneficiaries by $1.1 billion (or $900 on average per beneficiary who receives an affected drug from a hospital outpatient department) in the policy’s first year, CMS said.

The rate adjustment did little to calm hospital industry groups who’d railed against the 340B reimbursement changes as a threat to safety-net care and a gift to for-profit hospitals that do not participate in the subsidy program but would benefit from the budget-neutral offset. The American Hospital Association, the Association of American Medical Colleges and America’s Essential Hospitals each condemned the finalized policy in reaction statements, with the latter two issuing more forceful rebukes.

“CMS has based its reimbursement to 340B hospitals on the results of a hasty and faulty survey, failing to follow the clear requirements of the Medicare statute,” AAMC Chief Health Care Officer Jonathan Jaffery, M.D., said. “The drug payment cuts disproportionately affect and target non-profit and public hospitals serving low-income patients, while redistributing billions of dollars to hospitals that do not share the same safety-net and academic missions. These cuts threaten access to critical services made possible by the 340B program and will lead to worse health outcomes for patients.”

“Rather than strengthening and preserving safety net funding, CMS is instead prioritizing hospitals that do not serve the safety net and nodding to the pharmaceutical industry's interests,” AEH’s statement reads. “These cuts are landing at a time when essential hospitals and their mission are more important than ever, as millions of people risk losing their Medicaid coverage in the coming months.”

The Federation of American Hospitals, which represents for-profit hospitals that do not participate in 340B, struck a different tone, calling the rulemaking “a win for for patients, taxpayers, and the hospitals that communities depend on.”

“By bringing Medicare drug payments more in line with actual costs, this reform will lower seniors’ out-of-pocket expenses and redirect billions in Medicare dollars where they’re needed most—supporting access to care for patients served by small and rural hospitals across the country,” Charlene MacDonald, the group’s president and CEO, said in a statement. “We applaud the Administration for advancing a smarter, fairer approach to Medicare payment.”

Additionally, CMS finalized an increase in an annual OPPS conversion factor to non-drug items and services, speeding up the agency’s effort to offset a prior $7.8 billion recoupment to 340B hospitals it was forced to make after previously reducing 340B rates contrary to law. But while this accelerated payback—often described by non-340B hospitals as a clawback—was proposed as an increase from 0.5% to 3%, the final rule brings the conversion factor to 2%.

AHA Senior Vice President of Public Policy Analysis and Development Ashley Thompson described both 340B policy changes together as a “continued assault” on the subsidy program that “will further stress hospitals’ ability to care for their patients.” AAMC’s Jaffery said the combined policies’ impact “will be devastating to patients,” and that his group “remains committed to pursuing all available avenues to reverse these payment cuts.”

Further rankling the hospital industry was the final rule’s continued push toward site-neutral payment policy and a phase-out of the inpatient-only list.

For the former, the agency will apply Physician Fee Schedule payment rates for some imaging services to off-campus provider-based departments. In line with the proposed rule, CMS said it expects the plan to trigger first-year reductions in Medicare Part B expenditures and beneficiary cost-sharing obligations of $260 million and $70 million, while also reducing a market incentive for provider consolidation.

Meanwhile, CMS finalized its proposal to remove 647 services from the inpatient-only list, allowing them to be delivered and paid for in the lower-cost outpatient setting.

“Both policies are misguided,” AHA’s Thompson said, “and overlook the unique role hospital outpatient departments play in delivering care to patients in communities nationwide. Hospital outpatient departments treat sicker and more complex groups of patients than independent physician practices, including those with higher rates of disability and who are more likely to come from low-income and rural communities.”

CMS’ fact sheet on the 1,270-page annual rule also noted various changes to quality reporting programs for hospital outpatient departments and ASCs, a proposal to require prior authorization for eight botulinum toxin injection codes CMS said it has seen a jump in claim volume, and new permission for accreditation organizations to assess hospitals’ compliance with EMTALA (the Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act of 1986).

“CMS is committed to spending taxpayer funds wisely and efficiently, so America’s seniors can continue getting the care they need,” CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., said in a release. “By right-sizing payments and rooting out waste, today’s reforms will drive affordability in Medicare for current beneficiaries while ensuring it will still be there to serve their children and grandchildren.”

How the midterms could fuel the K-shaped AI trade

📅 2026-11-05  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The U.S. midterms could widen the AI divide: tighter data center rules may reward tech giants with capacity in hand and squeeze debt-funded companies trying to catch up.

Democratic victories in key gubernatorial races raised the prospect of tougher scrutiny for data center projects, adding to investor concerns about how quickly new capacity can be built and financed.

In Texas, Democratic governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has proposed a statewide moratorium on new data center permits, although projects already under construction would reportedly be exempt.

As the markets digested the results on Wednesday, Microsoft and Amazon each surged more than 6%, while AI infrastructure provider CoreWeave plunged around 10%. Chipmakers Micron and Nvidia fell 7% and 3.17%, respectively.

Differences in borrowing costs are playing a key role in the split, James Sullivan, managing director and co-head of global fundamental research at JPMorgan, told CNBC's "Squawk Box Asia" on Thursday.

Sullivan said borrowing spreads have widened significantly for neocloud companies, leaving their cost of additional capital substantially higher than that of hyperscalers that benefit from more diversified customers and revenue streams.

"The fact that you're seeing this bifurcation in equity performance is largely driven by the view that existing capacity is significantly more valuable now because of incremental supply constraints," he said.

The midterm results could reinforce that view if they make new projects more vulnerable to permitting delays.

BMI warned that a sharp slowdown in data center construction and AI investment could produce a greater-than-expected decline in overall investment, The Business Times reported, citing the firm's post-midterm analysis.

The potential impact is uneven across the construction pipeline. Much of the data center capacity expected to come online in 2027 is already under construction, limiting the near-term impact of new restrictions.

Projects planned for 2028 face greater exposure to permitting delays and regulatory uncertainty, investment bank Nomura said in a note Wednesday about the midterm results.

Tighter regulations on data center construction could become a more significant headwind to AI investment from 2028, the firm said.

Shares of Nvidia-backed neocloud CoreWeave, Nebius and Iren fell at least 30% in October, as tightening credit markets raised questions about how AI infrastructure projects would be financed. CoreWeave's five-year credit default swap spread has surged past 1,100 basis points, reflecting rising costs for investors seeking protection against a potential default.

The distinction may be how well companies can absorb delays and fund the capacity they do build. Hyperscalers have broader cloud businesses and more internally generated cash flow, while lower-rated neoclouds face greater exposure to borrowing costs, energy prices and refinancing conditions, JPMorgan Asset Management said in an Oct. 20 research note.

The asset manager said financially stronger hyperscalers could also secure cheaper capacity or acquire assets if weaker operators scale back.

Cristiano Ronaldo has called time on his illustrious career in international football.

Ronaldo, 41, scored a world record 146 goals in 234 games for Portugal and won the 2016 European Championship as well as two Nations League titles.

His retirement comes just weeks after his explosive falling-out with new manager Jorge Jesus, which ended with Ronaldo abandoning Portugal's camp during the most recent international break in September and October.

It appeared as if he could return for the upcoming break this month for clashes with Denmark and Wales, but he has decided to step away after 23 years.

In his retirement statement, he admitted that he had at times 'failed' and revealed that his decision was difficult, but felt proud of his work and willing to hand over the number seven jersey.

The Al-Nassr star is hoping to pass the baton to his son, 16-year-old Cristiano Junior, who this week has been called up for Portugal's Under-17s.

Ronaldo made his debut for Portugal in 2003 aged 18 and went on to play in six World Cups, a record he shares with Lionel Messi, as well as six European Championships, two Nations League finals, and a Confederations Cup.

His final act in football's greatest tournament was Portugal's 1-0 defeat by arch-rivals Spain at this summer's World Cup – a devastating day which saw him leave in tears.

And his final minutes in a Portugal shirt came against Wales in September, when he played 67 minutes in a 1-0 Nations League win over Wales.

How AI-Powered Automation Can Solve the Payment-Posting Bottleneck

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The healthcare industry has spent decades moving its payments off paper. Payers now send their payment and denial detail electronically through a standard file, the Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) 835, which cut out the paper and gave everyone a common format to work from. But while standardizing the data removed some of the manual effort, it didn’t make the work automatic. A file arriving electronically isn’t the same as a payment posting itself. The EDI 835 file lands, and people still open it, read each payer’s codes, post the payments line by line, and work the denials by hand.

That manual work is expensive, and the cost keeps climbing. In Experian Health’s 2025 State of Claims survey, 41 percent of providers reported that at least one in ten of their claims is denied, and Kodiak Solutions estimated that providers lost more than $48 billion to denials and bad debt in 2025, roughly a quarter more than the year before. Staff have to post, reconcile, and work every payment and denial before an account is settled, and most of that still happens by hand.

The reason posting stays manual has less to do with technology than with the rules themselves. Every payer handles their files differently, and the rules shift with each situation and exception so that the edge cases pile up faster than anyone can write them down. Many of those rules aren’t written down at all. They live in the heads of a few experienced billers, which means the organization can’t apply them consistently and can’t easily hand them to a system. When the rules aren’t captured, claims take longer to clear, avoidable denials slip through, and the first submission often collects less than it should have.

Traditional automation handles the clean, predictable claims and stalls on everything else. A rules engine only does what someone programmed into it ahead of time. The first time a payer sends an adjustment code it hasn’t seen, the claim moves to a person for review. Payers vary enough that a large share of claims end up in that queue.

A large language model closes that gap because it reads a claim the way an experienced biller does. It takes in the context; works out what the claim is, which payer rule applies, and how to handle exceptions; and hands it to the system that posts the payment. The model supplies the judgment, and the system posts the payment the same way every time.

Marlabs built a system that does exactly that for a healthcare billing company that handles payments for emergency medical services providers across North America. The company processes about 85,000 payments a month, and both the volume and the payer complexity were outgrowing what its team could keep pace with through hiring.

The most important part was how the system handled the rules the company hadn’t written down. Instead of loading it with a payer rulebook the company didn’t actually have, we had the AI learn from how the billers worked and from what the payers paid. It proposed rules, tested them against real outcomes, and tuned toward the result the business cares about most: collecting more of what insurance will pay on the first submission rather than after rounds of appeals. Knowledge that had lived in a few people’s heads became rules the company could finally see and improve.

Every claim still passes through a person before it’s final. The system posts the claim and shows its work so that a reviewer can see what the AI did on a clean claim and approve it in a few seconds. When the AI isn’t sure, or when a biller had to step in, the claim carries a plain explanation of what looked wrong and what the system recommends. In these cases, AI makes the review faster while accountability for every claim stays where it belongs: with the people and the company.

Those design choices, more than the technology on its own, produced the results. The company now posts about 85 percent of its payments automatically, which frees its team to spend time on the claims that genuinely need judgment. Posting errors fell by 95 percent, and reconciliation across systems is cleaner. The team resolves payer-specific denials faster, and for the first time it can watch its exceptions and their resolutions as they happen. Because we designed the framework to scale within each payer’s compliance requirements, more volume no longer forces more headcount.

Payment posting is one of the clearest places to start, because the sums involved are large and the return shows up directly in cash collected. By mapping the process honestly, teaching a system the rules an organization never wrote down, and keeping people in control of every claim, you can see measurable, tangible results. Handled that way, payment posting turns from a stubborn manual bottleneck into a process the business can scale.
