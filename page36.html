Meta President Dina Powell McCormick said that the Democratic wins in the midterm elections are going to make it a lot harder for companies to build data centers, adding that the industry has “not done as good a job as we needed to explain the benefits of these investments.”

Speaking at the WSJ Tech conference on Wednesday, the Meta exec pointed out that the backlash against data centers — which was very much on the ballot during this election season — was also tied to people’s feelings about AI technology overall.

“The visible sort of manifestation of AI is a data center, and it’s something that people can say, ‘I don’t want this in my community,’” she explained. “I think there’s an underlying bigger issue, which is how people feel about AI in general.”

Meta believes it could turn around the negative consumer sentiment around AI if it’s able to demonstrate the value, not only of the data centers coming to local communities, but in the technology itself.

“The stakes of who builds the technology that cures cancer, that gives access to education, that is also a very fundamental difference…Mark [Zuckerberg, Meta CEO] is very ‘long human,’ and he’s betting on humans more than machines,” Powell McCormick explained. “And that’s what we mean when we say the future is for everyone. That people should not be left behind. But the way to ensure that is that this technology is democratized, that people have access to it in a very fair and transparent way,” she said.

The exec also touted Meta’s experience in building data centers, saying that the company has been doing so for 20 years, as it takes a lot of compute power to offer the products it does in the social networking space. Today, the company has data centers in 23 states, she said, and five of them, including one in Texas, are planned as projects of one gigawatt or more.

But Meta is now worried that it could lose some of its investments after politicians ran on anti-data-center platforms during the midterms.

“I think it’s going to be harder,” Powell McCormick said, describing what the future of building data centers looks like. “It has been hard, I should say, but I also think that it’s becoming clear what models work, and the states want those models that work.”

Specifically, communities only want data centers when it benefits them in some way, she explained, laying out the approach that Meta today uses to close its data center deals in local communities.

Successful projects see Meta paying for its own electricity, directing any tax surpluses as a result of the data centers toward teachers and first responders, keeping the labor force local, and getting bipartisan support for the project, among other things.

Going forward, she expects data center projects to come under more review and be weighed against whether the constituents actually want them.

Meta has pinned its hopes on its AI investments, particularly its consumer AI agent Muse scaling far beyond the 10 million users it has today, to become a business that one day serves billions. But even if that’s not the case, it believes that its investments in data centers will not be for nothing, as the compute itself will be valuable.

“We’re getting calls every day to lease that compute [we’ve built], and so one way or another, we’re seeing a path to monetization,” Powell McCormick said.

Will US midterm election results spare Gulf states from further war?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The US midterm results showed how unpopular Donald Trump's Iran war is. Now, Gulf countries, trapped for months in the middle of the war between the US and Iran, see reason for optimism.

After his party's resounding defeat in the US midterm elections this week, President Donald Trump conceded that the war with Iran had likely played a part.

"I think a part of it was Iran, but, at the same time, people understood the importance of what I've done with Iran," Trump told Fox News, conceding that US voters were unwilling to accept the impact of the war on gas prices.

Observers say the United States and Israel had been holding off on major attacks on Iran until after both had held elections.

At the end of October, the Wall Street Journal reported that the Trump administration was already warning Gulf state governments to brace for a short, intense military campaign after the midterms.

While foreign policy is a US president's purview, analysts have pointed out that a Congress controlled by the Democratic party could ostensibly withhold funding for Trump's military expeditions. However newly-elected Democratic politicians won't take their seats until early next year.

As two US professors of political science wrote for Foreign Affairs magazine this week, past American presidents, like Trump, who become "lame ducks" — that is, a president who loses control of Congress and can't run for another term — have often ramped up international interventions.

"As they think about their legacy, lame ducks often turn to foreign policy, where the power of the presidency allows them to act unfettered by pesky domestic politics," they explained. And one of the places where Trump is most likely to act is Iran, the professors argued.

On the other hand, Trump also told several media outlets that he was in "no rush" to start bombing Iran again.

Opinions on Iran's next moves are also divided.

The Iranians have said they're ready for any attack and rhetoric from senior leaders indicates they may be willing to strike a wider range of targets than previously. That could include regional energy and economic infrastructure and even undersea telecommunications cabling or data centers.

If Iran thinks there is an existential threat to its leadership, then "all bets are off," Ross Harrison, a senior fellow at the Washington-based Middle East Institute, or MEI, and an expert on Iran, said during a virtual briefing hosted by MEI this week. "I think they will target differently in the Gulf states [this time] … so it really depends on how all this unfolds."

But other experts believe Iran was deliberately holding out to pressure Trump before the midterms. Now the election is over, the Iranians may be more willing to negotiate a peace deal.

All this leaves the Gulf states, who have been the main targets of Iranian attacks since the conflict began, in a difficult position.

"We find ourselves drawn within a crisis that we worked diplomatically very hard to try to avert," Anwar Gargash, a foreign policy adviser to United Arab Emirates leadership, said at a security conference in Bahrain last weekend, the Manama Dialogue hosted by the International Institute for Strategic Studies, or IISS. "We are clearly seeking a political off-ramp."

Qatar has been trying to negotiate between the US and Iran but has also been conducting emergency drills at its airport. The Emiratis have been doing similar drills. And Saudi Arabia is looking at a war on two fronts: The oil-rich state is already under attack from the Houthi militia in neighboring Yemen. Even Egypt has reacted, reportedly strengthening air defenses around the Suez Canal.

Still, no matter what happens next, the US-Israel-Iran war has already made Gulf states evaluate defense partnerships with the US. Rather than making them safe, US bases have made them a target.

"There is widespread disappointment across the region with the US over the folly of the war, the erratic nature of Washington's military and diplomatic conduct, and its decision to prioritize Israel's defense over that of its Gulf partners," Hasan Alhasan, a senior fellow at the IISS, told news agency Bloomberg this week. "If the US liberation of Kuwait in 1991 was the moment that established America's role in the region, the 2026 war may be remembered as the moment that undid it."

Gulf states are widening their outreach with, for example, the UAE getting closer to Israel and the Saudis entering the Mecca defense pact with Pakistan and Turkey.

"The Gulf states have already moved toward strategic autonomy and self-reliance," Andreas Krieg, a senior lecturer at the School of Security Studies at King's College London, wrote for online outlet Middle East Eye this week. "A lame-duck Trump will speed that process."

That's even though a Democrat-led Congress might make the US a slightly more reliable partner again.

Krieg argues that Gulf states should come together to pressure the US not to attack Iran and then negotiate a non-aggression pact with Iran — without US involvement.

Whether that kind of unity is possible in the Gulf is dubious, Ryan Crocker, a former US ambassador to the region and distinguished fellow with MEI, replied to a question from DW during the MEI's online briefing.

"I'm afraid that meaningful Gulf unity is not coming to a theater near us anytime soon," Crocker said. The Qataris remain untrusting because of how other Gulf states acted against them in 2017. And, he added, "there are well known differences between Saudi Arabia and the UAE we see in Sudan [and] in Yemen, and, of course, the profound differences … on relations with Israel. So I would not expect any meaningful moves toward [Gulf] security cooperation."

What's more likely in the long term is Gulf states eventually coming to some kind of understanding with Iran.

"The Gulf states understand that they probably can't buy their security only from Washington, and that they're going to have to buy it, to some degree, by some kind of a relationship with Tehran," the MEI's Harrison added. "It's inevitable. Geography reigns."

What that sort of non-aggression pact looks like remains to be seen, he added. That's why one of the big concerns for Gulf leaders now, is the conflict escalating again to a point where the path back to some sort of diplomacy is lost, Harrison concluded.

Ravens vs. Jaguars prediction: Does Lamar Jackson-less Baltimore have any chance in 'TNF' matchup?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In the Week 9 edition of "Thursday Night Football," the Baltimore Ravens play host to the Jacksonville Jaguars.

Baltimore is coming off a devastating loss to the Buffalo Bills in which Lamar Jackson returned from his ankle injury and was promptly injured on his first offensive play of the game. The Ravens also lost backup Tyler Huntley during the contest, and they are now decimated on offense as they head into Thursday night's game.

Jacksonville came off its bye last week and laid the smack down on the division rival Indianapolis Colts, winning 42-7 to improve to 5-2 on the season and further solidify their hold on the AFC South. Trevor Lawrence and the offense are rolling, and the defense has been largely excellent so far this year.

Will the Jaguars take care of business on the road, or will the Ravens pull off a shocking upset? We'll find out soon enough. Before we break down the matchup, here's a look at how you can watch the game.

The Jags enter this game having destroyed the Colts last week behind a monster performance from their running game. Bhayshul Tuten ran 16 times for 104 yards and a touchdown, while Chris Rodriguez carried it six times for 41 yards and another score.

Rodriguez was limited in practice this week with a hand injury but will play on Thursday night. Tuten's status is up in the air after he didn't practice all week due to an ankle injury sustained against Indianapolis. He played through that injury for the rest of the game, but Jacksonville might choose to rest him here. He's a game-time decision.

The Ravens are allowing only 3.8 yards per carry this season, with a mere 0.8 of those yards coming before contact, according to Tru Media. Jacksonville could have some issues running the ball, especially if Tuten is out, if the Baltimore front can hold up as it has for most of the season.

The Ravens did get clocked for 146 yards on the ground this past week by the Bills, but James Cook averaged south of 4.0 yards per carry. A lot of the damage was done by Ray Davis late in the game with six carries for 30 yards. Josh Allen also chipped in 30 of his own. Trevor Lawrence obviously isn't as mobile or as involved in the run game as Allen, but he can move, and if the Ravens get out of their rushing lanes, he can make things happen as a scrambler.

Lawrence has played extremely well so far this season, completing 65.7% of his passes at an average of 8.5 yards per attempt, with 17 touchdowns (and a league-high 8.6% touchdown rate) against just four interceptions. Lawrence's top target is clearly Parker Washington, who splits his time fairly evenly between lining up out wide and in the slot.

The Ravens are 21st in net yards per attempt allowed this season, and their cornerbacks (Marlon Humphrey and Nate Wiggins) largely haven't played up to expectations. Kyle Hamilton remains excellent against both the run and pass in the slot, so one could expect Washington's route split to lean more heavily toward the outside. Jacksonville may lean toward putting him where he can line up against Humphrey, whose struggles this season have been fairly well documented.

Elsewhere on the perimeter, Jakobi Meyers and, to a lesser extent, Brian Thomas Jr. and Josh Cameron are involved. Meyers and Thomas have only combined for three more targets than Washington has, though, so they are more clearly ancillary pieces in the passing game. The box-score expectations for them should be somewhat muted, but they can pop up at any time for key plays. (Thomas did score last week in his return to the full-time WR3 role, for example.)

The Ravens, meanwhile, have been relatively stingy against tight ends in the passing game, allowing only 35 receptions to players at the position, so it's probably not going to be the biggest night for Brenton Strange. Tight ends do have six touchdowns against the Ravens, though, so it might be worth it to at least watch out for Strange in the red zone.

You can throw out all of the Ravens' offensive stats. Not only is Lamar Jackson out, but so is backup quarterback Tyler Huntley. Jacoby Brissett is reportedly on the way to help, but he won't be active on Thursday night.

Emergency third quarterback Joe Fagnano entered the game in relief of Jackson and Huntley last Sunday and will make the start on Thursday night. Fagnano, an undrafted free agent out of UConn, went 10-of-21 for 91 yards and an interception against the Bills. He threw most of his passes to the running backs and did not connect with Zay Flowers even once.

Expect the Ravens to lean heavily on Derrick Henry and the run game, but do not expect them to find much success. Henry has been struggling of late to begin with (45 carries for 156 yards and a touchdown over the last three games combined), and the Jaguars have allowed 3.4 yards per carry this season — the single-best mark in all of football.

Unless Fagnano and offensive coordinator Declan Doyle pull off some kind of magic act, the Ravens are likely not going to be able to move the ball much on Thursday night.

The Ravens simply do not have the horses to compete with the Jaguars this week. Not with their third-string quarterback (who is coming off an injury of his own), and not on a short week. Jacksonville should roll and win with relative ease. Pick: Jaguars 27, Ravens 6

The Bluetooth Group has announced a new standard. Here's how it works and when you can expect to benefit from it.

Senior Editor / Home Security and Smart Home

Senior Editor / Home Security and Smart Home

Wireless tech is about to get a whole lot snappier. On Thursday, the Bluetooth Special Interest Group, the trade association that oversees Bluetooth, officially unveiled Bluetooth High Data Throughput, a flagship upgrade set to arrive as part of the Bluetooth Core 7.0 standard. The revision supercharges Bluetooth Low Energy by pushing maximum bit rates to 7.5 Mbps, effectively quadrupling the throughput of the existing LE 2M technology. Compatible devices are slated to hit store shelves starting in 2027.

That’s good news for phone users, tablet havers, laptop groupies, smart speaker stans and more. Bluetooth is still the most popular medium-to-short-range wireless standard for connecting devices. But this is a big rollout, and the changes will start with premium wireless, such as earbuds, with other applications following.

Those other applications could include spatial computing, exchanging AI-related info with local devices, connecting to health wearables and a host of smart home applications, like playing Spotify through an Amazon Echo Studio or dimming a smart bulb.

“Imagine a smartwatch that syncs hours’ worth of sensor-rich training data to your phone in seconds, smart glasses that deliver richer AI experiences by offloading data for processing, or a smart home product that completes a firmware update with virtually no user-visible downtime,” Øyvind Strøm, executive vice president of the short-range business unit at Nordic Semiconductor, said in a statement. “Bluetooth HDT paves the way for more sophisticated, data-intensive Bluetooth applications.”

A representative from the Bluetooth Special Interest Group didn’t respond to a request for comment.

The biggest change in this update is a new physical layer for Bluetooth. Layers are like different sections of the Bluetooth protocol, and the physical layer is the bedrock of the standard, the foundational layer involved in transmitting and receiving data signals.

Improvements in the physical layer can have very significant effects on all Bluetooth communications. In this case, new modulation schemes let the physical layer handle data more efficiently and transmit more data at once, which is why the speed increases are so significant.

These improvements also make the new standard more efficient, saving battery life in many applications. So your smart lock may last longer before you need to exchange the AA batteries, earbuds won’t need to go back in the case as fast, and so on.

We know the first flagship devices are expected to appear with the technology in early 2027, but don’t have an exact date.

All we know about which specific devices will get the technology first is that it will start with wireless audio.

With protocol changes like this, it can take some time for new products to reach the market, and manufacturers may take a long time, even years, to adopt it.

Based on Christopher Nolan’s filmography alone, I always assumed The Odyssey would be a hit. Then, when his stacked cast got announced, there was no doubt in my mind this movie would be a very big deal. Now that it is out and a certified megahit, I’ve been wondering if Matt Damon also knew this book-to-screen adaptation of Homer’s epic would be a phenomenon. Well, now he’s answered that question, and he did it in a way that’s really satisfying.

It always seemed like The Odyssey was “destined to be a smash hit,” as a reporter from GQ put it. However, when he said those words to Damon, the actor responded by saying, “There are no sure things, man.” That led to the question of when exactly the Ocean’s Eleven actor knew he’d been part of a major sensation. Here’s what the Oscar winner had to say:

I felt it when I saw it. There is that thing that can happen where you make a movie, and you feel like it’s really good, and it just doesn’t connect, and there’s just nothing you can do about that. But as the audience reactions started to come in and the critical reactions were great as well, it was like, Okay….

This certainly makes sense. I’m sure everyone can relate to the feeling of thinking you’ve done something great and then finding out it didn’t get the reactions you expected. However, in the case of The Odyssey, it was outstanding, and Damon realized that when he saw it. That fact was then solidified when The Odyssey’s first reactions came out, and then it was really validated when its stellar reviews were published.

Then, the box office made it abundantly clear that this film was one of the biggest hits on the 2026 movie schedule. Overall, The Odyssey made $1.7 billion worldwide, and $625 million domestically. At the time of this writing, that makes it the second-highest-grossing movie of the year behind Spider-Man: Brand New Day.

However, again, while this seemed like it would happen, anything could have happened. So, it makes sense why Damon wasn’t super quick to say he’d made a hit. It also makes sense why Christopher Nolan got nervous right before the movie premiered, as the Odysseus actor explained:

Chris [Nolan], the most nervous he is in the entire process, from the very beginning to the very end, is the eve of the film coming out. He’s genuinely nervous. He talks openly about it, because that is the moment that you put it out into the world and you have to live with the reaction. Ultimately, the audience decides. Once it opened and the responses were what they were, I think I felt like, We’re going to be okay. It was a big bet in the sense that it was a big budget. I’m not used to playing in that arena. The numbers that movies have to get to just to break even at that level are just so beyond the movies that I’m known for.

The Odyssey had a reported budget of $250 million, per Forbes. It also reportedly had a $125 million marketing budget. To break even, the movie had to make between $625 and $750 million, per the outlet. That’s no small feat, and the film managed not only to make its money back, but it ultimately made well over a billion dollars worldwide.

Knowing the risk and the literal cost of this film, it’s satisfying to see Damon talk about how he felt in the lead-up to the release. While it seems he knew what he was making was special, it’s impossible to know that’s the case until it’s done and the world has seen it. And for him to recall what it was like to see audiences embrace this movie is very cool.

Well, now the world has seen it, and people love it. Thankfully, the actor and director know that, too. Now, I have a feeling this phenomenon will continue into awards season, as The Odyssey and Damon are likely going to be major players in next year’s Oscars race. Which leads me to the question: When will Matt Damon realize he could be a contender for an Academy Award?

7 Netflix Mystery Shows That Hook You in the First Scene

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Leading the streaming crusade, Netflix has stood at the top of excellent content. From legendary shows during its early days like Orange Is the New Black to modern masterpieces like The Diplomat, viewers seek brilliant, risky storytelling for their binge-watching pleasure. The thrill of a good series is being drawn in so much that you'll keep watching until the very end. When it comes to Netflix's mystery options, a handful hook you within the first scene.

We are here to honor the incredible mystery-centric shows that hook you instantly. From a terrifying, disorienting wake-up that establishes the entire story to the discovery of a body that sets the mystery into hyperdrive, these shows master the art of immediate intrigue. While Netflix has some perfect shows that are incredible all the way through, they don't have the same brilliant hook as these seven entries.

Sometimes high-concept narratives scare people away, but Bodies is a highly addictive puzzle that pulls you in instantly. Created by Paul Tomalin, Bodies is based on the DC Vertigo graphic novel of the same name. The eight-episode sci-fi mystery thriller follows four different detectives in four distinct eras who all discover the exact same unidentified body in a London alleyway. As their investigations unfold across time, they realize their cases are deeply interconnected. Together, they must unravel a massive, century-spanning conspiracy that threatens humanity's future. A truly brilliant and gripping thriller, Bodies succeeds because you're dropped right into the seismic action.

To kick off the story, streetlights in London begin to flicker and pop just before Detective Sergeant Shahara Hasan (Amaka Okafor) is deployed to police a far-right protest. While monitoring the tense crowd, Shahara spots a suspicious teenage boy hiding behind a bin and carrying a gun. Against orders, she chases him through the streets until he leads her into Longharvest Lane in Whitechapel. There, instead of a simple cornered suspect, she discovers a naked, dead white male body lying on the ground with a gunshot wound to the eye. The victim doesn't just lie there cold—he gasps out a terrified, reality-bending statement before bolting, instantly elevating the mystery from a standard murder to something deeply unsettling. By plunging the audience into high-stakes, chaotic action that defies conventional crime-drama logic, the premiere makes it clear this mystery is unlike any other thriller you've seen. Rather than lingering as a slow-burn procedural, the premiere swiftly layers in parallel historical and futuristic teasers, creating an immediate puzzle of time and causality. It's an adrenaline-fueled opening that packs a thrilling punch.

While many people point to Squid Game as the foreign language thriller that changed the game, the German sci-fi mystery thriller Dark paved the way first. Created by Baran bo Odar and Jantje Friese, Dark follows four interconnected families in the small town of Winden as a child's disappearance uncovers a multi-generational time-travel conspiracy. As the characters search for the truth across linked timelines in 1953, 1986, and 2019, they become trapped in a deterministic loop of fate and cause and effect. For three strong seasons, Dark served as a brilliant time-travel drama that explores the existential implications of time and its effect on human nature and life.

In the first scene of the series, a voice-over recites a quote by Albert Einstein about time being an illusion, accompanied by a montage of ticking clocks, a complex cork board of interconnected strings and photos, and a dark study room. The sequence then moves directly to November 19, 2019, where Michael Kahnwald (Sebastian Rudolph) stands in his attic, seals a mysterious letter with strict instructions—"Do not open before November 4th at 10:13 p.m."—looks at a family photograph, and commits suicide by hanging himself. The shocking scene subverts the standard true-crime missing-persons formula and replaces it with cold, existential dread. The scene follows a calculated, deeply unsettling thematic blueprint that lets you know you are watching a tragedy already in motion. The camera lingers on this devastating, quiet moment, leaving you with urgent questions: why he did it, what's in the letter, and the significance of that specific date. By the time the hypnotic, mirrored opening credits roll to the tune of Apparat's "Goodbye," the show had already laid out its entire central puzzle.

A truly unique entry into the true crime genre, the psychological crime thriller Mindhunter, created by Joe Penhall, is based on Mindhunter: Inside the FBI's Elite Serial Crime Unit by John E. Douglas and Mark Olshaker. The series follows FBI agents Holden Ford (Jonathan Groff) and Bill Tench (Holt McCallany) as they pioneer the Behavioral Science Unit. Alongside psychologist Dr. Wendy Carr (Anna Torv), they interview incarcerated serial killers to understand their psychology and apply these insights to active murder cases. The series also explores the heavy emotional toll and institutional skepticism the team faces as it builds the foundation of modern criminal profiling. Tackling the crime genre in a unique way, this two-season series introduces a new way to analyze crime stories.

The series opens in Braddock, Pennsylvania, in 1977 with a tense hostage standoff that serves as the catalyst for the entire series. A deeply disturbed man named Cody Miller (David H. Holmes) robs a liquor store, steals a shotgun, and barricades himself inside a building with a woman and several other hostages. As local police bark orders, young FBI Special Agent Holden Ford arrives as the hostage negotiator. Holden tries to de-escalate the situation by establishing an empathetic, human connection with the suspect. He taps into the suspect's delusion as Cody demands to see his wife and begins shouting that he is literally invisible. Holden speaks softly, telling Cody that he can see he is cold, which coaxes Cody to pull his pants back up and take his primary hostage back inside. Holden learns Cody's wife is arriving, but advises against letting them speak. Realizing his demands aren't being met and that his "invisibility" is failing, Cody suddenly stops listening to Holden and blows his own head off with a shotgun. The scene closes with Holden returning home covered in blood, trying and failing to scrub the stain out of his white dress shirt.

The scene establishes the series' central conflict as Holden doesn't see the result as a success, left obsessed with the realization that traditional, authoritative FBI methods fail against abnormal psychology. Directed by David Fincher, the sudden, graphic shock instantly breaks traditional television formulas and signals to the audience that no one is safe. Holden's failure will eventually become his success, as the traditional rules of law enforcement are entirely useless against a changing landscape of irrational, deeply broken minds.

A seemingly forgotten series, The Madness is an action conspiracy thriller unlike anything you've ever experienced. Created by Stephen Belber, The Madness follows Muncie Daniels (Colman Domingo), a prominent media pundit and political commentator. While on a solo writing retreat in the Pocono Mountains, Muncie stumbles upon the body of a murdered white supremacist and is framed for the assassination. Forced to go on the run as a fugitive, he must navigate a web of mass disinformation and expose a deep political conspiracy to clear his name and keep his family safe. A high-octane thrill ride, The Madness is all about Domingo and his dominating performance from the jump.

In the opening scene, Muncie wakes up disoriented and alone in the Poconos woods, stumbles onto a nearby roadway, and flags down a ride. He goes to a local family restaurant where patrons stare at him, and he tells police officers Wagner (Hamish Allan-Headley) and Lutz (Dylan Taylor) he's had a rough morning. Suddenly, flashes of memory surface before he transitions to getting makeup done and preparing to host his CNN-style television program. The scene immediately grips you by using a flash-forward technique that forces instant empathy and high-stakes mystery. Rather than build to the crisis, the series drops you directly into the terrifying aftermath of a violent event. Injured, bloodied, and completely disoriented, the audience is instantly placed in his exact shoes as the protagonist. The peace is shattered as Muncie is shown frantically sprinting through the woods while a gunshot rings out in the distance. The shaky, claustrophobic cinematography mimics a raw survival instinct, generating immediate adrenaline and a palpable sense of danger. Then, by moving from frantic chaos to Muncie's normal life, you are desperate to figure out how an articulate, high-profile media analyst could possibly end up running for his life in a remote swamp.

Four Netflix characters, four completely different ways of showing up for somebody. This is the one who fits how you actually do friendship, and what having them in your life would be like.

You do not do friendship at half volume, and neither does he. What you want is somebody who says yes to the plan before you have finished describing the plan.

You are the friend who makes things happen, and so is she. Together you would be mildly exhausting to everyone around you and completely delighted with yourselves.

You have no patience for pleasant lies, and she has no ability to tell them. What looks like coldness from the outside is the most reliable friendship on this list.

You look after whoever is having the worst day. So does he. It is the least strategic way to move through the world and the best possible quality in a friend.

One of the greatest long-running Netflix series is The Night Agent. The highly addictive series, created by Shawn Ryan and based on the novel of the same name by Matthew Quirk, follows Peter Sutherland (Gabriel Basso), a low-level FBI agent who works in the basement of the White House monitoring a phone line that never rings—until the night it does. When a panicked tech CEO calls, Peter is thrust into a sprawling, fast-paced conspiracy involving a Russian mole at the highest levels of the U.S. government. As he runs to protect the witness and clear his own name, he uncovers secrets that lead all the way to the Oval Office. Moving into its fourth and final season, The Night Agent became an instant hit thanks to its simple yet evocative start.

In the opening sequence, FBI agent Peter Sutherland boards a DC Metro train during his commute and notices a suspicious man leaving a backpack behind, which he discovers contains a timer-triggered bomb. He immediately forces the train to make an emergency stop in a dark tunnel and helps panic-stricken passengers escape into the underground structures. The bomb detonates moments later, sending a massive fireball through the tunnel and killing one civilian while saving dozens of others thanks to Peter's quick actions. While recovering from the blast, Peter spots the bomber standing among the bystanders on the surface and gives chase, though the suspect manages to elude him after Peter is hurt in a car accident. This high-octane start raises the stakes beyond his life-saving moment. It paints the protagonist as a competent, brave, and quick-thinking hero. The frantic life-or-death scale of the flashback contrasts sharply with the quiet, mundane reality of his day job: sitting in a dark White House basement waiting for a silent emergency phone to ring. This quick-fire tragedy gives Peter instant sympathetic context and raises the emotional weight of every political secret and threat that follows.​​​​​​​

Easily the biggest travesty of 2025 was the cancellation of The Residence, as it was simply perfect. But while many fans mourn the loss of a brilliant vehicle for Uzo Aduba, let's honor why it was so good. Created by Paul William Davies and inspired by The Residence: Inside the Private World of the White House by Kate Andersen Brower, the Shondaland series is a comedic murder mystery set in the White House during a high-stakes state dinner. When the White House Chief Usher, A. B. The Residence is a fun, cozy, campy romp that delivers the right amount of mystery from the start.

In the opening scene, A.B. Wynter is found dead by the president's mother-in-law, Nan Cox (Jane Curtin), at 9:57 p.m. after a strange thump is heard. The death is immediately presented as an apparent suicide, with slit wrists and a suicide note. The president's chief adviser, Harry Hollinger (Ken Marino), and other officials try to rush a suicide ruling to avoid a political scandal during the state visit. But once the eccentric Cordelia Cupp arrives, she quickly spots fatal inconsistencies, including signs of poisoning and evidence that the body was moved. The first scene hooks you because of its abrupt, jarring contrast between extreme high-society elegance and sudden, chaotic horror. However, the show immediately subverts this stately elegance by ripping the audience up to the third floor. The story instantly becomes the ultimate locked-room mystery where everyone, even world leaders, is potential suspects. It thrusts the story into the messy inner workings of the staff who have just lost an integral cog of the building. With the arrival of Cupp, it's clear she will not play by the average rules, immediately leading you to adoring the lovable bird-watching underdog. ​​​​​​​

Smith and Elle Smith, the thriller follows Kyle Turner (Eric Bana), a special agent for the National Parks Service, as he investigates the suspicious and brutal death of a woman who fell from El Capitan in Yosemite National Park. From the beginning, Untamed shows you the majestic locale the story is set in. The camera pans over Yosemite National Park before zooming in on the sheer rock face of El Capitan. Here’s What It Takes to Keep a Pool Running During the Winter, According to Experts

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Before you dive in, it’s important to understand the risks and expenses involved.

When most people think of backyard swimming pools, relaxing outdoors in the summertime immediately comes to mind. But some homeowners don’t want the swimming season to end when temperatures drop. Fans of cold-water swimming are starting to consider ways to enjoy their pools throughout the winter as a part of an at-home wellness experience.

“Ten years ago, most people didn’t think winter swims were possible, especially in colder climates,” says Karen Larson, cofounder of Soake Pools. “Times have changed because these days, they’re seeing neighbors, friends, and others do it and post the proof on social media.”

The increasing interest in cold-water experiences is part of a broader wellness trend. In American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 Worldwide Fitness Trends report, cold therapies, including cold plunges, cryotherapy, and cold showers, earned the 17th spot among fitness trends for the year, up from number 20 in its 2025 report.

If you’ve previously closed your swimming pool around Labor Day but are interested in making cold-water swimming a part of your daily wellness routine, there’s a lot to keep in mind. Not every existing pool is a good candidate for keeping open for winter swimming, and it’s important to understand the risks and expenses involved. But if you’re curious, here’s what to consider about keeping a pool open year-round.

Why is cold-water swimming becoming more common? There are numerous purported mental and wellness benefits, from athletic recovery to stress relief and better sleep. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis published in PLOS ONE found that cold-water immersion led to a reduction in stress for 12 hours afterward as well as improvements in sleep quality and overall quality of life.

“Winter swimming often creates a time where friends and family can come together without distractions,” adds Larson.

That said, while swimming during the colder months has its perks, there are also several risks to keep in mind. A 2020 review in the International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health found that cold-water immersion can trigger an immediate shock response, leading to involuntary gasping, hyperventilation, and a rapid increase in heart rate and blood pressure. These reactions can make it difficult to control your breathing and increase your risk of drowning. Cold temps can also impair your muscle strength and nerve function, making it challenging to swim safely.

It’s a good idea to acclimate to the cold water gradually and avoid prolonged exposure. You’ll also want to make sure there’s someone else home while you take your cold-water plunge in the pool.

In addition, if you have underlying conditions like heart disease, consult your doctor before all else, as the cold water may place additional strain on your heart.

If you’re trying to consider whether your pool is a good candidate to keep open during the colder months, these are the things to look into.

The cost of keeping a swimming pool open for winter swimming will depend on your location, size, desired water temperature, and the type of heater you use.

“Routine pool maintenance generally remains about the same year-round, although chemical consumption may decrease depending on how frequently the pool is used,” explains Jim Eisch, Jr., vice president of operations at Pinch A Penny Pool Patio Spa. “The biggest expense to consider in the winter is heating the pool.”

For this reason, prospective winter swimmers may want to consider an upgrade to their heating system. Instead of using pricey natural gas heating or a traditional heat pump, Eisch says that inverter technology is one of the most affordable ways to maintain a swimmable pool temperature year-round.

For example, if you live in Florida and have a 10,000-gallon swimming pool, Eisch estimates your annual heating costs will be $2,467 with a natural gas heater, $741 with a traditional heat pump, and $434 for a heat/cool pump that uses inverter technology.

As aforementioned, the goal with wintertime swimming is not to be soaking in bathwater-like temperatures while it’s freezing outside. For safety purposes, winter swims are quick, and the heating system is to keep your chlorinated pool water well above the 19-degree Fahrenheit freezing point.

Unsurprisingly, a standard 14-by-28-foot swimming pool is going to be one of the hardest to use effectively year-round, unless you live in a region with a mild wintertime climate.

“You can technically use a large pool year-round, but it’s difficult to cover all its surface area well enough to hold in the heat, so heating bills can climb fast,” says Larson.

Therefore, smaller sizes like a 10-by-16-foot cocktail pool or plunge pool are better candidates for keeping open. Dedicated winter swimmers should also invest in an easy on-and-off cover—or a motorized auto cover—for added protection.

Regular pool cleaning and maintenance are essential, and this upkeep over the winter is no exception.

“Check the filtration system regularly to make sure it’s circulating smoothly, monitor the water chemistry, and plan for power outages—because there’s a good chance they will happen,” Larson explains. For this reason, you should also invest in a generator that can keep the pump running if the power goes out.

Safe pool access is just as important as any other aspect of the pool’s design. That’s why a nonslip surface, excellent drainage, and ample lighting are key.

“Your design should also provide a clear path from the house to the water,” says Neha Gupta, interior designer and founder at NU Curations.

Especially in the wintertime when ice and snow are a factor in your region, make sure you have a safe and smooth exit plan for getting out of the water, bundled up, and into your warm home without any obstacles. And then, of course, remember to cover the pool back up to keep it protected and ready for your next plunge.
