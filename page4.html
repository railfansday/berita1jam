Zidane: ‘France competitive, Lippi a master and I’d love to be Juventus coach one day’

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Zinedine Zidane is sure France will be ‘competitive’ against Italy even without Kylian Mbappé, and admits Marcello Lippi ‘taught me so much about tactics’ at Juventus, so he’d love to be their coach one day.

It kicks off at San Siro on Thursday at 19.45 GMT (20.45 CET).

Only the top two go through to the quarter-finals of the Nations League, and currently Les Bleus lead Group A1 with 10 points, followed by Italy on seven and Belgium with six.

Considering Italy and Belgium go head-to-head in Brussels for the final match, and France host rock bottom Turkiye, they are practically guaranteed at least second.

There was a heavy blow with news Mbappé will not even be on the bench tomorrow due to a hamstring issue, especially after the likes of Marcus Thuram, Esteban Lepaul, Ousmane Dembélé, Aurelien Tchouameni, Manu Koné and Pierre Kalulu pulled out.

“Mbappé will not be there, he felt some pain and we generally try to avoid risks for the players, but we hope to have him on Sunday,” said Zidane in his press conference.

“We sent Tchouameni and Thuram home because they were not in any condition to play on Thursday or Sunday, so I appreciated their decision to come to the training camp even when not fully fit, it was good to see their desire to represent France.

“Obviously, Mbappé wanted to play tomorrow as well, but we have to adopt a policy of zero risks. The good news is that he could be available on Sunday. It’s November, there are lots of games, it’s a complicated month, so players have to safeguard their health.”

With so many absentees in attack, who will start upfront against Italy?

“The objective is to pick the best possible line-up, and despite the absences, we have a squad capable of doing well. We will be able to field a competitive team,” added Zidane.

The coach has made a habit of adapting some of the players to roles they are not accustomed to at club level, such as Inter’s Andy Diouf going from right-sided wing-back to a left-back for France.

“We know that he plays in a different position at Inter, but he can cover various roles. When he is with us, it is to play on the left. I want to see players up close and I do try to use them in their natural positions. I can confirm Warren Zaire Emery will play in midfield, for example.”

Zidane has only failed to win one game so far on the France bench, and that was the 1-1 draw in Paris against Italy.

A former Juventus midfielder, he is no surprised by the rebirth of the Azzurri under Roberto Mancini.

“We had a complicated night there because we know that when Italy want to put in a good performance, they certainly can, especially when defending. I played at Juventus for four or five years, so I know that for an Italian coach, defence comes first, then the attack. It’s something Italy does so well.”

Zidane was asked in the press conference if he would like to be the coach of Juventus one day, a role Didier Deschamps already took.

“Yes, but you never know. Marcello Lippi taught me we must never stop, we’ve always got to keep pushing. At the start I didn’t play well in Turin, the fans and the media didn’t love me.

“I suffered a little, but Lippi told me I would play for his Juve. I owe him everything. I’d love one day to be at Juventus, why not, if I continue working as a coach.”

Although Zidane hit the heights of his playing career at Real Madrid, he assures the seeds were sowed at Juventus first.

“We spent so much of our day working on tactics with Lippi in training, so for someone like me who always wants the ball at his feet, it means a steep learning curve.

“A master like Lippi taught me so much on a tactical level. He also put so much faith in me, and I tried to repay him on the field.”

Of course, Lippi went on to win the World Cup with Italy in 2006, beating France in the Final with Zidane sent off.

CoreWeave turns to customer prepayments, shorter contracts to fund AI growth

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

CoreWeave is rewriting how it pays for the AI boom, according to the company’s third-quarter earnings, reported on Tuesday. CoreWeave, a neocloud that rents out the computing power behind AI, said it’s increasingly asking customers to pay up front, borrowing against specific contracts, and even pursuing short-term deals that could lead to higher prices.

The new strategies could help CoreWeave finance its growth more efficiently while squeezing more cash from existing infrastructure. But these bets only hold if today’s demand and pricing power continue.

CoreWeave reported $3.66 billion in revenue, up 168% from the year prior, but it also posted a $709 million net loss and $846 million in quarterly interest expense.

“We will not use the unsecured debt market to fund our business at current yields, nor do we need to,” CoreWeave CEO Michael Intrator said during Tuesday’s earnings call.

Instead, CoreWeave’s plan is to rely on loans backed by specific customer contracts, drawing on funds as needed to finance most of infrastructure it still has to build to deliver on its significant backlog of $132.5 billion.

CoreWeave also said it expects about $9 billion in customer prepayments and deposits over the next three months, including more than $3 billion tied to signing new customer contracts for capacity it has already built, which requires no additional spending on hardware.

Rivals Nebius and IREN have also relied on hefty Microsoft prepayments to pay for computing capacity, signaling that the industry is moving toward asking Big Tech to pay up for their buildouts.

Upfront payments could alleviate CoreWeave’s immediate borrowing needs, but they also commit the company to providing services at a price that might be less attractive down the road if compute demand continues to surge.

CoreWeave is also looking to capitalize on that growing demand by signing more shorter-term contracts that can command much higher prices.

The company raised prices across its offerings by about 25% in July and by another 10% later in the quarter. Prices are still climbing this quarter, in a way that “more than offset[s] cost increases,” said Intrator.

“We are also allocating more capacity to short-term opportunities, where the pricing justifies it, selling capacity at more than $40 million per megawatt annually, while pricing for new infrastructure on four and five-year contracts is moving to $20 million to $25 million per megawatt,” the chief executive said.

CoreWeave’s financing strategy now revolves around those opportunities. In August, the company closed a $2.6 billion loan, its first designed specifically for shorter-term customer contracts. Known as DDTL 5.5, it lets CoreWeave borrow in installments as it needs the money.

While short-term deals let CoreWeave charge higher prices and change them more frequently, it’s a tradeoff. Lenders give more weight to longer-term contracts, even if they’re priced lower, because they value predictable cash flow and consistency to repay the loan above all.

Even though Wall Street reacted positively to CoreWeave’s earnings, bond investors are much less sanguine. Last month, yields on some of the company’s bonds hit above 15%, highlighting that investors are concerned about the debt needed to fund data center buildouts before those investments see a return.

In short, CoreWeave is finding new ways to expand without relying on unsecured debt. The strategy it has chosen could boost profits, but it also carries greater risk around contract renewals and pricing — and it just goes to show how dependent the AI infrastructure boom is on sustained, and as yet unproven, demand.

"Well, that's not just because of pass rush at all. I think I understand what you're asking... I'm gonna keep those conversations between Will and me [private], as far as what he had to improve on. Doctor Doom has returned to the Marvel Cinematic Universe a month before Avengers: Doomsday, and the latest appearance of Robert Downey Jr.'s new MCU character retcons the villain's timeline in the franchise. Doctor Doom is one of the most powerful characters in Marvel Comics, and with magic and technology at his side in December's Avengers movie, he will be so too in the MCU.

After all, we have already seen Doctor Doom easily send Thor flying in Avengers: Doomsday's trailers. However, in terms of substance, Doctor Doom has not had many MCU appearances outside the footage for the upcoming Marvel movie. That was to be expected, given how the shared universe was originally set on Kang the Conqueror as the Multiverse Saga's Thanos replacement.

The pivot to Downey as Doctor Doom came late in the game, following Jonathan Majors' MCU firing, which meant that the character was entirely absent from the early phases of this multi-year plan, different from Kang and his multiverse variants, who had been set up for the scrapped Avengers: The Kang Dynasty through appearances in Loki and Ant-Man and the Wasp: Quantumania.

Before the VisionQuest finale was released on Disney+, Downey's Doctor Doom had appeared in The Fantastic Four: First Steps, which is not set in the main MCU Earth, and made his Earth-616 debut in one of Avengers: Endgame Encore's post-credits scenes, specifically the one where he showed up to take Mark Ruffalo's Hulk away following the events of Spider-Man: Brand New Day. Then came the VisionQuest finale, which shockingly had Downey return as Doctor Doom to resurrect Elizabeth Olsen's Scarlet Witch. Judging by how Marvel Studios presented the scene, using footage from Wanda Maximoff's death in Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness to then have Doctor Doom lift the ruins of Mount Wundagore, which fell on top of Wanda, and resurrect the character, it seems like Doom saved Elizabeth Olsen's MCU hero right after the events of the Doctor Strange sequel. This retcons his presence in the MCU and Earth-616, making his first appearance on that Earth set earlier in the MCU timeline.

Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness happened shortly after Spider-Man: No Way Home, with the movie being set in 2024 in the MCU timeline. Now, there are two key ways in which that could have happened. The other is that, like Downey's other character, Tony Stark/Iron Man, Doctor Doom cracked time travel, so he manages to go back in time to resurrect Wanda after arriving on Earth-616 following Spider-Man: Brand New Day. Bob Dylan’s Meeting With Pope Leo: People Fixated on the Hood But the Hands Spoke Volumes

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Timothée Chalamet fever aside, nothing Bob Dylan has done in the wild in recent years has been so widely discussed by mainstream media — and so instantly meme-ified — as the photos of Dylan, in a hood pulled up and over his head, meeting Pope Leo XIV at the Vatican on Nov. 4.

That one wore black, the other white had Twitter tripping over itself to make pithy comments like it was a New Yorker cartoon caption contest. And Dylan’s hoodie was instant headline fodder. The Wall Street Journal clutched their pearls. GQ announced that Dylan dressed like Chalamet. The Daily Beast reported outrage! Star Wars was mentioned… a lot.

But Antonio Spadaro, a Jesuit priest serving as undersecretary for culture and education at the Holy See who brokered the meeting, says Dylan’s attire spoke so loudly that people missed the larger message.

“People talked a lot about what Dylan was wearing that afternoon and very little about what the pope had written,” Spadaro says, referring to the fact that the pope quoted “Go Away You Bomb” — an unpublished 1963 Dylan work — in the preface of his 2026 book, Disarmed and Disarming: Peace Is a Gift.

In the work, Dylan wrote to the bomb: “I hate you cause yer man-made and man-owned, an’ man-handled.”

“In 1963, a young man just past 20, in New York, told a bomb to its face that it was the work of human hands,” Spadaro says. Sixty-three years later “the author of that sheet of paper — which no one had wanted to buy at auction — shook hands with his most authoritative reader. For anyone who believes in the weight of words, it is a small parable. The hood, by comparison, is a side note.”

Since the musical and papal summit, Spadaro — a native of Messina, Italy, and former editor-in-chief of La Civiltà Cattolica, who now writes a Substack —  has been doing the Lord’s work by sharing photos of the Midwesterners’ meeting on social media.

He posted a few shots of their handshake. One photo, which he posted twice, was striking: A zoomed-in shot of Dylan’s hand clasping the pope’s.  Editor’s picks The 250 Greatest Albums of the 21st Century So Far The 100 Best TV Episodes of All Time The 500 Greatest Albums of All Time 100 Best Movies of the 21st Century

With its blue veins, purple-ish hue, and dry white powdery skin, Dylan’s hand looking every minute of his 85 years. But the appendage was also a peek behind the curtain. A lift under the hood. That papery hand seemed to say: “I’m human and I need this spiritual touch as much as the next guy.”

We asked Spadaro, 60, to elaborate on the photos he posted on Instagram. Via email, the priest and author —  whose most recent books are A passo d’uomo with a foreword by Patti Smith, and Conversations on Faith written with Martin Scorsese — shared his thoughts on hands, hoods, and how David Sedaris fits into all this.

I was so moved by the photo you posted on Instagram, a close-up of their hands clasped. Almost like a Renaissance painting detail, a meeting of two old sages. What prompted you to zoom in that close?The hands said what the hood kept hidden. Bob’s face was in shadow; his hands, though, were bare, and he had bared them himself, taking off his gloves to greet the pope. That handclasp holds the hand of the young man, just past 20, who typed out “Go Away You Bomb” in 1963, and the hand of the pope who quoted it against nuclear rearmament 63 years later.

You’re right to think of painting. I thought of Dürer’s “Praying Hands,” the 1508 study in which the veins and the worn skin tell an entire life.

By tightening the frame, I took away the fame and the colors of their clothes. What remained were two men well along in years, clasping hands, and it seemed to me the truest photograph of that afternoon. Dylan morphs over the years, sartorially. When he was in his Nudie-suit-and-cowboy-hat-phase, he wore that to meet Pope John Paul II. I love that Dylan stayed true to his current phase here. Did the pope have a reaction to that? Did anyone?One detail about 1997 makes the story even better. In Bologna, Dylan performed in his cowboy hat, but when he climbed the steps toward John Paul II, he took it off. This time the hood stayed where it was, and what came off were the gloves, so he could shake the pope’s hand. In 1997 he bared his head; in 2026, his hands.

The only one who hesitated, I admit, was me. At St. Anne’s Gate, I asked his team whether it might be better to lower it, and they explained that the hood, these days, is part of how Bob moves through the world, onstage and off. That was enough for me.

Leo looked the man in the eye, and the hood receded into the background. On social media, some people brought up Emperor Palpatine [from Star Wars]. The scene was much simpler. The pope was perfectly at ease, as if he already knew Bob would show up that way.

What sparked this meeting? Did Dylan want to meet the pope or vice versa? How did you get involved?The wish was there on both sides: Bob wanted to meet the first American pope, and the pope needed no convincing. A man who has already quoted you in a footnote is ready to receive you.

I knew the right people on both sides, and I found myself in the middle, as a kind of hub. The real work was scheduling. Dylan played Milan on Monday and was due to play Rome Thursday. The pope was leaving for Latin America on Friday. That left Wednesday, a day when Bob had no show. That was all it took.

The pope seems to be a Dylan fan. He quoted from “Go Away You Bomb” in his book —that’s a deep pull. I’m a Dylan nerd, and had to Google it. I could only find 2013 articles about those lyrics going up for auction at Christie’s. They never even sold. Is the Pope a hardcore Dylanologist?It depends on what we mean by Dylanologist. The word makes me think of A.J. Weberman, who in the early 1970s went through Dylan’s trash in Greenwich Village. In that sense, I’d say definitely not.

[That] you had to Google, though, says a lot. “Go Away You Bomb” isn’t on any record. Dylan wrote it in 1963 and never recorded it. It was meant for a songbook against the atomic bomb that Izzy Young, of the Folklore Center, never managed to publish.

The sheet stayed among Young’s papers for half a century, then went up for auction at Christie’s — that’s how the lyrics became public. The pope came to it as a reader, so much so that in the footnote he calls it a “poetic composition” and places it next to Augustine and Paul VI, with title and year.

He could have chosen “Masters of War,” which everyone knows. I don’t see a Dylanologist so much as a reader who knows where to look.

You wrote about Jim Musselman, the pope’s Villanova classmate, on Instagram. Tell me more about their Dylan connection. The two met at Villanova in the 1970s, in a philosophy class, and immediately found common ground in two things, baseball and Bob Dylan.

After Leo’s election, Musselman recalled that the two of them talked about music a lot, and that he had given his friend an essay arguing that Dylan was the great poetic talent of their generation. Which essay, I don’t know.

I really like the rest of his story. Musselman founded a folk label, Appleseed Recordings, and for years released records by Pete Seeger and other singers of social conscience. Leo’s Dylan comes from there: from a classmate with a passion for songs that set out to change things.

Dylan had a private talk with the pope. You said you weren’t there.No, and I think that’s as it should be. I understood that Bob wanted to be alone with Leo, and we left the room. I saw the before and the after.

Before: two men sitting across from each other at a table in the library, some light banter, the exchange of gifts, a rosary on one side and a signed copy of Chronicles on the other.

After: Bob walking out in silence, deeply absorbed, with the look of a man who is happy and has no intention of explaining why. Then again, Dylan has spent 60 years not explaining his songs. He certainly wasn’t going to start with that conversation.

Dylan signed your copy of 64 Lyrics. I love that he used your four-color BIC pen. People jumped on that detail on social media.“64 Lyrics” is a bilingual anthology published in Italy by Crocetti in its poetry series: 64 songs — English on one side, the Italian translation on the other. It was edited by Alessandro Carrera, Dylan’s Italian translator, and Carlo Feltrinelli, his Italian publisher, who was with us that afternoon in the Apostolic Palace.

At some point in the afternoon I asked him if he would sign it for me. He smiled and said “maybe,” and I pulled the pen out of my pocket. I love those pens. I clicked down the black myself.

I understand why people had fun with it. On an afternoon of loggias and gendarmes, the object everyone remembers is a pen that costs pocket change. To me it seems perfect: even a Nobel Laureate, when the moment comes, signs with whatever pen is at hand.

You also connected Patti Smith’s meeting with Pope Leo this past summer.  Patti and I have known each other for five years. We met in Amsterdam, at a debate held by the Nexus Institute, which had invited us both; then I interviewed her in New York, and a friendship grew out of that, made mostly of long breakfasts near her home. When I’m in town we get together.

After the election of Leo, at some point it became clear to me that she wanted to meet him. In late July, Patti was touring Europe and was due to play Rome. That was the opportunity. The pope said yes, and I made two schedules fit together.

What was that like to witness? Again, I love the post you shared on Instagram — she’s gazing at the pope, almost like a kid. I saw a woman who has sung in front of enormous crowds become almost speechless. [Her] first sound was “Hi,” and anyone who knows “Wave” knows where that comes from. In that 1979 song, Patti imagines talking to John Paul I and begins exactly that way. Forty-seven years later, the imagined conversation became real. Leo caught the reference right away. They talked for about 40 minutes, and at the end she took her leave with “Bye. Bye.” Between those two tiny words was a conversation between two people from Chicago who recognized each other.
