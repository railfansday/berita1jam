7 Agatha Christie Books Inspired by Real Crimes

Agatha Christie did not write true crime. Instead, her talent was imagining complex and compelling puzzles surrounding a murder. Several of them were inspired by experiences from her own life, such as working as a nurse during WWI or traveling through the Middle East, accompanying her archeologist second husband to excavation sites. But not even the Queen of Crime could avoid the coverage of real crimes in newspapers and media. Although the stories and characters are always her own, some of Christie's most famous murder mysteries take inspiration from real-life cases.

Many real crimes and criminals are explicitly mentioned in Agatha Christie's novels and short stories. "Remember the long-continued successes of Jack the Ripper," Hercule Poirot tells Captain Arthur Hastings in the 1936 novel The ABC Murders. It is often believed the story of the Victorian serial killer inspired the plot of the book. Christie also held a deep fascination for the 1892 Lizzie Borden case, and the rhyme inspired by it is recited in After the Funeral. Other times, Christie did more than just borrow the general idea of a crime, and incorporated specific details related to a murder for her stories. Here are some of the most fascinating cases.

In 1916, during World War I, Agatha Christie was working in a hospital dispensary and was bored. Christie decided to use this time to write a detective novel. "Since I was surrounded by poisons, perhaps it was natural that death by poisoning should be the method I selected," Christie herself wrote in her autobiography. Inspired by the detective book tradition, she created her own iconic investigator. He would be Belgian, like many of the refugees she was interacting with. His name would be Hercule Poirot.

The Mysterious Affair at Styles was inspired by not one but two real murder cases. The fictional death of wealthy Emily Inglethorp at her Essex country house, Styles Court, has many parallels to that of Frances Garnett Orme. The 49-year-old spiritualist was found dead from poison in her locked room in the Savoy Hotel in Mussoorie, India, in 1911. Orme's companion was the main suspect. The book also echoes the story of William Palmer, an English doctor convicted in 1856 of poisoning his friend, John Cook, with strychnine, the same poison Agatha Christie uses in the book. It was suspected that Palmer had also killed several members of his family, including four of his children. In addition to the murder method, Agatha Christie's novel also includes several key details from the Palmer case, such as coffee left in a hallway, an inquest at the village inn and a pharmacist who sold the poison thinking it would be used to kill a dog.

Billie Carleton was a British musical comedy actress in WWI London. By 18, she was a star. Four years later, and days after the end of the war, Carleton was found dead of an apparent drug overdose. The night before, she had attended the Victory Ball at the Royal Albert Hall dressed in a scandalous semi-transparent dress designed by her friend Reggie de Veulle. An investigation found De Veulle had provided Carleton with the substances and revealed he had been part of a secret underground London cocaine trafficking ring. He was sentenced to eight months in prison.

Agatha Christie's The Affair at the Victory Ball, one of the first Hercule Poirot short stories, takes readers to the inside of British high society. It is set during this luxurious celebration of the end of the war, which a group of people attended dressed in the costumes of the Italian commedia dell’arte. During the ball, Lord Cronshaw, dressed as Harlequin, is stabbed to death. The morning after the ball, his fiancée, actress Coco Courtnay, is found dead in her bed from a cocaine overdose. Christie never said the story was based on Billie Carleton’s death, but the details speak for themselves.

On the evening of 31 January 1910, Cora Crippen, an extravagant music hall singer, disappeared from her London home. Her husband, the quiet American homeopath Hawley Harvey Crippen, claimed she had left him and moved to the U.S. Soon after, his mistress, Ethel Le Neve, moved into his house and began wearing Mrs Crippen's clothes and jewelry. When the case caught the police's attention, Dr. Crippen and Le Neve fled on a ship to Canada, with Le Neve disguised as a boy. However, the ship's captain recognized the pair and notified his superiors using wireless telegraphy. It was the first time this technology was used to track a criminal. In their absence, the police had searched the home and found human remains. Police officers then jumped onto a faster ship and chased the doctor and his mistress. They were arrested, and Dr. Crippen was found guilty of the murder. Le Neve was acquitted and later changed her name to distance herself from the case.

The story of the Crippen murder made international headlines, and even caught Agatha Christie's eye. She borrowed many details from the crime for her witty novel Mrs. McGinty's Dead, in which James Bentley is convicted of killing the elderly Mrs. McGinty. Hercule Poirot is asked to take on the case. The trail leads him to a newspaper clipping Mrs. McGinty kept that profiles women connected to notorious murder cases. One of them is Eva Kane, the mistress in the fictional "Craig case". Like Le Neve, Kane was found not guilty of the murder of her lover's wife. In the book, Kane changes her name and leaves the country. Her story is the key to the case.

The Mousetrap still holds the record for the longest-running play in the world. The production, which first opened in 1952, is the same that can be seen today in London's West End. It is based on a radio play written by Agatha Christie as a birthday present for Queen Mary, titled Three Blind Mice. Christie then expanded the plot into a short story, but she requested that it not be published until the play had closed. Since The Mousetrap is still running, this has not happened yet in the UK, although the story has been published in the U.S.

Both The Mousetrap and Three Blind Mice are based on the same case, the tragic death of 12-year-old Dennis O'Neill in 1945. Little Dennis and his younger brother, Terence (Terry) O'Neill, aged 9, were placed in foster care and sent to live with Reginald and Esther Gough on a remote English farm. Under their care, the children were beaten and barely fed. Less than a year later, Dennis died after being struck on the chest and beaten. The couple was charged with manslaughter and neglect in a grueling case made worse when young Terry was asked to act as a witness in the couple's trial. Terry's testimony angered the whole country and helped bring about the Children Act of 1948. The case became a clear inspiration for Agatha Christie's story, in which three fictional foster care children are sent to live in Longridge Farm, where one of them dies. Years later, one of their siblings takes revenge.

Toni Collette and Kit Harington are shooting a new Miss Marple and 20th Century has several more Christies in development, so the drawing room is busier than it has been in decades. Here is which of them you are, and the case for yours being the best.

Order, method, and a quiet confidence that you are the cleverest person present. Poirot is a retired Belgian police officer who solves murders by sitting still and thinking, and who is vain enough to make the reveal a performance every single time.

People tell you things because they cannot imagine you being a threat. Marple solves murders by comparing everybody to somebody she once knew in St Mary Mead, which sounds like a joke until you notice it works every time, because human beings are not as original as they think.

You have far too many ideas and roughly a fifth of them are excellent. Ariadne Oliver is a successful crime novelist who is sick of her famous foreign detective and keeps saying so, which is Christie writing about Christie and getting away with it completely.

You are steady, decent and the reason the difficult genius has anybody at all. Hastings narrates the early Poirot novels, misses most of the clues, and is the only character in the books that Poirot is unambiguously fond of, which tells you what he is really for.

The second Poirot story Agatha Christie wrote takes the detective to France. When he and Captain Hastings arrive, they are met with the news that the man they came to see, Paul Renauld, has died. His widow, Eloise Renauld, claims a group of masked men broke into her villa in the middle of the night, tied her up to the bed, and dragged her husband to the golf course, where they stabbed him to death. The investigation takes a turn when a second body is discovered, killed in a nearly identical way.

The setup of Murder on the Links follows that of one of France's most notorious murder trials. In May 1908, Marguerite Steinheil was found gagged and bound to a bed. Like Mrs. Renauld, Marguerite claimed she was attacked by intruders who killed her mother and husband, the painter Adolphe Steinheil. Marguerite was already infamous in Paris. Years before, she had been the mistress of French President Félix Faure, who had died in her arms after suffering a stroke. Marguerite tried to frame several people for the murder of her family members, but she was found out. It was widely believed that she herself had staged the intrusion to murder her husband. With no proof to back the accusation, a French jury acquitted Marguerite Stenheil in 1909 after an extremely sensationalistic trial.

Just as with a good murder-mystery, a list can take a turn. In this case, it is worth highlighting The Pale Horse as the only Agatha Christie book that inspired a crime, helped solve another, and saved several lives. In the novel, Mrs Davis gives her last confession to Father Gorman along with a list of names. Shortly after, the priest is murdered, and the people on the list begin dying from what seem to be natural causes.

At the heart of this book is a poison. It is made from an odorless heavy metal called thallium, which, if ingested in small doses, can cause symptoms such as hair loss and gastrointestinal distress that doctors routinely misdiagnose. The poison is said to have inspired Graham Young, known as "The Teacup Poisoner". In 1972, Young was accused of lacing his coworkers' tea and coffee with thallium and antimony, killing two colleagues. Some accounts say Young was caught thanks to a doctor who had read Agatha Christie's novel and became suspicious. Thallium was also the method used by George Trepal to poison his neighbors, the Carr family, killing Peggy Carr. An undercover detective who had been in Trepal's home recalled seeing a copy of The Pale Horse.

Agatha Christie's book also saved lives. In 1975, more than a decade after the novel was published, Christie received a letter from a reader in South America who had noticed a friend suffering from unexplained hair loss and pain. The reader remembered the plot of The Pale Horse and alerted the woman's physicians, who confirmed she had suffered thallium poisoning. Two years later, a one-year-old Qatari girl was flown to London. She was in a coma caused by an illness no doctors could diagnose. It was Marsha Maitland, a nurse in the hospital who had been reading The Pale Horse, who suggested thallium poisoning. The test came back positive, allowing doctors to find the treatment that saved the toddler's life.

One child was kidnapped. Six people died as a direct consequence. This is the tragic arc at the heart of what is perhaps Agatha Christie's most famous novel, Murder on the Orient Express. In the book, Hercule Poirot is tasked with investigating the murder of American businessman Samuel Ratchett aboard the luxurious Orient Express. All the suspects in the murder are staying inside the same train, trapped in Yugoslavia in the middle of a snowstorm. Poirot soon finds that the murder is connected to an older tragedy: the kidnapping and murder of little Daisy Armstrong. The conclusion to the story is what makes it so compelling. It is tragic and simply brilliant.

In the book, Agatha Christie aims to give a sense of justice to a real-life tragic crime. In 1932, 20-month-old Charles Lindbergh Jr, was taken from his crib in his New Jersey home. He was the son of the most famous aviator in the world, whose name he shared. The authors of the crime requested a $50,000 ransom. The parents paid. Soon after, the baby's body was found in the woods near the house. Weeks later, a young maid, Violet Sharpe, killed herself after being questioned by the police as a suspect. The crime was so famous it led to the passing of the Federal Kidnapping Act (the "Little Lindbergh Law"). Agatha Christie's fictional Armstrong case reproduces the Lindbergh case almost point for point. Except, of course, real crimes are rarely clear-cut. In 1934, a carpenter named Bruno Richard Hauptmann was arrested for the crime. Hauptmann was executed in 1936, although his guilt continues to be called into question even today.

The House of Mouse, written by longtime Wall Street Journal reporter Robbie Whelan, is an unauthorized biography and corporate history of the former Disney CEO that releases Dec. 1. Iger reportedly declined to be interviewed for the book.

In the book, Whelan claimed Iger had an extramarital affair with an ABC marketer in the early 1990s while he was running ABC Entertainment at the time, according to The New York Times, which obtained an advance copy. Jonathan Nolan's 103-Episode Sci-Fi Series Is Westworld But Better

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jonathan Nolan has enjoyed a hugely successful career, helping shape some of modern sci-fi’s most ambitious films and TV shows. However, among his many achievements sits a particularly frustrating misfire: HBO’s Westworld. Its spectacular opening season set up a show that felt capable of redefining science-fiction on the small screen, pairing blockbuster production values with deep thematic questions about the very nature of consciousness. Unfortunately, by the time Westworld finished, it had become synonymous with the idea of TV series that start strong but quickly fell off.

Westworld’s journey from being touted as the next great pop-culture obsession to a series many viewers would rather forget is now infamous. Fortunately, Jonathan Nolan’s filmography contains another show that tackles similar ideas while becoming more, not less, rewarding as it goes on. The specifics of Nolan's Person of Interest may not feel much like Westworld, but beneath the differences explores many of the same ideas about artificial intelligence, human freedom, and the responsibilities of creation.

Airing from 2011 to 2016 on CBS, Person of Interest is Jonathan Nolan's 103-episode saga of former CIA operative John Reese (Jim Caviezel), billionaire Harold Finch (Michael Emerson), and the advanced surveillance system, the Machine, that they use to identify people connected to imminent violent crimes. From this premise, Person of Interest gradually expands from a crime procedural with a technological twist into a gripping and deep exploration of humanity’s relationship with increasingly powerful technology. For Westworld fans still wishing that the HBO show's extraordinary beginning had delivered lasting greatness, Person of Interest is the answer.

While a crime procedural about anticipating crimes before they happen and a drama about a wild west theme park full of synthetic beings are wildly different, Jonathan Nolan’s Person of Interest and Westworld share a fascination with how humanity treats the intelligent beings it feels inevitable that it will one day create. Both series question whether artificial consciousness deserves compassion and whether its creators have any right to control it. Person of Interest explores these concerns through Finch’s relationship with the Machine. His efforts to give it moral boundaries become an ongoing examination of the difference between teaching something to care and demanding its obedience.

The procedural format of Person of Interest means the series doesn’t have to waste time diving into these ideas. Every episode can explore the same concept from a new angle with a self-contained story. Each person the team tries to save reinforces the importance of an individual life, making the Machine’s developing understanding of humanity feel earned. When the story eventually expands into competing visions of an AI-controlled future and a more serialized long-form narrative, viewers understand the risks of an advanced AI system that sees human beings as data. They also have a firm grasp of whose responsibility it is to ensure this doesn’t happen.

That gives Person of Interest a firmer emotional foundation than Westworld managed to create. Its arguments about the relationship between security and freedom in a world where technology is advancing at an exponential speed emerge through decisions made by characters that viewers have been on dozens of episodic adventures with. The show’s escalation from its crime-procedural roots in later seasons feels like the premise reaching its full potential rather than being forced. By the time Person of Interest starts asking viewers to think bigger, they’re more than ready.

It’s a stark contrast to Westworld, whose story was so complex that understanding it left little room to try and digest its core philosophical message. The HBO series lost itself within an overly convoluted mystery box. It had some great and intriguing ideas, but few were willing to stick around to discover them. Person of Interest certainly has its mysteries, but its central themes remain clear even as its sci-fi became more ambitious. For viewers disappointed by Westworld’s trajectory, that combination of intellectual ambition and emotional clarity makes it an exceptionally satisfying alternative.

Having Person of Interest available to satisfy the appetite for thoughtful AI-focused storytelling that Westworld should have done is welcome, but the comparison is bittersweet. There are very few out there who would say Westworld is a great show from start to finish, but it easily could have been if it lived up to its incredible first seasons throughout its run. The HBO show created enormous expectations for itself when it first arrived. Its luxurious cinematography, disturbing premise, and extraordinary cast performances made it feel it was poised to be a hit on the scale of the likes of Lost or Game of Thrones.

Westworld’s premise alone drew immediate attention. The idea of a playground populated by sentient but unaware AI that wealthy guests could indulge their worst impulses against was a strong hook, and one that had obvious potential for something bigger. The first season did a great job of taking this foundation and creating a story and characters viewers cared about. The anticipation of discovering how its elements connected and where the wider narrative would take the premise felt like it offered more than the satisfaction of solving a mystery.

The park itself was also key to why season 1 of Westworld was so good. It gave viewers a tangible and unique setting within which to explore the show’s ideas. Scripted conversations, repeated routines, and the contrast between the western landscape and clinical backstage facilities made the questions about free will feel worth answering. The show barely needed to explain why the arrangement was horrifying and why finding a new status quo mattered on an emotional level. The initial worldbuilding did all the work without requiring heavy-handed exposition. Despite the complexity of its ideas, Westworld’s early chapters had a clear sense of purpose.

Had Westworld sustained that balance and momentum after season 1, it would probably rank among the greatest science fiction shows ever made. At its peak, its cinematic scale and emotional force offered a jaw-dropping experience that inarguably outshined Person of Interest’s comparatively modest beginnings. Westworld’s decline isn’t just disappointing, it’s actually frustrating when thinking about how good it could have been.

Fidelity’s Chainlink ETF proposal would hold LINK directly and trade on Nasdaq if it clears the remaining steps. The proposed Fidelity Chainlink Fund, described in a November 25 registration statement, would use the ticker FLNK, although the filing is preliminary and no launch date or final annual fee has been provided.

The larger question is not whether Wall Street can package LINK. It already has. The question is whether Fidelity can attract fresh demand for the token rather than divide the existing market among another set of fund shares.

The proposed fund would hold LINK, not a basket of blockchain companies or derivatives intended to mimic LINK’s price. Its objective is to follow the token’s U.S. dollar performance, less expenses and liabilities. The prospectus identifies Anchorage Digital Bank, BitGo Bank & Trust and Coinbase Custody as custodians, with the trust intending to hold its LINK on Ethereum.

Fidelity also lays out how the price would be measured. Its proposed reference rate uses prices from eligible LINK spot markets, with a volume-weighted median methodology calculated every 15 seconds using rolling 60-minute market data. That is a mechanism for valuing the assets. It is not a guarantee that fund shares will trade at precisely the reported value when markets are thin or volatile.

Nor is this a yield product. The filing states plainly: “The Trust will not stake its LINK.” Shareholders would not receive staking rewards through the fund. They would be buying securities designed to reflect an underlying token’s price, not a wallet balance they can use in Chainlink services.

That distinction matters because the company’s use of Chainlink technology elsewhere is a separate story. NewsBTC has previously reported on Chainlink infrastructure used with Fidelity’s tokenized FILQ fund. An investment vehicle that owns LINK is not evidence of increased demand for Chainlink’s oracle services, and using the network’s technology does not automatically mean buying its token.

This is not the first attempt to make LINK available through conventional securities accounts. Grayscale’s Chainlink Trust ETF, trading as GLNK, had a share creation and redemption program in place from December 2025, according to its SEC disclosures. The Bitwise Chainlink ETF, CLNK, also appeared in a January 2026 Nasdaq information circular.

And fund shares are not the only conventional route. In November, NewsBTC covered Charles Schwab’s rollout of direct LINK trading to eligible crypto clients. These routes do different things, but they establish a useful baseline: access is no longer the same problem it was when crypto products were confined to specialist exchanges.

Fidelity’s possible advantage is distribution. Its name and brokerage relationships may reach customers who would not open a crypto wallet or buy LINK on a trading platform. That is a credible reason the filing matters. But branding, however recognizable, cannot tell investors how much money will arrive once trading begins.

There is also the price of access. The preliminary prospectus leaves the sponsor’s annual fee blank. Until that figure is settled, it is impossible to make an honest cost comparison with competing LINK products. For investors choosing between wrappers around the same token, fees, trading spreads and the gap between share prices and net asset value deserve more attention than a new ticker.

The mechanics are worth getting right. The proposed trust would issue and redeem large blocks of shares through authorized participants, using cash or LINK under procedures described in the filing. Everyday investors would generally trade the shares on an exchange instead of placing orders with the trust.

Those are two different markets. When one investor sells an existing fund share to another, the trade does not by itself require the trust to acquire more LINK. Sustained net creations can increase a fund’s underlying holdings. Redemptions can do the reverse. A rising share price, meanwhile, may reflect a higher LINK price rather than additional tokens entering custody.

That is why the announcement alone provides no evidence of fresh institutional inflows. The Form S-1 is a plan, not a subscription report. It does not tell us how much outside capital will be committed, what portion might come from rival products, or whether investor interest will survive a difficult crypto market.

There is a fair counterargument. Fidelity could appeal to a materially different pool of investors and grow total demand rather than simply redistribute it. The filing establishes a possible channel for that to happen. It does not establish the outcome.

First, the registration statement must become effective and the proposed Nasdaq listing must move forward. The final prospectus should also resolve the missing fee and other commercial details. Those are immediate milestones, not formalities to be assumed away.

If the product launches, the more revealing evidence will arrive afterward: the number of LINK held, changes in shares outstanding, sustained net creations or redemptions, trading liquidity and discounts or premiums to net asset value. Comparisons with existing LINK funds would help separate fresh demand from investors moving between products.

Until those figures exist, the sensible interpretation is narrow. Fidelity wants to sell investors exposure to Chainlink through a familiar brokerage format. Whether that changes the market for LINK is still an open question, and one that a filing cannot answer.

The production stepped up several gears as a marching band assembled and the song shapeshifted, interpolating The Supremes’ “Stop! In The Name of Love.”

Then, a change in mood as U2 paid tribute to Dolly Parton, the late queen of country’s smiling image splashed on three sails towering over the circular, center stage. Bono dueted with Dolly’s vocals for “Torn,” her very last recording which appears on the band’s 16th and latest studio album, Carnaval de Luz.

And, in a moment that earned a roar from the audience, the band slotted seamlessly into another Joshua Tree number, “Where The Streets Have No Name.” The sails came down, and U2 got down to rockstar business — with some support from scores of backing singers.

Without missing a beat, Bono and Co. reached into their glorybox with a rendition of “Pride (In The Name of Love)” a tribute to the assassinated civil rights leader Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., which appeared on their 1984 LP The Unforgettable Fire. And with that, U2 was done and it was game on.

Carnaval de Luz this month debuted at No. 1 on three Billboard charts, including No. 1 on Top Rock Albums, Vinyl Albums and Indie Store Album Sales, and became the band’s 14th top 10 on the Billboard 200 (opening at No. 4). It’s their 12th No. 1 on the Official U.K. Albums Chart and Australia’s ARIA Chart.

U2 this year chalks up its 50th anniversary, the celebrations for which will accelerate next year when the band returns to stadiums around the globe with Carnaval De Luz presents: The Greatest Show on Earth, its first stadium tour since 2019.
