Unfortunately, the theatrical numbers on her newest movie tell a very different story.

Mirren's new thriller A Talent for Murder has officially lost 842 theaters since its October 23 debut, shrinking from 876 locations to just 34 after earning approximately $1.13 million domestically. The film opened to $717,864 across 876 theaters before suffering a sharp decline in its second weekend, when its theater count fell to 332 and ticket sales dropped 79% to $152,296.

Things deteriorated further during its third weekend, with another 298 theaters dropping the movie and leaving it playing in only 34 locations. That represents a staggering 96% reduction from its opening footprint, despite the film receiving generally favorable reviews.

Directed by Anton Corbijn, A Talent for Murder follows celebrated novelist Patricia Highsmith, the creator of The Talented Mr. Ripley, during her retirement in the Swiss Alps. In the movie, Edward Ridgeway is sent by his employer, a publishing house, to see reclusive author Patricia Highsmith.

On the train there, he meets Clementine Balfour, who's on her way to visit her wealthy aunt. When Ridgeway meets Highsmith, sparks fly: She alternately toys with him and threatens to throw him out. When she teasingly begins outlining the new Ripley story, she asks him for a character name. He volunteers the name "Clementine Balfour," and then viewers start to see the new story unfold, as a new Ripley romances Clementine, with his eye on the family fortune. But soon fiction becomes dangerously entwined with life.

The film stars Mirren (The Queen) as Patricia Highsmith, Alden Ehrenreich (Oppenheimer) as Edward Ridgeway, Olivia Cooke (House of the Dragon) as Clementine Balfour, Juliet Stevenson (Bend It Like Beckham) as Peggy, and James Faulkner (Game of Thrones) as Douglas Balfour.

The Difficulty of Twin Estrangement

Posted November 13, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Twin estrangement is a really important issue that is often ignored because twin mythology suggests that all twins should get along, as their closeness creates a perfect interpersonal relationship. Raising twins is hard, and estrangement is very predictable when there is not enough focus on individual development. Believing that all twins should and will be able to get along causes shame and humiliation for twins and their parents when they don't. This is an unfortunate problem.

For most of my adult life, I have been writing about twins or gifted children. In general, my focus is on the social and emotional problems related to identity development that twins or other types of multiple births naturally experience because they share their caregivers and home environment. Twin development, in general, is quite different and more complicated than the developmental identity challenges of non-twins. The most common problems that twins have when they are young are questions and concerns that revolve around the sharing of their parents, day-to-day experiences, friendships, and education. Twins also have concerns about their twin's opinion, and their need for their twin to be present in stressful situations and understand immediately what is wrong.

Specific conflicts or pressures that lead to downright unhappiness, intense arguments, or confusion with life without their twin include the following:

The most common and unfortunate disagreements occur when twins continue to be estranged during the holiday season and cannot get the support they need from their twin. Twin estrangement is often seen when one twin is favored over the other by their parents or other caregiving adults.

Social development, including making relationships with non-twins, is also unique and very complicated as twins develop and mature. I can say with great certainty that fitting into the non-twin world is enormously difficult for most twins, who feel ashamed of themselves for fighting with their twin sister or brother and at the same time lonely for their twin sister or brother’s companionship and advice. Outsiders directly question why a twin cannot get along with their truest companion. These questions about closeness frustrate and annoy twins who are trying to get along and falling short of doing so.

Estrangement can grow out of frustration when twins disagree. When differences can be reconciled, the harmony between twins can be reestablished, and estrangement will be reduced. Twin estrangement is both very serious and hard to control. Do not minimize or ignore estrangement. Parenting for individuality helps twins get over estrangement more easily than twins who are encouraged to compete with each other and stay alike.

My interest in twins and their unique development is related to my own identity as a twin and why my twin and I could not resolve our love-hate relationship. As a USC graduate student in psychology and education, I learned a great deal about how twins were and are still used in psychological research to understand the relative importance of nature, nurture, and parenting in development. When genetic determinants are important, twins are often used to figure out what is inherited and what is a function of proper environmental care. There is no agreement about what is more important, genes or environmental care, for identity development, but there is a lot of research on this issue. In my opinion, all of these influences are extremely critical to twin identity development. In particular, parental attentiveness is extremely valuable.

Taking care of twins is extremely time-consuming and difficult physically, emotionally, and personally. Finding help and paying the bills for all of the extras is a challenge. Here are some of my suggestions for reducing your parenting stress levels.

1. Make sure that you get to know each of your twins' likes and dislikes. Know your twins as individuals with unique interests and feelings.

2. Teach twins how to get along with each other, even if this seems impossible when they are fighting intensely or arguing with you or each other.

3. Make sure that your twins know that some toys, food, attention, friends, and interests cannot be shared. This point of view will promote individuality and reduce anger, fighting, and estrangement.

4. Help your twins understand that friendships should not be shared.

5. Find twin families that want to be your friends and arrange family or twin play dates.

It is hard to be a twin, and it is hard to raise twins. Try to get the best help you can. Educate yourself on what twins need to thrive.

Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney has been in a bit of a one-sided beef with Valve co-founder and CEO Gabe Newell for a while now, lately about Steam's AI disclosure that requires developers to include a label informing Steam users of AI-generated content. Sweeney has had a number of complaints about Valve's storefront over the years, which makes sense as he owns one of its main competitors, the Epic Games Store, but he's been doubling and tripling down on the AI disclosure issue as of late. In a new interview, he not only sharply rebukes the AI label, but embraces AI in game development to degree rarely seen by him or other industry leaders.

Talking to GamesBeat, Sweeney gives one of the most bullish takes on AI generation in games I've seen in my many years covering this topic. Get on-board or die, is essentially his perspective.

"It's really clear to me that large-scale game developers–setting aside indies, where there's always going to be specialties and niches, large-scale game development will be totally changed by AI," Sweeney says. "Teams that don't adopt it will absolutely be left behind and rendered uneconomical, non-viable compared to their peers."

Sweeney says he's against any kind of legislation to curb the use of AI in game development in the US because it would give other countries the advantage. "Everyone is going to be adopting it," he says. "When one team can make a game four times more efficiently – 10 times more efficiently soon, perhaps – than another team can make the same game, there's just no possibility of competing the old way. No game developer has 75% to 95% profit margins that they can put at risk by not being as productive as possible."

There has been massive backlash to AI in games from players, voice actors, artists, and indie developers, but messaging from big studios and publishers generally ranges from cautiously optimistic to vocally and enthusiastically supportive. Meanwhile, a report from GDC found that 55% of respondents said AI was having a negative impact on the industry. Still, Sweeney wants to make sure companies aren't being bullied into choosing human workers over AI.

"Because there's a big resistance movement against AI that seems concentrated on trying to bully developers into not adopting it by attacking individuals, by review-bombing projects and so on, I've been trying to make my views known to as many game developers as possible," he says. "We have to power through this and build awesome games, use maximum acceleration, because it is a survival issue for all the companies in the industry."

Sweeney has called Valve's AI policy "abhorrent," politically motivated, and nonsensical among other things, and we can add another descriptor to the pile today: toxic. When GamesBeat pushes back and suggests Steam's AI label is there to inform players, Sweeney says he doesn't "buy it in the slightest," pointing to Steam's user review system as a means to let players know about "a game's quality, scope, entertainment value and so on." Ultimately, he says AI can be a force for good and bad in game development, and he sees Valve's mandatory disclosure as a judgment on the technology as a whole.

Bob Ferguson is proposing $10 million to fund basic infrastructure needed for the Northwest Advanced Clean Energy Park, a master-planned industrial area in Eastern Washington that will foster development of large-scale clean energy, advanced manufacturing and supply chains. The proposal will be included in his 2027–29 capital budget.

If approved by lawmakers, the funding would support sewer, water, electrical and road infrastructure at the park.

“This investment will support thousands of good-paying jobs in the trades and advanced industries, and unlock billions in private investment,” Ferguson said in a statement.

The 1,641-acre site was formerly part of the Hanford Nuclear Reservation, a sprawling federal property along the Columbia River near the Tri-Cities that is undergoing a massive environmental cleanup. Hanford was established during World War II to produce plutonium for the Manhattan Project.

According to the governor’s office, two projects have committed to locating at the park once infrastructure construction is complete: Atlas Agro’s low-carbon fertilizer production plant and the utility-scale Ruby Flats Solar Farm.

State officials said General Matter is also considering the location for producing advanced nuclear fuel for small reactors. That siting could potentially be beneficial for another project planned for the same area: Amazon, X-energy and Energy Northwest are developing plans for the Cascade Advanced Energy Facility, a nuclear plant comprised of clustered, small reactors.

In 2024, the Norwegian company Cenate expressed interest in building a factory at the park to produce lithium-ion battery components, according to the Tri-Cities Area Journal of Business, ultimately withdrew its plans.

The Tri-Cities is also attracting fusion-energy investment. Last year, Seattle startup Avalanche Energy landed a $10 million grant from the Washington State Department of Commerce’s Green Jobs Grant Program to develop FusionWERX, a commercial-scale testing facility for fusion technologies. The project is being developed in an existing Port of Benton building in Richland.

Washington is heading into its 2027–29 biennial budget with a multibillion-dollar shortfall, but officials have not yet settled on a definitive public estimate.

Ferguson has acknowledged the scale of the gap but has not publicly specified an amount. The shortfall concerns the state’s operating budget, which is separate from the capital budget funding proposed for the energy park.
