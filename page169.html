D&D’s World of Warcraft Designer Hid a Personal Easter Egg in Plain Sight

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The team also obsessively counted toes, banners, and mountain peaks

Mounts in World of Warcraft are status symbols, a way of showing off the challenging raids you’ve completed or how far in the PvP ranks you’ve climbed. Dungeons & Dragons: World of Warcraft lead designer James Wyatt couldn’t help showing off himself a bit in the book’s art, where he slipped in an image of his Night Elf warrior riding the Spirit of Eche'ro. Acquiring the spectral moose required spending hours digging up bone fragments with the Archaeology profession, an activity so tedious it was discontinued after the 2018 expansion Battle for Azeroth.

Fortunately, getting those rare cosmetic items is much easier in the Dungeons & Dragons expansion than in WoW. A character proficient in history can spend an hour or a short rest searching the site of Highmountain Tauren ruins for Archaeology fragments. If they gather six, they can be assembled into a staff that casts the Find Steed spell, summoning the famed moose once ridden by the Tauren hero Huln Highmountain. Wyatt also lets players acquire the Cenarion War Hippogryph, another mount he spent a lot of effort on, by raising their Renown with the Cenarion Circle through protecting the natural balance of Azeroth.

“Cosmetic rewards in WoW are about bragging rights, which is an individual achievement, and D&D is not a game about individual achievements. It's a game about stuff you do as a group together,” Wyatt told Polygon in a virtual interview. “Having mounts appear as Renown rewards and other special things was important for the feel of this cool element of WoW that I want to see in D&D, but the sort of rare achievement aspect of it falls into the category of mechanics that we didn't try to translate.”

Some of the book’s other Easter eggs are nods to WoW’s signature characters, with the Banshee Queen Sylvanas Windrunner appearing on a scale used by a Forsaken alchemist. Others are meant to establish the close ties between WoW and D&D. A gnome is used as the signature Warlock in both D&D: WoW and the Player’s Handbook, and the WoW book’s gnome art depicts them constructing a mechanostrider mount just like the Player’s Handbook version has them building a golem.

Wyatt and lead art director Sergio Jaramillo spent a lot of time playing WoW so they could provide artists with guidelines to ensure the book’s illustrations were true to the game.

“People have seen these places for thousands of hours on their computers,” Jaramillo said. “They know each peak that Icecrown Citadel is supposed to have and how many banners Ogrimmar has. We have to go through it, compare it, triple-check, and then maybe it’s ready. We sent it to Blizzard, and maybe Blizzard catches something we didn’t. A couple of times there were like, ‘We didn’t know this was there. Nice catch.’”

The team wanted to be precise with its modeling of characters, and even carefully counted the fingers and toes for Azeroth’s various species. They wanted to be sure to replicate unusual features, like trolls’ hunched posture, and the full range of eye and hair colors. They would build characters in the Wowhead Dressing Room, a digital tool that lets you recreate any character build and deck it out in equipment, and then commission artists based on the renders. There were times Blizzard couldn’t help them because the models simply didn’t exist in the game. Visit the Tauren city of Thunder Bluff in WoW and you’ll see plenty of Tauren adults and some babies, but no children. Jaramillo’s team had to come up with the proportions for the Tauren kids they wanted to show riding a kodo, the traditional Tauren mount, in the book’s character origins section.

WoW players can be very passionate about their preferences for playing Horde or Alliance, and Wyatt and Jaramillo wanted to make sure they didn’t appear to favor one faction over another. They made sure to have balanced representation across illustrations showcasing everything from the game’s professions to its boss fights. They even put the Collector’s Edition book in a slipcase so players could choose whether to have the Horde or Alliance logo displayed.

Getting the details of another game right was a challenge, but Wyatt said that in many ways, the project was easier than the work he did designing D&D books based on the Magic: The Gathering worlds of Ravnica, Theros, and Strixhaven.

“Worldbuilding on Magic is like building a movie set,” Wyatt said. “It feels like you’re setting up fronts that are held up by two-by-fours in the back, and if you start pushing too deep, you’re going to realize it’s all going to fall over. D&D worldbuilding has to account for characters who can do anything and go anywhere, and so does WoW. It is a very fleshed out world already. The lore is there.”

“The goal here is to recreate D&D in Azeroth, not to recreate the MMO experience.”

Borg Decks Dominated Our Star Trek Early Access MTG Drafts

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

We've just finished five drafts in the Magic: The Gathering Star Trek early access window on MTG Arena, and we have some initial thoughts on how the set drafts via the Premier Draft and Early Access Sealed game modes.

In short, it's a poor experience compared to Reality Fracture, but that also feels like a very high bar to measure against, and if we were instead to look only at other Universes Beyond drafts we've done this year, it's probably just behind The Hobbit and TNMT, while being better than Marvel Super Heroes.

Well, we can't say this was entirely unexpected, but drafting Borg Artifact decks in Star Trek will be quite the challenge in pick-one and pick-two situations. Throughout our early-access Star Trek drafts, it became clear everyone was fighting over the Borg and artifact cards, with our only real free picks coming in the first two or three slots.

This despite picking up Locutus, the One Who Speaks in pack one, pick one, the rest of our choices were too scattered and sporadic, leading to a mismatched deck that really struggled to stay on theme. Still, we drafted enough Borg and artifact creature cards to assemble a competent deck; we just ran into three straight Borg mirror matches after an early run of wins against red-green Landfall and green-white doctor decks, and that ended the first draft on a sour note.

We have ten draft archetypes in total, one for each traditional MTG guild or dual-color combo, with some looking far more exciting than others.

We felt infinitely more optimistic about this deck, given that it didn't feel like we were competing with anyone else in the draft class for the cards that worked best in this archetype, but after the first few picks, all dilemma cards appeared to dry up, or came in mana colors we simply didn't want to stretch to.

The downfall of our first deck came because we felt forced to go with three colors rather than two, and the same ultimately happened here, and we had to force a few red dilemma cards into the deck, then hope that we had done enough to still try and grab a few wins against the inevitable Borg wave coming my way.

Luckily, we had done enough to actually pick up a few wins with this deck, and while it's awfully reliant on you drawing your dilemma-reactive cards like The Caretaker, Melkotian Adjudicator, Obsidian Order Operative, and Elim Garak, Simple Tailor early, we packed in enough card draw to ensure that we always had a hand full of cards and were able to also play creatures or spells to stave off any early aggression.

That said, because of the nature of draft and having only a 40-card deck, by the time we had our big threats in hand, we often had little protection, so we had to hope our opponent had no answers to our creatures and spells.

Frustrated with how the Premier Drafts were playing out, we switched to sealed events, which, instead of drafting as a group, give you six Play Boosters to use to assemble your deck. We returned to the Borg archetype, having come up against it in more than half our previous matches across two drafts, and it became clear why it's proving so popular.

You can draft cards to build a really effective mana curve, so you can play from turn one and increase your power on the battlefield every turn. As much as we love the idea of strong archetypes standing out above the crowd, when one in particular is being so heavily played, it can suck the fun out of the draft modes in general as it all becomes too repetitive.

While not officially an archetype, the second round of sealed play gave us the cards we needed to build an aggressive red-white deck that used cheap but effective instant and sorcery spells and creatures that could snowball quickly. We still had some of the power 8+ cards, but not enough to say the deck was built around them.

The key, however, was finding a Flare of Fortitude in the packs, which let us protect all cards on the battlefield and our life total, and we didn't even have to pay the mana cost as long as we could sacrifice a white creature. This card was key to every victory, as it often caught opponents out when we were fully tapped and passing the turn.

Ultimately, the red-white build surprised us with how effective it can be, and while it's not as aggressive as some of the red-black Klingon decks we faced, it still managed to reach six wins under our control.

We returned to Premier Draft for the final run of the session, and it was a lot more fruitful than the previous two. Going into this draft knowing we wanted to avoid Borgs at all costs led to much more success in drafting an archetypewe were happy with.

Having played against a few Klingon decks before drafting one, it was a little easier to scope out the must-haves, and we also knew being ultra-aggressive was the best way forward. We wanted our opponents on the ropes or potentially beaten by turn four or five, and that's how most of the wins played out. The losses, as you might expect, came from games that went much longer; we simply ran out of steam, and our opponents eventually found answers to our bigger threats and controlled the game from then on.

Klingons were ultimately the archetype we had the most fun with, and that goes a long way toward making the draft experience feel much better overall.

We're basing this off the decks that we built and the decks that we played against. It's not a fully committed ranking, but just our snapshot impressions after 40 games played across these five drafts (and a few more unrecorded since).

Under the Surface

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Canali gives a behind-the-scenes look at sartorial excellence: the time, precision and mastery that transform raw materials into a jacket made to last

Under the surface, unseen layers lie. They stay there, silent. They don’t demand the spotlight. They don’t seek glory. And yet, like a beating heart, they form the innermost foundation of any real achievement. Where passion, enthusiasm, and discipline meet, the logic of excellence takes shape. Such is the story of Under the Surface, the newest creative collaboration from Canali.

Under the Surface is a look at the unseen architecture of a sartorial jacket by Canali. Every Canali jacket is born from a complex system of about 200 steps, each executed with meticulous care. Around 15 specialists are involved in the process—one that sees more than 520 feet of thread sewn into 22,000 stitches, both machine- and hand-made. Unseen when looking at the finished garment, the process informs each piece’s quality, comfort, shape, and durability.

It’s not just a matter of talent: rather, it’s about staying faithful to one’s goals, pursuing them with dedication even when no one is watching. It’s then that quality truly makes a difference, and the end result becomes the visible manifestation of a long creation process that demands great effort, experience and willpower.

That commitment of excellence unites the sartorial world of Canali and Ethan Hawke, the brand’s new Global Ambassador. The actor, writer, and director has dedicated almost four decades to perfecting his art, with endless, ever-learning curiosity. For their new Fall/Winter 2026 campaign, Canali has decided to capture what goes on behind the scenes. In the film, Hawke pores over his scripts, rehearses lines, refines each motion: a painstaking introspective process made of research, study, and exercise, preceding each of his performances.

The behind-the-scenes of a Canali jacket starts with selecting the finest fabrics, sourced from the best Italian wool mills. Then, each textile is examined and interpreted. While technology now allows extreme precision and control, some assessments are still left to the eye and expertise of human specialists. Even tiny details such as slightly different shades of color or variations in texture can influence the final product.

The fabric is then stabilized via steaming cycles and specific treatments, to ensure its behavior remains consistent over time. Then it’s time for digital mapping, guiding every cutting room decision: here, each of the jacket’s pieces is carefully positioned to also help keep waste to a minimum. It’s the first irreversible step.

The garment’s three-dimensional structure is then shaped upon the canvas. Canali crafts theirs in-house, adapting it to each model’s fit and fabrics. Made from horsehair, plant fibers, and natural reinforcements, the canvas is modeled on custom-made concave shapes, known as Schiena d’Asino (Donkey’s Back), which replicate the chest’s anatomy. The canvas is crucial for determining how a jacket drapes over the body, avoiding stiffness. It’s here that the volume of the chest, the balance of the silhouette and ease of wearing begin to be defined.

Then it’s time to craft the front, lapel, padded shoulder, and the sleeves. From planning to execution, nothing’s left to chance. The tailor observes, corrects, iterates. It’s a patient process, requiring time, knowledge, and skill. Lastly, the various layers are united through basting, stitching, and ironing processes. As these details interact, the garment takes on its structure and sophisticated character.

Something special also takes place behind the scenes with Hawke: day after day, the actor’s preparation subtly transforms his thoughts and behavior until, almost by instinct, he takes on each role’s intimate characteristics.

Time underpins the whole process. After being assembled and pressed, the jacket is allowed to rest, to dissipate the steam and stabilize the cloth. This is how the fabric finds its balance, and the garment gains a sort of memory: the ability to naturally find its shape again, even after having been worn, folded, or packed into a suitcase.

In over 90 years of heritage, Canali has refined their techniques through repetition, experience, and experimentation. Each garment is built upon the time-tested values of care, know-how, precision. The Canali style is not one made of grand gestures, but daily practice, intimate and subtle: the constant research into a shape that lasts. One hero of the Under the Surface project is the vicuña jacket, available exclusively at selected flagship boutiques by Canali.The campaign, and the collaboration between Canali and Ethan Hawke, speak to the art of shaping oneself, guided by an ambition to do things well with patience and self-awareness. Both in tailoring and in cinema, there is so much quiet work that happens behind the scenes to make creativity possible. Discipline and rigour do not constrain creative freedom; they make it possible. For Canali, excellence takes shape through the craftsmanship with which each element is made. For Ethan Hawke, it is the preparation that allows him to approach each role with confidence.And, as the Under The Surface campaign reveals, each of these steps adds something essential to the finished product. In the case of the Canali cashmere and vicuña blazer (the hero style of the FW26 campaign), it is structure, movement, character, durability, and a lightness that can only be achieved through the combination of expertly-chosen materials and complex fine tailoring. The knowledge required to execute this is almost tangible, felt in the quality of the piece.

The finished result only has value because of the strong, unseen structure underneath. It is a story we can all resonate with. Little by little, layer by layer, day by day, we each build ourselves in pursuit of excellence. Our actions create the people we are – even, or especially, when nobody is watching – in the same way that the apparent simplicity of a piece of clothing hides the depth of work required to craft it. This is the essence of Under the Surface.

After about $2 billion in buybacks, STRC reached the price at which Strategy could sell new shares to help finance Bitcoin purchases.

Strategy's STRC preferred stock briefly returned to $100 on Nov. 4, potentially restoring a funding channel for Bitcoin purchases after months of cash-consuming share buybacks.

The company announced on Nov. 4 that its STRC security had reached $100, prompting executive chairman Michael Saylor to declare:

The recovery followed about $2 billion in share repurchases since July, as Strategy sought to support the preferred stock after its prolonged decline below its $100 stated value.

Independent tracker STRC.live reported that the security touched $100 before retreating to close at $99.97. It also estimated that renewed at-the-market (ATM) activity may have financed the purchase of approximately 191 BTC on Wednesday.

Strategy has not confirmed those purchases.

Strategy's Bitcoin treasury stood at 849,327 BTC as of Nov. 1.

The $100 threshold matters because Strategy’s current policy is not to issue STRC below that level. Its Nov. 2 disclosure listed about $18 billion of STRC available for issuance and sale as of Nov. 1. New sales could help finance Bitcoin purchases without issuing additional common stock.

However, months of below-par trading constrained that approach, forcing Strategy to shift from raising capital through STRC to repurchasing the security at a discount.

STRC's rebound followed a significant change to its dividend structure, with Strategy introducing daily distributions from Nov. 1 to improve the security's price stability and appeal to income-seeking investors.

‘Wild Horse Nine’ Review: Martin McDonagh’s Prickly but Unconvincing CIA Satire Recasts ‘In Bruges’ as the Stuff of a Classic Western

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

[Editor’s note: This review was originally published on September 3, 2026 during the 2026 Venice Film Festival. Searchlight Pictures will release it in theaters on Friday, November 6.]

Another melancholically splenetic Martin McDonagh jag about a banter-mad pair of murderers who go on a boys trip to purgatory (where they search their souls as they wait for death to find them), the prickly but unconvincing “Wild Horse Nine” strains to translate the post-Guy Ritchie bark of “In Bruges” into the language of a classic American Western. In this case, an American Western set on Easter Island and backdropped by Augusto Pinochet’s rise to power in the fall of 1973.  Related Stories ‘North of North’ Season 2 Review: Netflix’s Defiantly Nice Arctic Sitcom Is for the Warmhearted F**k-Ups ‘Hen’ Review: A Cockeyed Comedy Sees Europe’s Migrant Crisis Through the Eyes of a Chicken

McDonagh’s decision to recast his same old script against the backdrop of such a real and terrible event grants this new telling a welcome sense of scale, both in terms of its characters’ morality, as well as in regards to their greater place in history. But that decision is a double-edged sword that cuts the film off at the knees. Where “In Bruges” kicked off with the killing of a fictional altar boy, “Wild Horse Nine” is overshadowed by the deaths of thousands upon thousands of very real people, and that reality bleeds into the edges of the frame even when the seriocomic absurdity of McDonagh’s writing demands a world of pure invention.

Does the overlap make “Wild Horse Nine” feel glib? Yes, and thank god for that — if not for glibness the movie would lose the majority of its few solid laughs. The problem is that it also makes “Wild Horse Nine” feel as transparently manufactured as the CIA-backed overthrow of a foreign democracy. I can accept that an inexperienced hitman might have a dark night of the soul after shooting an innocent child during confession. By contrast, I struggle to believe that a veteran CIA agent who openly brags about his involvement in the Kennedy assassination would have a come-to-Jesus moment after orchestrating the demise of Salvador Allende. McDonagh has some good fun wrestling with whether or not we should care about such a mottled change of heart, but the point is gradually rendered moot in a movie whose emotions are less credible than its conspiracies.

The original Company man, Chris (John Malkovich, more locked in than he’s been on screen since “Burn After Reading”) is a natural hinge for the off-kilter comedy that McDonagh builds around him, in that he can be a joy to watch even when you don’t buy a word he’s saying. More than just an ordinary CIA agent, he’s the living personification of America’s efforts to destabilize foreign governments across the 20th century. He was close with Patrice Lumumba until the day the Congolese leader was executed by a firing squad. He was in Cuba to spur on the exiles who failed to overthrow Fidel Castro in 1961. He was even in Dallas, USA on a Friday afternoon in November 1963, doing things his ornery boss MJ (Steve Buscemi) would much prefer he didn’t tell you about.

He traveled to all of those places, and got all of those people killed, with the blithe imperiousness of a flag waving in the wind. Chris says that he’s killed 68 people over the course of his career, and he offers that information as easily as a rumor — in a way that makes it sound like the number could be half that, or double, and it wouldn’t make a difference.

Recently, fate has blown Chris and his office husband/platonic life partner/exasperated straightman Lee (McDonagh vet Sam Rockwell) down south to Chile, where the two of them have been hard at work orchestrating the September 11th coup against the democratically elected socialist president, knowing full well that it would plunge the country into decades of brutal dictatorship. Chris appears to delight in sowing chaos, and the fact that he gets to wear a cute little Panama hat while he does it only boosts his joie de vivre.

But Chris is fraying at the seams in ways that we can’t see at first — ways that will only begin to reveal themselves when he and Lee decide to celebrate their imminent success with a sightseeing trip to Easter Island, a short 2,300 miles off the Western coast of Santiago. There are still a couple of months to go before the coup, but the wheels are in motion, and there’s little for these men to do for now besides fly to Rapa Nui, look at the “big heads,” flirt with a couple of beautiful young leftists (the prissier but gentler of whom is played by “Ema” mega-talent Mariana di Girolamo), and mordantly reflect upon their role in the awfulness Pinochet will soon inflict upon his own people. That task is complicated and confused by the fact that Chris seems to be losing his memory, which casts Lee in the role of his peevish but loving caretaker.

“Wild Horse Nine” is only the second feature that’s ever been shot on the ultra-remote soil of Rapa Nui (Kevin Reynolds’ 1994 epic “Rapa Nui” is credited as the first), and McDonagh rightly exalts in the fact that he’s brought Hollywood to the end of the world. The first half of his movie largely adheres to the fiction that Chris and Lee are just on Easter Island to fuck around, which offers a semi-relaxed opportunity for us to take in the sights. Ben Davis’ sun-bleached cinematography renders the hills and beaches of Rapa Nui with the veneer of a lost idyll, the ancient moai statues assuming an especially sacred charge when contrasted against such profane characters.

Chris bemusedly laughs the statues off with ethnographic glee, mocking one of the shorter figures as a dwarf (in case the “In Bruges” déjà vu wasn’t already strong enough), but it’s clear that he’s shaken by the fact that some of them almost 750 years old. That they’ve stood there in silent tribute to Polynesia’s ancestors as empires rose and fell on faraway shores, each of them a graveyard of forgotten people just like him. By the same token, it’s similarly clear that he’s trying to get at something in his own mind as he and Lee listen to Judy Collins in their rental car, argue over whether or not any Nazis made it into heaven (among other even more aggressively screen-written subjects), and whisper sweet nothings to the wild horses that run about the island, but the specific nature of that something is hard for him to pin down.

Malkovich has the time of his life with that search for Chris’ last speck of humanity, which shines up from the bottom of his soul as his mind continues to erode. At once both insidiously relaxed and also choked with Catholic guilt, Malkovich’s performance marries the nonchalance of a cowboy to the paranoia of a bounty target. One minute he’s waxing poetic about dinosaurs, and the next he’s rigging his luggage with explosives (maybe) and checking the “Memento”-like reminder notes he’s slipped into his jacket pocket.

“Wild Horse Nine” loves Chris too much to pretend like we’re not supposed to, but Malkovich never asks for our sympathy because he knows that he’ll get it anyway, and not just because the purpose of Rockwell’s character is to ensure that we do. It’s Chris’ job to make rooting for the bad guys feel like the right choice. It’s the cornerstone of America’s post-war foreign policy.

So while Chris might be cut from the same cloth as John Wayne (to the extent that Monument Valley is revealed to be a place of great importance to him), his arc is aimed in the opposite direction of the one that Ethan Edwards traces across “The Searchers”; Chris is a major asshole this movie gradually puckers into a wayward hero, and while it’s all too easy to root for him to complete that transformation before time runs out, at no point does “Wild Horse Nine” sell us on the premise that it would mean anything in the end — even to him.

The movie, blessedly, isn’t Catholic enough to offer Chris a big moment of divine expiation; in lieu of building to a grand confession, he and Lee simply run afoul of their CIA higher-ups until their PTO turns into a matter of American national security. Guns are fired. Hostages are taken. The word “fuck” is used like a punctuation mark, and regrets are muttered into the wind long after there’s any way for them to matter.

It all gets a bit too busy and madcap as “Wild Horse Nine” — freighted with sadness during even its cheekiest bits, as is McDonagh’s way — scrambles to make up for the general listlessness of its first half. Never as funny as it is sardonic, and always reaching so hard to be more of both, the film’s most successful moments are mined from the ambient tension between memory and erasure.

Chris, we begin to suspect, would do anything to forget what he’s done, and his own mind is graciously willing to get him halfway there. The rest of the CIA will do anything it takes to make sure that Chris doesn’t share whatever it is that he still remembers. Surrounding them on all sides are statues that predated America by several hundred years, and will likely continue to watch over Rapa Nui long after our empire has been wiped away like a tear in the rain. In “Wild Horse Nine,” American interventionism is less effective as the set-up for some jokes than it is as their punchline.

The seven new editions were “inspired” by the discovery of a “physical media vault” with “thousands” of Betacam, VHS, and D1 tapes and “other media assets,” EA says. As part of the digitization process, EA was able to recover master FMV sequences and reedited them to restore more than 1,500 scenes from the games. The updated Wing Commander games also officially incorporate some community-made mods.

You’ll be able to get the Road Rash and Wing Commander Anniversary editions for $9.99, and the other classic games, including Ultima and Crusader titles, will be available for $4.99. You can grab them on Steam, GOG, and the EA App.

Jared Verse was upset that Browns teammates didn't react when he took a cheap shot

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Browns defensive end Jared Verse was picked up and thrown to the ground by Steelers guard Mason McCormick on Sunday, and McCormick wasn’t the only person Verse was upset with afterward.

Verse told reporters in Cleveland today that he was upset both by McCormick’s cheap shot and by his teammates’ reaction, or lack thereof. Verse thought the rest of the Browns’ players should have had his back, and didn’t.

“To be quite honest with you, that was the play that made me really angry,” Verse said. “I was already upset about the game, the way things were moving, but at that point it became deep. You’re doing dirty shit, you’re trying to sweep my legs out. That honestly could have put me in a position to not be able to finish the season, put me in a position to change my life in a very bad aspect, so that upset me. The lack of reaction by those around me had upset me a lot. They were focused on the game and everything like that, but a similar play happened to Derek Barnett on the next field goal, and I spazzed, I lost it. Because those are my brothers, those are my teammates, and when you do something like that to them, it upsets me deeply. We have to get better, we have to be aware. These are my teammates, these are my brothers, and we’re not fighting for each other.”

Verse said he addressed his feelings with his teammates. Verse did acknowledge that he brought some of it on himself by the trash talk he was directing at the Steelers, but he still felt that his teammates should have protected him when it escalated from verbal to physical.

“That’s a conversation that we’ve had, we’ve spoken about,” Verse said. “I made my concerns known, I’ve made my issues known about the situation, but also, I didn’t put them in a position to let them know that, I didn’t let them know beforehand I was going to talk to the Steelers and Steeler Nation. I’ve got to be better.”

Verse, the 2024 NFL Defensive Rookie of the Year who arrived in Cleveland in the trade that sent Myles Garrett to the Rams, has been a disappointment this season. His teammates may be wishing he’d focus a little less on talking trash and getting into altercations on the field, and a little more on sacking the opposing quarterback — something he hasn’t done yet this season.
