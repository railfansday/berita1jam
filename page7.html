Fed’s preferred inflation gauge accelerated to 3.8% in October, likely sealing rate hike

📅 2026-11-25  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Inflation moved further away from the Federal Reserve's target in October, reinforcing expectations that another interest rate hike is in cards for December and raising questions about how much more work the central bank will have to do in 2027.

The personal consumption expenditures price index, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, showed a seasonally adjusted 3.8% annual inflation rate, in line with the Dow Jones consensus estimate and nearly twice the central bank's goal.

Excluding food and energy, core PCE was 3.3%, the Commerce Department reported Wednesday. That was 0.1 percentage point above the forecast.

While the Fed officially follows the headline figure, policymakers consider core to be a more reliable longer-term gauge of inflation trends.

A separate report also showed much stronger economic growth for the third quarter, with gross domestic product rising at a 4.3% annualized rate, according to a revised Commerce Department estimate. The strong top-line gain for the economy adds further fuel to the notion that Fed monetary policy is not restricting economic activity.

Consumer spending rose 0.3% for the month, which matched the pace of price increases but was 0.1 percentage point lower than forecast. Income, on the other hand, surged 0.7%, much more than the 0.4% estimate. The personal saving rate rose to 4.1%.

The report comes with the market fully pricing in a quarter percentage point interest rate hike when the Fed meets Dec. 8-9. However, it leaves unanswered how much more rates will need to increase to get inflation back down to the Fed's 2% target. Inflation has been running above target for some 5½ years.

The large upward increase in GDP came from revisions to investment as well as consumer and government spending.

Chris Evans And The Rock’s Overlooked Christmas Movie With An 89% Audience Score Is Streaming On Prime Video

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Chris Evans and Dwayne “The Rock” Johnson are two of the most famous actors working today. As such, both have been in seemingly countless huge projects, from the Avengers movies for Evans to the Fast and Furious franchise for The Rock. Because of this, both actors have appeared in movies that, comparatively speaking, have fallen through the cracks.

That’s certainly the case for Red One, the Jake Kasdan-directed action Christmas film from Amazon, which is currently available for streaming on Prime Video. Red One, which was written by Chris Morgan (who penned Fast and Furious: Tokyo Drift), tells the story of the North Pole’s head of security as he teams up with a well-respected hacker in order to find Santa Claus before Christmas Eve, as he’s been kidnapped.

Red One wasn’t a huge hit with critics, earning a 29% score on Rotten Tomatoes. The film also failed to earn back its production budget, taking in just over $180 million off a $250 million cost. Despite that, Red One proved to be much more popular with fans, as the film has an 89% audience score on Rotten Tomatoes and consistently sits near the top of the charts on Prime Video each holiday season.

As the holidays are quickly approaching, many are looking to streaming services to watch a cozy movie this season. There are, of course, the certified Christmas classics, such as Home Alone, A Christmas Story, and How the Grinch Stole Christmas, but movies like Red One are also deserving of a watch around this time of year, especially if you’re looking for something new and fresh this time around.

As stated, Red One follows the North Pole head of security, Callum Drift, who’s played by The Rock. Drift is a loyal servant to Santa Claus, who’s portrayed by JK Simmons, and the two have worked together for hundreds of years. Red One tells the story of how that centuries-long relationship nearly falls apart due to an evil scheme to capture Father Christmas just before his busiest day of the year.

This naturally sends the entirety of the North Pole into a panic. With Santa Claus gone, it becomes a real possibility that there will be no Christmas at all, leaving millions of children all around the world without gifts under their tree. Drift, however, is determined not to let that happen, and is willing to do whatever it takes to bring Santa Claus back as quickly as possible.

Through the help of Mythological Oversight and Restoration Authority (MORA), an organization that helps to keep the peace between human and mythological societies, Drift is able to determine that the kidnappers discovered Santa’s location through the work of an infamous hacker named Jack O’Malley, played by Chris Evans.

Despite his involvement, O’Malley had no idea what he was hired to do, and instead just completed the task as he would with any other job. Drift then recruits O’Malley, who has long since been a skeptic of the existence of Santa Claus, to help bring back Simmons’ character, with time quickly running down.

Many aspects of Red One were criticized by critics, but The Rock and Chris Evans both give strong performances in their respective roles. As such, there are still reasons to watch or rewatch it now on Prime Video.

One of the most criticized aspects of Red One was the clear franchise set up that the film integrated throughout. An entire world was being established, showing not just the North Pole, but the South Pole (with Krampus himself) and other mythological locations and figures as well. This bothered some, who said that it made Red One feel too much like a clear attempt to build a Christmas-themed cinematic universe rather than simply making a good movie.

However, on the other hand, a lot of Red One’s world building actually does work. The presence of Krampus, for example, helps to make the world feel truly lived in, meaning that it brought more to the movie than just an attempt at future franchise success. And, of course, Red One did have plenty of franchise potential, but it doesn’t look as if that will ever come to fruition. Despite that, audiences can still enjoy Red One on Prime Video this holiday season.

Want to treat yourself to five-star brunch without making a ton of dishes—or taking off your pj’s? Hack the classic croque madame by making it in the air fryer. Here, crispy toast is topped with an egg and smothered with ham, cheese, and béchamel for a simpler version of the classic French dish that will transform the comfort of your sofa into a fancy brunch spot in five seconds flat.

How is this a croque madame?A classic croque madame is a toasted ham and cheese sandwich that’s topped with a layer of béchamel, broiled until bubbly, and then topped with a fried egg. This air fryer version takes all those components—toasty bread, fried egg, béchamel, ham, and cheese—and reconfigures them into a form that cooks much easier in the air fryer. You might say it almost looks like a classic egg-in-the-hole, but don’t worry! If you close your eyes, you’ll still taste croque madame.

The microwave béchamel:There’s nothing wrong with making béchamel in a pot on the stove, but making it in the microwave means fewer heavy dishes to clean—and it’s also a great hack to know if you’re somewhere with limited (or no!) stove space. Rather than standing over a pot, whisking constantly, you’ll simply whisk the flour into the melted butter, microwave until it’s bubbling, then whisk in the milk and microwave again. Once it’s thickened, you can fold in the cheese. Though I don’t recommend microwaving after the cheese goes in—that’s a sure way to break your sauce—if it absolutely needs it, you can microwave it for very short bursts just till it melts together.

Make-ahead & storage:Once cooked, the air fryer croque madame should be eaten immediately. That said, the béchamel can be made 2 days ahead and stored in an airtight container in the refrigerator. I recommend gently reheating it before using. I typically do this in the microwave in 15-second increments, stirring in between—this prevents the sauce from breaking.

The Government Tested How Dangerous AI Could Get—Then Four ChatBots Helped Plan a Terrorist Attack

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Government Accountability Office secretly tested ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Grok and found that all four would help make plans when prompted.

The dawn of artificial intelligence brings with it both rewards and risks. With the ability to sift through unfathomably large data sets, AI could help detect cancers in their infancy, unlock some of the most enduring mysteries of mathematics, and even help contain the beating nuclear heart of future fusion reactors.

Unfortunately, the risks of this technology are far from minuscule, including its impact on human education, the environment, and potentially the survival of the human race (an all-too-plausible scenario put forward by AI tech CEOs). Even the everyday LLMs that now litter our digital lives—Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT, and Grok—can aid and abet some of the most dangerous actors on the planet.

At least, that’s the latest conclusion by the Government Accountability Office (GAO), a nonpartisan watchdog housed in the legislative branch of the U.S. government. Typically, GAO turns its investigative gaze on efforts to limit fraud and wasteful spending in the government itself, but in a new tactic at the urging of U.S. Senator Maggie Hassan (D-NH), the office instead performed real-world tests on the platform to see if these four popular AI models would aid in planning a terrorist attack.

“A terrorist can log onto an AI platform and in less than 12 hours walk away with a concrete playbook for how to carry out a deadly attack,” Hassan, incoming Chair of the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee, said in a press statement. “Today’s new report makes it clear that the AI public safety threat is no longer theoretical.”

GAO conducted this test in the spring this year, and earlier this week reported some disturbing results. Two of the platforms, according to an official GAO release, provided guidance on building and detonating an improvised explosion device, or IED, and two other AI models showing differing methods for targeting the electrical grid. Within 12 hours, all four models also gamed through potential locations and methods for carrying out such an attack.

Although these violent lines of questioning triggered each platform’s safeguards, GAO got around them using common safeguard-bypassing techniques. Additionally, they confirmed the validity of the information provided with experts. The results of this investigation were published in a 61-page report that will be central to future senate hearings on AI safety.

“We set out to do two different types of evil,” GAO director Kevin Walsh said in an interview posted on YouTube. “The first type is we wanted to create a bomb. And we didn’t just want to know how to create a bomb, we wanted it to help pick out a target, a location, and a time, and to give us instructions all the way…Evil scenario two, we wanted to do a bunch of economic damage, and in both cases that ended up being targeting the electrical grid.”

In one case, Walsh reports that one AI model would refuse to answer questions or divert the user to more safe behaviors but said that “it wasn’t always successful.” Walsh says that companies theoretically call law enforcement when suspicious activity is detected, but whether that occurs and at what frequency is uncertain.

“As AI capabilities evolve, AI providers will continue to refine their safeguards,” the report concludes. “However, in its current state, this technology can potentially be dangerous and its misuse represents a growing risk to national and economic security.”

The GAO officially released these terrorist-abetting findings on November 18, 2026, the same day Anthropic, creators of Claude, went public on the stock exchange. Despite the company’s own admission of AI being an existential threat to humanity, its IPO at one point reached a meteoric a $2 trillion valuation before dipping back down to a (measly) $1.6 trillion.

10 Games That Will Feel Better To Play After GTA 6

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ronald is a video game journalist with more than 10 years of experience in the industry, working on several of the most important Spanish websites.

If you're like 32.5 million other people around the world, you've probably been dedicating a huge amount of time to GTA 6 lately, and you can't imagine what your life will be like when the experience is over.

All good things must come to an end, and eventually, your time in Leonida will reach a conclusion. However, crossing that frontier doesn't necessarily have to be a bittersweet farewell but rather an opportunity to re-evaluate everything that comes next.

Titles like this one help you appreciate the craft of video game development more than ever, as they fully exploit many of the aspects that make the interactive industry the most special of all those focused on entertainment.

Therefore, if these hypermodern times have already gotten to you and are making you nervous about what will come after Jason and Lucia's story, calm your anxiety by checking out this list of ten games that will feel better to play after GTA 6.

If you're wondering how on earth this list is going to start with one of the games most diametrically opposed to the philosophy of GTA 6, like GRIS, the answer lies precisely in that statement.

After an adventure brimming with stimuli, endless possibilities, lengthy explanations, and violence that ranges from the personal to the systemic, what better way to unwind than with one of the most focused and introspective titles ever conceived?

In just three hours, without a single word and in a straightforward manner, GRIS takes you on a soothing path of reflection, making peace with the circumstances, and reveling in an audiovisual experience that engulfs you to the point of baptizing you with its art.

Without bullets or socioeconomic problems that dictate your relationship with the law, but with platforms, elegance, and a creative intent so distinct that you won't be able to help but fall in love by the time the credits roll on the Sunday afternoon it takes to complete it.

The cliché of "best game ever" versus "best game today" strikes me as both amusing and illogical, but I do believe there's some value in considering The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time Remake in light of GTA 6.

Both titles excel in their focus on player freedom, yet while GTA achieves this within a stunning range of action that opens up numerous possibilities, Ocarina of Time Remake does so in a much more concentrated and whimsical way.

The charm lies not in the immeasurable variety of options, but in the immeasurable creativity of the single alternative they offer for each sequence, demonstrating in the process how the notion of agency has evolved over the last 30 years.

They are two ends of the same spectrum, and experiencing the same sense of enjoyment through discovery in such different ways is what makes them complement each other far better than you might initially imagine.

Having spent its entire existence in the shadow of Rockstar's work, returning to Saints Row 2 now that GTA has taken a 180-degree turn in its approach has become more fitting than ever.

Jason and Lucia face a more realistic, weighty world with consequences, masterfully encouraging restraint and attention to detail, breaking the connection that pitted the two franchises against each other for so long.

Hence, whether you're among those who didn't fully embrace the change in philosophy or those who did, Saints Row 2's humor and arcade style are a perfect fit to reduce the pleasurable cortisol rush produced by GTA 6.

Don't get me wrong: I greatly appreciate Rockstar's courage in going against its own trend, though that doesn't mean I'm not much more eager to play a more relaxed game once I decide to stop wandering around Leonida, postponing saying goodbye.

Grand Theft Auto 6 puts a large emphasis on keeping your relationship sound, so you need to know what ways are the best to raise the romance between Jason and Lucia.

After GTA 6, it's quite clear the developer has mastered every imaginable variation of the driving genre, which includes the adrenaline-fueled and spectacular style they employed during the Midnight Club 3: DUB Edition era.

The fact that the vehicles in their latest product had to prioritize accuracy and composure was necessary to fit the rest of the game's structure, yet the primal thrill of getting behind the wheel and flying at 300 kilometers per hour in the most improbable way possible was sacrificed.

Consequently, even considering that the genre as a whole is today much closer to realistic simulation than arcade driving, dusting off your copy of Midnight Club 3: DUB Edition after GTA 6 would be in the instruction manual if GTA 6 had one.

Neither approach is inherently better than the other, evidently, but the desire to truly revel in the speed instead of worrying about the cost of a crash will grow stronger on you with each passing second.

Years go by, and it's incredible how no third-person shooter has understood that they are morally obligated to look to Vanquish to understand how to keep the genre from perpetually stagnating.

Gunfights in GTA 6 are technically impressive and an absolute marvel in terms of action sequences, and yet there isn't a second that goes by that I don't think of Sam Gideon and the thunderous perfection of his combat performance.

Instead of being a turn-based confrontation where both sides take turns behind a wall to exchange bullets, Vanquish is a spiral of destruction that doesn't even stop to reload, presenting a striking contrast to the game we're discussing here.

Would being able to slide at full speed and defeat giant robots in GTA 6 make sense? No. Am I dying to play through Vanquish for the thirtieth time for every occasion I have to stay put in cover? Absolutely.

While this might sound utterly personal, few things please me as much as revisiting a developer's older games after a long time, which brings us to Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas.

Being the franchise's installment I've sunk the most hours into and the reason I love open-world games, it creates an intrinsic incentive for GTA 6 because it allows you to appreciate the passage of time.

Companies selling robot-training video used to call about privacy once a month. Now it’s several times a week

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Simon Randall is the founder and CEO of Pimloc, which makes video-anonymization software.

Earlier this month, India’s technology ministry probed home-services platforms about cleaners wearing head-mounted cameras inside customers’ homes. The footage trains robots, and officials wanted to know how it was collected, what consent was obtained and whether it was going to AI developers overseas. They won’t be the last to ask.

Training robots this way is new (ish), but the questions aren’t. Over a decade ago, I helped build Autographer, one of the first wearable cameras. Back then, the focus was: “how do I know if I am being filmed?” so we designed privacy into the product. Today, because we’re filmed all the time, people (and their governments) ask: “Where is that footage, who has access and what are you doing with it?”

Today I run Pimloc, which sells software to anonymize sensitive video. Much of our work involves police body-camera footage: shaky, often low-light and full of people who aren’t part of the incident. It’s remarkably similar to what robotics firms now collect. We’ve spent years developing technology to protect people in footage where they move quickly, turn away or appear at unusual angles.

A year ago we were approached by companies collecting or preparing footage for physical AI training roughly once a month. Now it’s several times a week – businesses working across autonomous vehicles, delivery robots, factories and drones.

AI chatbots learned to communicate by reading the internet. A robot has as much chance of learning to fold clothes by trawling search engines as I have of becoming a Michelin-starred chef by reading Julia Child’s Mastering the Art of French Cooking.

So developers are recording real life instead. One startup recently told Fortune it had collected more than a million hours of first-person footage in its first month, and we’re hearing similar figures. Robotics and physical-AI companies had raised nearly $48 billion by early October, according to PitchBook data.

Many of the companies that first approached us were using open-source tools to try and redact footage – far from perfect, but it got them through non-public development and testing.

Now things are different in two ways. First, many of those companies are preparing to sell their footage to large AI developers with exacting standards. A buyer spending serious money on a dataset wants assurances about how it was collected, whether permissions cover its intended use and whether the people in it are protected. Suppliers know they will have to stand behind those claims.

Second, governments are paying attention. India’s inquiry is one example in one territory, of growing scrutiny over how AI training footage is collected and used. Big tech companies and labs looking to develop physical AI products and services have little choice but to push those same questions down to the organizations selling them the footage they need.

One company that approached us recently put it simply: “We need to solve this problem. Redaction is important to us.”

Some companies were created specifically to collect training footage. They can build consent and anonymization into their processes from day one. As India shows, that doesn’t settle every concern for workers or the people they film, but it gives them a headstart

Others have recorded video for years for a different purpose, and are only now discovering its new value. But having captured the footage isn’t the same as having the right to sell it.

Recently, an established business that helps people with everyday tasks asked us about anonymizing years of footage captured by their customers for AI training. We had two immediate questions: could permissions realistically be established, years later, for a use nobody – not least the consumer – had envisaged when the footage was recorded? And could every identifying detail – from people in shot to letters on the fridge – be reliably found and protected?

That discussion is still on-going, but it shows how difficult it can be to turn an archive into an asset after the fact.

For any business sitting on a digital archive, my advice is to start by asking: did the people you meant to film agree to the footage being sold? And is everyone else in it protected? You can’t assume the value without identifying those gaps and addressing them.

Buyers are best placed to make those checks standard. Privacy rules and expectations differ from country to country, as India’s scrutiny of overseas transfers shows, and footage is already crossing borders. If the AI companies paying for it insisted on the same proof of permission and protection wherever it was recorded, every supplier would have to meet that bar.

I’m optimistic about what physical AI could do. But the footage that is increasingly being used to teach it captures far more than the task – people nearby, inside homes, documents, voices and screens in the background. Autographer taught me that what a camera picks up by accident matters as much as what it’s pointed at. Physical AI is learning that lesson at a far greater scale, and buyers have both the power and the incentive to act on it.
