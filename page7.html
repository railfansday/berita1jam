Day 34: The Decision to Walk as a Family

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The premature grief of leaving the experience of the Camino lingered from the night before. Those emotions also surfaced in my dreams. So in the way I processed best, I began walking in the dark.

There were so many emotions for so many aspects of my life.

And even though the emotions were hard, I was thankful to be feeling them. Typically, tears didn’t come easy to me. So I was grateful that I could express anything at all.

I stopped at the crest of a hill because of the giant dog sitting outside the bar. The affectionate beast was the only reason I decided to stop and eat breakfast.

Another tortilla. Another cafe con leche.

Just as I hoped, my brothers arrived soon after and their presence helped shed some of the weight.

A pilgrim we knew came up and told us that someone stole his wallet while he was sleeping in the albergue last night. As a necessary precaution, I asked my one brother, who always forgot his things, if he had his. He did not.

Turns out, he left it at the restaurant where he ate breakfast, 8 kilometers away. The three of us called many places to sort out the problem and eventually coordinated a taxi to take him back to the location to look.

The American and the Argentinian tried to be the calm in the Italian storm, reassuring him that the Camino provides and that his wallet would be there. He didn’t believe us.

Then, fifteen minutes after he left in the taxi, he sent us a picture of his wallet.

He eventually caught up to me, cheerful and relieved. I took the opportunity to share something I’d been feeling.

What I wanted, more than anything, was to spend my birthday with my brothers. If we arrived in Santiago on my birthday, it’d be a dream come true, but I also knew we would have to do more kilometers each day, and I didn’t want to make them change their plans for me.

I told this to him. And he said that even though he started the Camino alone, he wanted to end it all together, just like me.

It was set then. Only one more brother to talk to.

As we walked, we found a tree carving of a Neanderthal. On the sign, it was written in three languages: Spanish, English, and Galician. Like the Basque Country, Galicia has its own language, though the two have very different linguistic roots.

My Italian brother and I arrived at the albergue the three of us reserved together in the morning, Pensión García en Triacastela.

We chose this place simply because it was €2 more than staying in the giant albergue with everyone else. And it was worth it.

We grabbed a late lunch at the restaurant below. A local dressed in neon construction clothes approached our table with a bucket. At first I was confused, but then he dropped a handful of roasted chestnuts on our table. With a big smile, he walked away.

They were still warm, and I knew they were picked from the forest nearby because of the multitude of nuts I’d walked past.

Our Argentinian brother arrived shortly after, along with several other pilgrims we knew.

One from Brazil, whom I met at the halfway mark, began telling us about the legend Jesús—the owner of the albergue we stayed at in Villafranca del Bierzo. The Brazilian told us that Jesús knows Paulo Coelho so well that Coelho actually began writing The Pilgrimage in the building where I slept. This was pivotal because that was the book that brought me to the Camino.

I struggled at that albergue because I wanted to have a meaningful conversation with the legend, but felt my Spanish wasn’t good enough. I blurted this out. The Brazilian reassured me and responded,

“I’m proud of you. I want to be like you.”

Many people on the Camino didn’t attempt to speak the language, but truthfully, most days I spoke more Spanish than English. I could feel myself improving, and the reflections from the people around me helped validate that feeling.

My Italian brother and I presented our idea to our Argentinian brother about getting to Santiago for my birthday. He agreed! Suddenly, all the grief I’d been feeling about leaving them went out the window.

On a walk to the small town supermarket, my Argentinian brother told me that he had an idea for him and me. In that conversation, he also said,

Both brothers had now said this to me one-on-one at some point during the Camino. The dynamic that was formed with these guys surpassed what I could’ve ever imagined for my walk.

Because we weren’t staying in the main albergue in town, there were only five of us in the whole place. The bunkhouse connected to the kitchen with sliding doors, and in that eating area we had the rare treasure of a TV with YouTube. Someone put on an American classic music playlist, and we all danced and had the most beautiful time.

The boys and I would now begin our journey to Santiago for real.

We each set our alarms for an early start.

SpaceX had hoped for a historic double-catch landing for the Starship Flight 15 booster and Ship. The booster didn't make it.

SpaceX has an idea why its latest Starship megarocket test flight missed the first of two planned landings on Friday (Nov. 13), triggering an automatic abort that destroyed the booster.

The giant Starship rocket, a massive two-stage vehicle that's the largest launcher on Earth, soared into orbit from SpaceX's Starbase in South Texas on Friday on the company's ambitious Flight 15 test flight. The goal: To land both the Super Heavy first stage and the Ship upper stage, for the first full Starship recovery — and the first complete recovery of an orbital launch system in history.

The Super Heavy stage, called Booster 22, came back to Earth mere minutes after liftoff as planned. Instead of returning to its launch tower to be caught mid-air by the tower's metal "chopstick" arms, however, the rocket veered off shore and detonated its flight termination system. A guidance issue appears to be the reason, SpaceX now says. Follow our Starship Flight 15 live updates for the latest on the mission.

Even if you can't see SpaceX's Starship in person, you can score a model of your own. Standing at 13.77 inches (35 cm), this is a 1:375 ratio of SpaceX's Starship as a desktop model. The materials here are alloy steel and it weighs just 225g.

"We were initially 'go' for a catch," SpaceX spokesperson Dan Huot said during live commentary about seven hours into the mission. "But in the later stages of the coast, the booster aborted itself for a GNC issue that it detected, and that put it into its contingency landing offshore." Super Heavy exploded a few feet above the waves of the Gulf of Mexico about 6.5 minutes after liftoff.

GNC is short for Guidance and Navigation Control, a criticial system used by rockets to control their flight and ensure they remain on their planned course. Such a system is vital for any landing attempt by a Starship Super Heavy or Ship upper stage to ensure the vehicle returns to its landing site safely and doesn't veer off course to damage SpaceX's Starbase or surrounding populated areas.

Super Heavy is powered by a cluster of 33 Raptor rocket engines that fire simultaneously during liftoff to send the 41-story Starship megarocket into orbit. During Friday's launch, all 33 engines fired as planned for the ascent, Huot said.

One engine did drop out after stage separation, when Super Heavy dropped away from Ship and flipped over for a "boostback" burn to begin its trip back to Earth, Huot said.

"We had 32 out of the 33 booster engines lit for boostback, which started sending it back here to the launch site," he added.

The guidance issue for Super Heavy appeared to occur during the booster's landing burn, when it fires 13 Raptor engines to slow itself for its tower approach before shifting to fewer and fewer engines. In SpaceX's launch video, the 13 engines can be seen activating, followed by the abort and the booster steering itself to a contingency landing zone in Gulf waters.

"It did that hover, cut the engines off, the FTS — the flight termination system — triggered, and it splashed down in the water," Huot said. (The FTS is designed to destroy a booster in the event of an emergency.)

As its name suggests, Starship Flight 15 is SpaceX's 15th test flight of the giant rocket, but it will be the first to attempt to catch a Ship upper stage with the chopsticks. SpaceX has successfully caught Super Heavy boosters on three test flights to date.

Flight 15's Ship vehicle is in good health and will orbit Earth a total of 14 times before landing back at its launch pad in Starbase at around 4:20 p.m. EST (1920 GMT) today, if all goes well.

"Our heat shield team reviewed all the data we got from our ground-tracking cameras, and from those satellite cameras and confirmed the heat shield is in a good posture for a reentry and return to the launch site," Huot said in the mission update. (Flight 15 hauled 26 of SpaceX's next-gen Starlink V3 satellites to orbit, and a few of those spacecraft carried cameras for heat-shield inspection.)

SpaceX designed Starship to be fully reusable, with the company banking on that reusability to make it an affordable heavy-lift booster capable of a wide range of missions to low Earth orbit, the moon and eventually Mars.

NASA has picked Starship as one of two commercial landers to carry Artemis astronauts to the moon in 2028, and SpaceX sees the rocket as vital to building out a 100,000 constellation of Starlink V3 internet satellites, as well as a new AI data center network in orbit called Starmind.

But first, SpaceX has to successfully land a Starship upper stage from orbit. That's the next step for Flight 15.

Huot says flight controllers will make one last check at the 20-hour mark in the mission to decide whether to proceed with the landing at Starbase or make an "early return" splashdown off the coast of Hawaii.

On Nov. 14, 1969, the Apollo 12 mission launched to the moon. This was the second moon landing of the Apollo program.

The two astronauts on board were Pete Conrad, commander, and Alan Bean, the lunar module pilot. Astronaut Richard Gordon, the command module pilot, stayed in the command module and orbited the moon while his crewmates went to the surface. The astronauts made a pinpoint landing in a lunar mare called the Ocean of Storms.

The previous moon mission, Apollo 11, drifted far from the planned landing zone. But Apollo 12 landed close to a scientific target. That target was a robotic lander called Surveyor 3. Scientists wanted to look at Surveyor 3 to see how well the spacecraft lasted on the moon since it landed there more than two years earlier.

The Apollo 12 moonwalkers also performed a lot of moon science. The astronauts picked up 75 pounds of moon rocks. Most of the rocks were basalts that formed from molten lava. These basalts showed the Apollo 12 landing site is more than 3 billion years old.

Apollo 12 proved that the Apollo 11 moon landing was not just a one-off stunt while setting the stage for even more ambitious missions.

To truly explore the moon and advance our understanding of how our solar system formed, the follow-on Apollo flights needed to be able to land where the geologists had identified as areas of interest. Apollo 12, with its touchdown in proximity to the Surveyor probe, proved this was possible.

The launch of Apollo 12 also underscored the importance of having a well-prepared Mission Control team, an asset that would be critical to outcome of the next mission, Apollo 13.

The ability to recover from a complete electrical short out with the simple call of a through of a switch "SCE to AUX" proved that knowing every detail about how the spacecraft worked was key to a successful program.
