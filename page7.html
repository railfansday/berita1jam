Attacker used 1Password employee’s GitHub account to copy customer records

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Identity security company 1Password today disclosed a security incident in which an attacker, working through an employee’s corporate GitHub account, copied account records belonging to a subset of its customers.

According to the company, the incident came to light on Nov. 19, when 1Password began investigating signs that the GitHub account had been compromised. Using the account, the attacker is said to have pushed malicious code to 1Password’s backend services, which then downloaded what the company describes as “administrative data pertaining to our customers.”

The records taken include names and email addresses, account and user identifiers, account creation and last sign-in times, public keys, Secure Remote Password protocol parameters and other non-secret account metadata. Rounding out the list are tokens found in certain emailed links for invitations, provisioning and account recovery.

Customer vaults, the items stored in them and the keys that protect them were not affected, according to 1Password. “At no time were customer vault contents accessed by, or available to, the attacker,” the company said in its disclosure. Account passwords and Secret Keys are not stored by the company. Also left untouched, the company says, were account recovery keys and the 1Password apps and browser extensions.

Beyond the customer records, the attacker is said to have copied source code from private repositories. Access was also gained to 1Password’s continuous integration and continuous delivery system, which contains infrastructure-related secrets needed for its product to work. According to 1Password, the source code contains no user secrets. Having triggered a rotation of all credentials that may have been exposed, the company claims there has been no impact to its services, availability or product security.

The tokens taken from emailed links cannot be used on their own to access an account, since completing an invitation or recovery also requires a sign-in through single sign-on or a six-digit code sent to the user’s registered email address. Users with provisioning invites or recovery requests pending at the time of the incident, said to be a small number, have been notified directly.

As a precaution, the emailed codes have now been lengthened from six numeric digits to 10 alphanumeric characters. 1Password says it has found no internal evidence of account takeovers using the exposed tokens. Other steps taken in response include disabling the affected GitHub account, revoking the stolen token, tightening internal controls and bringing in independent cybersecurity experts to validate its containment and remediation work.

Customers do not need to change anything in their 1Password accounts. The company is instead warning users to be cautious of phishing attempts, such as unexpected emails, texts or calls about invitations or account recovery requests they did not start. “1Password will never contact you to ask for your account password or Secret Key,” the company noted.

1Password, which claims to serve more than 200,000 businesses, did not say how many customers had records downloaded. How the employee’s GitHub account was compromised was also left unexplained.

This is not the first intrusion 1Password has disclosed. In October 2023, the company said that an attacker had accessed its Okta tenant using session data stolen in the breach of Okta Inc.’s customer support system. No user data was found to have been compromised in that case.

Today’s disclosure comes nearly four years after rival password manager LastPass US LP revealed that a hacker had copied encrypted customer password vaults, using information taken in an earlier breach of its development environment.

1Password says it will update its post if the investigation turns up anything that changes what it has disclosed so far.

5 Greatest Classic Fantasy Books To Read in the Winter

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

As the temperatures drop and the Christmas lights begin to twinkle, you might find yourself wanting to enjoy some type of media. Maybe it's a great classic movie — lord knows there's enough of those — or maybe it's playing Mariah Carey's signature tune on loop — more power to you, if that's the case. However, you might be looking for something a bit more long-term, and that's where books come in.

Fortunately for you, there's a fair share of great classic books to enjoy over the winter, especially in the realm of fantasy. Perhaps it's a good, old-fashioned chivalry romance set in the distant imaginary past, or maybe it's a thrilling adventure in a far-away, fantastical land. Whatever the story, these fantasy books are the ideal choice for you to enjoy over the next three months, wrapped in a blanket, with a cup of warm cocoa by your side and the will to immerse yourself in magical realms where everything is possible.

There have been several retellings and reinterpretations of the classic Arthurian legend, but none is more defining or prevalent than T.H. White's. Drawing from the equally seminal La Morte D'Arthur, The Once and Future King is comprised of four parts — The Sword in the Stone, The Queen of Air and Darkness, The Ill-Made Knight, and The Candle in the Wind — and chronicles Arthur's life, from his childhood as "Ward" to his time as Camelot's greatest ruler.

The Once and Future King offers an entire, comprehensive fantastical journey through the life of one of literature's most iconic figures. It begins as a whimsical and charming coming-of-age tale of magic and culminates in a severe and tragic story of duty and fate. Few books, fantasy or otherwise, have ever presented such a complete and rewarding depiction of the Hero's Journey. Everything you'd ever wanted to know about the Arthurian legend takes shape here, with the book featuring all the major characters. It's the definitive Arthurian tale, and a perfect book to experience throughout the chilly winter.

Peter S. Beagle's The Last Unicorn is a sad fairy tale that offers plenty of food for thought. The plot centers on the titular Unicorn, an immortal being who learns she might be the last of her kind. Thus, she abandons her enchanted forest and goes on a quest to find out what happened to her species, meeting colorful characters along the way and eventually reaching the castle of the depressed King Haggard.

The Last Unicorn might seem like an unexpected choice. However, its melancholy tone is perfect for a season where, despite all the joy, one can also feel a distinct and gloomy vibe at times. There's a real sense of loss here, with the book exploring themes of mortality and regret, two ideas that many find themselves wrestling with during the happiest season. It's not all gloom here, though, as the Unicorn learns the value of love and its importance to a well-lived life, but it is a rather wistful tale that might leave you feeling a bit blue. Yet, it's also tremendously enjoyable, a perfect combo for the winter season.

When discussing fantasy sagas, Robert Jordan's Wheel of Time ranks near the top. Producing a whopping 15 novels — 3 of which were co-authored by Brandon Sanderson after Jordan's passing in 2007 — the series is among the most complex and expansive in the genre. It all began with The Eye of the World, where a group of small-town boys go on a perilous journey after learning that one of them is the Dragon Reborn, destined to either save or destroy the world.

The Eye of the World might just be the perfect Fall book. It begins with a warm, almost pastoral approach as it introduces the main characters and their little town in the region of Two Rivers, before switching to a colder, darker vibe once they leave the safety of what they know to begin their journey. It's not exactly grimdark, though, so while the plot does get more somber, it never becomes actively tough to endure. Instead, The Eye of the World is a clever subversion of the Hero's Journey, featuring a cast of relatable characters and a world that is as inviting as it's dangerous and changing.

Now on to a book that is pure winter wonder. Philip Pullman's Northern Lights is set in a world where people's souls are physically manifested as animals. The main plot centers on Lyra Belacqua, a twelve-year-old who becomes entangled in the mysterious disappearances of children, including her friend, Roger. Eventually, her search leads her to the Arctic, where she'll face polar bears, befriend nomadic people, and learn about the mysterious substance known as Dust.

Out of all the books on this list, Northern Lights is the one that might seem the most obvious to enjoy during the winter. Everything about it, from its initial English setting to the Arctic scenes in the later half, screams winter — heck, the title alone is enough to get you in a chilly mood! The entire saga might be aimed at teen audiences, but it's actually rather deep, dealing with themes of oppression and religious hypocrisy, a fact that has made it quite controversial. Even so, His Dark Materials is a must-read for every fantasy fan, and there's no better time to experience it than in winter.

What better way to end than with a show where the very idea of winter is so central that it's practically a plot point in itself? "Winter is coming," George R.R. Martin told us in A Game of Thrones, a dark fantasy saga set in the hyper-violent and ruthless lands of Westeros and Essos. The novel follows honorable Ned Stark as he becomes Hand of the King to his drunken friend, King Robert Baratheon, placing him at the center of a dangerous murder mystery that will lead to his tragic end. in the North, a threat rises from beyond the Wall, while across the Narrow Sea, exiled princess Daenerys Targaryen marries a Dothraki warlord.

A Game of Thrones is one of the most celebrated fantasy novels of the last 30 years. Martin created one of the most vivid and complex worlds in fantasy, a grimdark masterpiece that would completely revolutionize the genre in the new millennium. The plot is set at the end of a long summer, as the threat of winter looms in the distance. Blending intense, brutal action with a more intimate mystery set in the cutthroat court of King's Landing, A Game of Thrones is a masterclass in high fantasy writing, kickstarting a franchise that eventually spawned arguably the most defining TV shows of the 2010s.

5 Things We’re Saying No to This Holiday Season

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 25, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Before you say yes this holiday season, ask yourself what you’re saying yes to. Consider the feelings that come up for you when you think about it. Is it excitement about seeing the people you love? Is it dread at the anticipation of planning, conflict, or grief? For some, it’s all of the above.

Recent polls find that nearly 9 in 10 (89%) U.S. adults report holiday stressors (APA, 2023). Tensions arise due to finances, family conflict, grief, and other factors. Stress takes away from the pleasurable aspects of the season, which should include reconnection and rest.

Sometimes that is easier said than done. Here are 5 things we're saying no to this holiday season and strategies to achieve the happy holidays we deserve.

Sometimes that family holiday announcement sounds like a demand rather than an invitation. “Dinner is at 4 pm sharp. See you there!” Everyone assumes you’ll be there, and no one asks questions. They don’t ask if you can take time off work, afford the trip, or have other plans. You feel obligated to attend despite any barriers you are facing.

Almost no one will tell you that you are allowed to consider your own needs before you RSVP yes. Ask yourself whether you have the time, energy, and money to participate this year. If the answer is no, it’s alright. Your family may be disappointed, and you may feel a little guilty, but it’s worth asking which will weigh on you more: the guilt of saying no or the cost of saying yes.

In toxic families, it’s the once-over as soon as you enter the room. Before you’ve even had a chance to sit down, someone has made a joke about your weight or graying hair, offered a backhanded compliment about the dish you contributed, or slipped in a dig about your personal life.

You may feel hurt, sad, or even angry, and you don’t have to ignore those feelings to preserve the holiday spirit. You can be silent and stone-faced to let the “joke” fall flat. You might excuse yourself and regroup in another room. If you want to address it directly and call out the hidden meaning, you can say, “That felt like a dig,” or ask, “What do you mean by that?” What you don’t have to do is tolerate disrespect.

When you want everyone to feel loved, it’s easy to slip into spending more than you intended. If you’ve ever hosted a large family gathering, you know how quickly the groceries and gifts add up. And if you’re the one who hosts every year, it can feel like the work and the bill are yours by default.

Before you do anything, set a budget. Then share the work in ways that play into everyone’s strengths. Maybe enlist the nephew who can roast meat or the cousin who loves to bake, and assign cleanup to the one you prefer to stay out of the kitchen. Suggest rotating hosting duties. Do a gift exchange rather than buying individual gifts for everyone. You can show your love to your family with limits.

In some families, playing the dutiful daughter (or son) means everyone assumes you’ll make the holidays happen. Often it’s the family matriarch or the eldest daughter who initiates the first call about the holiday plans and is the one still in the kitchen long after everyone else has gone home. She is the family “glue” who keeps track of the invites, remembers who can’t eat what, and figures out where to seat the relatives who aren’t speaking. By the time the holiday arrives, she’s too tired to enjoy the fruits of her labor.

If that sounds like you, name it. People who have never hosted may not realize all of the hidden effort that goes into an event. Bring it to their attention. Set limits on what you will do and ask for help, but don’t micromanage. If the person assigned to bring the rolls forgets, skip the rolls this year. If no one steps up, step back anyway. Plan a smaller, more intimate gathering that won’t leave you frazzled at the end of it.

The holidays aren’t the same after you’ve lost a loved one, and the first holiday season after a loss can be especially difficult. Family traditions may be upended. The person who always hosted and made everyone’s favorite dish is gone. Tensions that surface after the loss may still be hanging in the air.

Take a moment to evaluate your feelings. Ask yourself what you feel up to this year. Maybe it’s not the year to take on hosting duties, or perhaps you stop by for dessert rather than staying the entire day. You can choose to honor your loved one by retaining old traditions or step into the next chapter with new traditions in their memory. You can miss someone deeply and still make room for moments of joy this holiday season.

It might seem strange that saying no could be the start of a happy holiday. The beauty is that you get to decide what a happy holiday looks like for you, and that it can change from year to year. Ask yourself what would make the holidays feel good to you this year and do it.

A Very Serious Story About Falling Asleep While Belaying

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The East Face of Mount Temple, Canada, and the scene of this story's shenanigans. (Photo: Mitra Samavaki / Getty)

Big falls are commonplace while sport climbing and bouldering, but, in my opinion, the high alpine remains largely a safe space to take your brake hand off the rope. Why? With loose rock, aerated ice, and an often minimalist rack, falling off—even with a tight belay—usually spells disaster. A climber has every reason to follow the old “the leader cannot fall” ethos.

This old school state of mind deeply resonated with me earlier this month on a difficult alpine climb on Mount Temple in the Canadian Rockies. This 1,400-meter ice and mixed route began easily enough, with a hike up a hard-packed snow slope at 6 a.m. toward the deep cleft that marked the start. Neither my partner Rory nor I were burdened with rope management during the first hour of climbing, since our rope stayed neatly coiled in a pack.

Hundreds of meters higher, below one of the crux pitches, we flaked out our rope. Above us stood a terribly skinny pillar of ice no wider than my shoulders at its base. Blue light filtered through its entirety, a sign of its youth and untrustworthiness.

“I don’t think I should place a screw in that,” Rory decided.

I scanned the shattered quartzite behind the pillar, hoping to at least keep us attached to the mountain before Rory essentially soloed the pitch. Two nervous pitons and a shallow micro nut were all I could cobble together after 10 minutes of searching. I stomped out a waist-deep hole in the snow on the back side of the pillar and clipped into our wobbly anchor. My now four-piece anchor—plus Rory’s unshakeable climbing ability—gave us great odds of surviving his lead.

The runout pitch gave me ample opportunity to pay out slack as I pleased while munching on trail mix and rehydrating from our busy morning. After all, Rory was not allowed to fall, so he didn’t.

Falling has been a frowned-upon practice for most of climbing’s history, especially before sport climbing became popular in the 1980s. Only relatively recently, and only in some styles of climbing, have leader falls become no longer taboo—even acceptable and encouraged.

The crusty old-timer in me knows that, in any form of free climbing, it is always safer to climb to the top of a pitch without testing your equipment, be it a skinny rope, crashpad, or ice screw. This gut instinct evolved from a time when climbing gear was not completely trustworthy. Maybe you hand-stitched your quickdraw’s webbing yourself. Maybe you’d sourced your climbing protection from a hardware store. Or maybe you had tied your lead line around your waist because drop-seat harnesses hadn’t been invented yet. In this era—really only a few decades ago—your climbing ability was the first line of defense. Ropes and harnesses and removable protection served merely as dubious back-ups.

But the advent of regular whippers is a tragedy for one more reason: With newfangled belay devices, hang dogging, and constant pleas to “watch me!” belaying has become a real pain in the ass.

Nearly 12 hours later, Rory and I were still on Mount Temple. We’d topped out the ice climbing portion of the face and traversed across open snow slopes and through rock bands to a final serrated crux: the Black Towers. Countless turrets of dark limestone slice across the summit ridge of Mount Temple, creating difficult route finding even on a clear summer’s day. We were approaching midnight in November and a thick fog had choked the upper mountain. Visibility hovered around 10 meters. I had no idea where we were supposed to go.

Thankfully it was Rory’s lead again, and he was questing. Fifteen minutes ticked by as he wandered up a steep rocky groove, then another 15 of the rope unmoving. An hour passed. I clicked off my headlamp to save battery. More time passed. I figured I could tie off the rope and catch a few Zs, so I did. Sleep felt amazing. I was glad that even as we approached 24 hours on the move, Rory still knew the rules: The leader cannot fall. And the belayer gets to sleep.

I woke up to the sound of Rory’s voice. He’d been blocked by an overhang and was downclimbing. Thick, icy fog obscured our vision as I took the rack and traversed along a snow ledge looking for easier passage. The fog would sometimes lift for an instant—I’d spot a lonely star above—and I’d frantically flick my headlamp to full strength to catch a glimpse of the mountain. But before my eyes could adjust to the new sight, the fog would crash back in like a breaking wave, drowning us in uncertainty once again.

This story does not end in a climbing success. We did not ultimately find our way through the Black Towers, nor did we share a weary, satisfied hug on the summit of Mount Temple. We became lost and a little delirious on a large mountain in the middle of the night and needed to go down.

We stayed roped together as we retraced our steps through deep snow on the upper mountain. I was a little worried about triggering an avalanche, but mainly I was just exhausted. If I stumbled, I would prefer to slide 30 meters before being caught by the rope than well over a thousand into the next life.

Rory and I regrouped at an anchor I’d built in an outcrop of yellow rock. He led out across another snowfield which terminated at a gigantic notch in an otherwise sheer ridgeline. The notch marked the start of a classic ski descent, the 60-degree Aemmer Couloir, and our escape hatch. Rory trudged toward it and I paid out rope, nodding off again.

Suddenly, I was awake. I had not pooped all day, yet the kilogram of trail mix and sausage and cheese I’d downed was making a basement appearance.

“Rory! Do you need a real belay for this bit? I really need to poo,” I yelled into the fog.

I let the rope feed through the anchor, then unclipped it from my belay device and, without moving even a step away from our anchor, unloaded into the snow bank.

It would have been a disgusting mess had Rory fallen and weighted the anchor, dragging me into my own steaming waste. But he didn’t, and I made it back to civilization with both my dignity and my life.

Austin Reaves talks for first time ahead of Spurs clash: ‘Felt like I broke my foot’

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Austin Reaves, who has missed the last five games because of a bruised left heel, was a full participant in Lakers practice on Wednesday but his status remains unknown for Friday's game against the Spurs.

"It felt good to play basketball again," Reaves said in his first public comments since suffering the injury Nov. 11. "Wind was a little bad, but kind of to be expected being out two weeks. But felt good. You know, we'll see how I wake up [Thursday]. We're still going in the right direction, but felt really good today."

Austin Reaves, left, and Luka Doncic talk during a game against the Utah Jazz in Los Angeles, Wednesday, Nov. 18, 2026. NBAE via Getty Images

Reaves sustained the injury after landing awkwardly following a missed fast-break dunk in the third quarter of a game against the Bucks.

He said he's not quite sure what happened on the play.

"No, I had no idea," he said. "Tried to play through it, and then I drove baseline, planted on it, and was like, I can't do this. So walked to the back and was in some pretty bad pain. I didn't think I broke my foot, but it sure felt like I broke my foot."

Lakers coach JJ Redick, who suffered a similar injury in April 2016, has repeatedly described the heel bruise as a "pain tolerance issue." Reaves echoed that Wednesday.

"It's not a strain in your hamstring or something like that," Reaves said. "It's kind of just something that's going to linger around if you continue to play before you're ready. And basically, the main message was, let's get this back to normal before we put you back out there and put yourself at risk again so later on in the season you won't have to deal with a flare up or something."

The Lakers went 4-1 after Reaves suffered the injury, but they clearly missed his offensive production after falling to the Jazz on Nov. 18, 109-97, and barely squeaking past the Hornets (118-114) and Kings (106-104). Reaves is averaging 24.7 points (51.3% shooting from the field, 40.9% from beyond the arc), 3.3 rebounds and 4.7 assists.

"One of those things that just, it sucks," Reaves said. "You know, I was rolling, playing well. We were rolling as a team, wanted to continue to be out there and play games. Hate missing games. But it's one of those things, like I said, there wasn't much that we could really do. But we took care of business."

Austin Reaves #15 of the Los Angeles Lakers reacts during a game against the Utah Jazz at Crypto.com Arena on Monday, Nov. 23, 2026, in Los Angeles. NBAE via Getty Images

When asked how Reaves looked Wednesday, Redick didn't hesitate.

When reporters prodded him to see if he was being sarcastic, he doubled down, "Yeah, not joking," he added.

Austin Reaves (15) of the Los Angeles Lakers smiles during a game against the Utah Jazz at Crypto.com Arena in Los Angeles on Wednesday, Nov. 18, 2026. NBAE via Getty Images

The Lakers sure hope that Reaves can return Friday and that Redick is right considering the challenge they have ahead of them.

The Lakers and Spurs are tied for the best record in the NBA at 15-3 and Luka Doncic and Victor Wembanyama are currently in a two-man race for the MVP Award.

When asked if it's important to him to return against the Spurs, Reaves balked.

"I mean, I want to play," Reaves said. "…I didn't get hurt the night of Milwaukee and look at the schedule and be like, 'God, I need to be back before the San Antonio game.' If I'm back, good, we'll go to war. But I got to listen to my body and take care of myself. But it should be fun if I'm healthy and get out there."
