Breakfast News: What NVDA’s CEO Exit Means for You

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Huang built a company with the products, people, and customers to outlast him, and we expect Nvidia to come through this.

Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's (NVDA -4.34%) chief executive amid controversies over his personal conduct and questions about whether Nvidia's chips reached China through a customer in Singapore.

His exit followed a week of reports about his personal life. The first concerned an affair with an executive at Megaspeed, one of Nvidia's customers in Singapore. U.S. officials suspected the executive of spying for China, which China denied. On Wednesday, a second relationship, with one of Huang's direct reports, came to light. The board met that night, and the company announced Huang's resignation the following morning.

We think investors are right to take Huang's departure seriously. In our view, though, the sell-off prices in more damage than the business has shown so far.

If you've followed Nvidia for any length of time, you know how central Huang has been. He co-founded the company in 1993 and led it for all 33 years. His leadership structure was also known for being unusually flat, as he had 33 direct reports at last count, according to CNBC.

Huang pushed Nvidia as an innovator and technologist. He launched CUDA (software that lets developers program Nvidia's chips) in 2006 and boldly committed the company to artificial intelligence (AI) computing long before it became a mainstream market. He also managed Nvidia's largest customer relationships himself and represented the company in Washington on export rules for China.

That is a lot for one person to hand off, let alone overnight, and Nvidia has no named successor to take it on. For now, CFO Colette Kress, who has worked alongside Huang since 2013 and has joined him on every earnings call, will serve as interim CEO while the board searches for a permanent replacement.

We understand why the market reacted so sharply. A company that leaned this heavily on one person's judgment now has to make its next big decisions without him, and under federal scrutiny. Analyst Dan Ives estimates Nvidia is worth 25% to 30% less without Huang, and some discount is warranted. With shares dropping to $148.80 in Thursday trading and closing at around $166, the market has already applied much of it.

What reassures us is that Huang's exit on its own is unlikely to change Nvidia's revenue much over the next two years. Demand for its chips is still growing quickly. Last quarter, revenue reached $96.2 billion, up 106% from a year earlier, and the company guided to $108 billion for the quarter that ended in October. By any measure, that's a business in good health.

Chips take years to design, so the products Nvidia will sell through 2027 were planned while Huang was still in charge, and customers have already ordered many of them. Even if regulators cut off sales routed through Singapore, we estimate Nvidia would lose no more than about 5% of its revenue.

A broader crackdown would cost more, and the investigations will help decide whether one comes.

$1,000 in Main Street Capital's 2007 IPO Is Worth About $7,300 Counting Dividends. It Has Never Cut Its Monthly Payout.

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Main Street Capital is one of the best, most consistent dividend stocks out there.

Investors looking for a good dividend-income stock may not find too many better than Main Street Capital (MAIN -0.78%).

The business development company (BDC) has raised its dividend for 19 straight years, every year since it went public in 2007 -- and has never once reduced its dividend.

And it has already raised its dividend for the first three months of 2027, putting it on track for 20 straight years of dividend raises.

Main Street Capital is also one of the relatively few dividend stocks that pay a monthly dividend, so the income stream comes more frequently than most.

BDCs, like real estate investment trusts (REITs), are required by law to pay out 90% of their taxable income in dividends in exchange for getting certain tax advantages. So they are literally built to generate dividend income.

Main Street Capital went public in 2007 as a BDC that focuses on providing debt and equity capital solutions for mostly smaller companies that can't get funding from traditional commercial banks or private equity partners.

But what makes it different, and superior to most, is that its portfolio is internally managed, as opposed to being managed by a third party, so it has lower fees because it doesn't pay fees to a third party. But it also takes an ownership stake in its companies, in addition to its loans, so it is more invested in companies' success and can directly benefit from their growth.

That has helped it steadily feed its dividend for 19 years. It currently pays out a monthly dividend of $0.265 through the end of 2026, up from $0.255 in the same period a year ago. Main Street Capital currently pays out a high yield of about 7.68%.

On Nov. 3, Main Street approved a $0.27 monthly dividend for the first three months of 2027 -- marking a 3.8% increase over the first quarter of 2026. This would be the 20th straight year of dividend increases for Main Street Capital. It also authorized an additional $0.30 per share supplemental dividend for December 2026 because of excess taxable income.

Main Street Capital went public in October 2007 at roughly $15 per share. If you invested $1,000 in the stock at its IPO, that would have bought you roughly 67 shares. At its current share price of around $57 per share, that $1,000 would be worth about $3,800 today.

But then how much dividend income would you have accumulated?

Calculating all dividends declared to date, including the first-quarter 2027 dividends and the supplemental December 2026 dividend, Main Street will have paid $52.315 per share in cash dividends since its October 2007 IPO. If you multiply $52.315 times the 67 shares, you would have pocketed about $3,500 in dividend income, alongside the approximately 7.2% average annualized return you made on Main Street's stock since its IPO.

So, if you combined the dividend income with the capital appreciation, that $1,000 investment in 2007 would be worth about $7,300 today.

Inside the $3.25 Million Pasadena Glass Pavilion That’s Still Studied in Architecture Classes Today

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A three-bedroom midcentury modern home designed by Case Study architect Craig Ellwood has just come on the market in Pasadena, CA, for $3.25 million. It features a notable cantilevered glass pavilion.

Built in 1965, the Kubly House was originally constructed for less than $45,000. The 2,227-square-foot home has since been thoughtfully updated.

“What makes this house special is its architecture and design, and how true it has remained to the original,” says Chris Suarez, who is co-listing the property with Kimberly Benz of Suarez + Benz at Compass. “Nothing was significantly altered. Everything done was intended to restore the house to its original condition. The footprint, the floors, the glass—everything has remained true to the original design.”

Located in the Linda Vista neighborhood, the home has an iconic redwood, cantilevered glass pavilion that opens to a single-level floor plan. The private half-acre lot includes a pool and spa surrounded by lush landscaping with eucalyptus and oak trees.

With exposed post-and-beam construction, clean horizontal lines, and expansive walls of glass, the home was designed to give the illusion of floating above its natural surroundings. The deck was recently restored using specially milled wood.

“It was sourced from northern Washington and brought down here,” Suarez notes. “Everything was done painstakingly to avoid altering the house’s integrity, architecture, and design. That being said, significant work has been done, including updates to the roof, infrastructure, air conditioning, and electrical systems. All the major systems have been updated, but you wouldn’t necessarily know it because the work was done so carefully.”

Originally commissioned by ArtCenter College of Design alumni and educators Don and Sally Kubly, the home represents their understanding of design and the culture of the college community. It is still studied in classes there.

“Don was ArtCenter’s first president in Pasadena,” Suarez explains. “There’s also a connection through Craig Ellwood, who designed ArtCenter’s main campus building. The main campus is an incredible steel-and-glass building designed by Ellwood. Don played a significant role in moving the school from Los Angeles to Pasadena, where he served as president for many years. He’s very well known within that community, and this house is significant to the college’s history and culture.”

Based on available architectural records, the Kubly House is said to be Ellwood’s only documented residential project in Pasadena.

After remaining in the Kubly family for nearly six decades, the home changed hands for $3.6 million in 2022. Since then, the sellers have undertaken a preservation-minded restoration with Carl Bronson of Bronstruction, updating major systems to contemporary standards.

“A house that helped usher in much midcentury modernism was the Glass House, designed by Philip Johnson around 1948 or 1949,” Benz notes. “That design was definitely something Ellwood loved. You can clearly see that house’s influence here, which is important because it reveals some of the influences behind Ellwood’s work.”

Benz adds that the home’s design was also influenced by Ellwood’s peers, including midcentury designer George Nelson.

“A furniture plan developed for the house clearly referenced Mies van der Rohe and George Nelson, both important figures in midcentury modern design,” she says. “Even the deck and the steps leading to the house resemble a George Nelson bench. There’s actually a bench built into the concrete wall near the pool that resembles a George Nelson design. I don’t know whether Nelson designed or made it, but the influence is certainly there.”

The house is special because many of the original details have remained intact, she adds.

“You can see the influence of the people Ellwood admired throughout the house,” Benz says. “That significance is what makes this house important when you look at the midcentury modernist movement. All these examples are visible in the house and remain there.”

Some of the built-in furniture has a distinctive style, too.

“There’s a stunning floating credenza that looks like a George Nelson piece,” she notes. “We think it might be one of his designs. It’s in the living room. There’s also a built-in console that might be a George Nelson design as well. So, some of the furniture is actually built into the architecture.”

While some pieces are originals and others are reproductions, Benz says, “the most important thing was following the original plan. The history of this house is everything.”

When staging the home, she says they were “fortunate to discover an original interior design study among the blueprints, plans, and documents that came with the house. We were able to look at that study, and it became a guide and a road map for staging the property. Sourcing the furniture was a challenge because we had to find the specific pieces.”

The city of Pasadena has also identified the property as eligible for individual listing on the National Register of Historic Places.

Baldwin Group CEO: The biggest AI disruption in insurance is the advisor

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Artificial intelligence is going to disrupt insurance brokerage. There is not much question about that. The question that matters is what gets disrupted, and how differently firms are preparing for it.

Most firms are approaching AI the way they approached every prior wave of technology: take the work as it exists and do it faster. But AI is dramatically changing the economics of gathering information, comparing coverage and facilitating transactions. A firm whose value lives in those activities is competing in a race to the bottom.

A firm built on specialized expertise, deep client relationships and experiential human judgment can change the paradigm, turning risk into opportunity for clients. Firms that make that leap go from being a vendor of downside protection to a business partner that helps a client achieve the fullness of their ambition. Their value becomes constrained only by what their clients can achieve.

Showing up that way requires the fortitude to completely rebuild how the work gets done. At Baldwin, alongside our partners at Dell Family Office and Sequence Holdings, we have launched into that journey. We call it Refounding.

Consider a middle-market renewal: gather exposure data, prepare the submission, market it, compare quotes, build the proposal, and present. Weeks of cycle time, most of it waiting on the next hand-off. Apply AI to each step and you compress each one a little. Redesign the work around what AI does well and you compress the entire cycle, and you hand the recovered time back to the client advisor for the only parts a client will pay a premium for: understanding the business, anticipating what could go wrong and structuring solutions that unlock potential.

That is the work underway through Catalyst, our firm-wide transformation program. Over 17 weeks, we completed more than 47,000 AI-assisted tasks at quality rates above 98 percent while standardizing 27 processes in our Client Experience teams. Those numbers matter only for what they free up: capacity redeployed into advisory work, not simply banked as cost savings.

Three things happen when you make that shift. Cycle times compress meaningfully. Throughput rises because the same team carries more complexity. And complex knowledge work gets sharper. In one early claims-review application, AI identified coverage gaps in minutes that led to $71,000 in additional claim payment for a client. A professional still had to evaluate what the technology surfaced and decide what to do. That is the model: technology ingests and processes; the human provides the judgment; and experienced professionals remain accountable for every decision that carries real financial consequences for a client.

AI drives the marginal cost of information toward zero, and when information is free, the premium shifts to what you do with it. Clients will have more data than ever. What they will not have is someone who has seen 200 versions of their problem, who knows which carrier will actually show up when a claim is contested, and who will tell them the uncomfortable truth about a coverage gap before the loss rather than after. That is judgment, earned through experience. The relationship that lets a client hear that perspective and act on it with conviction is earned the same way.

It is also why insurance advice has to move earlier in the conversation. Too often, insurance enters the discussion after the consequential decisions about risk have been made. With AI compressing the mechanics, the best advisors will move upstream into the room where those decisions happen, helping clients understand how risk affects their investments, their capital and their growth. That is where the advisor’s value was always supposed to live. The transactional work got in the way.

For generations, early-career insurance professionals built judgment by doing the foundational work: reviewing policies, preparing submissions and sitting beside experienced colleagues. That apprenticeship was never efficient, but it worked. AI will eliminate the tasks it is built on. Remove those tasks and do nothing else, and we will produce a generation of advisors who can operate the tools but cannot challenge them. Judgment comes from working through hard situations, seeing the consequences of decisions and learning from people who have been there before. We have to redesign the apprenticeship for a world where the old on-ramp no longer exists.

At Baldwin, we are transforming how we work, and early-career colleagues are among the first to benefit. They now encounter real client challenges sooner than the traditional path allowed. We have begun retooling our internal university, The Azimuth Institute, using AI as a training environment where a new advisor can pressure-test a coverage program, or ask why a senior colleague structured a placement a certain way, and get a substantive answer in seconds. Because AI absorbs time-consuming tasks, these colleagues are now beginning to learn higher-level insurance concepts earlier and spend more time on client relationships and complex coverage questions. We are also committed to more structured mentorship, not less, because experienced advisors are best placed to teach how to rigorously interrogate what the technology produces. And we are rethinking performance: when a day’s work takes an hour, the question is no longer how fast a task was completed, but how well the day was spent on problem-solving and critical thinking for client solutions.

Five years from now, the role of the insurance advisor will look substantially different, and I expect the advisors who meet that moment to be more valuable than any generation before them, because what they bring is the one thing the technology cannot: experience, judgment and a relationship the client trusts.

The firms that win will not be the ones that focus on AI purely to drive efficiencies. They will be the ones that changed the work, then built the people who could do the new work well. That is the work in front of us, and we intend to get it done.

Detroit’s innovation lesson: what kids hear about careers vs. what they actually do

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Anika Goss is president and CEO of Detroit Future City.

Angelique Power is president and CEO of The Skillman Foundation. Their organizations co-produced Career-Connected Learning: Investing in Detroit’s Future Talent (May 2026), the report cited in this piece; Skillman funded the research.

Some of America’s most influential leaders will soon gather in Detroit for the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum to discuss innovation, the pace of change and what it takes to compete. In classrooms across the same city, the workers their companies will need a decade from now are hearing about careers they may never get to try.

When Detroit Future City and The Skillman Foundation surveyed 83 young Detroiters in 2025 about career exposure, 78 percent said they had heard from a guest speaker or professional mentor. Fewer than half had done an internship or paid work experience. Just 35 percent had received hands-on training.

Detroit’s teenagers are not short on talk about work. They are short on chances to do it.

Jordan, a composite of students in our focus groups, shows what that costs. At 14, she loved sketching buildings, rooms and imagined cities. But no one suggested her interests might point toward a career in architecture or design. She discovered architecture years later, by accident, and realized there were pathways she could have explored in middle school.

Her story is common. A student who loved performing did not learn about physical therapy until nearly graduating. A true crime enthusiast had no idea criminology was a career. Students in skilled trades programs wished they had discovered those opportunities earlier.

Some youth are best suited for college, while others may pursue careers directly after high school. Both need opportunities to explore options and find a path forward.

Across the country, employers report trouble finding the right skills and experience. In a 2025 U.S. Chamber of Commerce survey, just 38 percent of hiring managers said finding candidates with the right skills was easy; 37 percent said the same about experience. The report, produced with the College Board, also found that 84 percent believe most high school graduates are not workforce-ready.

That points to a blind spot. Workforce programs focus on the late stages: choosing a major, enrolling in training, applying for a job. By then, young people like Jordan have already formed assumptions about which careers exist and where they belong.

Our May 2026 report, which Skillman funded, started with young people themselves: surveys and focus groups with nearly 200 Detroit youth, along with conversations with educators, employers and community leaders.

What we heard was consistent: Young people want earlier career exposure, mentors to explain different pathways and opportunities to connect school with the world beyond it. They are not asking adults to decide their futures. They want the possibilities to be more visible and accessible.

That is not just a Detroit challenge. According to NAF’s 2025–2026 Student Survey, 47 percent of students who did not participate in work-based learning cited a lack of awareness of available opportunities. Meanwhile, 79 percent of students who participated said those experiences helped them identify and develop skills for their future careers.

Exposure alone is not enough. In Detroit, talk is plentiful and practice is scarce.

Businesses can build on Career and Technical Education programs by offering workplace exposure, mentors, internships, apprenticeships and other forms of work-based learning. These experiences help students test their interests, build skills and understand career requirements.

Apprenticeships show the payoff. According to the U.S. Department of Labor, more than 800,000 people participate in apprenticeship programs annually nationwide. And the department’s April 2026 ApprenticeshipUSA fact sheet reports that 93 percent of apprentices who complete their programs retain employment, demonstrating the value of connecting education with hands-on experience and career opportunities.

Business cannot do this alone; educators, workforce organizations, higher education, philanthropy and community partners all have roles. The goal is to help young people see their options, not steer them toward one path.

Detroit Public Schools Community District’s High School Redesign strengthens connections between education, career exploration and life after graduation. Beginning in the 2026–27 school year, it expands advising, academic supports and college- and career-connected experiences across neighborhood high schools.

Henry Ford Health’s RYSE MED program offers another example, providing hands-on opportunities to explore careers in medicine and health care. From 2022 to 2026, the program enrolled 120 high school students, including 45 who attended DPSCD schools.

If businesses want stronger pipelines tomorrow, they cannot wait until students graduate to begin building them. They need to become visible earlier, in classrooms, in after-school programs, in summer internships, in middle schools, through meaningful opportunities for young people to learn by doing.

Detroit’s executives will spend the Forum talking about innovation. The students in the city around them are already hearing plenty. What they need is the chance to try the work, and business can give it to them.

This Ford sculptor has carved cars out of clay for 40 years. AI and VR just cut his work time in half

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

John “Rex” Allen is 68, wears cowboy boots, a black baseball cap, and he’s spent four decades building life-size cars out of clay. These days he builds inside a virtual-reality headset, too.

He does his work upstairs at Ford Motor Company’s gleaming new headquarters in Dearborn, Michigan, where the bird sounds in the yard aren’t even real. Just over four miles away, the 109-year-old Rouge Plant looks much the way it did in the 1920s, except with 3D-printed parts patching the factory together and a “Green Roof” overhead that is one of the world’s largest.

The new Ford World HQ opened in November 2025 as a 21st-century expression of the Ford Rouge logic, according to Ford Land CEO Jim Dobleske. World HQ stacks modern teams vertically the way Henry Ford stacked steel and assembly at the Rouge. It used to take 30 minutes to move a car from one end of the old design center to the other; now it takes “just over three minutes.” The birdsong in the courtyard is piped in, predator calls included, to keep Michigan’s ever-present geese away from workers wolfing down their lunches. “Those are not real birds,” Dobleske said, “that’s all fake.”

Fortune visited Ford in 1947 to document the “rebirth” of the company, when founder Henry Ford died and his grandson, Henry Ford II, was leading the postwar modernization of the iconic American manufacturer. Nearly eight decades later, Detroit as a whole is 13 years on from its historic bankruptcy process, but General Motors was the only member of the “Big Three” auto firms to post a net profit in 2025.

All three OEMs, or original equipment manufacturers, announced major pullbacks in their electric-vehicle strategies in 2025 as the federal government scrapped subsidies, prompting Ford CEO Jim Farley to (accurately) predict that the EV market would be cut in half as a result. In December, Ford announced about $19.5 billion in charges and pivoted to another rebirth: a race against the calendar.

Upstairs at Ford World HQ, Allen is looking at his clay cars, his monitors, his plastic models and his virtual reality glasses.

The Ford modeling studio is massive, with ceilings 25 to 30 feet high and a wide-open, blank space that feels like an empty Hollywood studio, awaiting a Gilmore Girls set or a replica of the Titanic.

Allen, with his thin white mustache complementing his mane of white hair, is doing what he’s always done: building cars out of clay.

Like most Michiganders who still work in auto manufacturing, Allen has an unimpressed air about his remarkable, unusual career. Asked how he joined the ranks of elite auto sculptors, he explains that he felt forced into it by fear of automation. “I used to be a machinist, and then it started becoming robotic, so I got out of that business real quick.” He reasoned that he’d always been a “bit of an artist,” and in high school loved comic books and cars and ran a side hustle airbrushing the “Rat Fink” mascot and other Kustom Kulture motifs onto T-shirts for other kids. Sort of a Mickey Mouse by way of R. Crumb, Rat Fink was a neon-green rodent mascot that exemplified the proto-punk vibes of Southern California car culture of the 1960s, but Allen said his tastes also ran a bit less gonzo — he used to carve Disney characters out of wood and clay for extra credit in art class, along with watercolors and oil paintings for extra credit in art class. “I made good money in high school,” Allen shrugs. “I loved doing that stuff.”

He didn’t set out to become a clay-man, either. “Somebody told me they created cars out of clay,” he explains, “which I had no idea of, so he introduced me to a guy that owned a business. And I mean, I fell right into it.” Allen recalled that he “tried it once” and the results spoke for themselves. He was 26 and soon found himself working 100 hours a week, pulling down roughly $100,000 a year.

Allen wasn’t in-house at Ford, then. At that time, Detroit had a dense web of small model shops that handled overflow work for the Big 3 — Ford, General Motors and Chrysler (now Stellantis), along with their suppliers — and Allen worked at a half-dozen of them. Work was steady. “You could grab a job any day of the week in one of these places,” he said, recalling that he worked on the original concept for the Dodge Viper at a Chrysler satellite studio. He’s now been at Ford for 24 years.

So, why clay? Allen and his colleagues, Nick Navarro, Shawn Fairall and Michael Smith, explained that it’s been that way for nearly a century. “Having the physical property in front of you to have the conversation with multiple people has always been a key part of the process,” Fairall said. “There’s always kind of this general misconception that computers have taken over car design,” Fairall said. “Clay has never really gone away.”

Smith, who leads research and development for Ford’s design team, always describes it to interns the same way. “We don’t sell pretty pictures of cars,” he said. “We sell vehicles.” He said a director might look at the clay and say, “you need to pull this back three more inches.”

It used to take about two weeks for one modeler to sculpt one full-size model, Allen said, and every change meant more weeks of work to do by hand. About six years ago, that changed with virtual reality and 3D printing. Now Allen can knock out a design’s theme in five days.

Allen said he’s seen a lot of upgrades in 40-odd years. First came “sweeps,” templates that modelers could drag along a car’s side. “That was a wow factor there for a while,” Allen said. Then came machines that could map the body, cut pieces out of it, and eventually shape an entire side. Now the team can rough out a car in clay, scan it in about 20 minutes, put on a headset to refine the shape digitally, and have a mill carve out the new surface over a few shifts, then continue working the surface by hand for the finishing touches. They call it hybrid sculpting. “This one is leaps and bounds,” Allen said, “further than anything we’ve ever done.”

“Because of technology, we’ve reduced the people over the years, so there’s less and less of us,” Allen said, adding, “we still have the same amount of work.” He said he thought this “virtual reality way of working would help keep us employed, basically, and it seems like it has, because we’re able to flip more designs now because of it.”

Those saved weeks matter because of what Ford is trying to pull off.

So what is the shape of the new Ford being born? The answer is in four parts. Ford sold about 650,000 fewer vehicles in the U.S. than GM last year, yet brought in slightly more revenue: $187.3 billion to $185 billion. The difference is mainly Ford Pro, the commercial arm that sells trucks and vans to businesses and governments earned $6.8 billion before interest and taxes in 2025 at a 10.3% margin. That’s more than double the earnings of Ford Blue, the gas and hybrid business, which made $3 billion, while the EV unit called Model e, lost $4.8 billion.

Kumar Galhotra, Ford’s chief operating officer, explained to Fortune how Ford’s next plan for Model e started with a skunkworks operation in California. “We tore down multiple Chinese OEM vehicles,” he said. “It was clear that because of the labor advantage and certain subsidies and battery areas, that their batteries are cheaper than ours.” So the team stopped trying to win on battery cost and designed around a smaller battery instead, with cleverer aerodynamics, fewer frictional losses and more efficient motors. “In the end, we’re pretty much equal in total system cost,” he said of Ford’s projection against China’s BYD, calling it “first principles thinking.”

The first vehicle on Ford’s new Universal EV platform is the Fathom, a $28,350 midsize electric pickup due in 2027. Ford says it will be able to send more than 10 kilowatts into a house during a blackout without the expensive wall-mounted inverter the F-150 Lightning needed. Asked how Ford makes money on that, a spokesperson said: “We see it as a compelling reason to buy Fathom.”

The rebirth includes a brand new business, Ford Energy, built to get a return on the billions Ford has sunk into batteries. By around March of 2025, veteran executive Lisa Drake could see that her battery plants just “weren’t full,” but Ford had already licensed lithium-iron-phosphate cells from China’s CATL and was building batteries in Marshall, Michigan. The same chemistry turned out to be close to ideal for energy storage for utilities and power developers racing to meet demand from data centers and an aging grid. “We actually were very fortunate,” Drake told Fortune. “I had a license for the best tech on the planet and the right tech for storage.”

In December, the board gave her $2 billion to start Ford Energy and she is retooling a Kentucky plant that used to make F-150 Lightning batteries, reusing about 30% to 40% of the equipment, with production set to begin in late 2027. The target customers are utilities, renewable energy developers and even data centers and hyperscalers, which don’t buy batteries directly but need power.

It’s part of Ford’s giant race against the calendar — Galhotra said Ford will end up “refreshing about 80% of our North American portfolio” between 2027 and 2029. And that’s where Rex Allen comes in. Every week saved in the clay studio will get to that refresh quicker.

Allen isn’t the only unlikely face of innovation at Ford. Over the river in Dearborn, another wears a giant Luigi-shaped mustache. His name is Greg Foresi.

The Ford Rouge Plant shows its age in a good way — a living museum, the first fully integrated auto factory in the world, still turning out cars in minutes through a century-old assembly line. And walking around, like a friendly engineer beat cop, is Foresi, the 3D-printing guy.

Foresi, who wears the horn-rimmed glasses of a Buddy Holly or a young Elvis Costello, got his signature look when he shaved off his eight-inch beard to play Luigi to a friend’s Mario one Halloween. When “mustache guy” shows up, he takes random requests from nearly everyone on the factory floor. That’s because he manages the 3D-printing lab, where roughly a dozen machines whirr away, steadily adding to nearly 300 tools and components in Foresi’s database, including about 50 individual socket covers.

“I’m out on the floor all day talking to people, trialing tools, evaluating equipment,” said the 35-year-old Foresi. Sometimes he gets five requests in one day, and sometimes nothing for five days. Foresi and his team point me toward a factory-floor wheel that isn’t black like the other ones, but a bright royal blue. This is a specialized part that filled a gap in availability during a supply-chain disruption. When Foresi saw the shortage coming, he printed his own.

Foresi explained that they didn’t have time to test the part. The choice was either trying the blue wheel and potentially creating a line stoppage — or not trying and definitely creating a stoppage. Some versions of the wheel failed, but Foresi said iterative improvements were made. “We avoided the problem entirely,” he said. Galhotra explained that Ford makes a new F-150 every 54 seconds at the plant, making Foresi’s blue wheel a hugely important component, but also not inherently scalable.

“One of the most radical things about 3D printing is that it decentralizes your logistics,” Foresi said. Galhotra put the limits plainly, saying that right now it’s “good for patches here and there,” like when a part is back-ordered or only can be flown in from somewhere else. Foresi agrees: “It’s not remotely close to replacing our conventional supply chain,” he said, adding that he gets “crazy requests” he has to turn down constantly. Some people think 3D printing is a joke, and other people “think that you can just 3D-print anything.”

Foresi had his own doubts at first. After stints at a supplier and a Ford stamping plant in Kansas City, he landed at the Dearborn truck plant as the only mechanical engineer in the final assembly department — ”a pretty intense job,” he said. “I’ve seen everything in the plant break and fixed everything,” Foresi said, recalling a line of people always waiting outside his cubicle. So when his talent was recognized as a fit for 3D printing (his “boss’ boss asked,” he said), his first instinct, like Allen’s, was fear for his job. His first instinct was that the job wouldn’t last, that 3D printing would lose favor and he’d be moved to a new role.

“I was worried that it was just a fad with the company and that it would lose energy and I would get laid off or something,” Foresi said. “They were like, ‘Too bad, you’re doing it anyway.’” Foresi hastened to add that he never felt pushed into taking the job or truly feared that he would be laid off; but he did think he might get reassigned. “After two or three months it became clear both to management and myself that printing was becoming so useful that a full-time position was needed.”

Mike Aragon and Alicia Boler Davis are both newcomers to Ford’s C-suite. The two Amazon veterans said have grown close, Aragon said, as their teams depend on each other and they bond over similar big-tech career transitions. Aragon recalls showing up for a leadership meeting five minutes late and CEO Jim Farley turning to him and saying, “Hey Mike, I want you to take on this AI initiative.” Boler Davis, a Detroiter who spent nearly 25 years working at GM before seven years out of manufacturing, said her return was like “drinking out of a firehose” and that she’s still “relearning the language, learning the culture.”

Boler Davis described the services and efficiencies that come from her use of smart data connected to a nationwide fleet and said she’s working toward a Ford Pro that can predict when a car will need a repair, or provide AI-enhanced advice to customers. As president of Ford Integrated Services, Aragon leads the teams that develop and scale digital products. “This is another reinvention on top of the reinvention that we already had to do,” he told Fortune.

At the Ford Accelerate event in Detroit in late September, Farley described to Fortune during a media briefing how Ford Pro actually uses AI to help dealership technicians, citing the example of an engine removal from a Ford Super Duty truck. “It’s like a two-day job” to take apart a Super Duty, he said, and AI is invaluable in walking customers through the process. “We are using AI for them to say, ‘Okay, do this, do that,’” he said. “They’re good mechanics, they just don’t know. They’ve never done it before.”

Galhotra was more measured about how far Ford has to go. There’s the AI that software companies talk about, on the one hand, and then “AI that’s going to change factories of how we build physical things, and in that second category, I haven’t seen really good execution yet. And even ourselves, we’re at the beginning of that journey.”

Boler Davis said some of Ford Pro’s fleet customers have pushed back. “We have some customers that, you know, don’t want you monitoring the vehicle or connected or don’t want to use the AI,” she said. Stressing that the cost of downtime can be hundreds, even thousands of dollars per vehicle on a given day, she said Ford Pro wants to empower fleet managers to help unlock the potential for their business. The tool isn’t to “get rid of the fleet manager,” she said, but to “make their job easier.”

Part of Ford’s pull for Aragon is personal. He said he remembers being driven to school in his grandfather’s 1963 F-100 truck. “I just remember the smell, which is like the smell of the nylon, and the dust — and then the Camels [cigarettes].” He said he’s planning to rebuild a model from the early ‘70s, which he’s planning to drive to work every day.

He’s not the only one at Ford with a project in the garage. Fairall restores old Volkswagens — he’s finished an ‘84 Westfalia Bus and is working on a ‘65 Split Window Bus. Allen is building his own car on the side, and he’s 3D-printing its front end. Asked whose intellectual property that makes it, Allen immediately responds, “It’s my car. “

Allen once feared the future enough to steer his son away from clay, though. Years ago, he brought his son, Derek, into a satellite studio and paid Derek to work six hours a day for him. “He started heading down this path,” Allen said, “he was getting pretty good at it. But I wanted him to be much more than just a modeler because I was worried about where modeling was going.” He steered him into engineering instead. “I always thought virtual reality would be there, but not in my lifetime,” he said. “I didn’t think I’d be part of it exactly.”

Allen says he thought he was ready to retire eight years ago, but now he’s excited to train the next generation of modelers in both virtual reality and clay. “I’m very excited about this whole chapter in my life,” he said. “I hop out of bed in the morning so I can come to work.”

The 6 Most Hopeless Thriller TV Shows, Ranked

A hopeless thriller is one that keeps you grabbed by the neck but still ends in a painful manner. Someone in a thriller says, “We just need to find the truth,” and I immediately start worrying about them. Because finding the truth is one thing. Surviving what it does to your life is another. I've watched characters sacrifice everything to uncover a secret, only to discover that knowing the answer doesn't give them the slightest power to change it. It's infuriating. It's heartbreaking. And when a show earns that feeling, I cannot look away.

I think helplessness becomes truly frightening when you can understand every decision that leads somebody there. You wouldn't necessarily have behaved differently. You might have trusted the same person, followed the same evidence, or convinced yourself that one more sacrifice would finally put things right. These six thrillers are merciless about that very human tendency.

Sarah Linden (Mireille Enos) is preparing to leave the Seattle Police Department when the disappearance of teenager Rosie Larsen interrupts her plans. She works alongside detective Stephen Holder (Joel Kinnaman) and investigates a murder that tears through Rosie's family and becomes entangled with a political campaign. The Killing makes the investigation painfully personal, particularly for Rosie's parents, Stan (Brent Sexton) and Mitch (Michelle Forbes).

What gets under my skin is how little the truth can actually repair. Mitch can barely function inside the home where her daughter should still be living. Stan's grief keeps twisting into anger, leaving him vulnerable to decisions that could destroy what's left of his family. Meanwhile, Linden's inability to abandon the case damages her relationship with her own son. Everybody wants justice, but nobody can promise that justice will make living any easier. I find that distinction absolutely devastating.

Paul Spector (Jamie Dornan) has a wife, children, and a job as a grief counselor. He also stalks and murders women in Belfast. Detective Superintendent Stella Gibson (Gillian Anderson) arrives to investigate the killings, gradually closing in on a man who appears disturbingly ordinary outside his crimes. The Fall makes no secret of the murderer's identity. We know who he is, and the awful suspense comes from watching him move through a city full of people who don't.

Dornan's most disturbing moments aren't necessarily the violent ones. Sometimes Paul is simply behaving affectionately toward his children, and I want him as far away from them as possible. His domestic life offers camouflage without making his affection straightforwardly false, which is a sickening contradiction. Gibson is formidable, but intelligence and determination cannot undo what his victims have experienced. Even catching him means confronting the lasting damage he's inflicted. This is a thriller where the prospect of victory never feels remotely satisfying enough.

Mr. Robot begins with a thrilling fantasy of rebellion. Elliot Alderson (Rami Malek) works in cybersecurity while secretly hacking people and organizations he believes deserve exposing. When the mysterious Mr. Robot (Christian Slater) recruits him into a movement determined to destroy the financial power of E Corp, Elliot sees an opportunity to strike back at a system he despises. The movie gradually questions whether Elliot understands the consequences of his own actions.

I remember being almost embarrassingly excited about fsociety's ambitions. Then the economic damage starts spreading, ordinary people suffer, and the promised revolution becomes another nightmare. But Elliot's loneliness is even harder to endure. He can access almost anybody's private information while remaining terrified of genuine human intimacy. Sam Esmail, the show's creator, pushes that contradiction to its most painful extreme during Elliot's conversations with therapist Krista. The man determined to expose the world's hidden cruelty has spent years struggling to confront his own suffering. There is compassion here, thankfully, but very little comes without a terrible emotional cost.

Will Graham (Hugh Dancy) possesses a remarkable ability to understand how murderers think, making him invaluable to FBI investigator Jack Crawford (Laurence Fishburne). His increasingly fragile mental state leads him into therapy with Dr. Hannibal Lecter (Mads Mikkelsen), an extraordinarily sophisticated psychiatrist who happens to be a cannibalistic serial killer. Hannibal transforms their relationship into a psychological battle where affection, manipulation, and violence become almost impossible to separate.

I've never hated a fictional friendship quite so passionately. Hannibal recognizes Will's loneliness and offers him the intoxicating possibility of being completely understood. That attention feels almost loving, even as Hannibal deliberately destabilizes the person receiving it. Mikkelsen can turn a quiet dinner invitation into something sinister, while Dancy makes Will's desperate need for connection painfully convincing. The second season's confrontation at Hannibal's home is among the cruelest emotional disasters I've watched on television. What destroys me is that neither man can simply dismiss their attachment afterward. Understanding one another has become another form of imprisonment.

Your nemesis does not want to beat you. He wants to know you completely, and he is patient enough to wait for it. The frightening part is how good the conversation is.

A mysterious graphic novel called The Utopia Experiments draws a group of internet acquaintances into a conspiracy involving a ruthless organization known as the Network. Ian (Nathan Stewart-Jarrett), Becky (Alexandra Roach), Wilson Wilson (Adeel Akhtar), and young Grant (Oliver Woollford) find themselves pursued by killers who will do practically anything to obtain the manuscript. The original British Utopia turns their attempts to survive into an increasingly terrifying struggle against an organization whose influence seems limitless.

“Where is Jessica Hyde?” I swear, those four words become enough to make me nervous. Arby (Neil Maskell) asks the question with horrifying calm, as though killing people is simply an inconvenient part of his workday. The Network's resources are staggering, but the truly sickening part is its conviction that committing atrocities can be justified as serving humanity's future. Dennis Kelly's writing refuses to offer the comfort of uncomplicated heroes either. Survival gradually demands compromises that make the protagonists resemble the people they're fighting. The show was canceled after two seasons, leaving its extraordinary atmosphere of dread hanging over an unfinished story. Somehow, that feels appropriately cruel.

A young boy disappears in the German town of Winden, leading several families toward a mystery involving a network of caves, disturbing family secrets, and events separated by decades. Jonas Kahnwald (Louis Hofmann) becomes central to the investigation as Dark gradually establishes a world where time travel connects generations of people who barely understand the consequences of their own decisions.

Ulrich Nielsen (Oliver Masucci) is the character who really breaks my heart. His desperation to find his missing son pushes him toward an act he believes could prevent decades of suffering. Watching that certainty turn into something terrible is almost unbearable. But the show's bleakest idea extends far beyond one father's mistake. Parents damage children they haven't met yet. Lovers become trapped by histories they don't understand. Attempts to prevent catastrophes can help bring them about. ‘Facing The Numbers:’ Kieran Satour & Tyson Perkins On Telling An Authentic First Nations Story With Their Breakout Hybrid Feature

Facing The Numbers by Kieran Satour and Tyson Perkins is one of the most unique propositions to come out of Australia in some time.

Described as a “social impact campaign and film project,” the feature-length documentary explores the significant disadvantages First Nations people experience in Australia’s legal system.

Recent data show that First Nations people experience disadvantages across all areas of Aussie life, including child protection, justice, health, housing and education. The data is stark, but as the doc’s title suggests, Satour and Perkins are interested in more than just listing these figures. Watch on Deadline

“The statistics tell one story. We tell another,” the film’s opening frame reads.

Structured as a six-part anthology, Facing The Numbers explores these disparities through the real-life stories of First Nations people using a bold mix of dance, poetry, and spoken word.

Satour and Perkins wrote and directed the film together. Perkins also served as the DoP. Satour produced alongside Ramona Telecican. The film debuted earlier this year at the Melbourne Film Festival before screening across the Aussie circuit and premiering at the Sydney Opera House. The doc will screen on NITV in Australia on November 15 and will later be repackaged for distribution on social platforms.

Below, Satour and Perkins speak with Deadline about how they developed the anthology film and their bold plans for its release.

DEADLINE: This film has a very unique structure. How did you develop this approach to the material?

TYSON PERKINS: This kind of subject matter is often portrayed in a very academic way on film. It tends to take the form of talking-head interviews and fairly basic re-creations, which don’t get close to the emotional point of view—or the emotional suffering—that many victims of police brutality go through. We started with that as our aim, and this little, unusual thing came out of it.

DEADLINE: I read this started as a short project and then expanded. Is that right?

PERKINS: It was as much about treating the subject matter in a fresh way as it was about using a fresh format. For us, that format was short-form and shareable online. It came as a response to the Black Lives Matter movement. We were watching what was happening in America from Australia and the artistic responses that came out of it—not the news coverage, but the things that appeared on our Instagram feeds. It prompted Kieran and me to think about Australia. Some of our statistics on incarceration, youth incarceration, male suicide, and deaths in custody are among the worst in the world. We wanted to take some of those subjects and statistics and frame them through the short-form, viral storytelling many African American artists in the U.S. were using. By “viral,” we didn’t necessarily mean that it had to go viral; we meant something short, shareable, and to the point.

As happens with films, once we delved into the individual subjects and the stories, they expanded. We realised there was a way to tell the story of this country—its institutional racism, its history of oppression and its ongoing oppression—through these personal, first-hand stories. We thought we had six separate subject films, but when we put them together, they formed a truthful picture of what Aboriginal people face in this country.

DEADLINE: You incorporate several different art forms in the film. There’s dance, spoken word. But it feels seamless. How did you get it to work?

KIERAN SATOUR: One of the amazing things about Facing the Numbers is that so many First Nations artists came together to tell this story. The spoken word, poetry, and dancing feel seamless because everyone is telling the same story. We’re telling multiple separate stories, but they represent a collective Aboriginal experience. Tyson and I were lucky to work with all these incredible creatives.

DEADLINE: How have people been responding to the film, especially in Australia?

SATOUR: People have been responding really well. We premiered at the Melbourne International Film Festival and had a great turnout. About 75% of tickets were sold for both screenings. We had standing ovations, and the Letterboxd reviews came in almost immediately after the first screening. Nothing was under four stars, and the reviews were glowing. We screened at the Darwin International Film Festival. My sister lives in Darwin. She’s also a co-founder of the production company and an executive producer on the film. She told me that people have been stopping her in Darwin to talk about Facing the Numbers because they’d been at the screening. It’s been amazing.

DEADLINE: Where did you find your participants?

SATOUR: During the global movement after George Floyd’s murder, people were sharing their stories online, and we found some of these stories through social media. Geoffrey Johnson is one example. Otherwise, we were doing research and development, trying to find stories that spoke to this collective experience. It came down to meetings and phone calls.

PERKINS: It’s important to mention that it would not be difficult to find thousands of stories like these. Thousands of Aboriginal people have direct descendants who were taken during the Stolen Generations or sent to schools like Kinchela Boys’ Home. Probably every Aboriginal family knows someone who has been a victim of police brutality, whether in their own family or a family they know. The stories we chose are told by extremely courageous people. It takes strength to repeat your story on camera, allow it to be filmed, and make it public. But one of the key messages of the film is that these experiences are extraordinarily common—in people’s lives and statistically.

DEADLINE: How did you build this production? I can’t imagine the Australian public funding system was jumping to invest in something like this. Or maybe I’m wrong.

SATOUR: It was funded through a digital-series, online-production fund at Screen Australia, our national public screen-funding body. That fund doesn’t even exist anymore. The project was never meant to be a feature film. We made the anthology into a feature partly because that helped us unlock extra funding and become eligible for a significant tax offset. Production went through a few phases. At the end of the first phase, we had two strong rough cuts, which we used to secure more funding. We received a little more from the national broadcasting funding body, then a broadcaster attached through a pre-sale, and we brought in philanthropic support. It was a struggle, but we got there. A film like this doesn’t get made without the goodwill of philanthropists, but also without all the work everyone put into those first two rough cuts. They were strong, and I think the project sold itself from there.

DEADLINE: How will it be distributed? I know you’re screening at the Sydney Opera House, which is wonderful. Do you have plans for international screenings? In the UK and U.S., people involved in solidarity movements would be intrigued to hear from you and see a film like this.

SATOUR: Totally. In terms of craft, we were inspired by global Black filmmakers. Steve McQueen’s Small Axe was a huge reference for us, as was Spike Lee. We both admire the American director Khalil Joseph. We’re connecting with global Black and brown movements because we’re facing similar issues everywhere: over-policing, incarceration, and child removal. We’re still waiting to find out where the international premiere will be. In Australia, we’re doing a film-festival run, and then the film will be broadcast on NITV, the National Indigenous Television Network, which sits under SBS, our multicultural public broadcaster. The digital release will follow once the holdbacks for the national TV broadcast are over. The films will also be released online through a microsite.

A key partner is Common Ground, an Aboriginal-run and Aboriginal-led organisation focused on education. They’re running our impact campaign. The organisation’s founder, Rona Glynn-McDonald, is Warwick Thornton’s daughter, so filmmaking is in her blood. We’re excited to get these stories out digitally and reach younger people who may not watch television or attend film festivals, and to connect with an audience that isn’t already converted.

PERKINS: For me, the online release is what I’m most excited about. In film and television now, you see people who have built audiences on YouTube or Instagram bringing those audiences into more traditional film and TV. The ideal outcome would be for some of the storytellers to experience justice—whether for a family member’s death or, in Uncle Widdy’s case, by having more of the public learn what happened to him at Kinchela Boys’ Home. Not many people know that thousands of children went through that school or what happened to them. The survivors are trying to reclaim the land where the school stood. Once the films have been shared and completed their festival runs, I hope some of those outcomes can begin to happen. I’m really excited about that.
