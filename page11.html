Donovan Mitchell needs to shoot more, not less

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If you've been online recently, you may have noticed that Donovan Mitchell has… pissed off a lot of Cleveland Cavaliers fans.

Twitter is ablaze with criticism. People want him traded. Some regret that he was ever traded for. Mitchell has become the crux of all problems; the shot-chucking 'dictator' who will never be the primary option on a championship team.

But everyone has it backwards; Donovan Mitchell needs to shoot more.

Mitchell's field goal attempts and usage rate are at career lows to start the year.

His assist percentage and shot chart are nearly identical to previous seasons.

That means Mitchell has less control over the offense than ever, despite some insisting that he's grown into a ball-dominant tyrant.

How did we get here? And why are fans feeling such a disconnect?

Mitchell has always been a scorer more than a passer. He currently ranks in the 41st percentile in assist-to-usage ratio — a stat that tracks how often a player is ending their possessions with an assist rather than a shot attempt or turnover.

This means that roughly half of the league's players are more likely to get an assist on any given possession than Donovan Mitchell. That can lead to games where it feels like Mitchell is hogging the ball while leaving his teammates hanging.

That gung-ho play style caused some frustration throughout last year's playoffs, and the complaints have carried into this year.

Fans want to know why Evan Mobley and Jarrett Allen aren't getting the ball. Why do their leads disappear so often? And why haven't the Cavs gotten over the hump despite a roaring regular season?

Some have identified Mitchell's lack of playmaking as the problem.

Which, in fairness, is certainly a factor. Cleveland has lost big games in the past due to Mitchell's shot selection. He doesn't always make the right decision with the ball in his hands — and that can be difficult to watch as a fan.

But we should reframe this discussion. Let's start here.

Donovan Mitchell isn't a point guard. And maybe, he doesn't have to be.

Shai-Gilgeous Alexander was in the 29th percentile for assist-to-usage ratio when the Oklahoma City Thunder won the championship. He's in the 0th percentile now.

The truth is, championship-caliber players are allowed to have weak points. You are not disqualified if you can't do everything on your own.

Giannis Antetokounmpo never learned how to shoot. Nikola Jokic never became an elite defender. And the New York Knicks didn't win a championship last June because Jalen Brunson suddenly became an elite playmaker — they won because they delegated more playmaking responsibilities to Karl-Anthony Towns — leaving space for Brunson to do what Brunson does best… score the ball.

All of those teams identified their star's weakness, and built a roster around them that could insulate them.

The Cavs need a similar solution. They need someone who can take playmaking off of Mitchell's plate.

Darius Garland was the original fit. And, for a while, he would thread the needle beautifully. He sent the defense scattering into motion while Mitchell would catch the ball against a defense already on their back feet. That dynamic led to 64 wins — but unfortunately, Garland couldn't stay healthy.

James Harden was the next option. He's been more available than Garland, which was enough to help bolster the team to an Eastern Conference Finals run.

But Harden's style differs from Garland's. He's slower-paced, favoring a heliocentric style that inhibits stationary spacing around him. The result is fewer chances for Mitchell to attack in motion, nerfing his greatest strength as a slasher who can attack gaps.

All of this has culminated in the Cavs (and fans online) asking Mitchell to be more of a playmaker. But that is a fundamental misunderstanding of who he is — and how you win in the NBA by playing to your strengths.

I want to be clear that I am not advocating for Mitchell to go full Dion Waiters and ignore the other four Cavaliers sharing the court with him at any given time. He still needs to pass the ball — and he needs to choose the right moments to assert himself as a scorer.

You can't win by playing like a bull in a China shop. There has to be a delicate balance between scoring in bunches and releasing control of the offense to someone else. That's the key here.

Mitchell will never be a point guard. Any system that asks him to make reads at that level will learn the hard way. You can't force a square peg into a round hole.

You can, however, build a competitive team that understands who Mitchell is.

We know for a fact that Mitchell can go nuclear as a scorer in the playoffs. We've seen it multiple times.

But who can grab the reins and facilitate those high-scoring performances? Who is the Karl-Anthony Towns to his Jalen Brunson in this scenario?

The Cavs are still hoping that will be Harden. Additional support from Mobley would be welcomed. Whether that will be enough is certainly worth questioning.

But here's another cold, hard truth: Making it to the top of the mountain is hard. If we're being honest, it's damn near impossible. This franchise had the greatest player alive for 11 seasons and only made it to the finish line once.

Cleveland would need a myriad of miracles to win a championship with this group. Even if everything went right, they could still fall short. That's just the way it goes.

That's why sticking true to your identity is more important than anything. Being a bad version of someone else will always be inferior to being the best version of yourself.

I don't think Mitchell will lead Cleveland to a title through his playmaking. That feels like jumping the shark.

The Cavs do need him to be better defensively and to make better reads offensively, but that doesn't necessarily mean relinquishing his role as one of the highest-volume scorers in the league. That's who he is — that's who the team traded for.

Mitchell needs to be a world-class scorer. Nearly all of his attention on offense should be centered on getting his shot. Passing will always come second. Winning or losing comes down to Mitchell taking and making the big shots. That was the primary difference between him and Brunson last season.

Of course, any team would prefer a superstar who is capable of blending elite scoring with masterful playmaking and impactful defense. But, you work with what you have — and the Cavs have a dynamic scorer, not a floor general.

Accepting Mitchell's true nature and having the supporting cast insulate him with their playmaking is the most realistic path forward. It's the only path forward.

3 Coziest Movies to Watch on Netflix This Weekend (November 7-8)

We're late halfway through the Fall season, and with Halloween behind us, it's time to look ahead at what's coming. Right now, we're at that weird, in-between time before the winter holidays take over our cultural landscape. Normally, these few weeks are the perfect time to bask in the homey feeling of Fall, and there's no better way to do that than with a great, cozy movie.

When one thinks of cozy movies, usually genres like mystery, romantic comedy, fantasy, or animation come to mind. Luckily for you, Netflix has at least one major movie in each of those genres that would be the perfect choice for you to enjoy on a weekend at home. Here, we offer you the three best cozy movies you can watch on Netflix this weekend, ranging from a colorful and delightful animated adventure to a good, old-fashioned whodunit.

Let's start with a delightful mix of rom-com, mystery, and historical drama that deserves far more credit than it usually gets. Lily James stars in The Guernsey Literary and Potato Peel Pie Society as Juliet Ashton, a writer who travels to the island of Guernsey, which endured a harsh German occupation during World War II. There, she learns about the titular society and uncovers a mystery about the society's founder.

Benefitting from an incredibly charming ensemble cast that also includes Michiel Huisman, Penelope Wilton, Tom Courtenay, and Glen Powell, the film rises on the back of an intimate, heartfelt screenplay and Mike Newell's deft direction. James is winning as the lead, sharing sweet chemistry with Huisman and driving the narrative with the perfect balance of spunky determination and genuine emotion. There's a real sense of romantic warmth in this small but quirky island that makes the movie all the more entertaining and easy to revisit. Come for the romance and stay for the actually intriguing mystery and the sense of community between the oddball islanders.

What can possibly be cozier than a Wallace and Gromit adventure? Wallace & Gromit: Vengeance Most Fowl sees the intelligent and reliable dog Gromit growing concerned that his well-natured owner, Wallace (Ben Whitehead), has become overly dependent on the robotic garden gnome Norbot. Things take a turn when the criminal penguin mastermind Feathers McGraw steals Norbot to frame Wallace for a series of crimes, including the theft of the iconic Blue Diamond.

The film has a 100% rating on Rotten Tomatoes, and that perfect score is more than deserved. Wallace & Gromit: Vengeance Most Fowl is an enchanting claymation adventure that soars on the back of the dynamic between the two titular and universally beloved characters: one scene with Wallace and Gromit is more fun, nuanced, and lovely than most other movies out there. There's a lot of creativity on display, from the charming visuals to the clever, dry, incredibly British humor. The plot moves at a fast pace, the jokes land without fail, and the warm tone makes the whole thing endlessly enjoyable. It's one of those movies that feels like a literal warm hug, one that won't fail at bringing a smile to your face.

Any movie led by the talents of Celia Imrie, Sir Ben Kingsley, Dame Helen Mirren, and Pierce Brosnan is one that's worth seeing, especially if they're playing amateur sleuths. That's exactly what The Thursday Murder Club is about, based on the eponymous novel. The plot centers on four retirees at an English retirement village who enjoy theorizing about cold cases. They get their chance to level up when a murder happens right under their noses.

Stories about amateur sleuths proving their talent for actual crime-solving are a dime a dozen, but rarely are they more delightful. The Thursday Murder Club is a love letter to the whodunit genre with a nostalgic approach that makes it perfect for a quiet afternoon in. The plot is engaging but not particularly demanding, and the four incredible actors at the center are having the time of their lives. The result is a classic British movie that perfectly balances mystery with gentle humor and plenty of emotional moments. It doesn't really have many thrills, but honestly, it doesn't need them: in fact, the film is at its best when its four legendary protagonists are engaging with each other.

Rose Lavelle Suits Up to Build Gotham FC’s Future

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Committed to the reigning NWSL champions for three more seasons, the soccer star reflects on leadership, life lessons, and a monumental move to a shiny new stadium in New York City.

Rose Lavelle is breaking down her outfit of the day, though it’s not the ensemble you’d expect from a professional athlete’s social media feed.

“My hard hat, which is… something,” the Gotham FC midfielder says with a chuckle. She looks down to survey the rest of her mandatory construction-site-chic attire. “My gloves—gardener’s. My boots—too big. My vest—cool.”

It’s a Tuesday afternoon in September, and Rose is the first of her teammates to tour the unfinished Etihad Park in Queens, New York, the stadium that Gotham will call home in 2028. Earlier that day, Gotham announced that Rose had signed a contract extension through 2029. In other words, she'll be back when this concrete floor is an actual soccer pitch.

We weave past steel beams and through what will eventually become Gotham's tunnel to the pitch. From there, the grandstands rise around us, a preview of the 25,000-seat venue that will soon welcome Gotham's fans. Rose poses for photos, holding a jersey that says “2029.”

A few days later, Gotham’s president of soccer operations, Yael Averbuch West, tells me she smiled when she saw the images of Rose in her hard hat. The look may be a departure from her usual uniform, but Averbuch West sees something fitting about it. As Gotham prepares to make another postseason run in pursuit of defending their 2025 National Women’s Soccer League title, Rose has become central to the club's plans.

“She has been part of literally constructing this vision with us,” Averbuch West says. “She has been an instrumental part of the vision of the club and our aspirations and our ambition.”

The roughly $780 million Etihad Park is being developed by Major League Soccer's New York City FC, which will share the stadium with Gotham beginning in 2028. It's a significant upgrade from Gotham's current arrangement at Sports Illustrated Stadium in Harrison, New Jersey, where the team uses the visitors' locker room and covers up the New York Red Bulls’ logos with its own banners. At Etihad Park, Gotham will have a locker room of its own, merchandise space in the team store, and digital signage and video boards that can be fully branded for Gotham on match days—no covering up another team's logos required.

The move is another sign of just how much women's professional soccer has changed since Rose entered the NWSL nearly a decade ago. And in that time, Rose has changed, too. At 31, the once up-and-coming star has become a veteran helping set the standard for a club that has no plans to slow down.

The NWSL is not the same league it was when Rose was drafted first overall by the now-defunct Boston Breakers in 2017, playing home games at a 2,500-seat facility at Harvard University. When I sit down with Rose after our stadium tour, she tells me it’s difficult to compare the things that mattered to her as a rookie to signing a contract extension now. “I think of the amount of support staff we had, it was so few—how many people were dedicated towards ticketing and marketing and all of that,” she says. “I notice that stuff a lot more now than I probably did at the time, because you don't know what you don't know.”

Rose has come a long way since then. She won the 2019 World Cup with the U.S. Women’s National Team, earned Olympic gold in Paris in 2024, and navigated injuries and moves between NWSL clubs. She joined Gotham in 2024 and, after ankle surgery sidelined her for the start of her second season, returned to help the club win the 2025 NWSL championship, scoring the winning goal. In January, she was named U.S. Soccer's 2025 Female Player of the Year.

On the field, Rose’s influence isn't always captured in goals or assists. Gotham rookie Andrea Kitahata calls her the club’s “quiet momentum-builder and tone-setter” for how she initiates the team's defensive press and creates attacking opportunities that may not show up on a stat sheet.

Rose has learned plenty from USWNT veterans like Megan Rapinoe, Becky Sauerbrunn, Alex Morgan, Christen Press, and Tobin Heath, who showed her that there was no single way to command respect in a locker room. “Everybody led in their own way,” Rose says. “Everybody's leadership style might have looked different, but it wasn't any more or less impactful than the other.”

Now, the younger players in Gotham’s locker room are learning from Rose—especially since her 2025 ankle surgery, the first of her career, which forced her to find new ways to contribute off the field.

She initially thought surgery would be a straightforward fix. “I think I thought you get surgery to fix it, and when you're off the table, you’re fixed, and it's smooth sailing. And it was not smooth sailing at all,” she says. She pauses. “Yeah, it was really painful! It was really painful.”

The physical recovery was only part of the challenge. Rose says the mental toll of rehabbing—and the uncertainty that came with it—tested her in ways she hadn't anticipated. “It taught me I had a whole ’nother level of mental grind and fortitude that I could battle through,” she says. “I think you learn the most about yourself in the tough moments. I mean, it was hard—but I made it through, and I feel like I gained so much.”

Rose has never been the loudest voice in the locker room, but recent years have taught her that she doesn’t need to be.

Ahead of the 2025 season, Rose told Averbuch West that she would be more intentional about creating the change she wanted to see within the club. “When you're Rose Lavelle, you are a leader, whether you choose to be or not,” Averbuch West says. “It doesn't have to come in big speeches in front of the group. It’s not going to be that. But it's the little things she does behind the scenes, the little connections with players—pulling someone aside, talking on the field.”

Her teammates take cues from her, Averbuch West says. If Gotham is down a goal at halftime, they look to Rose to gauge how she's responding. If Rose is inspired, they’re inspired. And Rose has become increasingly intentional about the example she sets. “She's been very proactive during her time here to share really constructive feedback with us, and to also be part of creating a locker room culture that I think the players at this point are really enjoying,” Averbuch West says.

“Obviously, we had some cheaters in there,” Kitahata jokes. “Rose made sure that she held everyone accountable, made sure no one's looking it up on their phone.”

And Rose isn’t exactly a quiet participant. “She's in the meal room screaming out,” Kitahata recalls, imitating Rose’s clues: “Blank, blank, blank, blank. It's four words. Figure it out, guys!”

She’s equally serious about her teammates’ recovery. During a July road trip to Portland, Rose took Kitahata, still navigating her first professional season, along for a day of mental and physical recovery. They got coffee, browsed the shelves at Powell's Books, and headed to a sauna and cold plunge. A stickler for her recovery routine, Rose insisted they stay submerged up to their necks in the icy water for four minutes. “I'm like, ‘Rose. Rose, I'm freezing!’” Kitahata recalls. But Rose wouldn't budge. “I love that she always holds me accountable for my recovery.” They finished the day with sushi.

Rose is the teammate you want to run into when you’re having a bad day, because she notices, Kitahata says. “Even with a look, she's like, ‘Oh my god, what's wrong? Do we need to go get coffee?’ … It’s very rare to find someone of her stardom that is just so willing to give to the team.”

Rose has become more conscious of how much she'll miss these moments someday. “I'm such a painfully nostalgic person,” she says. “It's like, debilitating sometimes, honestly.”

It's the everyday moments with her teammates that seem to stick with her most. “I was thinking the other day, having moments in the locker room where we have this shared playlist that we're playing, and we're all dancing to it. And I was like, ‘How many grown adults get to dance with their friends in the locker room?’” she says. “It’s little things like that that it's so easy to take for granted, because we get to do it literally every single day, but something that I think is just so special.”

It's also clear how much she values the life she's built at Gotham, and how much she wants to keep being part of it. “I love my life off-field. I love my life on-field. I think we have such an incredible group of players and staff that we get to work with and learn from each day,” she says. “I just felt so content, but also challenged in a way that I felt like I was going to be happily growing.”

For Gotham, the move to Queens isn't just about having a place to call its own. It's also an opportunity to reach a bigger audience. Despite winning two NWSL championships in three seasons, Gotham has struggled to fill the stands in New Jersey consistently. But when the club hosted the Washington Spirit at Citi Field, home of the New York Mets, for its Queens Classic in July, the sellout crowd of 42,175 offered a glimpse of what might be possible across the street at Etihad Park.

The move isn't without its complications. Gotham has deep roots in New Jersey, and some longtime supporters are disappointed to see the club leave. “I was a little sad at first that the team was moving,” says Lindsey Smith, 37, a New Jersey resident and five-year season ticket holder. “But this is so much bigger than a location to me. As someone who wants to make women's sports accessible, I'm very excited for Gotham to move to Queens.”

Smith’s friend Krista Tonnesen, 37, has been attending Gotham matches regularly since 2021. An Astoria resident, that has meant long trips home after night games, sometimes with 40-minute waits between PATH trains. She's thrilled to have Gotham in her borough, and hopeful that the shorter trip will bring more fans into the stands. “The players and the team deserve better,” she says. “I really think moving to New York will turn that around.”

Rose says the atmosphere at the Queens Classic, where she scored the game's only goal, left an impression on her. “To think that we could be playing in front of a crowd and atmosphere like that, every single home game, I think it'll just add that much more to what we're building,” she says.

Back at Etihad Park, as we make our way through the unfinished concrete hallways, I ask Rose if she can picture what it'll be like when Gotham finally takes the field here. “I see the vision,” she says.

Earth's churning mantle may have created the Great Unconformity

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Explaining the Great Unconformity, where Cambrian deposits sit on top of older rocks.

In the Grand Canyon, there is a line in the rock where time seems to have gone missing. Ancient crystalline rocks mark its bottom, while sandstones laid down as life started to diversify during the Cambrian explosion mark the top. “It’s called the Great Unconformity, and it’s a gap in the geologic record,” says Nicolas Flament, a geoscientist at the University of Wollongong, Australia. “This gap can be anywhere between 200 million years, which is not that impressive, to more than a billion years.”

In a recent Nature study, Flament and his colleague Santosh Dhungana argue that this gap was carved with help from deep within the planet. According to their models, slow currents of hot rock in Earth’s mantle pushed entire continents up for hundreds of millions of years, letting glaciers grind them down and then let them sink, allowing fresh sediments to bury the scar. The largest hole in the rock record might be a fingerprint of processes happening deep in the planet’s interior.

The oldest sediments resting on the Great Unconformity in North America are between 635 and 482 million years old. Geologists have traced it across most of North America and parts of northern Europe and northern China. “Something seems to have happened, in particular in North America,” Flament says.

The leading explanation was glaciation. Between roughly 720 and 635 million years ago, glaciers reached the tropics, and some scientists think the entire planet froze over, a scenario called Snowball Earth. “Ice sheets are very good at grinding, scouring, scraping the surface,” Flament explains. He said that glaciers are about 10 times more effective than rivers at eroding rock away. But if the whole planet froze, the Great Unconformity should be found everywhere. And it’s not.

Other explanations included rifting, pulling and stretching of Earth’s crust, and a global drop in sea levels. But rifting acts mostly on the edges of continents, and a sea level change should have affected all continents alike. “There’s no doubt that there was extensive glaciation. There’s no doubt that there have been sea-level changes throughout time. The question is, was that enough to put the Great Unconformity puzzle together?” Flament says.

A fourth idea held that the mantle itself was moving continents up and down, a process called dynamic topography. “It had been proposed, but it hadn’t been quantitatively tested,” Flament says. So, his team set out to do so.

From roughly 100 to 200 kilometers beneath the surface down to nearly 3,000 kilometers, Earth’s mantle slowly convects. “An analogy would be a lava lamp, where some stuff comes up and some stuff comes down,” Flament explains. “Cold rock sinks and hot rock rises.”

The mantle is solid, yet a piece of rock can slowly travel from the surface to the deep Earth over some 200 million years. That motion tugs on the surface. Where cold slabs of ocean floor sink into the mantle at subduction zones, the ground gets pulled down; where hot mantle rises, it gets pushed up. The amplitude of this up and down motion is still debated, though. “The short of it is several hundreds of meters, and it’s still to be worked out exactly what that [precise value] is,” Flament says.

Dhungana and Flament ran six simulations of mantle convection going back 1 billion years, driven by reconstructions of how tectonic plates moved over that time. Then they grouped regions where the ground rose and fell in similar ways to find where the unconformity should have formed, and where it should have survived to the present. Flament, however, says the starting configuration of these simulations remains uncertain.

Magnetic records in ancient rocks reveal how far continents were from the equator, but not their longitude. “It’s possible that some continents may be in the wrong hemisphere,” Flament admits. The ancient ocean floor, long since recycled into the mantle, had to be invented entirely from scratch in the models. Still, the simulations produced a consistent story.

Laurentia, the ancient core of North America, had little or no subduction along its margins between about 930 and 510 million years ago, much like Africa today, so it sat above some rising mantle and was pushed up. Five of the six models showed this pattern of Laurentia seeing long uplift followed by sinking in the Cambrian. For northern Europe and North China, this pattern showed up in four.

But the uplift alone, Flament’s team concluded, was too gentle to strip away enough rock to create the Great Unconformity.

“The uplift could create a tilt for the whole continent, but it’s not great at creating a steep slope,” Flament says. The missing piece of the puzzle, he argues, were glaciers that ground through rock even on low ground and gentle slopes. The models show the uplift was still building when Snowball Earth hit. “This gentle uplift that’s continuous, and then that glacial erosion that comes on top, work together,” Flament explains. “They’re not coupled. They just happen to coincide in time.” When the continents sank in the Cambrian, seas flooded in and buried the eroded surface.

The models made by Flament’s team also explain why the gap is so obvious in North America but hard to find elsewhere. Compared with records of sediment preserved on each continent, North America and Eurasia show one long rise followed by one long fall, which the researchers interpret as the ideal recipe for the Great Unconformity.

South America and Australia, on the other hand, went through two cycles. “This would form potentially two unconformities, and then the Great Unconformity would be more difficult to see,” Flament says. Africa was a different case altogether—hemmed in by mountain belts at the time, its erosion and sediment were shaped by the mountains rather than by the mantle beneath.

But if the mantle goes through such up and down cycles on a regular basis, the remaining question was why we can see only one Great Unconformity, and not many.

The team says the Great Unconformity is unique in the geological record because of a combination of a rise-and-fall cycle lasting over 900 million years, a near-global glaciation that happened at the right moment, and rock records that get harder to read farther back in time.

Flament suspects there may be an older candidate, tied to a glaciation 2.4 billion years ago, but rocks that old lack the fossils that make younger sediments easier to date. “Having life in sediments makes it a lot easier to have a sense of time,” he says. His work, however, may help explain what happens right after the Great Unconformity formed.

The intense erosion that carved the Great Unconformity washed rock material into the oceans, possibly fertilizing them just before the Cambrian explosion, when calcium became abundant in seawater. “That’s when life learns to mobilize calcium and create skeletons and shells,” Flament says. He admits, though, that mantle dynamics likely wasn’t the only process that enabled the Cambrian explosion. “I think it would be misleading to say the mantle set the pace here. But I think it was one of the forces that worked together.”

Most of us have experienced what it’s like to struggle to pay attention to something. I’ll offer a recent example of my own.

My mind wandered further and further from the focus of the long academic meeting I was attending. I looked around at the others in the room. They seemed to be listening—or at least staring at the screen.

We were in a plush venue just off campus, the sort we often hire for these long meetings. Rain pounded on the glass roof. Beyond it, the grounds were lush and green.

A young waiter, about the same age as my son, clinked some glasses, replenished the water, juice and coffee, and left a tray of small chocolate brownies and fresh fruit at the back of the room.

I remember how I wanted one of the brownies and some plump green grapes, still shining with moisture. But walking to the back and helping myself felt rude. Everyone else seemed to be managing to pay attention. Weren’t they?

It felt as though I had lost all ability to focus.

But my attention hadn’t disappeared. I could focus on the rain, the trees, the grapes and the brownies. I could attend to them effortlessly. But I couldn’t keep my mind on the sixth presentation of the day.

I tried to care about what was being said. I really wanted to. But I simply couldn’t.

Something inside me had stopped cooperating. I’d reached a point of attentional saturation. It was a little like realising you’re full and that each extra mouthful feels forced.

I longed to get up, walk outside, or breathe fresh air. I wanted to think about something other than the presentation. I felt as though I was dying of boredom.

I felt resentful that I was expected to keep listening, but also guilty that I couldn’t find the attention the speaker seemed to deserve. What was wrong with me? Why was I so inconsiderate?

Perhaps you can remember similar experiences. Encountering situations in which our capacity to ‘pay attention’ is stretched is so often part of modern life.

I wondered what it reflected about me. Was I bored? Had the subject failed to interest me or feel important enough? Was it about motivation? Was I tired? Had I simply reached the limit of what I could sustain that day? Or was I reacting to an expectation that I should or must keep listening?

The idea that something can be ‘wrong’ with our ‘attention’ is now woven into the cultures and institutions in which we live. But psychologists don’t really use the term attention to describe one clearly defined ability.

In a 2019 paper, psychologists Bernhard Hommel and colleagues describe the common idea of attention as the mechanisms that help us make efficient use of our limited capacity to process, store and retrieve information. That sounds straightforward.

These are all capacities that might be (and often are) referred to when someone uses the word ‘attention.’ However, Hommel and colleagues argue that it is highly unlikely that the same set of functional or neural mechanisms would be involved in and responsible for each of these. They also caution that dividing attention into neat, separate functions can obscure what those functions have in common too.

They’re all examples of "attention," but they might not share the same underpinning anatomical or neurological roots. This matters more than you might think. Their argument has implications beyond the lab.

For example, if attention describes different things, what are we saying when we tell someone to “pay attention”? Furthermore, what are we referring to when we say that someone has an “attention deficit”?

What’s more, when someone struggles to stay with a task, the difficulty may indeed involve attention. But it may also involve motivation, intention or emotion. Perhaps the task feels pointless, the person doesn’t want to do it, or they are bored. The label 'attention' alone doesn’t tell us which of these is at play in a situation.

Psychological labels deserve care and scrutiny, no matter how deeply entrenched they might be. A label can help us recognise a pattern. But it can frequently give us the impression that we understand more than we do.

The authors of the paper above ultimately concluded that we’d be better to drop the term 'attention' as a label for a specific functional or neural system, because, they argue, it doesn’t actually identify a single functional or neural system. This is significant.

If attention were no longer treated as the label for a single mental or neural system, what would we make of things like Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)? The name itself seems to point to a deficit in a particular capacity we call 'attention.' But if there is no single system called attention, then what, precisely, is deficient in something like ADHD?

This doesn’t necessarily make ADHD imaginary, or the difficulties people experience less real. But it does make the label seem less like an explanation than it first appears. Perhaps it names a recognisable pattern of difficulty, while leaving the processes, circumstances and histories that give rise to it in any one person wide open.
