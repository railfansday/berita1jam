Malaysian War Drama ‘The Ghost and the Gun’ Sets Global Netflix Release (EXCLUSIVE)

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Netflix will stream “The Ghost and the Gun” (“Peluru Senja“), a Bahasa Malaysia-language war drama set in Malaya in the years after World War II, around the world.

The film is the third feature from Indian filmmaker Anshul Tiwari, who is based in Singapore and previously directed “Before Life After Death” and “Bayaar.” Abhi Kumar wrote the screenplay.

The story unfolds in 1948. When British forces pull out of Malaya, two soldiers are left behind – one Malay, one British. The pair have to come to terms with their pasts in the war while defending a village targeted by armed rebels. “While movies have explored World War II from every Western angle, the human cost paid across Malaya and Singapore remains largely unseen,” Tiwari said. “I was drawn to this story because the volatility of the Malayan Emergency feels painfully urgent today: the timeless tragedy of ordinary people caught in the crossfire of empires fighting for power.”

Malaysian actor Fabian Loo, an award winner known for “Rain Town” and “One Cent Thief,” heads the ensemble. He is joined by Hazman Al-Idrus, Asmara Abigail (“Impetigore,” “Stone Turtle”) and American actor Brett Tutor (“Monsters of Man”).

“Siti carries the weight of a whole region’s history in a very quiet way, and playing her in the Pahang dialect pushed me somewhere new,” Abigail said of her character. “I’m proud that such a Southeast Asian story can now reach audiences everywhere.”

The producers planned the film as an international collaboration from day one. Shooting took place at Viking Sunset Studios in Bali, where 14 sets were built to stand in for 1940s Malaya. The cinematographer came from India, a Malaysian team in Kuala Lumpur handled all of the film’s sound and color work, and a British composer wrote the score. Cast and crew members hailed from Malaysia, Indonesia, Singapore, India, Japan, the U.K. and the U.S.

“Bringing seven countries together on one production was never the easy route, but it was the right one,” said producer Debasmita Dasgupta of FilmsPositive. “Every department brought something of their own film culture to create a deeply rooted Southeast Asian story. What you see on screen is the result of seamless cross-border collaboration, proof that authentic regional stories have the power to command a global stage.”

Fellow producer Mikail Chowdhury of LDNO said films like this one rarely get made. “A Malay soldier in 1948 Malaya isn’t the story a traditional studio would back, but it’s one which audiences in this region have been waiting to see on screen and should be told,” Chowdhury said. “Independent film is where these stories survive and it is a privilege to have been part of it.”

Dasgupta and Chowdhury produce alongside Tim Willrich of Limbo Pictures and Bo H. Holmgreen, CEO of Viking Sunset Studios. Jessie White of Misfit Studios is executive producer with Mohsen Ashraf of Friendly Felix Studios, Tanveer Ali, Amreen Bashir of Amra Bangladeshi and Tahmid Khan of Auras.

White said Misfit enjoys supporting people who take creative chances on stories they believe in. “We’re proud to have helped make this film happen, and excited for audiences to discover it on Netflix,” White said.

After a nationwide cinema release in Malaysia and a smaller theatrical run in Indonesia, “The Ghost and the Gun” will launch on Netflix worldwide on Nov. 28.

Gut bacteria cannot see the sun so they use your meal times to control how your body absorbs nutrients

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Gut bacteria in mice read mealtimes, not daylight, and tell the intestine when to absorb, helping underfed mice grow and overfed ones get fat.

The body clock is supposed to run on light. Sunrise wakes the brain, and the rest of the body falls in line. But the bacteria in the gut live in the dark.

In mice, a common microbe keeps time another way: it reads when food arrives and tells the cells of the intestine to start absorbing.

The signal is a small molecule the bacterium makes while it digests phytate, a compound in grains, nuts and seeds. The molecule switches on genes that pull amino acids and fats out of a meal.

Give it to undernourished young mice and they grow. Give it to mice on a high-fat diet and they get fatter.

Zheng Kuang, an associate professor of biological sciences at Carnegie Mellon University, led the work from his lab. Colleagues at the University of Pittsburgh, UT Southwestern Medical Center and the Quadram Institute in England took part.

All of it was done in mice and in human gut cells grown in a dish.

In an interview with Earth.com, Kuang said what struck him most was that the bacteria convert the timing of a meal into chemistry.

“Their absolute numbers did not need to change substantially across the day – their metabolic activity changed,” he said.

The bacterium is Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, one of the most common residents of the human gut.

Kuang’s team raised mice with no gut microbes at all, then gave some of them this one species and nothing else. They sampled the lining of the small intestine around the clock.

Mice eat at night. In the hours after dark, the colonized mice carried more of the bacterial signal molecule in their gut lining than they did in the morning.

Germ-free mice showed no such rise, and neither did mice given a mutant strain missing the enzyme that breaks down phytate.

The bacteria themselves were no more numerous at night than in the morning, Kuang’s team found. What rose was the activity of that one enzyme.

“We are not claiming these bacteria have their own internal clock,” Kuang told Earth.com. “Rather, they respond to feeding cues and produce molecules called inositol phosphates, which activate a protein called HDAC3 in intestinal cells.”

That protein, HDAC3, works inside the cells lining the intestine and helps decide which genes are on.

The bacterial molecule makes it grip a partner protein more tightly, and the pair then turns on a set of transporter genes that carry nutrients across the gut wall.

In colonized mice, 14 amino acids and 14 fatty acids rose in the blood during the feeding hours, while in germ-free mice they stayed low.

Of 248 transporter genes the team tracked, 122 cycled more strongly with the bacterium present, and most of those handle amino acids, sugars or fats.

The body’s master clock genes kept their normal daily rhythm either way. The bacterium didn’t reset the clock; it strengthened the daily rise and fall of the metabolic genes that follow it.

The team then fed mice only during the day, which is their normal sleeping time. The signal molecule fell and the gut’s daily rhythm in gene activity flattened, though the animals ate no less than before.

Mice allowed to eat only at night looked like mice allowed to eat whenever they liked.

The feeding experiment is the result Kuang pointed to when asked what people should take from the work. “The key message is to align your feeding schedule with the natural day-night cycle – eating during your active, daytime hours,” he said.

Artificial light has pushed human eating into the night, he added, and the study suggests that misalignment disrupts the bacterial signals.

Kuang said there was likely a metabolic cost to that disruption. The feeding experiment itself tracked the signal and the gene rhythms, not the mice’s weight.

Young mice were weaned onto a diet with the usual calories but little protein or fat, a model for childhood malnutrition.

Those given the bacterium grew longer bodies and longer thigh bones than those given the mutant strain. They had heavier organs, more amino acids in their blood and better blood sugar control, and they ate the same amount.

The signal molecule on its own did the same, in mice with and without a normal gut community. In mice bred to lack HDAC3 in their gut cells, neither the bacterium nor the molecule helped, and a drug that blocks HDAC3 blunted the effect too.

In a public dataset of children’s stool samples, the bacterium was among 19 species more common in healthy children than in undernourished ones.

Adult mice fed a high-fat, high-sugar diet for 12 weeks gained more weight with the bacterium than with the mutant strain.

They carried more belly fat and liver fat and had higher blood fats and worse blood sugar handling. They absorbed more fat from food and passed less of it in their stool.

Again, the mice lacking HDAC3 in their gut cells showed none of this, and food intake didn’t differ.

Kuang said that double edge is the finding he most wants understood. “This illustrates why it is too simple to label a bacterium as inherently ‘good’ or ‘bad’ – its effects depend on diet and the metabolic state of the host,” he explained.

The team’s reading is evolutionary. A pathway that extracts the most from every meal was an advantage when food was scarce, and the same pathway can work against a body surrounded by high-fat diets.

Whether this bacterium even lives and works in the human small intestine is an open question. The team also could not prove that the signal molecule in the gut cells came from the bacteria rather than from the cells themselves, which make related molecules.

And phytate intake varies from one diet to another, so the signal’s strength probably does too, the authors wrote.

Whether the same signal shifts in people who eat on a jet-lagged or night-shift schedule is a question the study does not reach.

Asked how soon the bacteria could be tested against malnutrition, Kuang said it is too early to give a reliable timeline for human testing.

The next step, he said, is to look for the pathway in human intestinal samples and in children with undernutrition. Until then, the case that mealtimes talk to the gut through its bacteria rests on mice.

The full study was published online in a publication of the Carnegie Mellon University.

How Ferguson-inspired Pochettino can succeed where Carrick failed at Man Utd

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Maurucio Pochettino (right) was first mentioned as a contender to manage Manchester United when Louis van Gaal was sacked in May 2016

When Mauricio Pochettino was asked the three Premier League managers he would have loved to have played for, he was unequivocal.

"Sir Alex Ferguson, Sir Alex Ferguson, Sir Alex Ferguson," replied Pochettino, who on Friday was named Manchester United manager.

In 2023, the Argentine - then at Chelsea, and dressed in full Blues training gear - was being interviewed by Sky Sports. Still he felt a need to express his adoration for Ferguson.

Six years prior to that interview, while managing Tottenham, he had shared lunch with the legendary former United manager.

"When I was young, when I started my career as a manager, he was always my inspiration, my reference," Pochettino said.

"And when you can share two hours with a person who was the greatest manager in the history of football, you can only enjoy."

Hiring United managers has been complicated since Ferguson's departure in 2013, and one of the most prominent complications has been a back-and-forth around the club's DNA.

For some, winning by any means necessary should take precedence, while others believe the style of play and cultural heritage of United's most successful period is vital to the club's identity.

In recent years, United's managerial merry-go-round has alternated between European coaches with their own fixed styles of play, and former players from the Ferguson era.

But between his tactics of choice and his adoration for the former United boss, Pochettino may be uniquely placed to bridge the gap between both camps.

Pochettino a decade in Man Utd's making - but Carrick deserves better

Michael Carrick's United produced a series of good results to finish last season with a run unmatched by any other team in the league. They looked great, but it was a greatness built on attacking in a transition-heavy manner.

Under Carrick, in games they had less than 50% of the ball, they only lost once. In those 13 games, they boasted a win-rate of 62%. This dropped to 36% in games they had the majority of possession.

This style of play suited their direct attackers and protected their defenders.

At the same time, Casemiro's set-piece prowess provided United a reliable way to break the deadlock against deep defences - something they have struggled with this season.

With United a goal up, opponents stepped forward, giving Carrick's men more space. They could play to their strengths again, attacking quickly. It worked.

But teams at the top are reluctant to play in this way. Carrick's vision for United was a side that played slick football, with short passes in build-up intuitively, referencing Paris St-Germain's midfield three as a source of inspiration.

Building up in this manner, to entice and escape from pressure, before looking to find the forwards quickly, was the plan Carrick was working towards.

This didn't come to fruition and trying to take United from a successful counter-attacking side to one that could reliably beat teams while keeping the ball was the same hurdle that undid Ole Gunnar Solskjar and Erik ten Hag.

Former players under Sir Alex Ferguson, both Ole Gunnar Solskjar and Michael Carrick leaned into Unitedâs historic style when crafting their game-models

Pochettino gives United an interesting solution to this problem.

In his own words, he has described his sides as needing "to be protagonists, always press high, work hard and be dynamic". He refers to himself as a "disciple" of the legendary Argentina boss Marcelo Bielsa, who he played for.

If you wanted an immediate comparison in the Premier League to a coach who resembles Pochettino's ideas in possession, it would be worth looking at Liverpool's Andoni Iraola, who similarly cites Bielsa as his reference.

These are coaches who, while taking the initiative, encourage vertical football. For as different as the clubs are, both the fans of United and Liverpool have enjoyed success when their teams have played high-octane football.

This summer's World Cup was a strong showcase of Pochettino's style of play and his recent tactical adaptation.

Rather than a fixed, positional style of play - akin to the work of somebody like Manchester City's Enzo Maresca - Pochettino's USA had only certain players being tasked with holding their position.

This approach posed problems for teams who defended in a zonal manner - defending specific areas rather than individual players.

The US midfielders being able to move freely meant they could step into areas of the pitch between the zones that were being defended, which resulted in their players often being unmarked with time and space on the ball.

By playing in close proximity to each other in the middle of the pitch, the US found another benefit.

Small-space passing combinations helped draw the opposing team up towards the ball and combined with clever off-ball movement to stretch the pitch, allowed the US to find their striker with few defenders around him.

Pochettino's men looked to thread through-balls in behind and early after enticing the opposition's press and the speedy Folarin Balogun was able to turn those chances into goals.

Here is a good example of USA's fluid midfield four under Pochettino. The right-sided midfielder McKennie has placed himself near his three midfield team-mates, leaving the right half-space empty. With a four against three in that area of the pitch, USA are best placed to play quick combinations before looking to find their attackers in space.

Here we can the swapping of left-winger Pulisic and left-back Robinson. In this example, Robinson inverts into the fluid midfield four with Pulisic positioned on the touchline for balance. When out wide, Pulisic was able to showcase impressive one-against-one dribbling throughout the match too.

This combination of a fluid midfield, small-space passing and direct through-balls doesn't sound dissimilar to what Carrick was trying to move towards implementing this season.

And although that may worry United fans, the similarity in principles will benefit the players.

"My philosophy is to give a freer style with a good organisation," were Pochettino's words in 2014.

Although philosophically similar, that latter part - a clearer organisation to his freer style - is likely to be what Carrick was unable to reliably offer his players.

For fans of top sides, fast-paced, vertical football tends to be accepted when it comes from a place of being proactive - think Ferguson's United or Jurgen Klopp's Liverpool - wanting to keep the ball, but not passing it for passing's sake.

Fast-paced, vertical football tends to be frowned upon when it comes from a place of conceding possession and defending your own goal for long periods of the match.

The first half of Carrick's stint did appear proactive, while his 4-2-4 press worked well. Leaving four players high was a risk that allowed them to win the ball back and score quickly, but as teams figured this out, United failed to adapt.

There also appeared to be a drop in the team's fitness the longer United went from the departure of ex-boss Ruben Amorim, who left United well conditioned, albeit on the back of a schedule without European football.

Here we see United's narrow 4-2-4 defensive shape in the first half against Como

Hiring Pochettino will immediately begin the process of turning United into a well-drilled side, capable of applying intense pressure, likely in a co-ordinated manner.

Pochettino enjoyed relative success with Spurs, built on this intensity and co-ordination, that differentiated him from the rest of the league.

With that unique selling point more common now than a decade ago, how he builds on it will dictate how his tenure unfolds.

But watching Brighton this season, he can be assured that an approach founded on intensity off the ball can work in the Premier League in 2026, with Fabian Hurzeler's men second in the league, with the fourth highest average possession, 10 games in.

Pochettino, from a footballing point of view, feels extremely aligned to the values of Ferguson's United, and as a result will implement numerous similarities to the things Carrick wanted, but ultimately failed, to deliver.

His wealth of experience make him a more proven entity than the departing United manager and if he can get his team fit and pressing high, in typically Pochettino fashion, they should see an upturn in form.

Expanding on his managerial approach, while managing Spurs he explained how "the most important thing [was] to create a good shape for players to be comfortable and to show their real qualities".

Rather than relying on pre-defined pattern, he looks to empower individuals to solve attacking problems. Taking this approach means recruitment of top talent matters more than ever.

"He [gave] me the confidence and licence to go where I wanted on the pitch, where I [could] use my strengths," said Cole Palmer, who enjoyed his most productive Premier League season under Pochettino at Chelsea.

United have good attackers but they will need to take the same initiative Palmer did, if platformed to their strengths - something Pochettino does well.

If they don't, and if Pochettino is to stumble, it will likely be in United's inability to break down stubborn defences when asked to dominate the ball - in what would feel like Groundhog Day, again.

Step aside, Twilight, and move over, Harry Potter; Universal has the perfect answer to both. It may seem an unlikely combination; Twilight is a "sparkly vampire" romance, while Harry Potter blazed the trail for the current generation of magic schools (complete with a healthy dose of a Chosen One destiny). And yet, have you ever noticed how the best original stories are a delightful mix of multiple ideas, creating something refreshingly new and yet delightfully familiar?

Step forward Tracy Wolff's Crave, which is a stunning blend of the two. It stars Grace Foster, a young orphan who winds up sent to what turns out to be a school for vampires, werewolves, dragons, witches, and more. And, of course, the different groups don't get on at all. The book is delightfully self-aware (at one point the vampire love-interest gives Grace a gift copy of Twilight), and it turns so many of the central tropes upside-down in a fantastic way.

Crave launched in 2020, and it's expanded into a full six-book series. Katmere Academy isn't Hogwarts; it's much more dangerous, a little closer to the dark academia books I've fallen in love with of late. The various factions initially feel almost like your traditional high school, where the jocks and the nerds all hang out together, but it soon becomes clear the racial "tribes" are much more dangerous. Grace has no idea what she's dealing with, and initially there's an entertaining sense of denial as readers figure out what's happening even as she tries to tell herself she's going nuts.

That said, my personal favorite twist here is that the heroes are not the Chosen Ones. In fact, this is a franchise where the Chosen One went wrong; where the vampire love interest's brother was believed to be a Chosen One, and was raised to believe he was something special. It's fascinating to realize how much this kind of philosophy can twist and scar a person, making the Crave story a fun interrogation of a trope we've seen played out so many times. It reminds me a little of Anakin Skywalker in the Star Wars prequels, but a little better done (with apologies to George Lucas).

As a lover of Twilight and Harry Potter, and someone who very much enjoys romantasy, I can honestly say Crave is a must-read. Wolff is wonderfully creative, and her world is so very immersive; even when she plays with familiar tropes and character beats, she does so with a nod and a wink. If you like vampires and werewolves, magic schools, or even dragon shifters, this is the series for you.

But here's the annoying thing; Universal acquired the rights to Crave before the first book even came out, with Universal’s SVP of Production Jeyun Munford and Creative Executive Christine Sun set to oversee the production. We've had zero news about the film since then, though, which is something of a surprise; unfortunately, Munford isn't even at Universal anymore, which may well mean this particular movie has been lost in development hell. If so, it's a real shame; at a time when both Harry Potter and Twilight are re-emerging, this is an epic that should really be given a chance to flourish.

For now, readers would be wise to pick up Crave for something that will definitely scratch that itch in the best possible way.

Scenic Group has promoted Angus Crichton to head of global product, bringing his experience across sales, marketing and product development to the leadership of its product teams for Scenic and Emerald Cruises & Tours.

Now based at the Group’s office in Zug, Switzerland, he will lead product development across luxury river cruising, yacht cruising and escorted land journeys, strengthening the connection between itinerary design and the guest experience.

“As the only company to design, build, own and operate our ships, we have a truly unique ability to innovate across every aspect of the guest experience, from the luxury spaces on board to the destinations we visit and the journeys we create,” said Sture Myrmell, chief operating officer, Scenic Group.

“That same philosophy connects our ocean, river and land journeys, giving Scenic and Emerald Cruises & Tours guests the confidence to explore in different ways while enjoying a seamless experience with the brands they know and trust. Angus’s deep understanding of our business makes him ideally placed to bring these strengths together as we expand our global portfolio.”

Crichton previously led marketing, sales and product for Emerald in Australia and New Zealand, alongside responsibility for the land product division across Scenic and Emerald.

“Having been part of Scenic Group’s evolution over more than 26 years, I’m excited to help drive its next chapter. Bringing product development and our commercial teams closer together will help us deliver exceptional journeys for our guests and give our travel partners compelling experiences to sell,” noted Crichton.

The appointment comes as Scenic Group progresses what it says is its most significant expansion program in decades, with eight new ships planned for 2027 and 2028 and further announcements to come across its luxury river and yacht fleets.

River fleet additions include the Emerald Lumi on France’s Seine River. The ship recently touched water for the first time.

Scenic is also planning expansion to India’s Hooghly River in 2027, with a further ship planned for India’s Brahmaputra River in 2028.

The Scenic Spirit II and Scenic Spirit III are set to debut on the Mekong River from 2028.

The three new luxury yachts comprise the Emerald Raiya in 2027, followed by the Emerald Xara and the Scenic Ikon in 2028.

The Ikon will debut in the Mediterranean in April 2028, with Antarctica featuring in its inaugural season.
