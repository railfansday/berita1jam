Appian Expands AI Agent Controls and Unveils Ask Appian

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Appian expanded its AI-agent control plane and unveiled Ask Appian at Appian Europe in London on November 17, 2026, with tools for process-level oversight and outcome measurement. In the announcement, Raiffeisen Bank International reported that a hybrid agent-and-rules approach reduced token consumption and processing time by 80% in testing.

The control plane is designed to register Appian and third-party agents in one management view, monitor activity and cost, restrict usable models, track policy compliance and generate alerts. Its optimization layer uses evaluations to track accuracy and connects agents to the cases and process steps they handled, with business key performance indicators (KPIs) such as cost per case and cycle time supplying the operating context.

Ask Appian is a separate interface for requesting work in plain language. It is designed to draw relevant context from systems connected through Appian’s data fabric and dispatch agents to finish the task. The stated goal is to spare employees from moving between underlying applications to assemble information and complete a request.

Appian’s approach places agents inside deterministic business processes, where the process controls the sequence and each step is assigned to an agent, person, system or rule. Rules handle predictable work, while agents receive guardrails and shared context for steps requiring judgment. If an agent fails or is stopped, the task can go to a person, rule or another agent so the case continues.

Sanat Joshi, Appian’s executive vice president of product management, said permissions, guardrails and model access cannot establish whether an agent is doing useful work. “Appian puts business process at the center,” he said, describing the company’s focus on how each agent affects business outcomes.

A November 16, 2026 Appian blog post explains the trade-off. Process-led steps are consistent, repeatable and auditable; agent-led steps can handle open-ended work but add cost and latency, with outcomes that can vary by case. In one example, an agent assessing a loan hands debt calculations to deterministic formulas while a loan officer makes the final decision.

Appian’s control-plane documentation draws a line between current functions and later additions. Third-party agents can now serve as steps in Appian processes through MCP or direct integration without moving into Appian. Support for the A2A protocol, a registry encompassing third-party agents and tracing for agents running outside Appian come next.

Measurement has a similar boundary. Appian agents are already measured against process KPIs. The documentation lists estimated cost per case and per step for Appian and third-party agents, before-and-after comparisons with manual work and agent-improvement recommendations as forthcoming.

Many control-plane capabilities are available, including controls, orchestration and process intelligence, and customers can join an early-adopter program for access before general availability. Appian said additional capabilities would start arriving during 2026, but planned features and timing can change and purchasing decisions should rest on generally available functionality.

Raiffeisen Bank International intends to automate payment inquiries with Appian. Claus Mitterlehner, its head of smart automation, said the team initially gave most tasks to AI agents. “After testing it, the team changed course and moved the predictable steps to deterministic automation, keeping agents for the steps that need judgment,” he said.

That is the control plane’s broader optimization loop: evaluations and process KPIs identify how agents perform, and repeated agent steps can become deterministic workflows or business rules. The intended result is a process in which agent spending is concentrated on judgment-based work rather than predictable execution.

The landing page for a study by Appian and Harvard Business Review Analytic Services reports that 92% of respondents said agents need rules-based guardrails to be safe and effective, while 48% had such rules in place. The study surveyed AI decision-makers and examined modernization and workflow integration.

The control plane’s business case therefore depends on more than keeping agents within policy. Its useful test is whether process evidence can show where agents improve cost, speed or accuracy and where repeated work should become rules. Raiffeisen’s reported test supplies the concrete example; the planned cross-platform registry, tracing and measurement features will determine how consistently Appian can extend that analysis across agents built elsewhere.

IQE raised its full-year 2026 outlook in a trading update on November 17, 2026, forecasting year-on-year revenue growth above 40% after optical-communications opportunities in AI and data-centre infrastructure converted into revenue. The compound semiconductor wafer supplier also expects adjusted EBITDA, defined as earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, amortisation and specified adjustments, in the mid-teens of millions of pounds.

IQE had current cash of £41.5 million and remained free of bank debt. Chief executive Jutta Meier said disciplined operational execution supported the upgrade and that strong order-book coverage was already in place for 2027, giving the company order visibility into the following year.

The July 21, 2026 trading update set the previous guidance at revenue growth above 30% and adjusted EBITDA in the low teens of millions of pounds. That earlier upgrade followed first-half trading that exceeded management expectations. Demand for indium phosphide (InP), used in optical photonics products for data centres and AI infrastructure, was accelerating.

The September 7, 2026 interim results reported first-half revenue up 43% to £64.6 million and adjusted EBITDA of £6.0 million, against an adjusted EBITDA loss of £0.4 million a year earlier. Photonics revenue rose 45% to £38.5 million, driven by US military and defence programme funding releases and growth in AI and data-centre-related markets.

IQE attributed the first-half gross-margin expansion to higher manufacturing-asset utilisation and a product mix with a greater share of photonics sales. Photonics also serves defence programmes, so the segment’s growth is not a standalone measure of AI demand.

The improvement in adjusted earnings did not extend to reported net cash from operations, which declined to £1.2 million in the first half from £3.6 million a year earlier. Higher revenue and adjusted EBITDA were partly offset by a £4.0 million working-capital outflow and £2.4 million of restructuring and strategic-review costs.

The September interim results set out a second-half 2026 conversion of existing tooling to increase InP capacity for significant AI and data-centre demand. They also identified potential optical-communications upside underpinned by recently signed supply agreements, the opportunities now reported as converted into revenue.

The technology work extends beyond InP: IQE reported qualifications in gallium-arsenide-based optical interconnect technologies and gallium-nitride-on-silicon power epitaxy for high-efficiency AI and data-centre power supply units. It was also developing and sampling gallium-nitride-on-silicon microLED epitaxy with partners serving hyperscalers for high-bandwidth, energy-efficient data transfer in AI data centres.

The guidance sequence shows AI optical work moving from identified opportunity to recognised revenue, with supply agreements supporting the commercial ramp. The next execution test is whether the planned InP capacity increase and 2027 order coverage can sustain that growth while cash generation catches up with adjusted earnings.

Whale snot helps scientists sequence an entire genome

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 17, 2026 5:00 AM EST

Whale vomit can be turned into expensive perfume. Whale carcasses will sink to the bottom and become marine life habitats. Whale pee nourishes the entire ocean. But how about whale snot? The microbes and other biological material emitted from their blowholes when the giant cetaceans breathe has helped scientists sequence an entire whale genome: Take that whale vomit. The findings are detailed in a study published today in the journal Molecular Ecology Resources.

Whole genomes are crucial for animal conservation. With genomic data, researchers can measure population health, to see how much genetic diversity and inbreeding is present. It can also help identify individual animals for monitoring, help determine an animal’s sex, and estimate the population’s size.

To obtain genetic data from whales, marine biologists historically have had to conduct a skin biopsy. They’ll take a dart, stick it into the whale’s blubber, and take a small sample of the skin and blubber. However, during sampling, boats must get close to the whales, which can disturb them. In some locations and for some endangered species, getting close enough to take a biopsy isn’t permitted or possible.

That’s where whale snot—or blow—comes in. For this study, a team from Scotland’s University of St. Andrews, the University of Copenhagen in Denmark, the North Coast Cetacean Society, and Fisheries and Oceans Canada used several small drones off the coast of British Columbia. The drones were equipped with sterile petri dishes to collect the samples.

The team flew the drone through the exhaled breaths of one North pacific humpback whale (Megaptera novaeangliae kuzira) at a time, but still sampled several individual whales. The humpbacks generally took one to six breaths every time they surfaced—covering the drone and the dishes in whale snot.

The team collected 58 blow samples from 26 individual humpback whales. They also took repeated samples from the same whales, collected in different encounters and across two years to match the genomes. They then cross-referenced the genomes collected from the whale blow with genomes sequenced from traditional blubber biopsies taken from the same animals.

To keep the DNA from mixing, the team targeted the whale most upwind in the pod, since the blow downwind might mix with the blow from the whales up ahead. Resting whales were never sampled, and the team stopped the drone flights if a whale showed any reaction. More than one third of samples were collected by flying the drone directly from land—no boat required.

The DNA was sequenced to produce two types of genomes. Low-coverage nuclear genomes, which are inherited from both parents, and high-coverage mitochondrial genomes, which are inherited from the mother only.

The team found that the genetic diversity estimated from the blow samples was very similar to the estimates taken from traditional biopsies. Fortunately, the humpback population in the study shows little evidence of recent inbreeding. There is a small proportion of the genome that has long stretches of identical DNA, but the team believes that most likely reflects distant shared ancestry or past population bottlenecks.

According to the team, this research marks one of the first whole genome datasets for a group of North Pacific humpback whales. Previously, only one North Pacific humpback whole genome was publicly available.

“We will use these blow-derived genomes to not only assess population structure and health, but also assess kinship and family ties among humpback whales foraging in northern British Columbia,” Dr. Éadin O’Mahony, a study co-author and marine mammal ecologist who was at the University of St. Andrews at the time of the study, said in a statement. “The aim is to understand how their social bonds form: Are the whales that forage together family, or long-term friends?”

As expected, the North Pacific humpback whales were genetically distinct from their counterparts living in the North Atlantic. This confirms that data taken from whale blow works as well from data taken from conventional biopsies. The drone-captured genetic analyses also correctly determined every whale’s sex, and revealed three previously undescribed mitochondrial DNA variants, which could help show how some of these whales are related. The blow from whales swimming in groups also did not have any sign of contamination from neighboring whales.

About 16 percent of the DNA in each blow sample were also primarily from the microbes living in the whale’s airways. According to the team, this indicates that a single breath could tell us about the individual whale and its respiratory microbiome, opening up a potential new window into cetacean health.

That’s a whole lot of data from just a little bit of snot.

ROBOTERA Deploys Humanoid Robots at 11 Postal Hubs in Guangdong

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On November 17, 2026, ROBOTERA announced in a release that its China Post Group Guangdong Branch project had reached 11 postal hubs in eight cities, extending humanoid parcel-sorting operations across the province.

The rollout covers Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Dongguan, Jiangmen, Shantou, Shanwei, Jieyang and Heyuan. China Post Group Guangdong Branch jointly implemented the project with ROBOTERA, formally Beijing Xingdongjiyuan Technology Co., Ltd. ROBOTERA described it as the logistics sector’s largest and most geographically extensive deployment of embodied-intelligence robots to date.

The expansion grew from a pilot at the Guangzhou Postal District Center that began in March 2026. ROBOTERA reports that its robots have since averaged more than 1,500 parcel inductions per hour, a rate it compares with the performance of experienced human workers.

The deployment exposes the system to high-volume sorting and cross-regional logistics coordination rather than a single, uniform site. ROBOTERA identifies Guangdong as China’s largest province by express parcel volume, making the provincial network a substantial test of whether the technology can operate across varied real-world conditions.

Sorting parcels is labor-intensive and repetitive, often under harsh working conditions, with operators also facing labor shortages and high workforce turnover. Peak shopping seasons can push parcel volumes to several times their normal levels. ROBOTERA described the robots as filling capacity gaps and supporting stable operations during those peaks by taking on strenuous frontline tasks, allowing human workers to focus on robot maintenance, operations coordination and quality control.

Within the integrated system, ROBOTERA said irregular-item rejection and adaptive speed control are intended to reduce jams, collisions and missed items, while multiple power options accommodate different site requirements and support continuous operation. The hardware includes the company’s self-developed five-finger dexterous hand for flexible parcel handling.

Postal operations in East China have also deployed ROBOTERA’s integrated solution. Those installations adapt to different operating environments, parcel types and sorting-line configurations, placing the Guangdong expansion within a wider postal footprint that extends beyond one province or one line configuration.

ROBOTERA’s general-purpose robotics work combines humanoid platforms with in-house development spanning data, motion control, foundation models and dexterous hands. It describes this AI-native approach as full-stack, placing the postal deployment within the company’s broader effort to develop both complete robots and their underlying manipulation and control technologies.

The eight-city rollout shifts the emphasis from demonstrating a humanoid sorting task to operating it across a distributed logistics network with varied site requirements and high-volume demand. The central test is network-wide consistency: sustaining the reported sorting throughput across different hubs and peak-season surges, rather than only at the Guangzhou pilot site.

Most nonprofit hospitals' tax breaks outpaced their community spending in 2023: Lown Institute" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Just over half of the country’s nonprofit hospitals have charity care and community benefit investments that fall short of their tax savings, according to the latest of the Lown Institute’s analyses on more than 3,000 hospitals’ tax filings.

In 2023, the most recent year in which Internal Revenue Service Form 990s were available for review, the think tank identified $38.8 billion of industry-wide tax exemptions, or $12.8 million per hospital and 3.8% of total expenses on average. About $37.1 billion was collectively spent by the same hospitals on various community benefits, as reported in the IRS forms matched to other filings such as Medicare cost reports.

While the total exemption handily outpaces the benefits investments by Lown’s measure, the group underscored that these "fair share” deficits or surpluses also vary widely between facilities, and that some of the industry’s biggest names are on the wrong side of the divide.

Here, Mayo Clinic Hospital led the pack with a $366 million deficit, according to Lown’s analysis, followed by Tisch Hospital (-$294 million deficit), Stanford Hospital (-$255 million deficit), New York-Presbyterian Hospital (-$248 million deficit) and Massachusetts General Hospital (-$219 million deficit).

"Some of America's wealthiest hospitals fall hundreds of millions short of giving back what they receive from their communities, leaving those with fewer resources to pick up the slack," Vikas Saini, M.D., president of the Lown Institute, said in a release on the new analysis, released Tuesday.

Among those leading the latter group were North Shore University Hospital ($167 fair share surplus), Rochester General Hospital ($146 million surplus) and Englewood Hospital ($142 million surplus). Lown’s analysis includes a searchable hospital-by-hospital breakdown of the tax exemptions and community investment totals.

When splitting the 52% of deficit hospitals apart from the surplus hospitals, the former group logged about $15.5 billion above their reported community benefits—a deficit Lown said could wipe the medical debt of 12 million Americans. Fair share surplus hospitals outpaced their exemptions by $13.8 billion.

Total tax exemptions were also “very evenly distributed” among the hospitals, Lown said. The 30 hospitals, or about 1% of all hospitals, with the largest tax exemptions collectively avoided $6.8 billion, or about 18% of total exemptions. The 607 hospitals, or about 20%, accounted for three-quarters of the country’s hospital tax exemptions.

Lown’s measure of nonprofit hospitals’ tax breaks includes exemptions from federal and state corporate income taxes, state and local sales taxes, property taxes, tax-exempt bonds, tax-exempt charitable donations and federal unemployment tax exemptions. Not included are other benefits provided to many nonprofits, like Medicaid disproportionate share payments, 340B discounts or profits from indirect tax arbitrage.

Its tally of community benefit includes spending on patient financial assistance, subsidized care, payments to other nonprofits to improve health or various community health or social determinants of health investments.

Of note, it excludes Medicaid shortfall—the difference between hospitals’ costs and revenues when treating Medicaid patients—and spending on professional education and research. The hospital industry typically points to these billions of dollars of exclusions when discussing their tax breaks, with Medicaid shortfall alone accounting for nearly half of their preferred community benefit spending totals. Lown noted in its methodology that these accounting losses are often offset by other discounts and supplemental benefits and don’t always have a direct impact on community health, among other objections.

Lown said the disparities between hospitals and the outsized benefits being delivered by some are a call to amend, not eliminate, nonprofit status exemptions. The think tank accompanied its analysis with calls to increase reporting transparency and set spending targets for hospitals tied to variables such as size, exemption value, financial health and prior spending.

“Tax exemptions aren't a gift—they're an expectation, and regulators need to make sure all hospitals hold up their end of the bargain," Saini said.

Lown acknowledged the impending financial pressure hospitals will face with upcoming H.R. 1 policy changes, and said such changes should be accompanied by “short-term” financial protections.

Still, the group isn’t alone in drawing scrutiny to the tax breaks. Lawmakers and policy advisors have pointed to, and questioned the justification for, nonprofits’ exemptions when questioning health system leaders on collections policies or high prices.

Academics and researchers have also called for greater transparency or other overhauls of how community benefits are reported or calculated. Of note, a research article published earlier this month in Health Affairs reviewing hospital filings between 2013 and 2022 outlined a disconnect between different hospitals’ listed charity care eligibility thresholds and their relative spending, which more closely aligned with community need than their stated policy.

Unlocking Stable On-Policy RL on TPU with Bitwise-Zero Train–Inference Mismatch

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models is notoriously sensitive to numerical instability: even tiny discrepancies between rollout generation and gradient updates can destabilize training or stall convergence. In LLM RL, the model serves as the policy, π(action | previous tokens), and true on-policy learning requires every action in an update to be generated and evaluated by that exact same policy. While a synchronous loop ensures each rollout batch is scored using the same weights that generated it, a critical numerical requirement remains: the sampler and trainer must evaluate that policy identically.

In practice, production RL stacks run sampling and training through separate, specialized execution paths. The sampler chooses each action token during incremental decoding and records its behavior logprob. The trainer evaluates the same token in batched forward-backward passes to compute its current logprob and gradients. Under the same weights, these differing execution paths can still assign different logprobs to the same token and context. The training schedule is on-policy, but the numerical execution has introduced a hidden off-policy gap that compounds over multi-turn trajectories.

Our zero train–inference mismatch (Zero-TIM) implementation, developed in Tunix and verified at multi-host scale across 64 TPU v5p chips (DP8×TP8), closes this numerical gap. At the same weights, the sampler and trainer assign bitwise-identical logprobs to the same action and token history—bringing state-of-the-art, true on-policy stability to TPU RL training.

A reduction is a tree of floating-point additions. Floating-point addition is not associative, so changing the tree changes where rounding occurs. The effect is easier to expose in bfloat16, which retains less precision than float32.

The following bfloat16 example isolates the effect of reduction order:

The same issue appears in model reductions: matrix products accumulate partial sums, normalization computes statistics, and tensor-parallel shards combine partial outputs. In our original stack, sampler decode and trainer forward used different token-array shapes and forward implementations. JAX specializes computations to their input shapes, and the training forward also runs inside a differentiated program.

Across these paths, reduction order and intermediate precision were not consistently aligned. Floating-point rounding turned those arithmetic differences into different logprob bits. The weights and model equations were identical; the arithmetic was not.

The contract is simple: across sampler decode, serving prefill, and trainer forward, all three paths must produce bitwise-identical logprobs for every sampled action—comparing raw floating-point bytes under the same rollout weights, token history, and sampling settings, with zero rounding or tolerance.

Why three paths instead of two? In a decoupled RL stack, a direct sampler–trainer disagreement shows that a numerical gap exists, but not where it enters. Introducing serving prefill as a middle checkpoint turns alignment into a systematic two-step diagnostic that any RL stack can adopt:

Sampler decode vs. prefill verifies that the serving engine agrees with itself between incremental token generation and full-sequence re-scoring, while prefill vs. trainer verifies that the serving and training engines agree across compilation and differentiation boundaries.

Achieving Zero-TIM does not require merging the sampler and trainer into a single monolithic engine. Instead, we use a generalizable four-part recipe that bridges separate serving and training pipelines across multi-host TPU meshes: locking within-shard kernel accumulation, ordering cross-shard reductions, standardizing the logprob scoring tail, and wrapping serving kernels with custom differentiation rules.

Inside each tensor-parallel rank, batch-invariant Pallas kernels control accumulation order and intermediate precision. Projection kernels traverse fixed contraction blocks in order, keeping the reduction blocking independent of token-row count. Partial products accumulate in float32, with explicit output casts.

The production kernel initializes its scratch accumulator before the tile traversal, then applies this core to each tile:

The next tile continues from the float32 accumulator, not the cast output. This construction covers the attention and MLP projections and the LM head. Dedicated kernels fix RMSNorm reductions and SwiGLU’s intermediate casts. The existing Pallas paged-attention forward is retained.

For row-parallel projections, each tensor-parallel rank produces a partial output. We separate communication from addition: ppermute transports the partials, then every rank adds them in the same source-rank order.

The reduction order is explicit in the program rather than selected by a collective implementation.

Alignment must extend beyond model logits to the probability of the sampled action. Decode, prefill, and trainer apply matching sampling transforms, use the same fixed Pallas log-softmax construction, and select the recorded action token.

Prefill and trainer score the action generated during rollout rather than choosing another action. We also preserve sampled token IDs across environment turns so that every path evaluates the same token history.

The trainer reuses the serving prefill computation. Custom vector–Jacobian products retain the serving kernels for forward evaluation and provide their backward rules. The matmul example shows this separation:

The differentiable replica mirrors the kernel’s accumulation and casts, but is used only to construct the pullback. Forward values still come from the serving Pallas kernel.

Training places that forward inside a differentiated JAX program. We pass --xla_allow_excess_precision=false through XLA_FLAGS to the compiler process, constraining excess intermediate precision in the operations surrounding the Pallas kernels.

Long trajectories are evaluated in fixed-size chunks, carrying the paged KV cache forward. All action logprobs feed the complete train-batch RL loss. The reverse pass propagates gradients through those cache connections and combines parameter-gradient contributions before the optimizer update.

Across the AI systems community, eliminating train–inference mismatch has rapidly become a state-of-the-art requirement for stabilizing large-scale RL convergence. Recent open-source efforts—including vLLM with TorchTitan and RadixArk/LMSys Miles—have demonstrated how batch-invariant kernels and bitwise numerical alignment prevent off-policy drift during policy optimization.

Our work delivers an open-source, modular JAX/XLA and Pallas implementation of this contract for TPU in Tunix. Verified across a multi-host 64 TPU v5p (DP8×TP8) mesh, these kernel and collective patterns provide a reusable blueprint that teams can adopt to achieve bit-exact on-policy RL across distributed JAX/TPU stacks.

To benchmark our TPU kernels, we run a 200-step numerical verification test on the synthetic FrozenLake environment using a public Qwen3-8B checkpoint as a baseline test case using 64 TPU v5p chips with a DP8×TP8 mesh. The recipe uses a GSPO-token loss with leave-one-out advantages. We compare Standard training with trainer-old logprobs and no additional sampler correction, token-level truncated importance sampling (TIS), and Zero-TIM.

The Zero-TIM configuration also uses token-in/token-out (TiTO) handling to preserve sampled token IDs across environment turns. The comparison runs retain their existing tokenization paths.

Zero-TIM records zero mean absolute sampler–trainer logprob difference at every logged step in this window, while its training solve rate increases. Standard and TIS retain nonzero differences: TIS adjusts their contribution to the update without removing the underlying mismatch. Zero-TIM reaches higher observed solve rates in these runs.

We developed Zero-TIM prototype as part of Tunix, Google's open-source, JAX-native library for LLM RL and post-training on Cloud TPU. The kernel patterns and three-way verification contract are modular by design—making them straightforward to adopt across existing multi-host JAX/TPU serving and training pipelines—and we are actively extending support to linear-attention and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures.

Agents are sessions, not requests: size GKE node pools for AI agent sessions

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sorry, your browser doesn't support playback for this video

Your company wants to move its coding agents from developer laptops to a shared GKE cluster, for persistence, scale, and governance. Each of those agent sessions stays open all day but works only part of it, and how you run them decides how much of your node bill pays for waiting.

By the end of this article, you will know why an agent session needs a different runtime than a web request, how Agent Substrate and AX run sessions on GKE, and how to identify cost optimization opportunities in your GKE node pools. This article assumes you know Kubernetes Pods and node pools. If not, check out the GKE overview.

A web request lasts milliseconds. The server answers it, keeps nothing, and serves the next one, so Kubernetes scales a web app by its traffic: more requests, more Pods.

An agent session works another way. A coding agent's workspace can stay open for the developer's whole workday: it keeps its memory and its files between prompts, and it works only after a prompt arrives.

How much of that day does the agent really work? Much less than it waits. In a study of GitHub Copilot agent traces, Banruo Liu and colleagues found that in a typical session with several prompts, the agent waits for the user about 80% of the time. A turn, one prompt and the agent's whole response to it, takes 396.3 seconds of agent work on average.

Put those numbers on one developer's day. With 2 prompts an hour over an 8 hour workday, which is our assumption, the agent works 22% of the day and waits for the other 78%. If each session holds a machine, you pay for that 78%.

Teams usually pick one of two answers, and both work well for web apps.

The first is to give each session its own Pod. The session keeps its state, but its Pod holds 1 vCPU and 1 GiB all day. For example, a c3-standard-4 node fits 3 such Pods after GKE's reservations, so in our cost model 100 coding agents need 34 nodes and about USD 1,288 a month, for agents that work 22% of the time.

The second is to scale the Pod to zero between prompts. The node is free, and files on a persistent volume survive, but the process and its memory do not, so the next prompt starts a new agent process.

Based on that, you want a third option: keep the session, and free the machine while the session waits.

Agent Substrate and AX are built for that: the developer's session stays open, and the sandbox that serves it gives its machine back while the session waits. Together they cover the parts of an agentic platform that are the same for every team: the control plane, the runtime, and the sandbox. Agent Substrate answers where and how an agent runs. AX answers what the agent is.

Following the CNCF donation of gVisor, Agent Substrate, and OpenRL in November 2026, you have the flexibility to run this open source, agentic platform across any environment. Agent Substrate 1.0 is generally available on GKE.

One distinction helps from here on. The session is the multi turn interaction between the developer and the agent, with its conversation history. The compute that serves it is an isolated sandbox: an actor in Agent Substrate, or a Task in AX. Parking acts on the sandbox, and the session stays open.

Agent Substrate answers where and how an agent runs. It separates the stateful agent from the compute it runs on. An actor is the sandbox for one agent, and it holds the agent's running processes, files, and identity. A worker is a reusable sandbox drawn from a warm pool of Kubernetes Pods, and one worker hosts several actors.

An agent runs code that a model wrote, so anything in its environment can leak through a prompt injection or a bad command, API keys included. Gateways control the actor's inbound and outbound traffic and add credentials on the way out, based on the actor's identity, so the agent never holds the keys.

SuspendActor snapshots an idle actor to object storage and frees its worker, and the next request restores the actor on any warm worker. Using this approach, we can achieve higher density by multiplexing more agents to run on the same amount of compute.

AX answers what the agent is and what it knows. You declare a Task, which says what runs (image, command, and limits), and a Workspace, which says what the agent knows (repositories and files). AX runs each Task as an actor. ax suspend parks the task and keeps its /workspace. Today AX asks for a volume snapshot, so the files come back on resume and the agent's processes start fresh (runner guide).

AX passes suspend, resume, and delete to Agent Substrate. The AX roadmap adds budgets, policies, and gateways, which limit what an agent may do, and a record of what each task did.

Two details decide the bill before you size anything.

First, you decide when a sandbox parks. Agent Substrate does not suspend an idle actor by itself (#424), so your agent harness, or the service in front of it, calls the suspend API after an idle timeout. Until then, an idle actor keeps its worker. When idle actors hold every worker, a request for another actor waits up to 5 seconds by default, then fails with HTTP 503 (Agent Substrate docs).

Second, you decide how much CPU each sandbox is promised. An actor is sized by its CPU and memory limits, and each worker books those limits against the capacity you declare for its pool (API guide). Our cost model sets that capacity to what the node really has, so every actor keeps its full limit. You can declare more capacity than the node has, and then you overcommit CPU at the pool instead of in each Pod.

Our cost model prices parking for the same 100 coding agents, at Google Cloud list prices in us-central1.

With a 2 minute idle timeout, each session holds a worker 29% of its open time. A warm pool sized for bursts (the 99th percentile of sessions holding a worker at once, if sessions act independently, plus 15% headroom) needs 15 nodes. Add one node that runs around the clock for the Agent Substrate control plane and its PostgreSQL database, and a job that scales the pool to zero after hours:

Parking saves 40% of the node bill. The pool is full about once in 3,500 turns, so a prompt rarely meets that 503. In an hour at 2.5 prompts instead of 2, the same 15 nodes are full for 3% of turns, so size the pool for your busiest hour.

Keep in mind that parking pays most when your agents wait the most. At 2 prompts an hour, the saving is 40%. At 4 prompts an hour, the agent works 44% of the hour, the pool grows to 27 nodes, and the saving is 4%. Around 4.5 prompts an hour, one Pod per session becomes the cheaper option, so measure your prompt rate first.

Also, the pool size is yours to set. The autoscaling option in the GKE install guide follows worker Pods, not suspended actors, so scale the pool to zero after hours with a scheduled job: a pool that runs all 730 hours of the month costs about twice the one Pod per session bill.

Finally, the fleet size matters. The Agent Substrate control plane costs the same for 10 sessions or 1,000, so parking pays from about 37 coding sessions, or 13 for an assumed chat assistant that works about a minute an hour.

Overcommit changes the comparison. If your Pods request less CPU than their limit, Kubernetes places them by their requests. A Pod that requests 0.25 vCPU with a 1 vCPU limit packs 12 to a node, so the same 100 coding agents fit on 9 nodes, fewer than our parked pool. Agent Substrate can overcommit too, through the capacity you declare on the worker pool, and our cost model does not price that case.

Overcommit has the same cost on both sides: agents share CPU and slow down when their neighbors are busy, during builds and tests. Compare like with like before you decide to park: Pods and actors with the same CPU guarantee.

Back to the shared cluster. Agents are sessions, not requests: size the warm pool for the sessions that hold a worker, not the sessions that are open.

We would park long lived sessions that wait most of the hour, and keep one Pod per session when developers prompt more than about 4 times an hour. Size the warm pool for working time plus a short idle timeout at your busiest hour, and compare with your current Pods at the same CPU guarantee before you park.

Why Mediations Fail, and What You Can Do About It

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In August 2019, India was among the first group of countries to sign the Singapore Convention on Mediation, a treaty meant to make settlements reached through international mediation easier to enforce across borders. Four years later, Parliament passed the Mediation Act, 2023. On paper, mediation in India has never had more support. Yet plenty of mediations still end without a deal. Both sides turn up, spend a few hours in separate rooms, and leave with a report that says no settlement was reached. Why does that keep happening? Usually it is not because the dispute could never be settled. It is because of things that went wrong before anyone sat down. The wrong person is in the room This is the most common problem, and the easiest to fix. A company sends a manager who can listen and take notes but cannot agree to anything above a small figure. The other side makes a reasonable offer, and the answer is that it will have to go to the board. By the time the board meets, the moment has passed. Send someone who can say yes. Everyone treats it like a hearing Mediation is not a trial. The mediator has no power to decide who is right and cannot impose a settlement on either party. Yet many parties walk in with an opening statement built to win an argument. That approach makes sense in front of a judge. In mediation it mostly hardens the other side. A better opening explains what your business needs from the outcome. That is often different from what you claimed in the legal notice, and the gap between the two is where most settlements are found. Nobody brought the documents A mediator can only help the parties test their positions if the facts are on the table. When invoices, correspondence and delivery records are missing, every session turns into a debate about what happened rather than what to do next. Bring what supports your case, and be prepared to share it. People worry about what they say Some parties hold back because they fear their words will be used against them in court later. The Mediation Act addresses this directly. It treats mediation communications as confidential, bars audio and video recording of the sessions, and says what is said there cannot be used as evidence in later court or arbitration proceedings, with a few narrow exceptions. Knowing that tends to loosen things up. The process drags Under the Act, a mediation is meant to finish within 120 days of the mediator’s first appearance, and the parties can extend that by up to 60 days if they agree. Long gaps between sessions kill momentum. So does the difficulty of getting senior people from different cities into one room on the same day. This is where going online helps. Section 30 of the Act allows mediation to be conducted online with the parties’ written consent, as long as the integrity and confidentiality of the process are protected. If scheduling is your biggest obstacle, it is worth seeing how an online mediation platform in India runs joint and private sessions, shares documents and keeps the record secure. Shorter, more frequent sessions are much easier to arrange when nobody has to travel. What a good outcome looks like When mediation works, the parties sign a settlement agreement, and the Act makes that agreement enforceable in the same way as a court decree. It can be challenged only on limited grounds such as fraud, corruption or impersonation, and the challenge has to be filed within 90 days. Some provisions of the Act came into force in stages, so it is worth checking which ones apply to your dispute. Not every mediation will end in a deal, and some should not. But if yours fails, make sure it fails on the merits and not because the right person stayed back at the office.
