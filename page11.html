The 50 Best Thanksgiving Movies of All Time

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Grab a bowl of popcorn and gobble up these picks.

OKAY, LET'S UNPACK just what the heck makes a Thanksgiving movie a "Thanksgiving movie" besides, just like, something set in November around a gluttonous dinner table. Sure, you’ve got your traditional gather-around-the-stuffing affairs like the classic A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving. But there’s gotta be something a little bit more to this genre than turkey aesthetics. There has to be more than just theatrics.

Ultimately, Thanksgiving is about colonial hegemony and fictional parley feasts used to sugarcoat genocidal invasion and ... whoa, sorry about that; you caught us reading from the Book of Uncomfortable Truths. While the historicity of the holiday—particularly that involving Pilgrims and Native Americans—is hogwash, the spirit of the celebration—gratitude found at the beginning of the harvest season, when food isn’t just a superfluous decoration but a means of winter survival—can be found in all cultures.

Which is why a “Thanksgiving movie” doesn’t have to be an American movie. (And why the most American Thanksgiving movie is probably The New World, if only for showing what those first harvest seasons were really like: violence, starvation, lots of sinning.)

That’s not to say we won’t throw on some fun movies to watch during Thanksgiving; we’re not all about historicity and jagged truth pills. We want to have fun as well. And, if you can’t decide what to watch, there’s always football. What’s the name of the team the Cowboys usually play, now?

Here are the best Thanksgiving movies to watch this season.

Halloween has Halloween. Christmas has Black Christmas. Now, horror director Eli Roth has given Thanksgiving its very own terrifying, fun, slasher film: Thanksgiving. Based on his own fake trailer of the same name (from the theatrical version of 2006's Quentin Tarantino/Robert Rodriguez double feature Grindhouse), this movie is just about everything horror fans could want: a whodunit mystery, very well made, solid acting (from Patrick Dempsey and Big Shot star Nell Verlaque), and some absolutely brutal violence. Do not watch if you have a weak stomach!

Sam Raimi's Spider-Man may have reinvented the superhero movie genre as we know it, but it's also a covert Thanksgiving movie where one of the movie's most vital scenes takes place when Norman Osborn (Willem Dafoe) starts to carve a turkey. A timeless film for many reasons!

If you’re still feeling some type of way after watching You Can See Everything, look no further for your next watch than 1999’s American Movie, which similarly played with the format, drama, and intention of what a documentary (and a movie in general) really is, and is trying to say. This portrait of the American dream follows a regular guy obsessed with making a low-budget horror movie, and brings everyone along for the ride. It also features a vital Thanksgiving sequence that marks one of the only points in the film where characters aren’t working on the aforementioned film.

This historical adventure film (okay, maybe historical is a stretch) just feels like a Thanksgiving movie. Does it not? And Nic Cage is in top form.

In the beautifully-animated Fantastic Mr. Fox, Wes Anderson made a film that's fantastic viewing (ba dum tsss) for all ages—with an orange-brown aesthetic that feels just right for Thanksgiving. The voices of George Clooney, Meryl Streep, Bill Murray, and more make this a ton of fun.

This family-focused dramedy about a family of writers stagnant in various stages of life is set around a couple particularly dramatic Thanksgiving dinners. With a cast led by Greg Kinnear, Jennifer Connelly, and more (and a very brief glimpse of a young Glen Powell!), you're certain to be entertained around Thanksgiving time with this one.

Judd Apatow's follow-up to Knocked Up was Funny People, a movie about an aging comedy superstar (Adam Sandler) who, when facing potentially bad medical news, decides to take on a young new writer (Seth Rogen) to jump-start his career and get some joy back into his life. In one particularly notable scene, the older comedian spends Thanksgiving with his younger employees’ friends—and it's a funny, unnerving, and super memorable moment.

While The Big Chill doesn't explicitly take place during Thanksgiving, it does feel like Thanksgiving, given that it's all about loved ones gathering and hashing out some long-simmering arguments (and features a memorable Thanksgiving flashback as well). The movie has a very simple premise: a group of former college friends come back together after 15 years following the suicide of their friend. Fun fact? The unseen friend marked one of the very first roles for Kevin Costner, who had his scenes cut. The movie's actual cast is great nonetheless, including Tom Berenger, Glenn Close, William Hurt, Kevin Kline, and more.

She's Gotta Have It is Spike Lee's very first movie, and, despite his impressive resume through the next 30+ years, still one of his best. The movie follows a woman named Nola Darling who's choosing between her three gentleman suitors, and in one of the most memorable scenes in the film, all four have Thanksgiving dinner together.

Ridley Scott and Denzel Washington made fireworks together in 2024 with Gladiator II, but their first time working together came in 2007 with the crime epic American Gangster. Telling the saga of Frank Lucas, Scott and Washington both do some of their great work to bring this portrait of a violent but altruistic man to life. The movie spans a long period of time, but one particularly memorable (and shocking) moment comes during the Thanksgiving holiday.

Everyone knows the silly premise of Robin Williams's star vehicle Mrs. Doubtfire—where a man creates a new persona as a British nanny to spend more time with his kids after a divorce—but it's also a great Thanksgiving movie. What's better for pre- or post-turkey viewing than a movie about going to absurd lengths for family?

This Denis Villeneuve movie starts with a pair of families coming together for Thanksgiving—but things take a stark turn from there when a daughter from each family goes missing after dinner. This is one of the most interesting and gripping mystery thrillers in recent memory, with a cast—Hugh Jackman, Jake Gyllenhaal, Viola Davis, Paul Dano—that all bring their A-games.

Little Women is a timeless Thanksgiving text (both the actual novel and the many adaptations), but we lean toward the most recent version, directed by Barbie's Greta Gerwig, because it's just so warm and cozy. With a great cast led by Saoirse Ronan and Florence Pugh, this should be an annual Thanksgiving staple.

This family-focused film follows four adopted brothers (Mark Wahlberg, Andre 3000, Garrett Hedlund, and Tyrese Gibson) as they return home for their mother's funeral—only to discover that she's been murdered. The revenge tour then begins. The late great director John Singleton was behind this one.

This existential horror (adapted from a play of the same name) finds Richard Jenkins and Steven Yeun as members of a family all coming together for a Thanksgiving in Manhattan. If you want some dread and discomfort for Thanksgiving dinner, this is the movie for you.

For Your Consideration is one of the very funny Christopher Guest films (a group that also includes This Is Spinal Tap, A Mighty Wind, and Best in Show, among others) that features the famed actor/comedian and his many usual collaborators, including Catherine O'Hara, Eugene Levy, Fred Willard, and more. O'Hara, here, plays an actress who gets swept up in the annual mania of movie awards season—and Thanksgiving winds up playing a major part in her potentially award-winning role.

As just about everyone remembers on Thanksgiving... families can be tough. Perhaps to put it even more bluntly, families can be annoying—and messy. That's on perfect display in Dan in Real Life, where Steve Carell plays a widower who returns to his family home for Thanksgiving to find that he's got a little romantic thing going with a woman (Juliette Binoche) who just happens to be his brother's (Dane Cook) girlfriend. Whoops!

Because, well, no one comforts us during the holidays quite like Peanuts, and this short special is almost as magical as the Christmas one.

Controversial opinion: The New World is the best Thanksgiving movie. The origins of Thanksgiving aren’t exactly warm and fuzzy, and Terrence Malick’s (Days of Heaven, Tree of Life) underappreciated 2005 masterpiece wrestles with the costs of the settling of Jamestown, Virginia, on all sides (while still being gorgeous to look at, this being Malick). Even the deeply romantic moments between John Smith and Pocahontas are rooted in a real sense of place, and backgrounded by historical peril. Just let everyone know this isn’t exactly a feel-good Turkey Day experience.

True to its title, The Ice Storm couldn’t really be icier. One of Ang Lee’s best movies, it transports us to the mind of young, moody Elijah Wood as Paul Hood and then from there to the tragic and sometimes kinky ’70s messiness of two suburban families set over a Thanksgiving weekend. There’s a key party and Sigourney Weaver, so the film kind of sells itself.

Living Computers closed, but its spirit and vintage machines live on at a museum south of Seattle

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

by Kurt Schlosser on Nov 24, 2026 at 11:08 amNovember 24, 2026 at 11:09 am

When Seattle’s Living Computers: Museum + Labs closed for good in 2024, it seemed like the end of the line for anyone who wanted to do more than just look at old computers.

But in a Tukwila business park 20 minutes south of downtown, a sizable collection of vintage computers is still alive — kept running by a few of the same people who once did the same for Paul Allen.

The Interim Computer Museum is a nonprofit that opened to the public in 2024 and has quietly grown into a hands-on showcase of dozens of machines, from early Apples and IBM PCs to hulking DEC systems that once filled university labs. At 4,400 square feet of public space, it’s nearly as big as Living Computers was before that museum expanded in 2016.

ICM is led by Stephen Jones, who calls himself the museum’s “caretaker.” Jones spent two decades helping track down and restore machines for Allen, first at PDP Planet, Allen’s private collection of vintage systems, then at Living Computers, where he was senior engineering manager.

When the late Microsoft co-founder’s estate wound down Living Computers, the majority of the collection went to the Mimms Museum of Technology and Art in Georgia, where Jones is also a curator. The machines in Tukwila are privately owned, and Jones has helped bankroll ICM with personal funds, with plans to eventually donate the collection to the nonprofit.

He chose not to reuse the Living Computers name, which ICM now holds, because “it has a finite past.” Instead, he wants to carry on “the spirit of the engineering focus.”

“Between Mimms and here, this is the legacy,” Jones said. “We show the name and the connection to the past, but we’re doing new things.”

ICM occupies space in Seattle Exchange @ Southcenter, a sprawl of low-slung flex buildings off Strander Boulevard near Westfield Southcenter mall. Its neighbors include a car detailer, a pest control company, a welding shop, an embroiderer and an upholsterer.

More than four decades ago, Seattle Computer Products operated just down Industry Drive. That’s where Tim Paterson wrote 86-DOS, the operating system Microsoft bought and turned into MS-DOS for IBM’s first PC.

Jones calls that proximity part of the “magic” of the location. The museum has a Gazelle computer that SCP built nearby, using boards that Jones said the company hired high school students to assemble and test. Volunteers recently pulled data off its hard drive, which boots into MS-DOS 2.11 and still holds SCP’s own source code.

Allen’s fingerprints are everywhere. One display holds a 1971 letter from Hellmut Golde, director of the University of Washington’s Computer Science Laboratory, kicking Allen and his “friends from the Lakeside School” out of the lab. Among the offenses: tying up the teletypes to print “endless listings,” and taking an acoustic coupler from a professor’s office “without leaving at least a note.” Golde asked them to turn in their keys.

The letter sits beside an Imlac PDS-1, the same type of graphics computer Golde scolded the teens for using without permission. More than five decades later, the letter is headed back to campus: ICM plans to display it, along with the Imlac, at UW’s 60th anniversary celebration for its computer science program in March.

Jones recalled that Allen insisted visitors to Living Computers get “the 10-minute experience” of loading an Altair 8800 from paper tape, just as he remembered it. The kit computer was the machine for which Allen and Bill Gates wrote Microsoft’s first product, and Allen wanted to reinforce “how far we’ve come and how difficult old computers were.” ICM has an Altair 8800 on display.

Around the corner is Miss Piggy, a DEC datasystem 570 that Microsoft used in the early 1980s to support the group that built Multiplan, Word and later Excel. It was also the email host for much of the company, including Gates, who once had to print out an important message on its console printer when the system went down before a speaking engagement.

Recovered email shows Allen pushing to get Piggy networked. “Paul Allen is very keen on doing this,” Henry Burgess wrote in 1982. Retired in 1986, the machine now runs again in Tukwila. It’s one of ICM’s most popular remote systems, and some fans come in just to write programs while sitting next to it.

Other local ties include a PDP-7 that Boeing used to process flight data in the 1960s, and a Toad-1, a modern take on the PDP-10 built in the 1990s by Redmond’s XKL. Jones first got involved with Allen’s world through a 1997 Usenet post inviting PDP-10 enthusiasts onto a Toad. That machine became the cornerstone of PDP Planet.

In a 2024 video for Christie’s, Jones said what inspired Allen was “being able to recover old programs, mythical code, and get them running on hardware so that they can be studied.”

Asked what Allen would make of ICM, Jones didn’t hesitate. “I think he’d be happy,” he said.

On a monitor beside ICM’s PDP-6, a 1968 artificial intelligence program is having trouble.

“SORRY, I DON’T KNOW THE WORD ‘STEEPLE,'” it replies.

The program is SHRDLU, created by Terry Winograd at the MIT AI Lab. It let a user type plain-English commands to move blocks around a virtual world. Only the PDP-6’s front console and display survived, so ICM is rebuilding the machine as a “hybrid,” with the original hardware wired to an emulator.

Jones sees a lesson in that old error message. “You look at this and you think of how we use ChatGPT now,” he said. “The cool thing about SHRDLU is that it can tell you ‘I don’t know.’ It doesn’t try to fabricate anything.”

ICM has its own ties to the modern AI world. An Anthropic employee is a member and strong supporter, and in October the museum brought six vintage computing stations to an Anthropic event, where the company’s Claude chatbot wrote programs for machines like the Commodore 64.

Back in Tukwila, Claude has become one of ICM’s most useful restoration tools. The team feeds it huge stacks of scanned documentation, such as the full set of schematics for a PDP-10. When a machine has a logic fault, they ask where to look, and Claude points them to a page in the maintenance manual or a likely bad module.

“That’s turned things that would take a couple of weeks to solve into a couple of days,” Jones said.

AI can point the way, but the repairs remain hands-on work. When Miss Piggy wouldn’t run properly this year, former Living Computers engineer Josh Dersch stepped through the machine’s microcode one instruction at a time using a recreated DEC test board, until he found a single bad chip. A spare board brought it back.

Asked if it still takes a human to keep these machines running, Jones agreed — at least for now. If a humanoid robot with AI built in ever shows up to do the repairs, he joked, “then I sit at the front desk all day.”

Nearly everything at ICM is meant to be touched, switched on and played with, and that can be intimidating. On machines with nothing but a text prompt, “you kind of have to know the commands in your head already,” Jones said.

That’s by design. From the start, the idea was to challenge people “either by confronting them with their past or demonstrating how difficult it was,” Jones said. His example: two friends, one with an Apple IIe and one with an IBM PC, whose computers “could never share” software. ICM continues that legacy, he said, “challenging the visitor to go beyond what they might feel comfortable with.”

Volunteers have written cheat sheets to help newcomers, and Jones is encouraged when families come in and kids start typing BASIC programs. “That to me was the spirit of what PDP Planet and Living Computers was meant to be,” he said.

Very little is behind glass. Jones jokes that a Timex Sinclair 1000, which would wipe your painstakingly typed program if you bumped it, is “the only computer that deserves to be behind glass.” The goal, he said, is to avoid “a boneyard that people walk through.”

Other highlights include Galaxy Game, the first coin-operated video game, installed at Stanford in 1971 and now being restored, and a Thinking Machines CM-2 supercomputer, whose 40th anniversary ICM just celebrated with members of its original design team.

For younger engineers raised on modern tools, Jones has a go-to move. He asks if they’ve used Linux, then walks them over to the PDP-7, the type of machine Unix was first written on more than 50 years ago. Many of the basic commands they type every day started there.

The collection totals about 6,000 artifacts. Volunteers are also slowly working through roughly 3,000 magnetic tapes and disk packs from Living Computers’ software preservation program. And ICM keeps 32 historic systems online, where members can log in from anywhere to use vintage operating systems, programming languages and games.

That remote access helps explain why ICM has about 500 members and sponsors, even though only 81 members have visited in person this year. Memberships start at $36 a year. Along with a King County grant through 4Culture, they help cover rent, with personal funds from Jones filling the gap. The museum hopes to become self-sustaining within five years, and maybe someday buy its own building.

The museum’s name reflects the urgency. “We’re in this magic window right now, where the hardware can still run, we can still maintain it, you can replace parts,” Jones said. “But slowly we’re getting further and further away.”

ICM is open Saturdays through Mondays from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. Admission is $9 for adults and free for those under 18. Last May, the museum revived Vintage Computer Festival Pacific Northwest, which had been canceled in March 2020. The event drew 981 people, and it returns May 1–2 at the Tukwila Community Center.

Among the machines on the floor is MIT-AI, a DECsystem-2020 that once served as the ARPANET host for MIT’s Artificial Intelligence Lab. As Living Computers shut down in 2024, Christopher Zach, who sold the machine to Allen, told GeekWire he was ready to drive a U-Haul to Seattle to rescue it.

When a friend of Zach’s stopped by ICM’s booth at WorldCon and asked about “Chris’s computer,” Jones had a simple message to pass along: “Please just tell him it’s safe.”

Francis Lawrence Signed On for One ‘Hunger Games’ Movie. Five Films Later, He’s the Keeper of Panem

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The 'Sunrise on the Reaping' director looks back on the pressure of taking over a franchise, his decade-long collaboration with Suzanne Collins, why the series endures, and where things stand on ‘Constantine 2’ and 'Red Rising.'

The morning after the New York premiere of The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, Francis Lawrence is still taking in the view from the night before.

On one red carpet stood nearly every era of Panem. There were the newcomers: Joseph Zada, Whitney Peak and Mckenna Grace, along with franchise first-timers Kieran Culkin, Elle Fanning, Jesse Plemons, Ralph Fiennes and Glenn Close. Tom Blyth, Rachel Zegler and Josh Andrés Rivera who starred in Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes prequel, were in attendance. And there were the originals, Jennifer Lawrence, Josh Hutcherson, Woody Harrelson, Elizabeth Banks, Jena Malone and Willow Shields assembled like the franchise’s own Avengers. “Last night at the premiere, for the first time, we had three generations of cast,” Lawrence tells The Hollywood Reporter of the Nov. 16 event. “There’s something really special about the time we’ve all spent together and the quality of people I’ve gotten to work with.”

Lawrence has been a constant across all of them. Since taking over from director Gary Ross with 2013’s Catching Fire, he has directed every Hunger Games film that followed, working alongside producer Nina Jacobson to turn Suzanne Collins’ dystopian saga following teenagers forced into fatal combat into one of Hollywood’s most dependable franchises. The original four films grossed roughly $2.98 billion worldwide, and Collins’ five books have sold more than 100 million copies globally, growing into one of the most successful young adult publishing franchises in history.

Still, the scale didn’t fully hit him until the final Mockingjay films. Around the release of the films, The Hunger Games: The Exhibition opened to fans, and Lawrence walked through it with his stars.

“Walking through it for the first time with Jen and Josh, and I think Liam [Hemsworth] was there, we were all just stunned that we had made something museum-worthy, with our music, our costumes, our props, pieces of our sets and the lore,” he recalls. “There’s something really special in that, knowing these stories have connected with people in a certain way.”

Amid the success, it’s easy to forget that Lawrence arrived in Panem as an outsider. He had never made a sequel to someone else’s first film, and on Catching Fire he was “joining an existing family,” with a cast “so big and so seasoned” that he felt he had a lot to prove. He also assumed it would be a one-time job.

“The weird thing is, when I signed on to Catching Fire, I thought that was going to be the only one I did,” he says.

Instead, the studio asked him to stay for the two-part Mockingjay. “A huge part of me is very appreciative of being invited to keep making these movies and be part of this team.”

That invitation, however, came with a nerve-racking stretch. A few weeks into shooting Mockingjay, production paused for a month so Lawrence and the team could promote and release Catching Fire. He would face its box office verdict with most of the next film’s shoot still ahead of him.

“Man, what if this thing bombs?” he remembers thinking of the pressure for Catching Fire success amid filming the third installment. “I now still have like 125 days of shooting with all of these people that are going to be looking at me like, ‘We just made a movie with this guy that bombed.'”

He needn’t have worried. After its 2013 release, Catching Fire became the franchise’s highest grossing film, and the once outsider became the keeper of the series. “The series was quite popular at that point, and there was a lot of excitement and confidence at the studio, so that was nice,” he says.

When Lawrence and Jacobson returned eight years later with 2023’s The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes, the odds felt less certain. The prequel had no Katniss and centered on the rise of the tyrant Coriolanus Snow, played by Donald Sutherland in the original films with Tom Blyth taking on the role for a younger version.

“A bunch of time had gone by, and you wonder if there’s still an appetite for Hunger Games, especially Hunger Games without Katniss,” he says. “It had a very different structure, and it was a story about the person everybody hates, so a lot of things made me a little nervous.”

Sunrise on the Reaping, which traces the origins of the fan-favorite mentor Haymitch Abernathy, felt different.

“Weirdly, this one I kind of felt less pressure,” Lawrence says. “I felt much more confident that this was one that fans really wanted to see, and it felt like a return to form for movies like Catching Fire and the original.”

There being a new Hunger Games book came as a surprise to Lawrence, who assures he, along with Jacobson, are just as in the dark as everyone else. After more than a decade of working together, Lawrence and Collins have a routine. She keeps quiet while she writes, and only once her publisher confirms she has a book does she bring in Lawrence and Jacobson, always with the same modest question of whether the story is a movie. Lawrence and Jacobson always say yes.

For Sunrise, Lawrence and Jacobson got word of the novel earlier than ever, and the secrecy was tighter as a result. The manuscript was kept at Collins‘ agent’s home, and then then moved to a locked safe kept with Jacobson. Screenwriter Billy Ray wrote on an offline laptop in Jacobson’s office, while Lawrence stayed busy making secret trips to meet with Collins.

“This one we got further in advance, while she was still going back and forth with her editors,” he says. “I had read it a couple of times, Nina had read it a couple of times, and I went to New York to work with Suzanne on breaking down the book into what we thought the movie would be. We hired [original screenwriter] Billy Ray. He was already writing and we were having calls about the draft. So a lot of work was done before the book was even announced, and I couldn’t talk to anybody about it.”

The book and its film adaptation were announced together in June 2024, which brought “a lot of relief,” he says. “At least then people knew we were working on it and that it was the Haymitch story. We just had to keep quiet about the storyline itself.”

That left the biggest challenge: finding a young Haymitch, the carefree kid from District 12 long before he became the drunken, broken mentor Harrelson made iconic. The search led to Zada, the breakout from Prime Video’s We Were Liars and Netflix’s East of Eden. Lawrence put him “through the wringer” in auditions before landing on the actor to carry the beloved character’s origin story. The seeds for the origin story were “planted over twenty years ago” when Collins began the original trilogy, the author reflected in a letter shared on social media amid the release of Sunrise.

Collins praised Zada’s performance as “remarkably powerful and moving.”

Long before he was steering a multibillion-dollar franchise, Lawrence spent years making some of the most memorable music videos of the MTV era for artists like Britney Spears, Lady Gaga, Justin Timberlake and Jennifer Lopez. He doesn’t think much about those days when he’s making a movie, he says, but their influence is still there nearly 20 years later.

“Having done maybe around 120 videos, it gave me a playground to learn the toolkit I have for making things look a certain way,” Lawrence says. “I got to create different worlds, try different things, learn about visual effects and learn about working with performers. So in terms of visual style, I just had confidence in the image in general: if I imagine something, how can I actually make that happen? Because of videos, that became second nature, so I could focus more on performance and story. That’s probably the biggest way it’s affected my moviemaking in general.”

It’s fitting, then, that music runs through Panem. The films have long used it to amplify their themes of resistance. Jennifer Lawrence’s Katniss sings “The Hanging Tree,” which became a rebel anthem. In the prequels, Zegler’s Lucy Gray Baird and Peak’s Lenore Dove are both musicians who capture the hearts of Snow and Haymitch.

Lawrence approaches that music the way he approached his videos: “much more music-forward than lyrics-forward.”

“I take the mood, tone and feel of a song, and most of the ideas I built for my videos were based on the mood of the song rather than the lyrics. Not always, but I’d say 90 percent of the time. It’s the same here: I’m primarily thinking about the mood, tone and emotional value of music and what I want something to feel like,” he explains.

Music is also common ground with Collins. “She’s a real music aficionado with a library in her head, and I had long conversations with her and with [executive music producer and songwriter] Dave Cobb about the creation of songs. She [Collins] usually has a historical background for why and how she wrote certain things, and I would talk to Dave, our music producer, mostly about how I wanted songs to feel. So whether it’s ‘The Hanging Tree,’ a Covey song or ‘The Old Therebefore,’ it’s really about mood,” he says.

The music doesn’t stop when the cameras roll. “I also listen to a lot of music when I shoot,” Lawrence says. “Often I’ll be watching a scene with music I have as inspiration playing in my ear, whether it’s score from our movies or from other movies, because I’m evoking a mood, a tone and an emotional value I want to feel in the scene. So sometimes I’m listening to dialogue and to music on my phone at the same time.”

His usual choices: The franchise’s scores, of which there are five movies worth, and “Lily’s Theme,” Alexandre Desplat’s piece from Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows – Part 2.

“That has always felt very Hunger Games-ish to me,” he says of the Potter score. “I also listened to some more disturbing stuff for this one, because I thought of it as a bit scarier. Instead of listening to action-oriented music during fight sequences, I’d listen to things that are a little more disturbing and scary. So it was kind of all over the place.“

Building worlds became Lawrence’s calling card when he made the leap to features. His 2005 debut, Constantine, was a dark take on the DC Comics antihero, and he followed it by envisioning a New York City post-plague for the Will Smith hit I Am Legend (2007). Then came the Great Depression-era romance Water for Elephants (2011), a reunion with Jennifer Lawrence on the spy thriller Red Sparrow (2018), and more recently Slumberland (2022) and the Stephen King adaptation The Long Walk (2025).

The genres vary, but in hindsight, the dystopian streak running through several of them looks a lot like a rehearsal for the road to the Panem Capitol. Amid revisiting Hunger Games in the prequels, the director says he’s “trying to” return to where it all began with his first feature, Constantine. It was announced in 2022 that Lawrence would helm a sequel with star Keanu Reeves reprising his role and Akiva Goldsman writing the script. “We have a script,” Lawrence confirms.

Part of the appeal of a sequel is getting the band back together. The other part is unfinished business with the ratings board, and this time Lawrence wants to lean into the R rating he once tried to avoid.

“We got saddled with an R rating even though we followed all the PG-13 rules, and that was a tough blow,” he says. “On Hunger Games, every time I turn in a cut, it’s an R, we go back and forth a little, and we get to PG-13. With Constantine, they said, ‘We’ve stopped taking notes. There’s no getting a PG-13 on this movie.’ So we were stuck. Part of it was: let’s make another one because we enjoyed it, but this time let’s make the hard-R version. If we’re going to get an R, let’s make the R-rated Constantine.”

However, the road to a sequel has been anything but smooth.

“We’ve been trying, and we’ve hit roadblock after roadblock,” Lawrence says. “The character was given to somebody to make a network television show, which I never watched, and I don’t think it lasted long.” He continues, “Then J.J. [Abrams] somehow had the rights, or control of the character, for a while and was going to do something with Constantine. But he switched over, and he’s now involved in our project too. He’s a great guy, and he’s pro making this Constantine movie, which is fantastic.”.

The other obstacle, Lawrence says, is “there were new people running DC with their own plans for the characters.”

Though he doesn’t name anyone, Lawrence notes there has since been a regime change at DC. It was announced on Nov. 9 that James Gunn and Peter Safran are stepping down as co-CEOs of DC studios four years after taking the job. Skydance, which now oversees the division following the acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery, has appointed former Marvel exec Jeremy Latcham to the post of president and chief creative officer of DC Studios.

With new leadership in place, and with relationships of his own among the executives now running Warner Bros., Lawrence sees an opening. “Maybe there’s some renewed hope to get Constantine through,” he says.

“I’m a believer that we could make a sequel to our Constantine with Keanu, and if people at DC wanted to do a different version of Constantine in some sort of shared universe, they could do that, and the two could coexist.”

Constantine isn’t the only long-gestating project on his plate. With Sunrise now in theaters, Lawrence is also turning his attention to Red Rising, Pierce Brown’s bestselling sci-fi book series, which he has been developing with the author, a friend, for about eight years.

Development of an adaptation first began as a series at Netflix, with two pilot scripts he calls “fantastic,” but it stalled. “There’s just regime changes, and it was an expensive buy-in for them,” he says. “We kept getting shifted around to different departments.”

“They never really pulled the trigger,” he adds. “Now there’s a renewed excitement because we’re at Paramount and it’s theatrical again. We have a screenwriter working on it, so I’m very excited.”

All roads seemingly lead back to Panem, with Lawrence acknowledging how Red Rising can have a clear thread back to Hunger Games character Plutarch Heavensbee, the Gamemaker turned rebel played by Plemons in Sunrise and the late Philip Seymour Hoffman in the original films. “One of the things that I love about Plutarch is that Plutarch is a bit of an infiltrator,” Lawrence says. Red Rising is an infiltration story too. The story follows Darrow, described as “a rebel forged by tragedy, battling to lead his oppressed people to freedom.”

“It allows characters to really question themselves, cross weird moral boundaries and question the people around them. What happens when the people you hate suddenly become more relatable than some of the people that you’re fighting for?” he says. “I’ve always been fascinated by those kinds of stories.”

Also on deck is BioShock, an adaptation of 2K Games’ 2007 first-person shooter. “We’re doing a rewrite on a script now,” Lawrence says. “I think that’s getting closer and closer.” In fact, he had a call about the project scheduled for shortly after his conversation with THR.

Thirteen years after the Hunger Games franchise began, the odds remain firmly in Panem’s favor. In its opening weekend, Sunrise on the Reaping grossed $120 million, becoming its fourth-best debut in franchise history, besting 2015’s second Mockingjay movie ($102.6 million) and 2023 prequel The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes ($44.6 million) but behind 2013’s Catching Fire ($158 million), 2012’s series-launching The Hunger Games ($152.5 million) and 2014’s first Mockingjay ($121.8 million).

For Lawrence, who has lived in this world for more than a decade, the prequels have done more than extend the franchise. They’ve changed how he understands it.

“Going through each of these books page by page with Suzanne over time has given me a pretty deep knowledge of the world and the lore. But now that she’s writing these prequels and reframing characters, I’m also learning more each time she releases a book,” he says.

He counts himself a fan, but perhaps not the biggest one on set. “I’m sure there are super fans, even people like Mckenna Grace [Maysilee Donner in Sunrise], who is a super fan of these stories, who know more about all the little nooks and crannies than I do,” he says with a laugh.

After so many years of working side by side with Collins, the page and the screen have started to merge in his mind. “The lines between the book and the script start to blur, and you forget what’s just in the movies and what’s exactly from the book,” he says.

Plenty of dystopian YA series followed in The Hunger Games‘ wake, Divergent and The Maze Runner among them, but none has lasted the way Panem has. Lawrence credits Collins’ finger on the pulse of the world as the reason for the story maintaining its popularity.

“Part of what originally separated these books from other YA publications: they had real meat on the bone thematically,” Lawrence says of the story’s lasting appeal. “She also created a world, a narrative and characters people fell in love with: Katniss, Peeta, Effie Trinket. But if they didn’t have the ideas underneath them, they would feel a bit empty and surface-level, and they wouldn’t land the same way. That’s one of the things I feel really lucky about, honestly: we get to make movies that lots of people go to see, but that really have something to say about the world we live in. For me, that’s why they endure.”

Does telling this story in today’s climate feel different than it did when the original films came out? “A little bit,” Lawrence says. But the ideas, he argues, have always been built to last.

“Suzanne’s original inspiration was watching war footage, then switching channels and seeing reality game shows, and she decided to write this series about the consequences of war for teens. At the time, it was about an idea that was relevant in the moment but also timeless. That’s what she does: she looks at the world and finds ideas that are relevant but weirdly still timeless,” he says.

Whether Collins has more to say in Panem is a question only she can answer. In the meantime, the world she built keeps showing up in the real one. Fans dress as the characters at premieres, Lawrence notes, and the films’ songs and three-finger salute have even appeared at protests, “whether it’s Hungary, whether it’s Paris.”

“So much of what is created is so disposable,” he says, “and I feel like luckily Hunger Games is not.”

As the 2026 Major League Baseball offseason continues to unfold, something vitally important to the future of the sport quietly hums in the background: the ongoing negotiations over a new collective bargaining agreement.

The collective bargaining agreement, or CBA (or Basic Agreement, as it's known in a formal sense), is the negotiated accord between players and clubs -- i.e., the team owners and commissioner Rob Manfred, who represents their interests -- that governs the working relationship between the two parties, typically for a period of five years. It covers things as mundane as players' meal allowances and travel protocols and as vital as minimum salaries, the structure of free agency, revenue-sharing specifics, and roster sizes. Every CBA can be distilled down to a fight over money, or, more specifically, how the league's revenues will be divided between players and team owners.

The first CBA covered the 1968 and 1969 seasons and was the first in professional sports history. That was the handiwork of Marvin Miller, the pioneering head of the Players Association (i.e., the players union) and belated Hall of Famer. It was Miller's organizing skills, foresight, and training as a union economist that allowed him to make the Players Association a viable and effective labor organization. Every CBA negotiation from 1972 through 1994-95 resulted in some form and scale of labor stoppage, whether a players' strike or an owner-implemented lockout. After the cancellation of the 1994 World Series because of the worst labor stoppage in the sport's history, a long run of peace followed. That peace lasted until the prior CBA negotiations, which resulted in a 99-day lockout forced by team owners and delayed the start of the 2022 season.

The current CBA negotiations have been underway for some time, and not surprisingly, the two sides remain far apart on several key issues. The biggest fight will be over the owners' renewed desire to implement a salary cap, which the union continues to view as a non-starter. The push for a cap has less to do with the notions of competitive balance and more to do with the ownership's internal belief that being the only non-salary-capped league among the major North American sports is harming franchise values. The cap, though, is but one of many pressing issues on which players and owners must find congruity.

Moving forward, the process will be uncertain and rife with posturing and pointed public comments from both sides. The hope, though, is that a new CBA will be agreed to before the middle of March 2027, at which point there's a high risk of having the 2027 season delayed or even abbreviated.

To keep you informed of the twists and turns along the way to a new CBA, we've provided a detailed timeline of negotiations. That timeline will illuminate the important and finer points of the talks and, along the way, explain the implications of the various proposals coming from each side. The hope, of course, is that all of it culminates in a timely agreement that permits a fully intact and on-time 2027 season.

In earlier iterations of negotiations, MLB proposed a salary-escrow system like the ones found in the NBA and NHL. This would mean a percentage of player salaries is withheld in case league revenue projections fall short for a given year. If that's the case, then that money in escrow goes back to the league to cover the revenue shortfalls. If revenues are higher than projected, that excess is shared with the players. The MLBPA has pointedly opposed the escrow system on the grounds that it undermines the notion of guaranteed player salaries. In its latest statement on the negotiations, MLB withdrew the escrow proposal. "For months, union leadership has attacked the escrow provision used in other sports and mischaracterized the issue as the end of guaranteed contracts," MLB spokesperson Glen Caplin said in a statement. "MLB took another step today by withdrawing our escrow proposal, removing any doubt that every single guaranteed player contract will remain fully guaranteed in the new salary cap and floor system."

I Learned How Baby Carrots Are Made &amp; Haven't Bought Them Since—Here's Why

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

I have some very strong feelings about a very small vegetable.

As a lover of all veggies, I love every type of carrot out there—except packaged baby carrots. That’s right: those bright orange cylinders with suspiciously perfect rounded corners that lurk in vending machines, school lunchboxes, and convenience store cold cases across our nation. They might be easy to eat on the go and better for you than a bag of Cheetos, but there’s a lot more going on behind the scenes of this seemingly simple healthy snack.

Here’s the truth about baby carrots—and why you might want to think twice about snagging a bag.

The bag might claim otherwise, but packaged “baby carrots” are typically larger carrots chopped into 2-inch pieces, then peeled and polished down to that perfect snackable size. Real baby carrots are immature carrots harvested before they reach full size. They’re sold in bunches—unpeeled, often with the tops still attached.

While a whole carrot has a delicate, nuanced sweetness, the taste of these so-called baby carrots is best described as... factory-fresh. Don’t believe me? Grab a bag of regular carrots and do a side-by-side taste test.

Baby carrots were originally “invented” in the 1980s to make misshapen or broken carrots sellable. This was a smart way to reduce food waste and save money, but it also helped reinforce the unrealistic expectation that produce must look perfect in order to taste good. Today, baby-cut carrots are grown and processed to meet specific standards for size and shape, all to produce those identical little orange cylinders.

Here’s the bottom line: When you buy a bag of baby carrots, you’re buying into a world that’s seemingly as obsessed with unnatural perfection as the modern-day beauty industry.

Energy conservation is top of mind for consumers these days, but making baby carrots look so uniform takes a lot of machinery. After they’re sorted, they chop the carrots into roughly 2-inch pieces and shave them—twice. Then, they go through a rough cutter, which gives them a general shape, before being polished to create those unrealistically perfect rounded edges. That’s a lot of extra work to make something that was completely edible to begin with look a certain way.

To prevent bacterial growth, baby carrots may be rinsed with a diluted chlorine solution before packaging. They’re then rinsed with water, and the chlorine treatment itself is an “acceptable” food-safety practice which doesn’t make baby carrots unsafe to eat. Still, do you really want your “fresh” snack to have undergone chlorine treatments of any kind? For me, that’s a hard no, and I’d rather buy whole carrots and wash and cut them myself.

Because baby carrots have been peeled and cut, they're more prone to drying out and developing something called “carrot blush,” a thin white film caused by dehydration. The film itself is harmless, but it certainly doesn’t make them look appetizing. Worse, they can turn slimy inside the bag, even before you open it. And that’s downright gross.

Prices vary depending on where you shop, but baby-cut carrots can cost more per pound than regular whole carrots. You're essentially paying extra for someone else to handle the peeling, cutting, and polishing. For a task that takes barely any time at home, I'd rather keep the difference in my wallet.

Seriously. Enjoy the taste and texture of whole carrots, potentially save some money, and skip the extra processing by simply buying a bag of whole carrots and cutting them yourself. It’ll take you less time than you spent reading this article.

Scientists Build 6,986 Digital Brain Twins to Study Dementia

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An international consortium has created personalized biophysical “digital brain twins” for 6,986 individuals across 16 countries to uncover how aging, neurodegenerative diseases, and environmental conditions shape neural dynamics. The computational models simulate excitation-inhibition balance across 90 connected brain regions, revealing distinct mechanistic signatures for Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal degeneration, and environmental exposures ranging from air pollution to democratic governance.

Standard magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides an invaluable window into the living human brain, yet it records only indirect proxies of neural activity, typically relying on group averages that obscure individual variation and say little about the underlying cellular mechanisms driving those signals.

Now, an international research team led by corresponding author Dr. Agustín Ibáñez (Scientific Director of BrainLat at Universidad Adolfo Ibáñez, strategic director of core scientific assets at the BarcelonaBeta Brain Research Center, and faculty at the Global Brain Health Institute at Trinity College Dublin) and first author Dr. Rubén Herzog (BrainLat and Distinguished Professor at the University of the Balearic Islands) has transformed static brain scans into dynamic, personalized virtual laboratories.

Published today in Nature Biomedical Engineering, the study introduces biophysical “digital brain twins” constructed from resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) scans of 6,986 individuals spanning ages 18 to 100 across 16 countries.

Rather than treating brain scans as passive statistical snapshots, biophysical brain twins apply mathematical equations grounded in neurobiology to simulate how populations of excitatory and inhibitory neurons interact across 90 connected brain regions.

For each participant, the investigators tuned their computational model until its simulated fMRI output closely matched the individual’s actual resting-state brain recordings. Independent validation showed that model-derived regional excitability closely correlated with structural grey matter volume, confirming that the virtual simulations capture real biological architecture.

“Every brain ages in a different context. Our biophysical twins help us study how the variations in our biology and the social and environmental conditions of our lives relate to brain function,” explained Ibáñez. “The importance of biophysical brain twins is that they connect what brain scans record with the underlying processes that could produce those signals. Each twin acts as a personalised virtual laboratory: researchers can adjust how its neural populations interact and examine how those changes affect the whole brain. This opens a route from observing differences to testing possible explanations, mechanisms, potential treatments and interventions.”

“MRI records indirect measures of neural activity. Biophysical twins let us simulate the neural processes that might generate those signals and explore why they differ from person to person,” added Herzog.

Applying these nearly 7,000 personalized models across diverse clinical cohorts exposed clear mechanistic boundaries between normal aging and dementia:

To overcome the historical divide between neuroimaging and real-world lived context, the team linked individual brain models with macro-level environmental indicators across 10 domains alongside personal socioeconomic histories.

Robust associations emerged in seven of the ten environmental domains, most prominently in indicators measuring democratic conditions (free choices, civic participation, political representation, and rule of law), soil and water quality, and air pollution. Complementary analyses of individual life circumstances further linked brain dynamics to healthcare access, childhood experiences, and financial stability. These relationships persisted even after controlling for age, sex, clinical diagnosis, country, and MRI scanner hardware.

By releasing their open-source code and model parameters, the authors have established a global testbed for in silico experimentation. Future studies can overlay neurotransmitter receptor maps onto these digital twins—simulating how targeted modulation of glutamate (excitatory) or GABA (inhibitory) signaling, or public health improvements in air quality and healthcare access, might shift an aging brain back toward healthy functional patterns.

What is a biophysical “digital brain twin”?

A digital brain twin is a personalized computer simulation built from an individual’s fMRI scan using biological equations. By modeling how 90 connected brain regions interact, it allows researchers to estimate underlying cellular processes, such as the balance between signals that excite or inhibit neurons—and test virtual treatments safely on a computer.

How do brain dynamics differ between normal aging and dementia?

In the study of nearly 7,000 people, normal aging primarily changed how regularly or unpredictably brain activity fluctuated over time. In contrast, Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal degeneration disrupted overall activity levels, variability, and the balance between excitatory and inhibitory brain signals all at once.

Can environmental and social conditions really affect how the brain functions?

Yes. Even after accounting for age, sex, diagnosis, and scanner differences, researchers found that environmental factors, especially democratic conditions, soil and water quality, air pollution, healthcare access, and financial circumstances, were measurably linked to underlying simulated brain dynamics.

Biophysical brain twins map the phenotype–expotype architecture of ageing and dementia

Biophysical whole-brain models may reveal causal links between neural dynamics and biological–environmental variation, yet no framework has explained how these factors become embedded in mechanistic dynamics.

Here we develop individualized biophysical digital twins mechanistically linked to phenotypes and expotypes (that is, combinations of exposomal influences) leveraging resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging data (n = 6,986, 16 countries) of healthy controls and participants with mild cognitive impairment, Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal dementia.

Personalized twins improved goodness-of-fit over group-level models. They reproduced structure–function coupling between regional excitability and brain volume, captured age, sex and neurodegeneration, and encoded 68 expotypes.

Associations remained robust after controlling for demographics, data quality, model fit and scanner/site, with the strongest effects for social and physical (pollution) exposomes. We release an open resource (empirical data, code, >3 million simulations) for future research.

These findings ground phenotypic–exposomic variation in individualized biophysical twins, enabling in silico testing of mechanisms and potential model-informed interventions.

Bitcoin hardware wallet maker Coinkite has detailed how a security flaw in its Coldcard devices went undetected for more than five years despite several independent security reviews, including those using artificial intelligence (AI).

In a Nov. 23 postmortem report, Coinkite said that while its security checks confirmed the wallets had the correct system for generating secure recovery phrases, the company never verified whether the wallets were actually using it. As a result, a software error caused the wallets to use a weaker system instead, making the recovery phrases easier for hackers to guess.

A Bitcoin hardware wallet is a physical device that stores private keys offline, while a recovery phrase is a series of words that allows users to restore access to their funds if the device is lost or damaged.

Coinkite said at least 15 independent security reviews and research efforts failed to catch the flaw before the thefts began. It added that the weaker system went undetected because the recovery phrases looked normal and could still pass some security tests.

The flaw allowed hackers to steal at least 1,789 Bitcoin (BTC), worth around $114.7 million at the time of the thefts, Galaxy Research reported in August. Coinkite said it fixed the flaw on July 31 and introduced further security measures in August.

The report says that as of Nov. 18, Coinkite had received around 300 loss-related support tickets associated with 242 accounts. The company also said that the actual number of victims was likely higher and that it is still exploring ways to help affected customers but has yet to decide on a compensation plan.

Coinkite said it still does not know who stole the funds or how the hackers found the flaw. The company said law enforcement authorities are also investigating the thefts, and the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security has opened a separate case.

The company continues to urge affected users to update their software, create new recovery phrases and move their funds to new wallets, warning that software updates cannot make previously affected recovery phrases safe.

Cointelegraph contacted Coinkite for comment on its compensation plans but had not received a response by publication.

2 Montgomery County magnet stories raise 1 question about access

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

“Minorities in Magnets” was the winner of the ABA Section of Civil Rights and Social Justice’s 2026 High School Civil Rights Film Competition.

A high school filmmaker saw a problem in Montgomery County, Maryland’s selective programs: Lack of representation of students of color had an impact on their educational experiences. At nearly the same time, a lawsuit challenging admissions to the county’s middle school magnet programs reached the U.S. Supreme Court.

Although these stories involve different programs and raise different questions, they share an important issue: How do we determine whether an admissions system provides fair access to educational opportunities, and what evidence should matter?

The ABA Section of Civil Rights and Social Justice recognized Minorities in Magnets, directed by Toftaa Fedhaa, as the first place winner of the section’s 2026 High School Civil Rights Film Competition. Her film explores the experiences of students of color in the Montgomery County Public Schools’ high school magnet programs and the effects of their underrepresentation.

Separately, on Oct. 19, the Supreme Court agreed to hear Association for Education Fairness v. Montgomery County Board of Education. The case concerns admissions to four selective middle school magnet programs, not the high school programs discussed in the film.

While the court did not rule on whether the district’s admissions policy is discriminatory, it agreed to consider a narrower legal question about the evidence plaintiffs must present when challenging a facially neutral admissions policy as racially discriminatory.

The Association for Education Fairness alleges that Montgomery County’s admissions policies intentionally disadvantaged Asian American students. The school district maintains that its admissions system is designed to provide equitable access to rigorous educational opportunities.

After initially challenging a “field test,” the plaintiffs challenged a revised lottery-based system introduced for the 2021-2022 school year. That system screened students for their potential to benefit from enriched instruction and then used a lottery to assign students to magnet programs or enriched instruction at their neighborhood schools.

The federal district court dismissed the challenge to the revised plan, finding that the plaintiffs had not adequately alleged discriminatory effect or intent. The 4th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals at Richmond, Virginia, affirmed, applying circuit precedent on disparate impact allegations.

The Supreme Court will now consider whether a plaintiff challenging a facially neutral admissions policy must demonstrate that the affected group experienced an aggregate “disparate impact” compared with its share of the applicant pool.

Put simply, “disparate impact” refers to a policy that produces unequal outcomes for a particular group, even when the policy does not expressly classify individuals by race. An equal protection challenge also raises the question of discriminatory purpose.

The court’s decision will address the legal standards governing these types of claims and whether a particular showing of aggregate impact is required before such a claim may proceed, not whether Montgomery County’s policy ultimately violates the Constitution. The court has not yet decided the merits of the case.

The film underscores why the conversation should not only focus exclusively on admissions policies, statistics and legal standards but should also consider the voices and experiences of students.

The file and the lawsuit should not be confused or treated as evidence of one another. They involve different grade levels, programs and legal questions. The experiences described in the film do not establish what happened in the middle school admissions process, just as the allegations in the lawsuit do not resolve the concerns raised by high school students.

Although selective educational programs can open doors to advanced coursework, specialized instruction and opportunities that shape a student’s academic future, simply making a program available to everyone does not necessarily mean everyone has an equal opportunity to participate.

We must also consider who knows these programs exist, how students are identified and encouraged to apply, what barriers may discourage participation, and whether students feel represented and welcome once they are admitted.

These are important questions for educators, families, policymakers and communities to consider. They may also inform legal analysis, although the applicable legal standards determine what evidence is relevant to a particular claim.

For lawyers, the Supreme Court case highlights the importance of understanding how statistical evidence, applicant pools and allegations of discriminatory intent intersect when challenging facially neutral policies. It also reinforces the need to examine the specific admissions process and how that process may have changed over time. The court’s decision may affect how courts evaluate statistical evidence in challenges to facially neutral admissions policies.

For school officials, families and advocates, the broader questions are equally important:

• How are students identified and informed about selective educational opportunities?

• What criteria determine eligibility and admission?

• Are admissions outcomes regularly examined across schools and student populations?

• Are the policies and measures used to assess access transparent and understandable?

These questions deserve attention regardless of how the Supreme Court ultimately resolves the legal issue before it.

The case is also a reminder that we must distinguish between allegations in a lawsuit, a school district’s stated reasons for its policies, procedural court decisions, and a final ruling on the merits. Understanding those distinctions is essential to an informed discussion.

The winning film, Minorities in Magnets, brings student perspectives into a debate that is often reduced to admissions criteria and demographic statistics. The Supreme Court will address a specific legal question about the evidence required in an equal protection challenge.

Neither alone answers the broader question of what meaningful educational opportunity should look like. But they invite a broader conversation about how communities define opportunity, how they measure access, and how they respond when students say the system is not working for them.

As lawyers and members of our communities, we have an obligation to listen, ask difficult questions, and examine whether the systems we create are providing genuine opportunities for all students.

Ultimately, educational access is about more than who gets admitted. It is also about who gets the opportunity to succeed, who feels that they belong, and whether every student has a fair chance to reach their full potential.

On the one hand, it’s bright and colorful, with enough goofiness and action to serve as an acceptable diversion for family audiences over the long holiday weekend. But it’s hobbled by an overly familiar story that never really does anything to make it memorable.

Hexed is the first non-sequel to come out from Walt Disney Animation since 2023's Wish, which earned a 48 percent on Rotten Tomatoes from critics and made just $254 million worldwide. It's too early to call this movie's true RT score, but there's similarly middling reception so far. AV Club's Jacob Oller had this to say:

The basic beats of the ensuing adventure are painfully predictable, with all the rescue attempts, training sequences, familial bonding moments, and climactic showdowns coming right on cue. But these checkboxes aren’t just predictable, they’re the most obvious versions of themselves.

The movie features Hailee Steinfeld voicing a rebellious teenage witch named Billie, as she finds out she has magical powers. Rashida Jones voices her overprotective mom, and Jodie Foster voices the movie's villainous witch, Celeste. The movie was co-written by Walt Disney Animation's CCO Jared Bush and Moana 2's Dana Ledoux Miller.

Both of these reviews seem to sing a similar tune. They seem to find Hexed to be predictable and pretty unimaginative. The Guardian's Benjamin Lee had comparable thoughts, writing these words to go with his two-out-of-five-star review:

Like Wish, it’s a film clearly crafted by people who have deep affection for the Disney back catalogue but who can’t quite turn reverence for what came before into any real idea of where to take things going forward. Again, there’s something too calculated and businesslike, with nods to previous films and regurgitated life lessons, and while Disney adventures have always emerged from a Goliath-sized mega-corporation, the best ones can make you briefly forget that.

The Hollywood Reporter's Angie Han had more positive things to say about Hexed, but made a point of saying the visuals dazzled her more than the storyline itself. In her words:

More than as a fairy tale, an inspirational fable or a complex exploration of the bonds between mothers and daughters, Hexed shines brightest as a travelogue through an enchanting world only accessible through the portal of a screen. If that’s not enough to make for a new cinematic classic, it’s more than enough to make for a pleasurably transportive trip to the theater.

It's certainly worth pointing out that Hexed will likely be much more of a hit with its target audience of young girls. That remains to be seen, but not all the reviews are bummers. The Wrap's Amelia Emberwing celebrated the movie's girl power and made these comments:

This is a film that is boldly sure of its own identity, and refuses to be anything other than blissfully witchy and womanly. The girl power aspects never extend into girl-bossery, and the film offers not just exciting normals that should be discussed more often in young adult media, but an ending that, while the logical conclusion of the story, also goes against traditional Disney norms and the standard teen movie tropes.

With regard to the box office, Hexed is projected to make around $40 million behind the new Hunger Games, which isn't great. As a lifelong Disney fan who wants to support original animation and see it thrive, I'm concerned. The studio really needed an original hit, and so far the news isn't trending up.

As with all reviews we talk about, we definitely recommend you check out the movie anyway, especially if you were already looking forward to it. Who knows, maybe you'll disagree? Oh, and Hexed has a Lilo & Stitch short before the movie not to miss. Hexed hits theaters on Wednesday!
