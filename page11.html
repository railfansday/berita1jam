real motors brings futuristic project: ARES electric motorcycle to life with new prototypes

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

with sculptural bodywork and an analog-inspired experience, project: ARES is preparing for its first 250 production units.

South Carolina-based Real Motors has brought its futuristic electric café racer, Project: ARES, onto the road, with new working prototypes and a limited production run planned for 2027. First introduced in 2024, the motorcycle attracted attention for its sculptural bodywork and sci-fi influences. Two years later, the company has opened preorders for its first 250 units, while a recent independent test ride offers a closer look at how the machine actually performs.

The latest prototypes retain the distinctive proportions of the original design, with broad body panels wrapping around the electric drivetrain and a sharply tapered seat extending over the rear wheel. In bright red, the 03 prototype takes on the appearance of a futuristic racing machine, complete with oversized white graphics and a rectangular headlight. Its front forks are concealed beneath prominent aerodynamic covers, giving the motorcycle a particularly solid presence from the front.

Another prototype by Real Motors, numbered 04 and nicknamed ‘Ghost,’ explores the same design in black. Its dark, translucent bodywork allows glimpses of the mechanical components beneath the surface, including the electrical wiring running through the motorcycle. Without the familiar arrangement of a combustion engine and fuel tank, the design team has been able to reconsider the proportions of a traditional café racer, creating a continuous surface across much of the bike’s body.

Beneath this exterior, the electric powertrain has already demonstrated approximately 100 horsepower, with Real Motors reporting 1,000 Nm of torque at the rear wheel after gearing. The prototype carries an estimated weight of 485 pounds, giving it considerably more mass than its streamlined appearance might suggest. The low handlebars place the rider in the familiar forward-leaning café racer position, with the broad central body occupying much of the space between the knees.

The riding experience is central to the project for Real Motors. The company describes its approach as ‘Electric Analogue,’ an attempt to preserve the physical connection between rider and machine even as the traditional engine gives way to an electric drivetrain. The controls remain familiar, while a small digital display provides essential riding information. The emphasis is on the sensation of operating the motorcycle and the immediate response of its electric motor.

During a recent test ride published by The Verge, journalist Tim Stevens found the Ghost prototype’s acceleration surprisingly smooth, with power delivered progressively across three riding modes. Its handling told a slightly different story. The motorcycle required more deliberate steering through corners than its racing-inspired silhouette might suggest, with the substantial battery weight contributing to its character on the road. The experience offers an early look at how Real Motors’ unconventional design translates into an actual riding machine.

Real Motors is now preparing Lot 01, a limited production run of 250 motorcycles worldwide. The currently listed production configuration uses a 65-kW electric motor paired with a 13.4-kWh battery, with an estimated top speed of 110 mph. These specifications differ from those of the higher-powered prototypes and Founder’s Edition. Prices for Lot 01 begin at $20,000, with North American deliveries currently anticipated in early 2027.

The company is also offering a selection of exterior liveries, allowing customers to choose how the sculptural bodywork will be finished. Its earlier Founder’s Edition is already sold out, while final specifications for Lot 01 are expected to be disclosed before manufacturing begins. The production timeline has shifted since the motorcycle’s initial announcement, but with working prototypes now on the street, Project: ARES is steadily approaching its first customer deliveries.

Tesla Extends Cybertruck Production Run To Work Through Order Backlog

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tesla will now make the Cybertruck for a little while longer to fulfill more orders, Elon Musk said on Wednesday.

"In order to work through more of the Cybertruck order backlog, production will be extended through the end of summer 2027," he said in an X post, adding in a reply, "This is not the smartest move from a profit-maximizing standpoint btw, but there must be some room in this world for sentimentality or what’s the point?"

In a late-night tweet last week, Musk announced the forthcoming demise of the stainless-steel pickup in the middle of next year to make more room for production of steering wheel-free Cybercabs at Tesla's Texas factory. He later clarified that the truck would be made for eight more months, pointing to an expiration date of July 2027 for what is surely the strangest and most polarizing pickup ever made.

Now production is poised to last through most of September, giving the Cybertruck a roughly 10-month runway. Musk had previously said Tesla would have to return some deposits as it wouldn't be able to deliver all the trucks customers had ordered before production came to a close.

Elon Musk said Tesla is extending Cybertruck production so it can fulfill more orders.

It's still an untimely end for a model that attracted more controversy than cold, hard sales. Musk once targeted an annual output of 250,000 trucks, which would make it a top seller in the pickup market. Tesla ended up selling some 70,000 of them, per industry estimates, since sales kicked off at the end of 2023.

In part, extending the truck's production run may be a way to placate Tesla's die-hard fans, some of whom expressed outrage at the decision to cancel it so suddenly and unceremoniously. Say what you will about the truck itself, but it has amassed a loyal following like few other vehicles have. Over 5,000 people have signed a petition to keep the Cybertruck in production. Unplugged Performance, which makes Tesla accessories, said it's trying to get investors on board to take over Cybertruck production from Tesla.

Now those Tesla devotees have a little more time to buy a Cybertruck. It'll cost them dearly, though. Since announcing the truck's cancelation, Tesla made the top-trim, tri-motor Cyberbeast the only one available—and hiked its price to $110,000.

How KROQ’s ‘Klein/Ally Show’ Slogged Its Way to the Top in Los Angeles Radio Morning Drive

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As KROQ gets ready to hold the 35th edition of its “Almost Acoustic Christmas” concert event next month, the “world famous” alternative rock station has even more reason to celebrate: Not only did the station hit its best ratings in September since Summer 2004, but KROQ is once again a force in Los Angeles morning drive radio.

Variety noted last year that KROQ has been on a comeback ever since the returns of longtime program director Kevin Weatherly in June 2022 and former morning show host Kevin Ryder, who was given the afternoon drive-time slot in April 2025. But the rebirth of KROQ can also be attributed to the massive growth of “Klein.Ally.Show” in the mornings, which has seen a remarkable rise, particularly over the past year. Related Stories 'The Notebook' Musical Sets West End Run at Newly Renamed Judi Dench Theatre 'The Peanut Butter Falcon' Breakout Zack Gottsagen and Anna Enger Ritch to Star in 'A Reason to Stay' From Director Rippin Sindher (EXCLUSIVE)

In September, KROQ as a whole posted a 6.7 share in the Los Angeles market among adults 25-54 — the station’s highest number in that measurement in 22 years. In October, it rose to No. 1 with 25-54 in the market, ahead of Hot AC KBIG “My FM.”

Meanwhile, this September in L.A. morning drive, hosts Kevin Klein and Ally Johnson were No. 1 in all major demos: 18-34 (10.8 share), 18-49 (9.9 share) and 25-54 (9.4). In 25-54, the show has been tops for six out of ten months so far this year. In October, the show dipped to No. 2 in 25-54, as it continues to battle it out with competitor “The Woody Show” on rival KYSR (“Alt 98.7”). “The Woody Show,” which regained the No. 1 slot in the demo this October, has nonetheless seen its once-hefty advantage over KROQ evaporate as the two duke it out. (“The Woody Show” didn’t do itself any favors by switching out a few of its longtime players, including Renae Ravey, which upset some listeners.)

“This just shows that KROQ, in Southern California, people have a really strong relationship with the station,” Weatherly says. “That can be positive or negative. When you break that trust, they can get really pissed. But you can build it back over time if you have a good morning show and good personalities.”

As recently as a year and a half ago, KROQ’s “Klein/Ally Show” was still slogging through morning drive. Throughout 2024 and the first half of 2025, the show was consistently hovering between 10th and 18th place in Los Angeles’ radio ratings among the key adults 25-54 demo, mirroring the massive declines that KROQ had faced after making some terribly misguided decisions (including changing up its music mix and firing Ryder) at the start of the pandemic in 2020.

Klein (first with Ted Stryker and then along with Ally) was tasked with taking over morning drive after Ryder was let go — and had to bear the brunt of angry KROQ fans for several years. It was a move made by previous management that was made harder by happening right at the start of COVID, when listeners desired the comfort of familiar voices.

“We were put in a bad spot, and we had to really fight to do the type of show we wanted,” Klein says. “There were a lot of meetings we had where we were literally interviewing our replacements. It sounded they were going to add another person to our show, but we could tell what was happening: they were ready to punt. We really did fear that any day our job was going to be stripped from us.”

Klein knew he and Johnson had big shoes to fill: “Kevin & Bean” was an L.A. morning radio institution, running for 30 years (and landing in two broadcasting halls of fame) until Gene “Bean” Baxter left the station at the end of 2019. Ryder continued with co-hosts Allie Mac Kay and Jensen Karp, but the three of them were all fired in April 2020.

“It was a tough position they were put in, replacing Kevin Ryder,” Weatherly says. But it was not only that; then-KROQ program director Mike Kaplan had changed up the station’s music mix, and there was a sense that the once-mighty alternative rocker had permanently lost its way.

When he returned to KROQ, Weatherly quickly tweaked the sound — but he also decided to give “Klein/Ally” time to build as he set out to revive the station. “If you’ve got a really funny show, you ignore the ratings until the ratings prove it right. We believed in the show and knew that it would find its audience if we were patient — and that’s what happened,” he says.

Says Klein: “He basically said, ‘Do the show that you guys want to do and I think it’ll work. But if it doesn’t, at least you did the show you wanted to do and you’ll know you went out doing what you thought was right for LA.’”

At that point, “Klein/Ally” dug in: The show went deeply personal, including Johnson’s reports on her attempts to get pregnant (she was even injecting her IVF treatments live on air).

“I’m sorry you had to see my ass,” Johnson says of the fertility shots, which were shared on the show’s YouTube page. “But we have gotten pretty transparent about our lives, and when big things come up, it feels weird not to talk about them. So when this journey started for my wife and me, it was like, there is going to be some good stuff and some sad stuff. And in a way, I’ve gotten used to processing personal things through this show.”

Johnson says that’s given her and Klein a unique bond with their listeners. When she opened up about her own miscarriage, Johnson heard from emotional listeners who had gone through similar struggles.

But there’s the silly stuff too. The show launched a campaign defending In-N-Out after USA Today proclaimed that the rival Habit chain had the nation’s best burger. “We just knew that’s just patently false,” Klein says, “so we put up a giant billboard next to their billboard that said ‘No. 1 is overrated. In-N-Out’s always better, signed, LA’s 14th most popular radio show.’”

“Klein/Ally” also got some local media attention when Klein’s wife had her car stolen one morning — spurring the hosts to enlist listeners to help them track down the Range Rover (which they found!). And when L.A. eatery Mel’s Diner removed the disgraced Sean “Diddy” Combs’ signed photo from its walls, Klein and Johnson lobbied the restaurant to replace it with their own publicity shot.

“Some of these things were stupid at the time and are still stupid now,” Klein says. “But these were little things that ended up getting out to people. Then I would see comments under the posts, and they would say, like, ‘Oh, I haven’t thought about KROQ in years. I used to love KROQ!’ I think it just reminded people that there’s stuff still happening that’s interesting on KROQ.”

Weatherly had managed to resuscitate KROQ by April 2025, when Ryder’s return sent a signal to former listeners that it was OK to tune back in. (And it worked: Ryder was No. 2 in afternoons this September.) Another former KROQ jock, Doug “Sluggo” Roberts, also returned to middays.

“Over the last couple of years, we definitely have seen listeners coming back to KROQ,” Weatherly says. “And I think as people have come back and discovered ‘Klein/Ally,’ they’re like, ‘This is a really great morning show. They’re real. They’re authentic, vulnerable on the air. They’re funny. I think that Nielsen has finally caught up to it.’”

KROQ and “Klein/Ally” also aggressively expanded ways to sample the morning show. Last summer, the program added an extra hour, broadcasting until 11 a.m. when most morning drive shows wrap at 10. “Klein/Ally” rebroadcasts were also aired at 10 p.m. in order to catch a different audience. And the show went all-in on social media, including its daily livestreams on YouTube.

“It’s been really nice to feel like we have support, even if the ratings weren’t showing it yet, and room to breathe,” Johnson says. “You feel so stifled creatively if someone’s constantly looking over your shoulder, and then the ratings reports you start obsessing over, and you can’t work from that place.”

Klein notes that ratings are fickle: “We couldn’t enjoy it for long because as soon as we were told we were No. 1, we immediately were told by Weatherly that this could be a fluke,” he jokes. “So right now we’re in fluke phase, and we’ll see how long that goes. To someone driving down the street tomorrow, they don’t really care if we’re No. 33 — where we’ve been — or No. 1. They just want to laugh and have fun and feel connected. So that keeps us hungry.”

Adds Weatherly: “We know ratings will go up and go down. But we have confidence in the show that they put out every single day. They’re hitting their stride from a ratings standpoint at the exact same time that we just announced the 35th annual ‘Almost Acoustic Christmas,’ and we felt like we’ve got one of the better lineups we’ve had in years.”

That lineup for the Dec. 12 event — first revealed on “Klein/Ally,” naturally — includes Foo Fighters, Weezer, Sublime, Interpol, Joyce Manor, Julia Wolf, Almost Monday and The Linda Lindas.

“It just it feels good to have everything kind of clicking at the same time,” Weatherly says.

Avelo Brings First Commercial Flights to McKinney

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Avelo operated its inaugural flight from McKinney National Airport in McKinney, Texas, on Wednesday.

Avelo Flight 1848, operated with a Boeing 737-800, was the first commercial airliner service from the airport to Fort Myers, Florida.

“We target the secondary airports of very large metropolitan areas where we can offer a different experience to the customers,” Avelo founder and CEO Andrew Levy said in an interview with AirlineGeeks.

McKinney is the third airport in the Dallas/Fort Worth Metroplex to offer commercial service, with Dallas Love Field and Dallas/Forth Worth International Airport being the other two.

“For a lot of folks we’re going to be closer and a more convenient drive. And hopefully they’ll take advantage of that,” said McKinney airport director Ken Carley.

Carley said the airline has been wanting to be in the market since 2018. McKinney is the first new airport in Texas in 20 years to be certified for commercial air service.

“We had to build all this infrastructure because it did not exist. There was nothing on this side of the airport. So it’s not just this building, it’s the ramp and the taxi lane and all of that stuff, the taxiway that connects to the runway system. So it was a building, all the infrastructure, and meeting all those requirements,” Carley said.

McKinney is about 30 miles northeast of Dallas in Collin County. Levy said the area is growing like crazy.

“What we’re going to offer people who live close to this airport is a very different experience than getting on the highways and driving all the way to DFW or going down to Love Field,” he said.

The Texas-based airline will serve 11 destinations from McKinney: Las Vegas, Tampa, Orlando, Fort Myers, Sarasota/Bradenton, Nashville, West Palm Beach, New Orleans, Denver, Atlanta, and Fort Lauderdale.

“I think it’s phenomenal that Avelo has announced additional routes before they’ve even started service. That’s a testament, I think, to what they’re seeing in the response they’re getting from for bookings. So they think it’s a strong market,” Carley said.

Levy said the approach was to offer a lot of selections and get a better sense of the market.

“All the markets we’re serving have massive proven demand from this area. And so of course, the trick for us is just to get people to know we’re here and then give us a try,” Levy said.

McKinney is a crew base for Avelo, its fifth, with pilots, flight attendants, airport operations personnel, and aircraft technicians, about 150 people behind the operation, Levy said.

She Trained Through Alaskan Winters to Run a Sub-3 Marathon. These Strategies Helped Her Fight the Cold and Stay Consistent.

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Snow, ice, and short days didn’t stop Michelle Baxter from regular runs outdoors.

We earn a commission for products purchased through some links in this article. Why Trust Us?

When winter arrives, it’s tempting to put your running goals on hold until the temperatures warm up, the days get longer, and the roads clear. Cold temperatures make it harder to leave a warm bed, while snow and ice can turn a familiar route into a much slower—and more slippery—run.

For months at a time, Michelle Baxter’s running routes in Anchorage, Alaska, were covered in packed snow and ice. After a snowstorm, plows could take days to reach every neighborhood, and by then, cars had compressed the fallen snow onto the roads. Even after the plows came through, the slick surface often remained.

Baxter—now an RRCA- and V.02-certified run coach—spent 15 years living in Alaska and training through winters like these, figuring out how to keep running through harsh, unmotivating conditions.

She trained for 12 marathons in that time, continuing to build fitness year after year. Eventually, she even achieved her most ambitious running goal: breaking three hours in the marathon. After eight years of attempting to break the barrier—and a heartbreakingly close 3:01:14 at Humpy’s Marathon in Anchorage in August 2021—Baxter finally ran a 2:58:24 at the California International Marathon in December 2021. She has since lowered her PR to 2:56:57 at the Indianapolis Monumental Marathon.

Your winter running routes may not be covered in snow and ice for six months straight, but chances are you’ll face your own challenges as the temperature drops in the coming weeks and months. The good news: Baxter’s experience can help you navigate tough terrain and make the most of your training.

Here’s how you can put her lessons to work this winter so when the ice thaws, you’re ready to run your fastest race yet.

What you wear can make or break a winter run, Baxter says. When you’re too cold, too warm, or worried about taking a spill around every tight corner, it’s hard to focus on any other aspect of the run. Baxter spent years figuring out what works, and her gear advice starts with two essentials: finding reliable footwear and appropriate layers for the conditions.

In Anchorage, finding reliable footing was Baxter’s biggest winter challenge. She initially resisted buying studded shoes, assuming they wouldn’t make much difference. But once she tried them, she was surprised by how much more confident she felt in every step.

For packed snow, Baxter often wore regular trainers fitted with screws in the outsoles, which she added herself.

On sheer ice, Baxter preferred shoes made with built-in carbide studs; she swears by the Icebug brand. “You can wear these shoes and make a 90-degree turn and feel so confident running on ice—like sheer ice,” Baxter says. “They were a game-changer for me.”

You may not encounter sheets of ice on your regular running routes, but consider the conditions you encounter most often and look for an option suited to them.

Now living in Minnesota, Baxter says she reaches for studded shoes if she expects at least half of a run to cover potentially snowy or icy ground. She notes that while the shoes may be slightly less comfortable than your normal trainers, protecting your body from harm is the number-one priority during the winter months of training.

“I just think it’s worth it to feel confident running on the slippery surfaces and not risk injuring yourself,” she says.

One of the most common winter gear mistakes Baxter sees runners make is overdressing. It’s easy to look out the window, see cold weather, and pull on every layer you own in an effort to stay warm on the run. But remember you’ll warm up once you start moving.

Baxter’s rule of thumb echoes the advice of other run coaches: Start your run feeling a little chilly. After about 10 minutes, you should warm up and feel comfortable for the rest of the run.

Don’t forget to consider the wind, too; Baxter generally recommends dressing for the wind chill temperature. She recalls learning this lesson during one particularly windy Alaska run when she dressed for the ambient temperature rather than the wind chill temperature and ended up feeling sick from potential hypothermia.

If you’re unsure where to start when it comes to gearing up for a winter run, use our temperature-based layering guide, then pay attention to how you feel after each run. Were you still cold after warming up? Did you get sweaty and uncomfortable? Use your responses to adjust your layers on your next run.

For Baxter, keeping her legs warm as temperatures approached subzero was a recurring problem. She first turned to wearing multiple pairs of tights, which helped with the cold, but the layers restricted her movement. Eventually, she found a thick, fleece-lined pair of running tights that solved the problem, recommending them to other runners for temperatures ranging from 15 all the way down to minus 10 degrees Fahrenheit.

She made a similar change on top: Instead of wearing a base layer, fleece layer, and wind layer, she found a high-quality warm jacket to wear over a base layer.

Your biggest frustration might be cold hands or wet feet. For these issues, Baxter suggests putting hand warmers in your gloves, running-specific wool socks, and a waterproof shoe.

For visibility during the winter months, Baxter wears a lighted vest. She began treating it as a non-negotiable in her running wardrobe after noticing, as a driver, how much easier it was to see runners wearing blinking lights rather than simple reflective clothing items.

“Investing in a few quality pieces [of winter gear], I think you’re just going to enjoy your run more,” Baxter says. “You’re going to be safer and more comfortable.”

Even with the right layers, leaving your warm house and getting out the door often proves difficult when it’s cold and dark.

Baxter understands the struggle better than most. Anchorage gets less than six hours of daylight during the darkest winter months, and the city’s winter high temps hover around 25 degrees Fahrenheit.

So, how did she keep getting out there? “Running just became a habit of mine,” she says. “It’s how I start my day. I get up, I go for a run.” Even during peak winter in Alaska, Baxter often ran seven days a week simply because it was a part of her daily routine.

Baxter says each individual needs to find what helps them keep going, whether that’s a future goal, a training partner to run alongside, or a plan that lets you check off workouts.

For Baxter, her motivation and discipline to make running a habit often came from looking ahead.

“I just know how important those base miles are during the winter,” she says. “So I know I need to keep putting in the work, keep doing some workouts, because it’s only going to pay off down the road.”

If you have a spring marathon, a summer 10K, or another goal in mind, remind yourself how your winter routine fits into that bigger picture. You can also adjust your expectations for the season: Baxter says it’s okay to scale back during winter if that’s what works for you. A simple maintenance routine can help you arrive at your next training cycle ready to build.

The point is, doing something is better than doing nothing.

A goal months away isn’t always enough to get you out the door on a cold morning. Baxter suggests finding something closer to look forward to, too.

Consider signing up for a winter race, even if it isn’t one of your main events for the year. A Turkey Trot, holiday run, or another low-pressure race with friends can provide a checkpoint and make winter training more fun.

Soon after moving to Anchorage, Baxter joined a training group that held workouts at The Dome, an indoor athletic facility featuring an indoor track. The group helped her settle into the running community while giving her a reason to show up for a workout each week.

Once she developed those friendships, other runners expected her to show up, and those weekly track workouts were no longer optional.

If you’re looking for a similar boost, check for a local run club or training group. A standing workout appointment can give your week structure, while supportive company can make a difficult session feel less daunting.

Baxter generally followed the workouts on her schedule, but she changed where she ran to make them possible. The day after a substantial snowfall, she would head to the treadmill rather than plodding through fresh snow, for example. She also took many of her harder workouts to the treadmill or indoor track, where she felt more comfortable running fast.

Her easy and long runs, meanwhile, almost always happened outside.

You can take the same approach by considering the purpose of the day’s run. If you have a faster workout planned and the outdoors only offers slippery conditions, an indoor option may help you complete it with more confidence. If you’re running easy outside, give yourself room to slow down when conditions make your usual pace unrealistic.

Most of all, remember that one winter is part of a much longer running journey. Baxter’s progress—and eventual sub-three breakthrough—came from conquering multiple training cycles and years of running. The work you do this winter can be another step in that process.

“You simply can’t deny the power of consistently stacking marathon block upon marathon block or just years and years and years of running,” Baxter says. “Endurance running is a long game.”
