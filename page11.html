Atlas Ocean Explores Color Concepts for New Sailing Yacht

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Atlas Ocean Voyages is asking guests for their opinions as it explores color concepts for its new sailing yacht, the Atlas Adventurer.

In a social media update, the company said that four liveries are currently being considered for the hull: wine red, a steel blue, a graphite gray or an emerald green, inviting guests to vote on their favorite options by commenting on posts on its Instagram and Facebook profile.

Set to enter service in late 2028 as the fourth ship in Atlas Ocean Voyages’ fleet, the 400-guest Atlas Adventurer is currently under construction at the China Merchants shipyard in Haimen, China.

In addition to being the brand’s largest, the 26,000-ton ship will be the first sailing vessel in Atlas Ocean’s fleet, which now comprises three expedition vessels.

Earlier this year, the company built a miniature of the ship to study hydrodynamics and real-world performance, conducting trials at MARIN (Maritime Research Institute Netherlands) in Wageningen, the Netherlands.

The design is also highlighted by its green features, which include a 9-megawatt battery system that will enable near-silent operations when sailing under wind power.

With an ice-class rated hull, the Adventurer will offer both traditional and expedition itineraries, expanding Atlas Ocean’s deployment to new areas.

For its maiden season, the ship is scheduled to offer 15 departures across Asia, Africa and the Mediterranean, including new-to-the-company areas, such as Southeast Asia and Eastern Africa.

AI debt fears return as CoreWeave’s bond yield tops 17% and futures slide

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

CoreWeave’s bond yield topped 17% as the cost of insuring AI debt rose for Oracle and Nvidia. Man Group says Big Tech is on track to spend more than it makes in cash.

A candlestick chart on a dark screen shows prices sliding lower, mostly in red candles.

The sell-off in artificial intelligence stocks returned on Wednesday. It came two days after Nvidia’s rally on Jensen Huang’s return as CEO. Nasdaq 100 futures slid 3% and S&P 500 futures lost 2% before the open, CNBC reported. Dow Jones futures dropped 682 points, or 1.4%.

Nvidia fell more than 5% in premarket trading. Anthropic dropped about 4.6%, further below its $83 IPO price. Oracle lost about 5%, Alphabet 3% and Dell about 6%, despite Dell’s strong quarterly results, according to CNBC.

Asian markets fell first. South Korea’s Kospi closed 6.16% lower. Japan’s Nikkei 225 lost 2.78%, and Hong Kong’s Hang Seng fell 2.56%. SoftBank Group dropped 9.55%, chip-testing firm Advantest 12.54% and Tokyo Electron more than 10%. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix each fell about 7%.

In Europe, ASML fell more than 3%, Jason Karaian reported for The New York Times. The wider Stoxx 600 index edged higher, CNBC reported.

Investors in AI debt grew more nervous on Tuesday, Michelle Fox reported for CNBC. CoreWeave’s shares sank 13%, and the yield on its 9.75% bond due in 2031 rose above 17%.

Credit default swaps insure against a default. The spread on CoreWeave’s five-year swaps jumped 8.5% to more than 1,200 basis points. A wider spread means investors see the debt as riskier. Oracle’s spread rose 7% to 355.68, and Nvidia’s widened by 3.4%. Meta’s and Amazon’s rose by more than 2%, according to CNBC.

Ben Emons, chief investment officer at FedWatch Advisors, called it a waning of the “Jensen effect”.

“Markets see government intervention as ‘moral suasion’ that carries credit and interest-rate risks,” Emons told CNBC.

Huang is considered one of the AI industry’s top financiers, CNBC reported. Nvidia has struck deals with neoclouds such as CoreWeave to buy its chips, some backed by Nvidia guarantees. Nvidia said on its earnings call that it had $118bn in guarantees for AI projects as of 25 October. It also spent $58.1bn on stakes in other companies in nine months.

During Huang’s absence, chief financial officer Colette Kress told analysts that Nvidia would be careful about new investments. That suggested Nvidia would rein in its financing, Paul Meeks of Freedom Capital Markets told CNBC. He said Huang’s return should put that concern to rest.

“He’ll double down on the circular financing,” investor Steve Eisman said on CNBC.

Stifel analyst Ruben Roy took the opposite view in a note on Monday.

“Given scrutiny of disclosure around financing and guarantees … we do not expect Mr. Huang’s return to bring a quick reversion to broader customer financing,” Roy wrote.

John Lloyd of Janus Henderson Investors holds CoreWeave in the portfolios he manages. He told CNBC the company does not need the high-yield market for now.

Microsoft, Amazon, Meta and Alphabet are on track to spend more on capital projects than they generate in cash, in both 2026 and 2027. That is according to Man Group analysis cited by CNBC.

Heavy spenders and debt issuers fell 25% to 45% from their June peaks to Friday’s close, Man Group found. They include Oracle, Alphabet and some chipmakers. Apple, which has stayed out of the AI spending race, rose almost 20%.

PIMCO expects the biggest AI companies to raise about $250bn through bond sales this year, citing Goldman Sachs data. Borrowing across the wider AI industry could reach $300bn to $570bn.

“There are vast capital needs in this AI story. The only problem is there’s just not enough money to go around,” Cole Smead, chief executive of Smead Capital Management, told CNBC.

Dan Taylor, deputy chief investment officer at Man Group, said Big Tech needs spending discipline to rebuild its cash flow. Chipmakers, he said, need those same companies to keep spending heavily.

“It is hard to see how both sides get what they need at the same time,” Taylor said.

The Federal Reserve is widely expected to raise interest rates next month, the Times reported. That would make debt more expensive for companies building data centres. Monday’s rally put December and January hikes back on the table, Steve Liesman reported for CNBC. Traders are also watching reports of new US tariffs and October’s inflation data, due on Wednesday. US markets close on Thursday for Thanksgiving.

Some analysts see a correction rather than a bubble bursting.

“We see the retreat as a broadening, sentiment-driven correction rather than a deterioration in fundamentals or a popping of the A.I. bubble,” Allianz wrote, according to the Times.

So far, the moves do not look like the bear markets of recent decades, Deutsche Bank’s Jim Reid wrote in a note cited by CNBC. Liz Ann Sonders, chief investment strategist at Schwab, told CNBC that the sell-off in AI stocks began before the news about Huang.

U.S. proposes new tariffs on 14 trading partners, including China and the EU

📅 2026-11-25  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The U.S. has proposed new tariffs on 14 partners including China, the European Union, Japan and India, as President Donald Trump doubles down on levies, despite a large chunk of his previous duties being struck down by the Supreme Court.

The targeted economies create, sustain, or contribute to structural excess capacity and production in manufacturing sectors which burden or restrict American commerce, a senior U.S. official said.

The 14 partners have been notified of these actions, with neither country's tariffs exceeding rates announced as part of framework deals on reciprocal trade, the official added.

The new tariffs will not stack on top of existing duties, except for China, which faces a 20% tariff on top of the 25% imposed in 2018 over forced technology transfers.

The new duties on the world's second-largest economy could jump to 40% if it does not hold up its end of the trade truce agreed in Busan, South Korea, in Oct. 2025, after which tariffs on Chinese exports to the U.S. were slashed to 47%.

The administration says the tariffs target what it calls "overcapacity" — excess industrial production, often supported by government subsidies, that allows cheaper goods to flood global markets and undercut U.S. producers.

"The so-called 'overcapacity' does not really exist," China's foreign ministry said on Wednesday before the official reports were released by the U.S. Trade Representative, according to Reuters.

The new levies are being imposed under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which allows Washington to counter foreign trade practices deemed unfair, discriminatory, or burdensome to U.S. commerce.

CNBC earlier reported that the new tariffs would be "country specific," rather than a blanket rate, citing a source familiar with the matter.

Tariffs have been a signature of the Trump administration since the president unveiled sweeping "Liberation Day" duties in April 2025 before pausing them after a sour reaction from bond markets.

In February, the Supreme Court struck down tariffs that Trump had enacted under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act.

Trump used Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 to impose a 10% global tariff after the court's decision, before replacing them with levies of up to 12.5% on 60 economies under Section 301, alleging forced labor practices.

Bitcoin’s crash protection could make its next selloff worse as US inflation data looms

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Options dealers could amplify price swings between $58,000 and $64,000, even after much of Bitcoin's vulnerable futures leverage has been cleared.

A Bitcoin put can cushion losses for its buyer, while the trades a dealer makes to hedge the same option can make a fresh selloff harder to stop.

In its Nov. 24 report, Glassnode estimated that options dealers could reinforce declines by selling and rallies by buying when Bitcoin traded between $58,000 and $64,000. The US inflation report due Nov. 25 gives that setup a near-term test.

On a seven-day average, 1.7 Bitcoin puts traded for every call, the highest ratio since June 2022, according to Glassnode’s Nov. 24 report.

The put-to-call ratio for outstanding contracts separately reached its highest since May 2021. Put trading includes sellers and positions that serve purposes beyond crash protection, so the volume ratio shows where activity shifted without revealing who took which side.

A put gives its buyer the right to sell at a fixed price, or receive the equivalent cash payoff. The lower Bitcoin finishes below that price, the larger the payoff before the option's cost.

A dealer who sells the put takes the other side of that exposure and can hedge by selling Bitcoin or futures. As Bitcoin falls, the put becomes more sensitive to the decline, and the dealer may need to sell more to keep the hedge balanced.

Gamma measures how quickly an option’s price sensitivity changes. If prices reverse, a dealer in short gamma can buy back part of the hedge, adding to the rebound.

The feedback can be stronger when few buyers or sellers stand ready to take the other side, a pattern that researchers studying equity options found in less liquid stocks.

Glassnode estimated positive dealer gamma in the adjoining $56,000-$58,000 and $64,000-$66,000 bands. Dealers hedging positive gamma can buy weakness and sell strength, helping absorb a move. Those were modeled zones for one snapshot, and new trades or expiries can shift them.

A separate estimate from GEX Monitor from Nov. 24 also showed negative net gamma near $60,000, with the largest concentration by absolute exposure at that strike.

Glassnode’s Nov. 24 estimate put short gamma between $58,000 and $64,000. Bitcoin was near $59,700 later that day.

Within the estimated short-gamma band, dealer hedges can reinforce both a selloff and a rally.

The bands describe an estimate at one point in time. Dealer positions, liquidity and the influence of hedging can change as prices move, trades arrive or contracts expire.

Range widths are proportional to the reported dollar spans. Glassnode’s estimates use Nov. 24 data at 16:00 UTC. The later CryptoSlate observation at 21:12 UTC is rounded to $59,700. The paths illustrate short-gamma dealers hedging a move.

A cool print could ease rate pressure and prompt a rebound that the same dealers reinforce by buying. If inflation comes in close to forecasts, the GDP figures and demand for Bitcoin could matter more.

Winter Weather Hits EV Range Hard. One Automaker Handles It Better Than Tesla

With the first signs of winter making their presence felt in some regions, it’s handy to remember that electric cars lose range when temperatures drop considerably. It happens across the board, but some automakers and models handle the cold better than others, and a new study of over 59,000 EVs from Recurrent shows which battery-powered cars retain the most range when the thermometer reaches 32°F (0°C).

This year, Tesla has been dethroned in the automaker rankings by an unexpected competitor, though the American EV manufacturer still holds the first two spots when it comes to individual models.

Polestar, the Swedish-headquartered, Chinese-owned company that’s no longer allowed to sell cars in the United States after its inventory dries up, surpassed Tesla in the EV winter range rankings. The company’s models retain, on average, 88% of their range at 32°F (0°C) compared to ideal conditions, and 83% when the thermometer reads 20°F (-7°C).

Meanwhile, Tesla, which has historically been the top performer in Recurrent’s rankings because of its early investments in heat pump systems and other winter optimizations, is second on the list this year, with an 85% average range retention at 32°F (0°C) and 77% at 20°F (-7°C).

Audi completes the automaker podium, with the German marque’s EVs retaining 79% of their range at 32°F (0°C) and 71% at 20°F (-7°C), according to Recurrent.

At this point, it’s worth noting that the comparison point for these figures is not the official EPA range figure, but the longest range achieved by a particular EV in ideal conditions, as recorded by Recurrent’s system. As a reminder, the company gathers real-world data from EVs through owners who are willing to connect their cars to Recurrent’s servers.

This year, Toyota is at the bottom of the list, with its EVs retaining just 70% of their summer range at freezing temperatures, with Mercedes-Benz not far ahead. Rivian ranked sixth, with an average range retention of 78% at 32°F (0°C), ahead of Hyundai and Porsche, but below Jaguar and Nissan.

When looking at individual electric models, Tesla still has the lead, with the heat pump-equipped Model S ranking first. The discontinued electric sedan retains 90% of its summer range at 32°F (0°C), followed by the soon-to-be-discontinued Cybertruck, with an average retention rate of 89%.

Despite Tesla losing to Polestar in the overall rankings, the Model S still has the lead in the models list.

Next up is the Polestar 2, the entry-level sedan that put Polestar on the map. The four-door EV can still deliver 88% of its summer range at 32°F (0°C), followed by the Tesla Model X with 87% and the Tesla Model 3 with 85%.

The popular Hyundai Ioniq 5 is 10th on the list, with an average range retention rate of 81%, below the Ioniq 6, Audi e-tron GT, Tesla Model Y, and Chevrolet Silverado EV, but above the Audi e-tron, Rivian R1S, and Nissan Ariya.

The pre-facelift Toyota bZ4X is at the bottom of the list with 70% of range available when the temperature is 32°F (0°C), ahead of the Mercedes-Benz EQS, Chevrolet Blazer EV, Mercedes-Benz EQS SUV, and Cadillac Lyriq. Interestingly, the Volkswagen e-Golf, which is over two decades old and doesn’t have a battery thermal management system, ranked above Cadillac’s Lyriq.

On average, the 43 electric models included in Recurrent’s dataset retain 78% of their range in freezing temperatures compared to ideal conditions. That said, the best EVs out there top out at 90% range retention, while the bottom of the pack gets only 70%.

A heat pump makes a world of difference when it comes to boosting winter range. Compared to traditional resistive heating systems, an EV equipped with a heat pump will have 8% more range in the winter, which can make the difference between getting home and calling a tow truck.

Speaking of home, home charging and preconditioning the car’s interior before setting off will also preserve precious miles when it’s time to go somewhere with a thick coat on. By using electricity from the grid to heat up the cabin, the battery will last longer before needing a recharge.

“I loved your script. It is perfect. Now change everything.”

While probably far from a joke for many screenwriters who have had similar conversations, this particular line is taken from upcoming satirical picture book “We Make a Movie.”

Designed to look like a children’s book from the 1960s (particularly the U.K.’s famed “Key Words” educational series by Ladybird), “We Make a Movie” comes from artist Miriam Elia and film industry analyst Stephen Follows and features 20 hand-painted pictures accompanied by short, cheery lines merrily roasting Hollywood and the film industry at large. At the bottom of each are three “new words” to explain what’s really going on (in the case of the script line, the words are “squash,” “original” and “voices”). A brush with... Zanele Muholi—podcast

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The artist Zanele Muholi and host Ben Luke

In this podcast, based on The Art Newspaper's regular interview series, our host Ben Luke talks to artists in-depth. He asks the questions you've always wanted to: who are the artists, historical and contemporary, they most admire? Which are the museums they return to? What are the books, music and other media that most inspire them? And what is art for, anyway?

Content warning: this episode has some discussion of sexual and homophobic violence.

Zanele Muholi, Zanele Muholi, Tembisa, Johannesburg, 2014, from Faces and Phases, ed. by Renée Mussai (MACK, 2026)

Muholi was born in 1972 in Umlazi, Durban, South Africa and lives today in Cape Town. They are a self-described visual activist, who, since the mid-2000s, have used photography to document the lives of LGBTQ+ people, initially in South Africa, where they were born.

Zanele Muholi, Diana Cristina Nascimento Ramos, Salvador, 2025, from Faces and Phases, ed. by Renée Mussai (MACK, 2026)

They have since expanded their project around the world and work in discrete, often ongoing series that have a common aim: to respond to violence and discrimination against queer and trans communities, to reflect their defiance and resistance, and to expand their visibility in visual culture in South Africa in particular but also far beyond.

Zanele Muholi, Ashara Ekundayo, Venice, 2026, from Faces and Phases, ed. by Renée Mussai (MACK, 2026)

The people they depict have, since the start, been far more than sitters or subjects; they are collaborators, participants who help define the way they are represented. A crucial factor is that Muholi turns the lens onto themself. “When I position myself in my own frame,” they have said. “I do so on my own terms attempting to articulate my own way of seeing and wanting to be seen.”

Zanele Muholi, Christine Madonsela, Daveyton, Gauteng, 2017, from Faces and Phases, ed. by Renée Mussai (MACK, 2026)

And whether it is their unflinching and tender portraits of their collaborators or their imaginative and improvised self-portraits Muholi’s photographs are always beautiful objects, with a fastidious attention to the properties and possibilities of their medium.

Zanele Muholi, Mantedieng ‘Mantis’ Mamabolo-Cunningham, Parktown, Johannesburg, 2018, from Faces and Phases, ed. by Renée Mussai (MACK, 2026)

They discuss the influence of their mother, who was a maker herself, and was a profound influence on one of their most notable works, Somnyama Ngonyama (2012). They reflect on the enormous impact of photographers including Rotimi Fani-Kayode, Lola Flash and Joan E. Biren, writers including Audre Lorde, and musicians including Brenda Fassie. Plus, they answer our usual questions, giving insight into their daily practices and the rhythms of their work, and reflecting on the ultimate question: what is art for?
