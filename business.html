The New Airbus A220 Coming In 2027

📅 Nov 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Airbus A220 is currently preparing for a major upgrade, although the manufacturer is falling short of launching a new variant altogether. This year, new Airbus A220 deliveries will be outfitted with the manufacturer's Airspace interior, aligning the A220 with the interior feel of the A350, A330neo, and A320neo. Highlights include new upgraded extra-large overhead lockers, which will be around 20% lighter while offering more than 15% additional baggage capacity.

These aircraft will also feature 16-million-color LED lighting, allowing airlines to tailor their brand or flight phases. Airbus also plans on offering this unique interior as a retrofit option for existing Airbus A220 customers. Meanwhile, starting in 2027, a higher-density Airbus A220-300, one that features a 160-seat layout, will be offered, which provides operators with additional revenue and unit-cost gains without introducing a larger model to any given route.

However, the much-discussed Airbus A220-500 variant remains unannounced, and it does not look like Airbus has any plans to make any quick moves there as well. The manufacturer has looked to continue improving the profitability of the Airbus A220 family while also improving production rates, offering even more flexibility for operators. Let's analyze all the upcoming changes to the Airbus A220 family.

To contextualize the upcoming upgrades to this extremely popular aircraft program, it is important to begin by breaking down the Airbus A220's origins and discussing the aircraft's industrial footprint. The Airbus A220 began its life as a member of the Bombardier CSeries, a five-abreast narrowbody jet which was launched on July 13, 2008, with the intent of bridging the gap between regional jets and larger single-aisle aircraft.

It was first introduced to the market as the CS100, and it first took to the skies on September 16, 2013, before being certified by Transport Canada on December 18, 2015. The jet entered service with SWISS on July 15, 2016. The Bombardier CS300, which flew on February 27, 2015, would be certified on July 11, 2016, and it entered service on December 14 of that year with launch customer airBaltic.\

In July 2018, after Airbus took a majority stake in the company, it was quickly rebranded as the Airbus A220 and later organized as Airbus Canada Limited Partnership in June 2019. The aircraft program was now fully in the hands of manufacturing giant Airbus. The manufacturer's industrial capacity was expanded beyond Mirabel, Quebec, with a second final assembly line opened in Mobile, Alabama, in August 2019.

This allowed the manufacturer to avoid hefty import duties from US regulators. The manufacturer's ownership stake was increased to 75% in February 2020, with Bombardier electing to exit the program. The Airbus A220 remains the only commercial program managed by the manufacturer entirely outside of Europe. After entering service, the aircraft quickly established itself as offering strong dispatch reliability and improved passenger satisfaction over other models.

The Airbus A220 is powered by Pratt & Whitney PW1500G geared turbofan engines, and the aircraft offers a carbon-composite lightweight wing and an aluminum-lithium fuselage. Fly-by-wire controls and refined aerodynamics help deliver long legs and low-cost operations. Family weights range from 63 to 71 tonnes (139,000-156,000 lbs) of maximum takeoff weight (MTOW).

The aircraft family offers a range of 6,390-6,670 km (3,450-3,600 nautical miles), making it capable of serving almost all transcontinental niches in the United States and even longer-range lower-gauge routes within Europe. The 35 m (115 ft) Airbus A220-100 seats around 108-133 passengers, while the longer A220-300 seats a larger 130-160 passengers, depending on the specific configuration.

Airbus also launched the ACJ TwoTwenty business-jet variant, which was launched in late 2020. Delta remains the largest customer for the aircraft, and, globally, more than 1,100 Airbus A220s have been ordered, with over 500 delivered to date.

Globally, the fleet has surpassed 1.5 million flights and 2.7 million block hours, with the aircraft carrying more than 100 million passengers. The aircraft has also managed to maintain an exceptional safety reputation, with only one non-fatal smoke-related accident showing up on its rap sheet. It mainly competes with the Embraer E-Jet E2 family, holding a 55% share in this small-airliner niche, demonstrating the success and competitive value this program offers.

Airbus already operates several variants in its A220 series.

The A220 has slowly matured from being a promising upstart in the industry to becoming a core single-aisle workhorse for most carriers, especially with airlines wanting more revenue, lower unit costs, and a cabin that matches the rest of the manufacturer's portfolio. The five-abreast Airbus A220 already offers exceptional passenger comfort and impressive fuel burn, but bin space, lighting, and brand cohesion lag behind the newest Airbus models. Bringing the Airspace cabin to the aircraft helps close this gap.

Airlines today are extremely focused on premium and brand perception, and improving interiors is a key element of this overall strategy. From a commercial perspective, airlines need a low-risk way to add seats without adding a new aircraft type. The 160-seat Airbus A220-300 adds about ten more seats while providing more attractive costs per available seat mile, which ultimately proved useful for capacity-constrained layouts where upgauging to an A320neo would negatively impact profitability.

From a strategic perspective, Airbus needs to widen the A220's overall addressable market and strengthen the plane's business case before choosing to green-light a stretch to the Airbus A220-500. After all, the manufacturer wants to continue improving cabin appeal for legacy operators first.

Throughout this retrofitting and upgrade process, Airbus is mainly looking to pull two key levers. This year, new Airbus A220 deliveries will adopt the new Airbus Airspace cabin, a similar design that has been seen on Airbus A350, Airbus A330neo, and Airbus A320neo jets. Headlines are quick to discuss the 16-million-color LED lighting, which brand-matches the ambiance and circadian cues across different flight phases.

However, the Airspace kit also standardizes modern trim, surfaces, and touchpoints so that the aircraft will resemble any other Airbus model as closely as possible. Airbus is also quick to point out that it will be offering flexibility to operators, specifically when it comes to high-density Airbus A220-300 models. This ensures that its upgrades are not only providing optionality to future customers, but also letting existing operators improve their fleets.

The idea of stretching to the Airbus A220-500 also brings a lot to the table, as this model would upgauge the family and could potentially fill a capacity gap (albeit a small one) that exists in the Airbus commercial lineup. These upgrades are an extremely low-risk way to improve its product offering quickly.

Do Airbus or Embraer's narrow-bodied airlines dominate the short-haul and regional market?

From a financial perspective, the A220 upgrades represent a relatively low-risk lever being pulled by Airbus. For operators, these upgraded cabins will lift premium appeal, especially when it comes to corporate travelers who are interested in flying more modern aircraft. This will also result in more loyalty captured across the board.

Larger overhead bins are beneficial for operators, as they enable them to have lower gate-check-related costs and faster aircraft turns. Higher-capacity A220 variants can lower unit costs by spreading overall expenses out over more seats and adding capacity at slot-constrained airports and during peak leisure travel seasons.

Investing in these upgrades comes with low risks as aircraft downtimes are relatively low. This refresh supports overall pricing and will hopefully accelerate Airbus' order momentum: Delta, for example, is in the process of replacing its aging Boeing 717 fleet with advanced Airbus A220 models. This will improve learning-curve economics all along the way, ultimately pushing the program toward profitability before the A220-500 stretch.

At the end of the day, all of this means relatively little for passengers. The upgraded A220 cabins will likely provide a marginally more comfortable passenger experience, thanks to the cabin lighting being a little nicer, and it is also less likely with these new cabins that passengers will have to place their bags far away from where they currently are.

In the grand scheme of things, these are very marginal changes likely to have a small impact on your experience as a traveler. On the other hand, the higher-capacity variants of the aircraft will likely result in less available room per passenger, reducing comfort across the board. It will certainly be interesting to see if the high-capacity variant coming in 2027 will pave the way for a stretched A220-500.

‘Outlander: Blood of My Blood’ Bosses on the Season 2 Finale Twist That Would Rewrite the Series’ Lore, Who Won’t Appear on the Show — and Its Uncertain Future

📅 Nov 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

SPOILER ALERT: This post contains spoilers from “Twelfth Night,” the Season 2 finale of “Outlander: Blood of My Blood,” now streaming on Starz.

“Outlander” was never a series to hold back on a brainy twist that took a minute to digest. But its prequel series, “Outlander: Blood of My Blood,” may have outdone its predecessor with a twist that rewrites at least one family tree.

Since the prequel premiered last year, it has always operated on its own narrative timeline, separate from author Diana Gabaldon’s book series. In those books, Julia (Hermoine Corfield) and Henry (Jeremy Irvine), the parents of original series heroine Claire (Caitriona Balfe), died when she was 5 years old in a car crash. But with “Blood of My Blood,” executive producers Matthew B. Roberts and Maril Davis ponder what might have happened if they actually time-traveled through the same magic stones their daughter would decades later, and were left to survive in 1710s Scotland.

It has been one tragedy after another for the lovebirds, and that’s saying something, given that they weathered World War I. But one question has loomed large over the Beauchamps: What will happen to their son, William, with whom Julia was pregnant when they went through the stones? He’s not in Gabaldon’s books, so Roberts and Davis have a bit of freedom to play with his fate. And they seemingly gave fans their answer in the Season 2 finale.

Julia and Henry were dealt their biggest setback yet earlier this season, when a seer informed her she isn’t a time traveler, she was just pregnant with one. William has his father’s mystical gene, and now that Julia is postpartum, she won’t be able to go back to her own time unless they get pregnant again, which itself is a moral quandary. The news doesn’t stop them from trying to run away together again. But in the process, Henry is shot by Lord Lovat (Tony Curran) in a case of mistaken identity, and Julia’s attempts to flee Lovat’s henchman leave her lifeless at the bottom of a waterfall. Fearful for wee William’s life without his parents, Julia’s confidante Davina (Sarah Vickers) takes him to the safest place she can think of –– to her son Brian (Jamie Roy) and his new wife Ellen (Harriet Slater). Mere moments after Brian and Ellen’s long-awaited wedding, the Frasers have a new addition to their future home at Lallybroch. This is where the series goes for broke, and makes a frankly brazen claim. Brian and Ellen are, of course, the parents of future “Outlander” hero Jamie (Sam Heughan). According to Gabaldon’s original books, he had an older brother named William (aka Willie), whose death when Jamie was young forever affected how he saw the world and cared for those he loved. (Not for nothing, Willie was so formative for Jamie that fans latched onto the wooden snake he carved before he died, and the onscreen prop sold for more than $5,000 at auction earlier this year.)

If “Blood of My Blood” is to be believed, Brian and Ellen being given an infant named William could mean that Claire’s heretofore unknown younger brother was actually raised as a Fraser 200 years in the past. It’s a twist that completely alters how fans should look –– at least in the TV series’ timeline –– at Claire and Jamie’s relationship. Not only does the prequel depict their parents as unexpected allies, but it is now suggesting they may have even shared a sibling, not by blood thankfully.

Now before anyone gets too excited (or angry!), Roberts and Davis aren’t exactly making this twist official on a birth certificate just yet: Even when pressed multiple times for confirmation, they dodged answering whether this really is the Willie of the Fraser family tree. But with Season 2 coming to a close without news about a Season 3 renewal, it’s hard not to feel the impact of this twist, given it could be the revelation the prequel leaves fans as a parting gift. For now, Davis is tying it all back to the original.

“With Willie/William, even if they’re not the same person, I think it’s so interesting to think how these two stories are interwoven now,” she tells Variety. “It binds Jamie and Claire’s parents, and how will that affect them in the future? We always loved the idea that Jamie and Claire were meant to be, and we’re seeing how that fate all comes together.”

Roberts has a bit more fun with his response about the mystery of it all. “I feel like this is the natural place to go, and I like to manipulate people,” he says with a grin. “That’s my job.”

He isn’t kidding. Alternate endings were filmed for the finale, one where Ellen reveals to Brian that she is pregnant again right before they are handed William (an emotional beat, given that his father caused her miscarriage this season). So this could have been a case of two Williams. But Roberts suggests it was less of a possibility, and more of a security measure.

“I like to do some alt endings to throw people off in case scripts get leaked,” he says. “I like to keep actors on their toes, too. I have something in my head that I want to put on screen, but we did multiple endings with the finale of ‘Outlander’ as well.”

But rewriting lore to this degree is risky business, especially given the series isn’t yet guaranteed to come back. Still, Roberts and Davis have already planned out what would happen next should they get the go ahead, and there is one thing audiences won’t ever see in this prequel: young Jamie.

While diehard fans have undoubtedly done the math to figure out just how soon their favorite Highlander could make his debut, Roberts says he is hesitant to bring the action too close to the stories told in Gabaldon’s books and the original series.

“It’s hard to think about the stories with young Jamie in them, because that feels like you’re starting to tread on ‘Outlander,’ and I don’t want to do that,” he says. “It feels like you want to get maybe as close to the birth of Jamie as we can, but that still gives us quite a bit of time.”

If “Blood of My Blood” is at least in step with Gabaldon’s book series’ timeline, Willie is born in 1716 –– the same year of Brian and Ellen’s wedding, as seen in the Season 2 finale. Jamie will be born in 1721, so five years could be what’s left of the narrative runway for the series.

With those parameters in place, Roberts and Davis also continue to look beyond “Blood of My Blood” for future “Outlander” franchise opportunities. Even with the wealth of Gabaldon’s world building at their disposal (her 10th and final book has yet to be released), they continue to be vocal about a particular contender.

Lord John, a fan-favorite character played by David Berry in the original series, is the subject of Gabaldon’s companion series of novels, and Roberts and Davis previously tried to spin off those stories while the original “Outlander” was still airing. The series didn’t move forward then, but they seem to be actively hoping the franchise’s continued success will reignite interest.

“There are certainly other things out there for us to explore, and Lord John remains one of them,” Davis says.

In 1982, scientists studying nuclear fusion discovered something unexpected. Once the heating power inside an experimental fusion machine reached a certain level, the fuel suddenly became much better at retaining heat. This discovery offered a way to overcome one of the biggest challenges in fusion research. More than four decades later, predicting how much power is needed to trigger this change remains a difficult problem.

Nuclear fusion releases energy when light atomic nuclei combine, the same process that powers the Sun. Recreating this process on Earth requires heating a gas to extreme temperatures, turning it into plasma, a state of matter made up of electrically charged particles.

Machines called tokamaks use powerful magnetic fields to confine this plasma inside a doughnut-shaped chamber. However, keeping the plasma in place does not necessarily keep it hot. Turbulent motion within the plasma can carry heat away, making it difficult to maintain the conditions needed for fusion.

The phenomenon discovered in 1982 occurs when heating power crosses a critical threshold, causing turbulence near the outer edge of the plasma to drop sharply. Scientists call this the transition from low confinement (L-mode) to high confinement (H-mode), a state in which the plasma retains energy much more efficiently.

Achieving H-mode is essential for future fusion reactors. The difficulty is that the amount of heating power required depends on several factors, including how densely packed the plasma particles are and the direction of the magnetic field. Existing theories have struggled to explain both effects together.

In a study published in Physical Review Letters, researchers led by Brenno De Lucca, a doctoral assistant at EPFL’s Swiss Plasma Center (SPC), developed a theory that explains these effects within a single physical framework.

Using computer simulations, the researchers recreated the behavior of plasma inside a tokamak as heating power increased. They identified the physical processes responsible for the transition, developed a mathematical model to describe them, and compared its predictions with experimental measurements from existing tokamaks.

Their results showed that turbulence generates flows within the plasma that move at different speeds across its outer region. As neighboring layers move past one another at different speeds, they help disrupt the turbulent motion that created them in the first place. This reduces heat loss, allowing the plasma to retain more energy.

The researchers also found that the direction of the magnetic field, the movement of the plasma, and collisions between particles work together to make H-mode easier to reach in one magnetic field configuration than in the other.

Plasma density presents another complication. Increasing the density can reduce the amount of heating power needed in one range and increase it in another. The researchers derived equations describing these changes and other operating conditions, explaining why there is a particular density at which the heating requirement is lowest.

The model could help researchers determine whether future fusion machines will have sufficient heating power to achieve H-mode. For ITER, the world’s largest international fusion project, currently under construction in France, the researchers predicted a minimum requirement of 43 megawatts, comfortably below its planned heating capacity of 73 megawatts.

The outlook is tighter for SPARC, a compact tokamak that uses powerful magnetic fields and is being developed by the U.S. company Commonwealth Fusion Systems. Its predicted minimum heating requirement is 26 megawatts, slightly above its planned capacity of 25 megawatts, leaving considerably less room for uncertainty.

Like Mrs. Renauld, Marguerite claimed she was attacked by intruders who killed her mother and husband, the painter Adolphe Steinheil. Marguerite was already infamous in Paris. Years before, she had been the mistress of French President Félix Faure, who had died in her arms after suffering a stroke. Marguerite tried to frame several people for the murder of her family members, but she was found out. It was widely believed that she herself had staged the intrusion to murder her husband. With no proof to back the accusation, a French jury acquitted Marguerite Stenheil in 1909 after an extremely sensationalistic trial.

Just as with a good murder-mystery, a list can take a turn. In this case, it is worth highlighting The Pale Horse as the only Agatha Christie book that inspired a crime, helped solve another, and saved several lives. In the novel, Mrs Davis gives her last confession to Father Gorman along with a list of names. Shortly after, the priest is murdered, and the people on the list begin dying from what seem to be natural causes.

At the heart of this book is a poison. It is made from an odorless heavy metal called thallium, which, if ingested in small doses, can cause symptoms such as hair loss and gastrointestinal distress that doctors routinely misdiagnose. The poison is said to have inspired Graham Young, known as "The Teacup Poisoner". In 1972, Young was accused of lacing his coworkers' tea and coffee with thallium and antimony, killing two colleagues. Some accounts say Young was caught thanks to a doctor who had read Agatha Christie's novel and became suspicious. Thallium was also the method used by George Trepal to poison his neighbors, the Carr family, killing Peggy Carr. An undercover detective who had been in Trepal's home recalled seeing a copy of The Pale Horse.

Agatha Christie's book also saved lives. In 1975, more than a decade after the novel was published, Christie received a letter from a reader in South America who had noticed a friend suffering from unexplained hair loss and pain. The reader remembered the plot of The Pale Horse and alerted the woman's physicians, who confirmed she had suffered thallium poisoning. Two years later, a one-year-old Qatari girl was flown to London. She was in a coma caused by an illness no doctors could diagnose. It was Marsha Maitland, a nurse in the hospital who had been reading The Pale Horse, who suggested thallium poisoning. The test came back positive, allowing doctors to find the treatment that saved the toddler's life.

One child was kidnapped. Six people died as a direct consequence. This is the tragic arc at the heart of what is perhaps Agatha Christie's most famous novel, Murder on the Orient Express. In the book, Hercule Poirot is tasked with investigating the murder of American businessman Samuel Ratchett aboard the luxurious Orient Express. All the suspects in the murder are staying inside the same train, trapped in Yugoslavia in the middle of a snowstorm. Poirot soon finds that the murder is connected to an older tragedy: the kidnapping and murder of little Daisy Armstrong. The conclusion to the story is what makes it so compelling. It is tragic and simply brilliant.

In the book, Agatha Christie aims to give a sense of justice to a real-life tragic crime. In 1932, 20-month-old Charles Lindbergh Jr, was taken from his crib in his New Jersey home. He was the son of the most famous aviator in the world, whose name he shared. The authors of the crime requested a $50,000 ransom. The parents paid. Soon after, the baby's body was found in the woods near the house. Weeks later, a young maid, Violet Sharpe, killed herself after being questioned by the police as a suspect. The crime was so famous it led to the passing of the Federal Kidnapping Act (the "Little Lindbergh Law"). Agatha Christie's fictional Armstrong case reproduces the Lindbergh case almost point for point. Except, of course, real crimes are rarely clear-cut. In 1934, a carpenter named Bruno Richard Hauptmann was arrested for the crime. Hauptmann was executed in 1936, although his guilt continues to be called into question even today.

A Very Serious Story About Falling Asleep While Belaying

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The East Face of Mount Temple, Canada, and the scene of this story's shenanigans. (Photo: Mitra Samavaki / Getty)

Big falls are commonplace while sport climbing and bouldering, but, in my opinion, the high alpine remains largely a safe space to take your brake hand off the rope. Why? With loose rock, aerated ice, and an often minimalist rack, falling off—even with a tight belay—usually spells disaster. A climber has every reason to follow the old “the leader cannot fall” ethos.

This old school state of mind deeply resonated with me earlier this month on a difficult alpine climb on Mount Temple in the Canadian Rockies. This 1,400-meter ice and mixed route began easily enough, with a hike up a hard-packed snow slope at 6 a.m. toward the deep cleft that marked the start. Neither my partner Rory nor I were burdened with rope management during the first hour of climbing, since our rope stayed neatly coiled in a pack.

Hundreds of meters higher, below one of the crux pitches, we flaked out our rope. Above us stood a terribly skinny pillar of ice no wider than my shoulders at its base. Blue light filtered through its entirety, a sign of its youth and untrustworthiness.

“I don’t think I should place a screw in that,” Rory decided.

I scanned the shattered quartzite behind the pillar, hoping to at least keep us attached to the mountain before Rory essentially soloed the pitch. Two nervous pitons and a shallow micro nut were all I could cobble together after 10 minutes of searching. I stomped out a waist-deep hole in the snow on the back side of the pillar and clipped into our wobbly anchor. My now four-piece anchor—plus Rory’s unshakeable climbing ability—gave us great odds of surviving his lead.

The runout pitch gave me ample opportunity to pay out slack as I pleased while munching on trail mix and rehydrating from our busy morning. After all, Rory was not allowed to fall, so he didn’t.

Falling has been a frowned-upon practice for most of climbing’s history, especially before sport climbing became popular in the 1980s. Only relatively recently, and only in some styles of climbing, have leader falls become no longer taboo—even acceptable and encouraged.

The crusty old-timer in me knows that, in any form of free climbing, it is always safer to climb to the top of a pitch without testing your equipment, be it a skinny rope, crashpad, or ice screw. This gut instinct evolved from a time when climbing gear was not completely trustworthy. Maybe you hand-stitched your quickdraw’s webbing yourself. Maybe you’d sourced your climbing protection from a hardware store. Or maybe you had tied your lead line around your waist because drop-seat harnesses hadn’t been invented yet. In this era—really only a few decades ago—your climbing ability was the first line of defense. Ropes and harnesses and removable protection served merely as dubious back-ups.

But the advent of regular whippers is a tragedy for one more reason: With newfangled belay devices, hang dogging, and constant pleas to “watch me!” belaying has become a real pain in the ass.

Nearly 12 hours later, Rory and I were still on Mount Temple. We’d topped out the ice climbing portion of the face and traversed across open snow slopes and through rock bands to a final serrated crux: the Black Towers. Countless turrets of dark limestone slice across the summit ridge of Mount Temple, creating difficult route finding even on a clear summer’s day. We were approaching midnight in November and a thick fog had choked the upper mountain. Visibility hovered around 10 meters. I had no idea where we were supposed to go.

Thankfully it was Rory’s lead again, and he was questing. Fifteen minutes ticked by as he wandered up a steep rocky groove, then another 15 of the rope unmoving. An hour passed. I clicked off my headlamp to save battery. More time passed. I figured I could tie off the rope and catch a few Zs, so I did. Sleep felt amazing. I was glad that even as we approached 24 hours on the move, Rory still knew the rules: The leader cannot fall. And the belayer gets to sleep.

I woke up to the sound of Rory’s voice. He’d been blocked by an overhang and was downclimbing. Thick, icy fog obscured our vision as I took the rack and traversed along a snow ledge looking for easier passage. The fog would sometimes lift for an instant—I’d spot a lonely star above—and I’d frantically flick my headlamp to full strength to catch a glimpse of the mountain. But before my eyes could adjust to the new sight, the fog would crash back in like a breaking wave, drowning us in uncertainty once again.

This story does not end in a climbing success. We did not ultimately find our way through the Black Towers, nor did we share a weary, satisfied hug on the summit of Mount Temple. We became lost and a little delirious on a large mountain in the middle of the night and needed to go down.

We stayed roped together as we retraced our steps through deep snow on the upper mountain. I was a little worried about triggering an avalanche, but mainly I was just exhausted. If I stumbled, I would prefer to slide 30 meters before being caught by the rope than well over a thousand into the next life.

Rory and I regrouped at an anchor I’d built in an outcrop of yellow rock. He led out across another snowfield which terminated at a gigantic notch in an otherwise sheer ridgeline. The notch marked the start of a classic ski descent, the 60-degree Aemmer Couloir, and our escape hatch. Rory trudged toward it and I paid out rope, nodding off again.

Suddenly, I was awake. I had not pooped all day, yet the kilogram of trail mix and sausage and cheese I’d downed was making a basement appearance.

“Rory! Do you need a real belay for this bit? I really need to poo,” I yelled into the fog.

I let the rope feed through the anchor, then unclipped it from my belay device and, without moving even a step away from our anchor, unloaded into the snow bank.

It would have been a disgusting mess had Rory fallen and weighted the anchor, dragging me into my own steaming waste. But he didn’t, and I made it back to civilization with both my dignity and my life.

Camino Day 21: Hórreos and the Father of the Modern Camino

We leave so early from Casa Cruceiro de Ferreiros that we have to unlock the door and let ourselves out. I can tell Suzanne is itching to go, but we only have one headlamp, which effectively yokes us until dawn, or first coffee. Then she will rabbit away. The last few days we’ve not walked together, which is not to say we’re walking alone—there are 1,000s of pilgrims to walk with.

The Way isn’t that different from days past, and the *turigrinos* are getting harder to distinguish from the rest of us. Yes, they have their cute little daypacks, but the clothes aren’t shiny anymore, and I see a weariness, a gift of several days on trail.

We walk by stone fences on rolling, rural lanes, and I try to imagine what it must have been like when resources were so slim that split stone was the material available for fences. There is a gentle roll to The Way, and we pass the 100.000k marker to Santiago—unremarkably just there, with no fanfare, like 1,000s of others. The end is 62 miles away!

Time and millions of pilgrims have worn down the Camino

I am so fascinated by the hórreos of Galicia

We walk between six-foot high dry lay stone fences, and later on a sunken path, worn down by this pilgrimage. I admire hórreos of every make: stone, brick, wooden, and a mix. They seem randomly dropped more than thoughtfully placed, at odd angles with what is around them. I know that many have been here since the 13th century, but neither they nor the landscape yield to one another. Hórreos evolved to dry millet until the arrival of the New World crop: corn. Galicia’s climatic peculiarities prevent grain from completely drying in the field. Done mechanically today, in years past the ears were shucked and stored whole to allow air to pass through, to cool and dry the grain to prevent spoilage and fermentation.

In an information sheet I found at tonight’s hostel, it reads:

In 1973, a Decree was approved by the Spanish state to protect all the old granaries and huts existing in Galicia and Asturias, which attempted to address the consequences of the abandonment of the rural way of life and the loss of use of the granaries.

I guess I won’t be transporting any hórreos home to my backyard.

We’re in pulpo territory, and find pulperías and copper cauldrons smoking away outside roadside bars. The Galician preparation for octopus is smoked paprika, sea salt, and a splash of olive oil, all served on a seven-inch wooden plate and eaten with toothpicks. Suzanne largely doesn’t partake because the octopus is known to be an intelligent sea creature; I’d feel bad too if they weren’t so tasty. Kinda like bacon—if they are so smart, then why so tasty? It is a tragic, delicious bargain we’ve made with nature’s smartest species. On a lighter note, we’ve stumbled into a soft homemade cheese, queso con miel, that is drizzled with local Galician honey, and the seasonal padrón peppers.

Padrón peppers – fried with olive oil and sprinkled with course salt.

I walk with David and his mother Maxine, from Australia. She is a 12-month cancer survivor, and as David tells the story, when she was at her lowest… she interjects “so exhausted I couldn’t even hold a cell phone” David pitched walking the Camino. That was 18 months ago, and here they are. I think back to other Aussies I’ve met, and each seemed to be recovering from a major health crisis.

Then, there was Fritz, born in Nebraska, but sent away to boarding school at a young age and never returned, living abroad and traveling the world in the business of finance. He tells me a different side of the story of how the modern Camino came to be. His friend was named Manuel Fraga Iribarne, who in the mid-1990s was the regional governor of Galicia, then one of the poorest, most isolated agricultural regions of Spain. At that time, the Camino was a quiet trail mostly walked by devout Catholics, historians, or avid hikers. (When they invent time travel, this is where I’m returning, to walk the Camino of those days). Working with Father Elías (the priest of O Cebreiro), who was the spiritual father and painted the yellow arrows to guide the way, Fraga had the political and marketing know-how to rebuild the infrastructure we enjoy today. One hears of Father Elías in Catholic stories, but not Manuel Fraga Iribarne. Thank you, Fritz.

This is his eighth Camino, and he is reluctantly walking it because it is a graduation requirement for the two-week course he took in Santiago. Fritz has been so interesting to listen to and walk with, but I recognize in him that, like myself, much of our success has been given to us through the privilege of the stations our parents gave us. As men, much of how we see ourselves is through our work, and Fritz, David, and the other unhappy conservative I walked with are all now retired. Their importance and reason for being is gone, and I think they are looking for something that replaces what they have lost. SH*T, I think to myself, this is the third man I’ve walked with that turns out to be angry and older—what is she, The Camino, trying to teach me?

He has seen the world and chooses to be angry. I see his unwavering support of the current administration being driven more out of retribution for the direction the country was going than any hope or vision for its future. If our country was a garden and overrun by weeds, his solution would be to drown it in Roundup and pave it over. No more weeds. My Dad would say that weeds are just misplaced plants and can have a lot to say diagnostically about the health of the soil. The goal isn’t to keep a weed-free garden, but to produce food, knowing that for the health of the soil, weeds must coexist.

So I walk with Fritz until I can’t. Yes, he is a fast walker, unencumbered, and fresh on the Camino, but I tire of his raw anger, in such contrast to the Anglican faith he professes. My exit comes when he sees his pickup van in case he is ready to stop walking. He goes over to check in and I, who have no pickup van, sit and rest. Fritz continues on, walking over the hill and out of my Camino never to be seen by me again.

DEADLINE: How did you and Jesse talk through making the script?

So, I just knew that one of the last things that I wanted to do was work on this in a linear way.

When Jesse came on, he had the tough task of working the way I wanted to work, which was just out of order. He would write something in Act 2, then go to Act 1, then jump to Act 3. We went around and around, putting up reels in a hodgepodge way, and then started shaping it to make sense. It’s madness to work that way, but it made the movie what it is because I think that was the only way we could go so wild and take so many swings in so many parts of the movie. We had an anarchic way of our process.

Nvidia's billion-dollar board bet: Who's protected as lawsuits mount?

📅 2026-11-25  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Jensen Huang is back as CEO of Nvidia, after President Donald Trump urged the company's board to rehire Huang. But investigations into the company, its executive leadership and its board continue as outside criticism mounts.

"Clearly they caved to external pressure," said Betsy Atkins, an author and longtime public-company director. "The board failed," she said, when it initially allowed Huang's removal. "The board's role is to think of all stakeholders."

California Attorney General Rob Bonta is investigating whether Nvidia misrepresented information to investors. "There is classic corporate securities law on the books in California that prevents investors and the public from being misled by companies," Bonta said on CNBC's "Squawk on the Street" on Tuesday.

Directors are already defendants in a shareholder lawsuit accusing them of failing to oversee Nvidia. A separate securities class action targets the company and Huang, its co-founder.

Now the board’s insurance choices are part of the story. A risk advisor with direct knowledge of Nvidia’s coverage says the company built an approximately $1 billion D&O tower, insurance jargon for directors and officers insurance policies from different companies that combine to cover one massive risk.

Nvidia "loves insurance," the advisor said. “They buy insurance as if they have no balance sheet.” A billion-dollar tower would essentially mean Nvidia "bought the market," purchasing all the coverage it could get, according to an industry expert with 25 years working in financial insurance. The expert asked not to be named out of concern for past or potential future clients.

But Nvidia directed all that coverage to Side A only, according to CNBC's source. Side A protects directors and officers personally when the company cannot indemnify them. Side B covers the company for legal defense costs or settlements on behalf of directors and officers, and Side C protects the company when it's named as a defendant in a suit such as a securities claim.

The risk advisor says Nvidia believed it had "the war chest" to protect itself against a securities class action suit.

That choice is raising questions about whether the board protected itself at the company’s expense. CNBC asked Nvidia for comment but received no response.

An insurance CEO characterized a billion-dollar policy as a lot of coverage, but said a Side A-only allocation was a surprising move because it results in imbalanced coverage, protecting only directors.

That structure is not automatically a governance failure, according to a D&O insurance expert with long experience in coverage for banks, drugmakers and other businesses that often face litigation. Side A-only programs are common among companies with the capital to manage the corporate risk internally.

"For a company with Nvidia’s resources, retaining the company-side risk can make financial sense," the advisor said. It's Side A D&O that's "imperative to corporate governance."

"It is a capital-allocation decision," the expert told me. The company decides that buying insurance for its own balance sheet is not worth the premium. In other words, Side B and Side C are considered too expensive for the coverage the company gets.

The question for shareholders is whether the board’s decision to hold that risk was a sound use of the company’s resources and whether the company can absorb the costs if the claims grow.

A shareholder derivative complaint filed Nov. 11 names Huang, directors and other officers. It alleges that they failed to oversee risks involving Huang’s relationship with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang and made misleading statements. It also alleges that some defendants sold roughly $2 billion in Nvidia stock while holding material nonpublic information. Those remain allegations, not findings.

A separate securities class action, filed Nov. 9, names Nvidia and Huang. It seeks damages for investors who bought Nvidia shares between Oct. 9, 2025, and Nov. 8, 2026, alleging the company and Huang misled investors.

The distinction matters: The derivative suit is brought on Nvidia’s behalf, while the class action seeks recovery for investors.

"Insurance is already at work," says Michel Léonard, chief economist at the Insurance Information Institute, describing standard policy mechanics, not Nvidia’s specific policy. "Once the suit is filed, D&O starts paying defense costs, long before anyone proves anything."

A claim still has to meet the policy’s terms, and defense costs can reduce the remaining limits. Nor does a drop in Nvidia’s market value by itself establish an insurable loss. Plaintiffs would need to prove legally recoverable damages; the policy would respond only to covered losses.

The board has already taken steps it says are aimed at accountability. Nvidia announced an independent investigation on Nov. 8, and independent directors retained outside counsel two days later. Huang resigned as CEO and a director on Nov. 11; the board appointed CFO Colette Kress interim CEO. Nvidia said it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets had been compromised by Huang's extramarital relationships.

Atkins, who joined the board of Wynn Resorts in 2018 after founder Steve Wynn resigned, argues that allegations are not conclusions and that boards should also weigh governance concerns against the value a founder brings to a company’s innovation. Nvidia's investigation is continuing even as Huang resumes leadership.

“Will companies’ hyper-accelerated innovation speed be the same without them? I don’t think so,” said Atkins, author of "Don't Waste a Board Seat: A Decision-Maker's Guide to Choosing Directors Who Create Value."

Nvidia’s 2026 proxy also says the company has indemnity agreements with its directors and officers — separate from its insurance. Those agreements provide for the company to cover certain costs and liabilities when the law permits.
