After an eye-catching summer of transfer business, it has been an excellent start to the season for Middlesbrough in the Championship.

Middlesbrough were beaten by Hull City in last season's play-off final, but as they look to bounce back from their Wembley heartbreak, Kim Hellberg's men head into the November international break sitting second in the Championship table.

After missing out on promotion in agonising fashion last season, Middlesbrough owner Steve Gibson provided Hellberg with strong backing in the transfer market this summer, and the ambitious rebuild has certainly had the desired effect so far.

While Will Lankshear was Boro's marquee summer signing, with the striker joining from Tottenham Hotspur for an initial fee of around £10 million, plus a further £10 million in add-ons, he was far from their only expensive addition.

Another Boro transfer deal that made headlines in the summer was the signing of goalkeeper Radek Vitek from Manchester United for an initial fee of £7 million, which could rise to as much as £14 million if add-ons are met.

Homophobia on the rise in football - what is your club doing about it?

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Rainbow laces were a common sight for several years across football and other sports

Warning: this article contains offensive language

Homophobia and transphobia are on the rise in English football, with new data revealing record numbers of discrimination reports in the game - so what is the Premier League doing about it?

Last season, with little fanfare, the league introduced a new LGBTQ+ celebratory activation in February, Premier League With Pride.

The lowkey launch contrasts with the landscape of a few years ago, when England internationals like Jordan Henderson and Conor Coady were proudly proclaiming their allyship.

"The one thing it [Premier League With Pride last season] was lacking was engagement from players," said Jon Holmes, founder and lead of news organisation and campaign group Sports Media LGBT.

"Not only would you have the rainbow armbands, but clearer allyship from players - that was more around the late 2010s. That has been in decline."

A report last month from anti-discrimination charity Kick It Out says they received a new high of 1,744 discrimination reports during the 2025-26 season, a 25% increase on the previous campaign. In this, reports of homophobia rose 46% to 203.

Kick It Out said it had received 142 reports of discriminatory mass chanting during the season, a 41% increase on 2024-25 and the highest number recorded. These reports included increases in homophobic and transphobic chants.

A prime example is the 'rent boy' chant predominantly aimed at Chelsea players and fans, for which incidents had dropped since it was criminalised in 2022. However, the combined 24 reports of homophobic chanting in 2025-26 season were the most in three years.

According to Kick It Out,, external many of the incidents over the past few seasons can be linked to domestic and world events, such as the Supreme Court ruling on gender leading to the FA's ban on transgender women in the women's game, which it says led to a spike in transphobic abuse.

The first Premier League With Pride event included rainbow branding at grounds - but nothing involving players

BBC Sport asked all 20 Premier League clubs what diversity policies they have regarding the LGBTQ+ community, and how much of their budget is dedicated to such schemes.

Nine clubs responded to the BBC request. None said how much money they invest in these areas.

A Nottingham Forest spokesperson told BBC Sport: "The club commits significant funding to this important area, and has an Equality, Diversity and Inclusion lead whose role is dedicated to ensuring inclusion is at the heart of everything we do."

Some clubs do visibly engage with their local LGBTQ+ community. Leeds United have been a sponsor of the city's Pride celebrations since 2018. Luke Ayling, then Leeds captain, marched in the Pride parade in 2022.

Brighton are annually represented in their city's extensive Pride events, as are the Manchester clubs.

Manchester City host an annual Progress flag-raising ceremony, while Manchester United said staff marched in the city centre parade. They also ran a flag competition with LGBTQ+ supporters' club Rainbow Devils, with the winning design displayed at Old Trafford for all matchdays.

Tottenham said that at the start of 2024, Spurs in the Community helped launch a weekly football session for members of the LGBTQ+ community.

And for the first time this season, all 20 current Premier League clubs have dedicated fan groups for LGBTQ+ supporters.

"It feels like there is a renaissance in the LGBT fan movement," said Holmes. "There is a rise in homophobia, but it does seem like the authorities are sitting up and taking notice. We can feel a degree of optimism."

But it is difficult for Premier League teams to encourage LGBTQ+ allyship and diversity, when representation within clubs is a mixed bag at best.

According to Kick It Out, which gathered data on the senior management teams from 17 out of the 20 current Premier League clubs, just 0.3% of people in those roles openly identify as LGBTQ+ - well below the UK population average according to latest national census data, with 4% of the British population identifying as LGBTQ+.

At a senior coaching level, from 11 clubs which responded there is no-one from the LGBTQ+ community.

Kick It Out says, comparing the dataset to relevant census data, that if true diversity in football's workforce were to be achieved, over 100 more people from the LGBTQ+ community would have to be employed at Premier League clubs.

The Premier League previously had a high profile association with LGBTQ+ charity Stonewall's Rainbow Laces campaign - but this was ended before the 2025-26 season

Rainbow Laces started in 2014-15 as a collaboration between Stonewall, Paddy Power and the Premier League. For two game weeks, usually around the end of November, players would wear rainbow-coloured laces or armbands, and the colourful branding would be seen in grounds and on broadcast idents.

Holmes said by the end it was doing more harm than good.

"I think the novelty of Rainbow Laces wore off," he told BBC Sport. "It also became more newsworthy to talk about the controversies; it became more difficult for players to engage without difficult questions.

"Also, club media departments are more risk averse, given growing toxicity on social media.

"It has been misconstrued that players were forced to engage, I think it was because Rainbow Laces became de rigueur, and it was perceived that captains would need to lead with the armband and allyship."

Rainbow armbands were introduced in 2017 for captains, having first been worn by MLS club Orlando City following the mass shooting at Pulse, an LGBTQ+ nightclub, in the city in 2016.

But the Premier League held its final Rainbow Laces campaign from 29 November to 5 December, 2024, and it was surrounded by controversy.

Marc Guehi, then Crystal Palace captain, wrote 'I Love Jesus' on his armband in what was widely viewed as a protest against wearing the armband (which was not mandatory).

Ipswich captain Sam Morsy refused to wear the armband, while Manchester United scrapped plans for a Rainbow Laces-themed jacket during the pre-match ceremonies following an objection by Noussair Mazraoui.

Simon Blake, CEO of Stonewall, admitted to BBC Sport that by the end of its 12-year lifespan in the Premier League, Rainbow Laces in that form had run its course.

"When it started, getting a few people to wear the laces was success," he said. "When it ended, when a few people didn't wear them there was criticism.

"If a campaign no longer has a positive vibe, perhaps it is time to do something different.

"Everything must evolve and change, but visibility still matters, it is important not to throw the baby out with the bathwater."

Stonewall held its first ever Rainbow Laces Day on 8 October this year, to encourage LGBTQ+ people to engage with sport. It had no connection with the Premier League.

Marc Guehi and Crystal Palace did not face any action after he wrote on his rainbow armband

It is notable that the players most ardently against Rainbow Laces were also vocal in their religious beliefs.

Guehi has spoken of the importance in his life of his faith and was one of several devout Christian players in the Crystal Palace dressing room. Both Morsy and Mazraoui are practicing Muslims.

Both Holmes and Blake acknowledge that this may have had an impact on Rainbow Laces.

"The influence of religion is clearly quite significant, but I think more understanding is coming in too," said Holmes.

"We have seen many parts of life less willing to engage with Pride, and football reflects that. The nature of LGBT campaigns is peaks and troughs, and we are in a trough."

"We don't have any specific data about what people's beliefs and views are, but we are in a world where people have different views, even within religions themselves," added Blake.

"The important thing is about behaviours, and we must learn to live with people who have these different beliefs and respect them.

"I don't want to speculate, as it would be too easy to assume people of faith would not be inclusive, I know from my work across 30 years that is not true; I have worked with many religious people who are LGBTQ+ supportive. Freedom of belief is important."

The current picture is a difficult one for gay and trans people in football at all levels.

The grassroots game is where the bulk of the increase in abuse has occurred, says Kick It Out, which claims it received 140% more reports last year than in the 2024-25 season. Three quarters of these reports are from senior grassroots games, where the players are over 18.

Away from football, research by Stonewall published in October found 11% of LGBTQ+ people in the UK avoid the gym, exercise classes or sports groups for fear of discrimination. Among trans people, the figure rises to 29%.

It said 15% of LGBTQ+ people who had been to a live sporting event, or watched sport in a bar, in the past year had experienced discrimination there. This rose to 26% for trans people.

"What we know is that there is a lot of work going on around equality and diversity; we also know homophobia and transphobia continue to be rife," Blake said.

"We are in a world, whether at Premier League or grassroots football, where views that may have been perceived to be unacceptable are now being expressed freely. That influences peoples' relationship with sport."

Premier League With Pride will return in 2026-27.

"The matchday activations take place in February in line with LGBTQ+ History Month, when the league and clubs will showcase their commitment to LGBTQ+ inclusion," a Premier League spokesperson told BBC Sport.

"There will be two match rounds dedicated to Premier League With Pride. This means all 20 clubs have a home game during that period, providing an opportunity to highlight and celebrate the LGBTQ+ community in football and showcase the work undertaken to make the game more inclusive.

"There is no centralised activation lined up for November."

Hyperliquid's derivatives business has grown large enough to challenge some familiar centralised exchange names on a key measure: open interest. A third-quarter research report put positions open on the platform at approximately $16.8 billion at the end of September, a major increase over the previous quarter. For a decentralised trading venue, the scale is striking. But comparing it with conventional exchanges requires more than ranking a single number.

Open interest is the value of contracts that remain outstanding, not the amount of cash a venue has collected in fees or the value of trades settled that day. It reveals participation and leverage, while leaving important questions about market depth and resilience unanswered.

The Block's report on Hyperliquid's Q3 figures described open interest reaching roughly $16.8 billion, nearly 80% above the level recorded at the end of June. The report compared that snapshot with data for exchanges including Bybit, Kraken and OKX, though rankings depend on which products, venues and measurement conventions are included.

The headline number suggests that traders are comfortable maintaining substantial derivative exposure on Hyperliquid. It does not prove that all of that exposure is unhedged directional speculation. Some participants may be market makers or arbitrageurs carrying positions that offset risk elsewhere, while other traders could be using leverage to express very concentrated views.

The same Q3 analysis estimated approximately $191 million in protocol revenue, an increase of about 21.7% from the second quarter but a decrease of roughly 36.5% compared with the year-earlier period. That contrast is a useful warning against assuming that more open contracts automatically translate into stronger year-on-year economics. Revenue depends on trading activity, fees and the mix of products used.

Stock-linked perpetual products were another standout, with reported quarterly trading volume of about $153.8 billion, up approximately 161% sequentially. Yet volume can be affected by incentives, volatility and the churn of high-frequency participants. Healthy trading infrastructure also needs reliable pricing, liquid order books and a credible process for handling extreme market moves.

Perpetual futures let traders maintain leveraged exposure without a fixed expiry date. That flexibility comes with funding payments, margin requirements and liquidation risk. During a sharp market decline, forced closing of positions can magnify volatility. A platform's decentralised components do not eliminate the economic risk of carrying leverage or the operational need for robust safeguards.

This is particularly relevant after the week's crypto sell-off. Market watchers should compare open interest with trading volume, funding costs and liquidation activity rather than treating rising positions as inherently bullish. A platform can be technologically impressive and still host dangerous levels of speculative risk.

The rise of on-chain perpetuals adds to the broader discussion about where exchange activity should take place. NewsBTC's coverage of institutional interest in DeFi through Aave shows that traditional crypto firms are exploring decentralised infrastructure. Meanwhile, Aave's reserve-factor debate illustrates the governance decisions that shape the economics of on-chain services.

Hyperliquid's Q3 performance is evidence that decentralised derivatives can operate at considerable scale. The harder test comes during severe volatility, when users will judge it on execution quality, orderly liquidations and transparent risk management. Those outcomes will say more about the durability of its position than any single exchange league table.

The Design Fiend’s Guide to Tangier

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Moroccan city, a complex, culturally kaleidoscopic crossroads between Europe and Africa, is home to a savvy community of creatives whose stylish impact continues to grow. Word is out.

Above: The pool terrace at Villa Mabrouka, a 12-room hotel that was once the home of Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé, with gardens designed by Madison Cox.

Writing about Tangier in the 1950s, Truman Capote observed that “the days slide by less noticed than foam in a waterfall.” Decades later, this city on Morocco’s coast, across the Strait of Gibraltar from Spain, is significantly changed from Capote’s time, when it was an exotic retreat for café society’s demimonde, people such as Barbara Hutton and Malcolm Forbes. In recent years Tangier’s mosaic of villages—from the medina’s warren of whitewashed lanes to the Ville Moderne’s blocks of Art Deco towers to the hillside villas and gardens on Jebel Kebir—has been reinvigorated, in part by an enterprising mix of Tanjawis (the locals) and expats who continue to infuse the city with a fresh creative spirit.

Yet there is much that remains the same: the dreamy light that seduced artists from Eugène Delacroix to Henri Matisse to Mariano Fortuny, the overgrown gardens of casbah courtyards, and the cooling breezes coming off the confluence of Atlantic and Mediterranean waters. And there’s still plenty of the languorous lifestyle that captivated Capote.

“Tangier is all about settling in,” says Charlotte Claydon, whose whitewashed Moroccan Art Deco house has been in her family for more than a century. Known as Dar Sinclair, the residence sits in a grove of papyrus and lilies between thick palms and weeping willows. It’s one of numerous historic villas and townhouses renovated by international designers and other creatives who live here that can be rented. There’s arguably no better way to ease into the rhythms of daily life in Tangier. Pin Affleck, who arranges travel and accommodations through the Tangier Bureau, says that “going into a private house, you get the sense of being inside Tangier.”

When Claydon and her sister Jacquetta Wheeler began renting out Dar Sinclair, they received messages from guests worried about filling up a week. “We spent hours coming up with lists of activities, knowing full well they might only get to one or two,” Claydon says, noting that most guests make their own discoveries and end up on unplanned diversions.

Among the sisters’ can’t-miss activities: tea at Café de Paris, Sunday service at historic St. Andrew’s Church, and a camel ride, especially with kids, at Achakar Beach. A typical morning in Tangier might begin with breakfast under a banyan tree at El Morocco Club. Around 11 a.m. the markets buzz to life, including the legendary fish market in the Grand Socco, the Fondouk Chejra (weavers’ market), the antiques mall on Rue de Hollande, and the Casabarata flea market, where a sharp eye is required to spot the gems amid the seedy furniture and cheap fabrics.

Lunch in town at Alma Kitchen or El Dorado is a must for crispy falafel and plates of lamb chops. Later, as the sun sinks into the sea, it’s hard to beat the views from the roof of Villa Mabrouka, the onetime home of Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé that Jasper Conran transformed into hotel with gardens by Tangier resident Madison Cox. After a martini, the hotel’s L’Orangerie restaurant is a smart spot for a dinner of thistle tagine and grilled sole; down the street, the atmospheric Hamadi serves standout harissa soup and sizzling skewers.

Claydon and Wheeler, for their part, also advise guests to reserve time for relaxing and being transported at home, one of the great pleasures of staying in a design-centric private residence. Other places available to rent include the red-and-white-striped riad owned by decorator Gavin Houghton and photographer Boz Gagovski (who took most of the photos in this story), as well as antiques dealer Gordon Watson’s large ochre house. Both weave together Moorish and European details, with objects from Tangier and around the world that reflect the city’s cosmopolitan history. As Hamish Bowles noted in his foreword to the book Inside Tangier, “The magic of this place was that everyone who had chosen to make a home here brought their own fantasy with them.”

That book was produced by my husband, designer Nicolò Castellini Baldissera, and our home here, a 1929 townhouse named Casa Tosca, is filled with a worldly mix of objects and furnishings that we designed. Outside, our terraced garden was overseen by Umberto Pasti, whose own Tangier residence is a collection of 1930s bungalows set within meandering gardens of datura trees, rambling roses, and alocasia. For us, so much of the pleasure of this place is being at home and having people over. Creating the setting with local craftspeople and market finds becomes a pastime in itself.

It’s the same for Marco Scarani and Jamie Creel, whose house, Dar Zero, has had an illustrious lineage of owners who have contributed to its rich layers. The home is in the casbah, with a bushy fig tree and bougainvillea spilling from its courtyard over the front wall. Inside, iron klismos chairs from when designers Yves Vidal and Charles Sevigny lived there sit on well-worn bejmat and zellige tiles. There are also pieces of their own creation, such as brass bird-foot candlesticks they make in Tangier and sell in their New York shop, Creel & Gow. “There is a lot of transmission from one generation to another,” Scarani says. “Friends here inherit objects and even homes. We live in houses that have the spirits of the people who came before.”

For designers Frank de Biasi and Gene Meyer, it was the relationship with artisans that prompted them a few years ago to open Habibi Burton, their boutique where they offer pillows, painted tables, and a rotation of decorative objects, all sourced locally. “Making things for the shop gives me more freedom than making things for clients,” says de Biasi. “Because we aren’t designing to a deadline, I can have four artisans contribute to a single piece.” He also recommends Las Chicas for fashion, Kasbah Collective for regional crafts, and the bookshop Les Insolites.

Down the street, artist and curator Hicham Bouzid oversees Kiosk, a cultural salon and shop that hosts talks and exhibitions bringing together creatives from Africa, the Arab world, and Europe. It has a selection of books and artist prints, as well as a restaurant and live music. Bouzid is one of the native Tanjawis who are animating the city’s cultural life. Another is Yto Barrada, perhaps Morocco’s best-known contemporary artist, who founded the popular Cinéma Rif, where screenings of contemporary and classic films share the program with talks and other cultural events.

Across town Clos Nadir, the house shared by designer Jean-Louis Deniot and William Holloway, exists in a different world, perched among pines and looking out across the strait. Focusing on spaces for entertaining, Deniot placed a 30-foot dining table on a roof, while his living room is outfitted with a large fireplace, plush carpets, and a welcoming mix of furniture, mostly made locally. “Tangier is a land of artistic opportunity, a place for aesthetes, where stylistic adventures, contemplation, and the freedom to create are central to daily life,” says the ELLE Decor A-List designer, who is opening an office here.

Tangier’s close-knit community of creatives comes alive during the summer, which is when Veere Greeney, another ELLE Decor A-List designer, hosts an annual theatrical performance in the gardens of his mountaintop villa, Gazebo. Producer Rob Ashford assembles actors from Broadway and the West End (last summer it was Dames at Sea, featuring Darren Criss and Julianne Hough), and the shows are open to the public, though tickets are limited. It’s a full sensory experience colored by the scent of jasmine and gardenias, one that captures the eccentric artistic allure of Tangier.

The energy and interest in the city continue to grow. Deniot is creating a hotel with theater producer Jordan Roth, the Four Seasons recently announced a new property on Malabata Bay, and King Mohammed VI is behind a luxurious renovation of the historic Hotel Continental.

And while there is excitement around these new projects, what appeals most about Tangier is something older. Over lunch on Grenney’s terrace, looking out at Spain, we discussed the city’s past lives. “What makes Tangier unique is its varied history and strategic position,” he observed. “The strait made it a city of diplomacy, openness, and inclusiveness. That is its bedrock.”

COMAC’s CE-25A, China’s 19-seat battery-electric commuter plane, flew with a crew for the first time on Saturday in Binzhou, Shandong. Two pilots and a flight test engineer were aboard the roughly 10-tonne demonstrator, and COMAC says every system worked normally.

It wasn’t the plane’s first flight. The CE-25A had already completed 19 autonomous test flights with nobody on board since February 2, and China’s aviation regulator is now reviewing it for a type certificate.

The CE-25A is a T-tail commuter plane with 10 electric ducted fans on the wings, five per side, instead of propellers. COMAC lists a 300 km/h cruise speed and a 400 km (250-mile) range.

The CAAC accepting COMAC’s type certificate application starts the airworthiness review. It doesn’t mean paying passengers are close, and COMAC hasn’t given a date for commercial service.

The program has been quiet for a long time. COMAC showed a CE-25A model at the China International Import Expo in Shanghai in November 2023, with a placard listing 400 to 500 km of range and a 400 km/h top cruise speed. The full-size aircraft, registered B-0EFH, only became public when photos leaked on Chinese social media on October 28.

The obvious battery candidate is CATL. In July 2023, COMAC, CATL, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University formed a joint venture with CNY 600 million in registered capital to develop electric aircraft, as we reported when CATL launched its aviation unit.

CATL had just unveiled its “condensed” battery at the time, claiming up to 500 Wh/kg at the cell level, and said it was testing the cells to aviation standards. Neither company has confirmed what’s inside the CE-25A, though.

~500 Wh/kg has long been seen as the target for battery energy density to enable electric flight on a commercial scale.

COMAC isn’t alone in flying a large electric demonstrator. In August, Heart Aerospace flew its X1 in Plattsburgh, New York, for 27 minutes. It’s a 25,000-lb aircraft with a 106-foot wingspan, which Heart calls the largest battery-electric aircraft ever flown.

Eviation flew its 9-passenger Alice back in September 2022, then laid off most of its staff in early 2025.

Heart is the closest Western comparison. It dropped its 19-seat ES-19 for the 30-seat ES-30 back in 2022, and the ES-30 now relies on reserve turbogenerators to stretch its range, with BAE Systems working on the battery. Heart is targeting 2031 for service and claims “more than 40%” lower operating costs.

For years, the go-to excuse in the West for doing nothing about emissions was China. Why should we bother, the argument went, if China won’t? Now China is pulling ahead, and it’s doing it by cutting its dependence on fossil fuels.

Look at trucking. Electric trucks hit 50.3% of China’s heavy truck sales in September, and they’re already eating into the country’s diesel demand. In the US, electric models were about 0.3% of heavy-truck sales last year, according to the ICCT.

Now it’s planes, starting where batteries make sense today: short regional routes. The CE-25A’s 400 km shrinks once you keep the reserves regulators require, and a Beechcraft 1900 turboprop in the same class covers well over 1,000 km. So it’s not replacing turboprops anytime soon. But that’s how trucking started too. Short-haul first, then long-haul tractors once the batteries caught up.

And this couldn’t happen fast enough. We’re in the middle of a diesel and jet fuel crisis. Jet fuel averaged $3.50 a gallon in early August, up 63% from a year earlier, according to Heart Aerospace. Every truck and plane China moves to batteries is one less exposed to that.

China also has the batteries to pull it off, with CATL in the joint venture. Meanwhile, Heart has pushed to 2031 and added a fuel-burning backup, and Eviation is a cautionary tale.

The ultimate adulting task: signing up for health insurance

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Not many 22-year-olds share DJ Mikunda’s enthusiasm for health insurance coverage.

“You know what sounds like a good Friday night? It’s looking at health insurance,” he joked.

But Mikunda has a reason to be eager. He has gone without coverage since he left home and started working in 2023. He’s now a manager at a gas station in St. Louis.

In years past, he has looked at plans on the health insurance marketplace at healthcare.gov — but the options proved overwhelming and a bit too expensive.

“I feel like I’m a well-informed individual,” Mikunda said. “I try to stay updated on what a deductible is and what the premiums are and copays. And I’m still finding myself a little bit lost.”

To sort through the basics, HealthQ introduced Mikunda to independent assister Jackie Shrago, who has been helping people navigate the insurance marketplace since the early days of the Affordable Care Act.

Search for a free “independent assister” on healthcare.gov to better understand your insurance options.

Shrago started by asking Mikunda about his income. How much money you expect to make in the coming year determines whether you’re eligible for reduced monthly payments. The system uses a sliding scale for households making less than 400% of the federal poverty rate — which means, for 2027 enrollees, an individual with no children can make up to $63,480 and still qualify for a discount.

A person in their mid-20s making $50,000, for instance, would get an average 23% discount, according to KFF’s subsidy calculator. For someone making $30,000, the tax credit would cover nearly three-quarters of the cost.

It’s important to estimate next year’s income as accurately as possible. If you end up making more than anticipated, you may owe money back to the government.

“The last thing you want is a surprise when you file your income taxes,” Shrago told Mikunda.

Short-Term Affordability vs. Long-Term Needs

Then, Shrago pivoted to the other considerations: How much can you afford to pay every month? And what kind of coverage do you really need?

It’s a personal decision about financial planning and risk tolerance, balancing up-front payments with out-of-pocket costs. Mikunda knows he’ll need regular doctor visits and prescription medication, because he has a chronic joint condition that has gone virtually untreated since he started working and lost Medicaid coverage.

So he might consider stretching his monthly budget for a plan that covers more of the medical cost.

Those financial trade-offs drive much of the decision, forcing you to find a “comfortable medium” between the level of coverage you want and the amount you’re willing to pay, Mikunda said.

There’s much to consider, especially for a first-timer. But one-on-one help is available at healthcare.gov.

If you click on the link to “Find local help,” you can filter specifically to search for “independent assisters” like Shrago in your ZIP code. Unlike insurance agents and brokers, assisters are forbidden from charging for their services or receiving compensation from insurance companies. (The brokers listed at healthcare.gov far outnumber the independent assisters, but the assisters are findable.)

Shrago also recommends starting the selection process early enough to step away and think.

“If you’re dealing with something that is uncertain enough and you feel really awkward about it, you don’t need to make a decision today,” she said. “Take your time and think about it.”

Assisters tend to have the least availability right before the federal marketplace’s Jan. 15 deadline, so reach out early.

The Trump administration cut funding for independent assisters and navigator nonprofits by roughly 90% in 2025. When making that announcement, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services said the program was inefficient, costing more than $1,000 per person enrolled. The reduction in funding means fewer people helping. In Ohio, for example, the number of paid navigators serving state residents dropped from 50 to five at the start of open enrollment in 2025.

Barack Obama says work-life balance isn’t real for successful people

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If finding success in your career is on your bucket list, you might need to get used to working lunches and staying past 5 p.m. That’s, at least, according to former President Barack Obama, who revealed that achieving proper work–life balance will not always be possible.

“If you want to be excellent at anything—sports, music, business, politics—there’s going to be times of your life when you’re out of balance, where you’re just working and you’re single-minded,” he admitted on The Pivot Podcast in 2024.

It’s a lesson he—and his family—learned the hard way.

The 65-year-old said when he first ran for president, he was on the constant grind for over a year and a half. While he said he would take some breaks during the weekends, his wife, Michelle, was largely left picking up the slack at home, like taking care of the kids, even while she had a full-time job of her own.

Though the time away from his family led to two terms in the White House, Obama admitted it wasn’t sustainable. As president, he set a rule where he had family dinner every night at 6:30 p.m.—even if he had to go back to work later. And while some leaders might think it takes away valuable work time, he said the opportunity to decompress with family was actually incredibly impactful.

“They ground you and give you perspective, and over time, that makes you better,” he said.

Even as a former president, Obama said his political accomplishments—what kept him away from his family—won’t be what he remembers at the end of life. Instead, it’ll be the love and memories he had with his children.

“I don’t think there’s a perfect formula,” he said at an Obama Foundation event in 2019. “I think it starts with recognizing that on my deathbed, I am confident that I will not remember any bill I passed, I will not remember any speech I gave, any big crowds.”

That’s not to say career aspirations are not important, he said, it just means the level of commitment someone can give to their work all depends on what life is like at home—and vice versa.

“There will be phases in your life where you have to prioritize different things,” Obama said. “There are times where it will be okay for you to just throw yourself into work because everything’s in a pretty good place. A Desk Jockey Passed on 40 Brown Bears. The 41st Charged

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 15, 2026 10:00 AM EST

This story, “The 41st Bear,” appeared in the September 1961 issue of Outdoor Life.

John Yenter turned away from a 20X spotting scope and looked me right in the eye. He said, “Bill, you’re absolutely crazy. Forget your foolish specifications and collect that bear. You’re acting like a decrepit old desk hand, not a hunter.”

I had just refused to go after the 39th bear that failed to meet my specifications, and John, my hunting partner, was again giving me a piece of his mind. He never uses profanity, but he can do a blistering job without it. He had killed his bear like an expert, and now I, the junior member, was giving him trouble. Being six years older than I am, he felt perfectly justified in straightening me out.

It started months ago back in York, Pennsylvania, where my job — director of purchases for a large manufacturer of air-conditioning equipment — classifies me as a professional desk hand. I guess I look the part — gray, balding, and past several of man’s milestones.

My business associates in Borg-Warner are fond of trying to decide whether I was born a penny-pincher, which made me a purchasing agent, or the other way around. While they were debating that issue, I was concentrating on a 60-day toughening-up routine of golf, bowling, hiking, and calisthenics to convert my body from paper-shuffling to hunting condition. This was important because John C. Yenter, a building contractor from Tyrone, Pennsylvania, and I were planning a Kodiak bear hunt. We had so completely enjoyed our polar bear hunt in Norway’s frozen arctic region (“Desk Hand vs. Polar Bear,” Outdoor Life, December, 1957) that John decided his work with West Virginia Pulp & Paper Company could wait while we took a brown-bear hunt.

I wrote down some rigid specifications for my trophy brown bear and made a silent vow to live up to them. I wanted a male with a 10-foot-square hide, brown in color, a perfect pelt, and weighing 1,000 pounds or more.

Research indicated that Kodiak Island raised the largest, brownest bears, and a spring hunt would be my best bet for a trophy with a full-grown, unrubbed pelt. The idea was to get a bear fresh out of hibernation — before he ruined his pelt with mating-season fights or started to shed his winter coat. We scheduled our hunt for late April and early May, which appeared to be the prime time for Kodiak brownies.

We met our outfitter, Alf Madsen, in Kodiak Village, on April 27. We knew that most Kodiak bear hunting is done from the salt-water bays around the island, but records show that more big bears are taken in the interior. So we were bound for Karluk Lake, in the center of the southern part of the island.

The flight up the valleys, around and between the snow-covered mountains, was a sight so beautiful that we almost forgot we were in a little single-engine pontoon plane and at times many miles from any water to sit down on. We were in camp and ready to hunt on the morning of April 28.

Our camp consisted of one frame cabin, one tent cabin, and an outhouse, without door, 100 yards north on the main bear trail. All the camp equipment, including two outboard boats for lake travel, had been flown in.

Karluk Lake looked a lot like one of the fjords of Norway. The blue water of the lake was now entirely clear of ice, but there were deep snowdrifts on the mountain peaks above the lake. If we’d never seen a bear, the wild beauty of the place would have repaid me for the trip in full.

We started the hunting campaign with all energy, and in two days we saw and classified 16 bears. I refused to stalk any of them because they failed to meet my specifications. John eventually suggested that I was displaying the stubbornness of a mule, rather than the calm judgment of a purchasing agent.

Our guides were by reputation the best on the island, and they soon proved that they deserved their standing. Some would call them Aleuts, but in reality they were members of the Koniaga Tribe, which has been considerably diluted by the Russians who owned the island until we bought Alaska from Russia in 1867. Our guides were proud of their Russian and Koniaga lineage, but they’re loyal United States citizens.

My guide was Grishka Nicoli, and John’s was Nick Malutin. These guides spoke their native Koniaga language, which is spoken only and not written. They also spoke fluent Russian and awkward English. They spend about three months of the year as hunting guides and four months as commercial salmon fishermen. For the rest of the year they’re Koniaga gentlemen of leisure. Like my hunting partner John Yenter, the guides were convinced that I was entirely too meticulous in my trophy specifications.

The only encouragement I had came from Kim Clark, who had become a very important part of our expedition. His full name was Webster Kimberly Clark, and he was a refuge biologist employed by the U. S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Kim had been reared in Greenfield, Massachusetts, had studied at the universities of Michigan, Massachusetts, Missouri, and Yale. He has a Doctor’s degree. Clark had done a vast amount of field research and had become one of the top authorities on North American bears. He was stationed on Kodiak Island by the federal government to study Kodiak bears.

Kim Clark had read my stories in Outdoor Life.* When Kim learned from outfitter Alf Madsen that I was planning a Kodiak hunt, he met me in Kodiak Village and asked if he could join the group to observe and record the data on bears killed. We were glad to have a man of his ability and knowledge.

During the early part of our stay on Karluk Lake, Kim worked alone and permitted us to hunt as hunters, not as scientists. He was a great addition to our expedition, because his lectures at night permitted us to do practical field work the next day in connection with our hunt. Between hunting, field and night classes, we were extremely well occupied.

Had it not been for Kim encouraging me to stick to my specifications, I might have given up. He encouraged me because accurate field data on a big male brown bear just out of hibernation is apparently very scarce.

By the third day my partner John was adjusting his own specifications to suit the bear that was in sight. We were glassing a brownie on the shoreline across the lake, and through the glasses it looked good — perfect hide, pleasing color, but slightly under my requirements as to size. John wanted it.

In a few minutes we were on our way across the lake, approaching very quietly so as not to alarm the bear. We landed several hundred yards from the bruin, and John started stalking the animal with his guide. In spite of the skill they displayed, the bear caught the man smell. He was on his way up the mountainside before John could shoot. He and Nick stood on the shore watching the occasional motion of a bush which indicated that the bear was going higher and away.

The bear was 150 yards from the lake shore when he came into a little opening in the brush where we could see him. At that point he made the biggest and last mistake of his life. He stopped and looked back. At that moment John fired. The bear fell. Two more shots finished him completely. John’s performance was a credit to him, to his .375 H. & H. Winchester, Model 70 rifle, and its Bausch & Lomb scope sight.

It was without question a big bear, the color was excellent, the 8½-feet-square hide was prime and perfect. John was a thoroughly happy hunter.

With his bear hide strung up to dry, John concentrated on the wonderful trout fishing near camp, with time out now and then to think up some new criticisms of my ideas about a proper trophy.

At the end of the ninth day we sized up the situation and found that we had glassed 40 bears, killed one. I was still holding out for a bigger and better one.

The weather was perfect for the first three days, which was probably why we saw so many bears. There was rain every day after that, and visibility was often too poor for good work with binoculars. There’s no climate on Kodiak, just a vast assortment of unexpected and undesirable weather.

When we started out the 10th morning, we found that what had been rain at the lake camp during the night had been snow above the 1,500-foot level on the mountain. In spite of that, our hunting began early, and at 8:30 we saw a bear that looked very interesting. He was a mile from the lake shore, 1,600 feet higher, just above the new but temporary snow line.

Trying to get a better look at the bear, we moved up the bed of Cottonwood Creek. This took us out of sight of the bruin momentarily, but we knew exactly where he was because the snow line established his elevation and the pass made in the mountains by the creek marked his location the other way. Yet, an hour’s looking from our new position failed to locate him.

We then climbed up high on the right bank of Cottonwood Creek, and from there we glassed constantly until noon. We felt sure that the bear was there because we certainly would have seen him if he’d walked away.

At noon we abandoned that position and moved to a spot across the lake, a mile farther away, which gave us a better perspective and a view of more of the ground area. We took turns eating and resting, but we were constantly looking. The rain returned in full force and it was a very uncomfortable situation.

We never saw a sign of the bear until 4 o’clock, when suddenly he stood up and was in plain view again. It was now evident that we’d first seen him when he finished his morning feeding, and he’d bedded down for the day. Now he was up feeding again.

After a few minutes of careful observation, the guides decided that this bear would meet every point in my specifications. This was a fine display of skill on the part of the guides and is something I could never hope to duplicate. To the inexperienced, a bear two miles away through a 20X Bausch & Lomb telescope is just a bear, but my guide was able to classify him very accurately as to sex, size, and condition.

I knew enough by now to trust this guide’s judgment, and in a few minutes we were on our way back across the lake to start the stalk.

Before we reached the far shore the clouds had dropped so low that the mountains were completely obscured.

Since John had killed his bear, we decided that he’d stay with the boat and the two guides would accompany me on the climb up the mountain. It took a few minutes to strip for action, which meant down to heavy underwear, a sweat shirt, rain suit, and hip boots. This sounds like unusual mountain-climbing equipment, but the first mile of our climb would be accomplished by wading up the creek. It was much easier that way than climbing through the murderous alder thickets on either side.

By 5:30 we’d accomplished the most difficult part of the climb and were at about the same height as the bear and 500 to 800 yards to the right of him. At this point we climbed out of the creek and dived into the alder thicket. All alder thickets are nasty, but this one was the worst that I’d ever seen.

Soon we picked up a prominent bear trail, a path so worn that one of the guides called it “a bear highway.” It was obviously the main bear trail on that particular mountainside, and we felt sure that my bear was on it. We had no idea which way he was traveling, so it was possible that we’d meet him at any moment. It was also possible that he’d park a few yards off the trail to observe and properly take care of anything that might be using the bear highway. It’s this kind of thinking that prevents daydreaming on brown-bear stalks.

Grishka, who was in front of me, explained that he’d lead and watch ahead. Nick would bring up the rear and watch behind us and to the sides of the trail. I was free to watch any angle that suited my fancy. I think this was a polite way of telling me that they didn’t trust my powers of observation.

I was planning what I’d do if certain things happened. I realized that any shooting would be at very close range because of the thick alders and the heavy fog. While my guides were both carrying .348 Winchesters with open sights, I was carrying a .375 Winchester with a Bausch & Lomb scope. No scope is ideal equipment for a quick shot at 10 yards, and I wished for an old-fashioned V notch and front post.

Nick must have been reading my mind. He said, “Bill, if we meet the bear and you have trouble with your rifle, just throw it down and grab mine. I’ll be right behind you.”

Nothing could have done me more harm than that speech. I knew now that he had no more confidence in my equipment than I had, and I was scared stiff. I had enough acid in my stomach to pickle a ton of iron castings.

We soon came out into the little open spot on the mountainside that we’d marked as the location of the bear when last seen almost two hours before. This clearing was about 100 feet in each direction — no alders, just dead grass almost knee high. We spent 20 minutes searching the open area and forcing our way into the brush around the edges, trying to pick up a trail or some sign of the bear.

Suddenly my head was gripped by Grishka, a powerful man but only five feet tall. He pulled my head down to his level, slapped his wet face against mine. Pointing to the right, he whispered in my ear, “Bear there. Shoot!”

About 75 yards away, through a little opening in the brush, I could see a dark spot. It could have been a bear. As I dropped to one knee, my perfect guide braced his knees to my back to steady my position. His 9X binoculars were tight to his eyes and just above my head as he watched the bear.

When I picked up that brown spot in my 2½X scope, I couldn’t tell whether it was a bear or not. If it was, I couldn’t for the life of me see which way he was pointing.

In a whisper, I asked Grishka, “Which end is the head on?”

After a full minute’s careful observation through his glasses, he put his mouth to my ear and said, “Other end. Shoot!”

That concise data from Grishka was fed through my fire-control center down to the trigger finger, and my gun fired. I was so excited I didn’t hear the report of the rifle, nor was I conscious that there was any recoil. But I did hear the bullet hit something, and then a fraction of a second later I knew it was a bear. He roared his complete disapproval of the whole situation.

Then it seemed that the brown bulk went up in the air several feet. An instant later it was on the ground and for a split second was completely still. At that moment I fired my second shot, and the bear’s reaction to that was a quick lunge to the right, toward the alders. I fired my third shot as he disappeared.

Grishka had said the first shot was good, but I didn’t see how he could possibly know. The bear had disappeared into the brush just as though he hadn’t been touched.

Both guides were now running as hard as they could to the right — in the same general direction the bear was going when we last saw him. It was quite obvious that they were attempting to get ahead of the bear before he reached the creek bed, which was 600 yards away. I caught them quickly and stayed right with them, not due to any bravery on my part, but simply because it was obviously much safer to stay close to them and their two rifles.

Our travel in the next few minutes was parallel to that of the bear, and we could follow his location by the noise he made. I thought — and hoped — that the amount of noise was due to the fact that he was badly wounded, running blind and knocking brush to one side.

A few seconds later we were on the edge of the creek where we could look down a steep bank to the water 100 feet below. Then I realized that the noise of the bear was not to my left, as it had been, but almost back of me and getting closer. I turned to face the noise. A second later that bear came into sight less than 20 yards away.

It was the most stirring sight a thrill hunter could ever hope to see. That bear was coming at us at top speed, and it looked as though he knew exactly what he was going to do when he got there.

I slammed the gun to my shoulder and looked at the bear in the scope sight. The contour of the ground was such that I could see the back of his neck, so I selected a spot where the neck joins the shoulders. The crosshairs settled on that spot. I tightened on the trigger, adding pressure and waiting for the recoil.

But the bear rolled dead as Caesar before my rifle went off. I lowered the weapon still cocked and loaded. I never have felt so cheated in all my life. In another 1/10th of a second I could have been the Man Who Stopped the Charging Brownie.

Later examination showed that the first shot had gone very effectively into his side just back of the ribs, forward under the spine, just grazing it, cutting the main blood vessels just back of the heart. Clearly the bear had been too far gone to make a determined charge. He was only making a blind run with his last spark of life, and we happened to be in his path.

That bear was coming at us at top speed, and it looked as though he knew exactly what he was going to do when he got there.

I examined the bear closely, and was glad that I’d stuck to my specifications. It was a male, the color dark brown, the hide in perfect condition, and the size was enough to flatter any desk hand. It was the 41st bear we’d seen on this trip.

Suddenly I realized that it was beginning to get dark. We’d have to leave the bear and return the next day to do the skinning. I was worried about what might happen to the hide overnight, but Grishka said, “We leave strong man smell here. Nothing bother.” With a grin he added, “The strongest thing here is white-man smell of your sweat shirt. We leave it.”

I peeled off the shirt and he tied it to a limb above the bear. When we returned the next morning the shirt and the bear were there and nothing had bothered it.

The guides, Kim Clark, and I left camp early next morning and climbed the mountain to skin the bear. John remained in camp. He and the cook, Pete Unger, had planned a fishing trip.

Kim was keenly interested in the bear from our description so we went in loaded with camera equipment, scales, surgical instruments, test tubes, and specimen bottles. Very few Kodiak bears have ever been accurately weighed and examined, and this is especially true of big bears just out of hibernation.

My bear was an ideal specimen for studying the effects of hibernation, because he evidently had just come out of that lethargic state. The stomach was found to be about 1/10 full, and the contents weighed 16 pounds. Carefully separated and weighed, we found that the contents were 55 percent blue-joint grass, 20 percent nettle, 20 percent wild celery, and 5 percent poque, which is a parasitic growth on the roots of the alders. There was no animal matter present.

The intestine was then examined and the length was accurately determined to be 77 feet 6¾ inches. It was then split from end to end for internal examination. There was no food in it. This indicated two things: first, that the bear had not been out of hibernation long enough for food to pass through the stomach into the intestine, and second, that he had gone into hibernation with the intestine completely empty, which conformed to the generally accepted theory. Further examination revealed there wasn’t a sign of ulcer, scar, scab, or scale in the stomach or intestine.

We also observed that there were no parasites of any kind in the entire digestive tract. This is one of the strange results of hibernation, because this bear during the six months of feeding eats carrion of all kinds, specializing in dead fish. He also digs up and eats worms, insects, and roots. When winter comes, he’s literally and actually a fur coat full of worms. There are thousands of them, including 10-foot-long tapeworms. The bear knows instinctively that he cannot go into hibernation with any food in his body; it would ferment and cause serious trouble before spring.

So when winter approaches he goes on a bedtime diet of certain plants and berries. Then, after a few days of fasting, he’s completely empty. The worms go with the food, and he goes to sleep physically clean. His stored body fat keeps him going till spring.

Every piece of this bear was carefully weighed. He weighed 840 pounds at the time he was killed. Kim Clark figured his weight when he was fattened for hibernation in the fall would have been about 1,300 pounds.

His age was estimated at ten years. The rough field measurement of the skull indicated a Boone and Crockett rating of 28½, against the world record of slightly above 30 inches. Unless the skull shrinks more than an inch during the drying period that must precede official scoring, the trophy should rate a place on the Boone and Crockett record list. This was in every respect an outstanding bear. I learned a lot from him, and much more from Kim Clark.

The skull and all the data collected were sent to Dr. Robert Rausch, Arctic Health Research Center, Anchorage, Alaska, where it became a part of their records.

The black "Y" sign was the one that initially piqued our interest, which, by the way, highlights that a Y-shaped intersection is to be found up ahead. That led us to look at other weird letter- and symbol-focused road signs drivers may come across.

The obvious candidates are "P" for parking areas and "H" that marks hospitals, which are universally understood, but throughout this article, we will also explore what those weird "T" or upturned "U" signs mean, in addition to winding "S" and plus-symbol signs. That includes where drivers are most likely to find them and how best to react when they do. Listed in no particular order, here are our picks of eight letter- or symbol-focused road signs that we thought are worth exploring in a bit more detail.

As already explained briefly above, a sign that depicts a capitalized "Y" is there to let drivers know of a Y-shaped three-way intersection or a forked road ahead. The sign will typically be a diamond shape with a yellow background, which means it's a simple warning sign alerting drivers that the road splits. The choices of where to go are obvious — either left or right — but what might be less obvious is how the right of way is determined in a three-way intersection that isn't controlled by any other signs.

The general rule is that drivers must yield to the car on the right. Then there's always the first-to-arrive rule, which works as you would intend — the first to arrive at the split goes first. Of course, some drivers will assume right of way when it is not deserved, or perhaps they just do not see the other car(s). In such instances, it's best to yield to the other vehicle even if you believe you should have the right of way to ensure everyone gets past the intersection without incident.

When a road splits like this, it pays to be especially cautious. Drivers may attempt to dart from one lane to the next when correcting a last-minute wrong decision, plus motorbikes or cyclists could be hiding in your blind spots, so try to ensure you have sufficient space around you if possible. The NHTSA highlights that roughly one third of all traffic fatalities occur at signposted intersections, so doubling down on cautiousness here is never a bad idea, no matter how simple it may first appear.

Surprise surprise, the letter "T" on a road sign warns of a T-shaped intersection — we would hope that you managed to work that one out. The road you are traveling on is about to end and merge with another. Once again, this is a classic warning sign finished in a yellow and black colorway, on a diamond-shaped sign that lets drivers know something's coming up and they will have to act accordingly.

In this instance, drivers must yield to traffic on the intersecting road — they don't have the right of way here — before heading either left or right. There will usually be a stop or yield sign prior to the intersection itself, and to avoid any frustrating bumps with other drivers making late decisions, it's best to get into the required lane early rather than last-minute. Thankfully, of the deadliest intersections on U.S. roads, not many are T-shaped, but the same rules as above apply here. Give yourself space, keep an eye on your blind spots, and don't rush. Oh, and don't forget to use your turn signals either, even if you are in a dedicated turn lane.

Again, we won't be handing out prizes to anyone who is able to correctly guess this one. A cross or "plus" symbol indicates that a crossroad is coming up, and just like the other aforementioned sign, this too will be depicted on a black and yellow diamond-shaped warning sign. Here, traffic can and likely will be coming from all directions, so it's imperative to take the time that's needed and approach it with care. Be aware that drivers on the opposite leg might try to cross ahead of you as you pull out, and be sure to check traffic from both left and right directions. As always, make sure to follow the instructions depicted on any regulatory signs posted in your direction, as these might detail give-way instructions.

There is a good case to make here in favor of the roundabout. In many ways, it is a far better design than the traditional intersection, with fewer conflict points, no left-turns across the flow of traffic, and lower speeds. Roundabouts might seem perplexing to some, but according to data from the Federal Highway Administration, they have been proven to reduce injury and fatal crashes by 76% and 90%, respectively, plus they are far more time-efficient than traffic signals, so perhaps road users ought to just get used to them.

Drivers would be wise to pay attention to these speeds, as around 25% of all fatal road incidents happen on a curved section of road, with common causes being cars either drifting off into trees, rocks, or other fixed objects, or indeed drifting across the center of the road and causing a head-on collision with other traffic.

Drivers should continue on, take a left or right turn if available, and find another suitable route. Exceptions will not be made for those in classic red-and-white Gran Torinos.

For those who may question the legality of U-turns when this sign isn't present, drivers can usually perform such a move in these circumstances: They should be in the leftmost or center-left lane, signalling their intention, and keeping an eye out for traffic as per any other manoeuvre.

A sideways "T" sign indicates that a side road is coming up. Depending on which way the T is oriented, the sign highlights whether the side road is on the right or the left. If the sign instead looks like an F or an E, but with an extended top or bottom, this means that two side roads are coming up on the right-hand side.

Specifically, this sign will appear when the side roads on the right are particularly close to one another. The hazard this poses to drivers on the major road is quite obvious; they should be wary of traffic entering from the right, and should be prepared to slow down. Getting the right lane is crucial for avoiding accidents; the leftmost lane is used for turning left, whereas the rightmost lanes are used for turning right, and both can be used for going straight on. These are the rules on multi-lane roundabouts unless signposted otherwise — this way, traffic in either lane does not cross unnecessarily.

Where was Nvidia’s security team?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Outsiders had little reason to notice when Nvidia’s longtime chief security officer and head of product security stepped down in August. Just two and a half months later, CEO Jensen Huang himself left the company over growing questions about his extramarital relationships — starting with an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore data center company that U.S. investigators suspect of funneling Nvidia chips to China, then widening to reports about two separate affairs with Nvidia employees.

At the time, David Reber, who spent four years as CSO and seven at Nvidia overall, said he was leaving for health reasons. “This wasn’t a pivot I had planned. But I know what needs to come first: family and health,” he wrote on LinkedIn in late August, disclosing that he’d been diagnosed with young-onset Parkinson’s more than two years ago.

Reber’s replacement, Saša Zdjelar, previously spent 17 years at ExxonMobil in Houston, leaving as a senior IT manager before spending one year at Salesforce and several years at the cybersecurity company ReversingLabs. Zdjelar didn’t respond to questions Thursday about the transition or what the security team knew.

Nothing public connects Reber’s or Zdjelar’s role changes to the Megaspeed matter, but it seems fair to wonder what Nvidia’s security team knew about the government’s scrutiny of Megaspeed and about Huang’s relationships, and whether anyone raised concerns. Reber didn’t respond to questions Thursday. Nvidia also has not answered our related questions.

Not all of those relationships are the business of the security team. Relationships with subordinates typically fall to HR, legal, and the board. But an affair with an executive at a company under federal investigation is a different animal. (Megaspeed has denied any wrongdoing.) Security specialists say affairs can create vulnerabilities that foreign intelligence services look to exploit.

Catching that kind of exposure isn’t easy. The way intelligence services collect information, much of it through everyday conversation rather than hacking, makes it especially difficult for security teams to identify risks, according to retired senior intelligence officer Nicholas Eftimiades, who says he has trained more than 1,000 corporate security professionals.

Some people inside Nvidia clearly knew parts of the picture. Nvidia’s top lawyer was engaged with the government’s Megaspeed inquiry more than a year ago, The New York Times reported earlier this week. (Nvidia has said it regularly contacts officials.) Reports about Huang’s relationships with employees suggest colleagues also knew of them, even if the behavior is not expressly forbidden by Nvidia’s code of conduct. The Times cited five Nvidia employees who said he was involved with a vice president who reported directly to him, and Reuters, citing two people, later reported a second relationship. What’s unclear is whether any of that reached security.

Even if it had reached Nvidia’s security team, Eftimiades, who wrote Chinese Espionage Operations and Tactics in 2020, said a security team’s resources are often limited. For one thing, they’re often underfunded because, unlike some other cost centers, they don’t drive revenue. It’s astonishingly common for CEOs to answer a security team’s warnings with “not a nickel more than compliance,” he said.

Many companies also define security very narrowly, specialists say. In an opinion piece in The Hill, Don Aviv, CEO of the corporate investigations firm Interfor International, and author Jeremy Hurewitz, wrote, “Too many companies still approach security primarily as a matter of protecting facilities, preventing cyberattacks, and responding to physical threats.” The two argued that “[s]ecurity teams need to pay greater attention to insider threats, including the cultivation of executives and employees by foreign intelligence services, suspicious business relationships, conflicts of interest and attempts to obtain sensitive information through trusted personal contacts.”

Still, some of it is beyond what any team can do. Speaking generally, not about Huang, Eftimiades offered a hypothetical in which a CEO complains that he can’t do something because “I’m fighting a board who doesn’t want to do this.” That tells an intelligence asset who the obstacle is. The service then asks, “Who’s the sticking point here?” and looks for ways into that person’s email and phone.

What a rival intelligence service wants is often less technology than policy insight, including who’s pushing to change export rules and who the biggest blocker is. “Intel world tends to be like cancer,” he said. “Once it gets into the body, it looks to spread and looks to reach out.”

Warren Buffett Said This 1 Mistake Cost Berkshire About $10 Billion. Here’s How Investors Can Avoid It.

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Buffett said that hesitating over a small price gap on Walmart cost Berkshire about $10 billion. Here’s how to avoid the same trap.

Warren Buffett never hid from his investment mistakes. Instead, he wanted to learn from them. One of his most expensive blunders wasn't a bad purchase, but one he never completed.

At Berkshire Hathaway's (BRKA +0.47%)(BRKB +0.53%) 2004 annual meeting, Buffett confessed that his worst mistake was Walmart (WMT +1.51%). While he initially wanted to buy 100 million shares of the retail giant, Berkshire bought only a small amount because, as the price went up, Buffett waited for it to come back down, which never happened. His reluctance to pay a little more turned out to be a $10 billion mistake.

The mistake wasn't buying a bad stock. It was getting too anchored to a price on a good one. That's a trap almost every investor falls into, and it's easy to avoid once you know what to look for.

Walmart was exactly the kind of business Buffett wanted to own. It was a dominant low-cost leader. He set his sights on buying 100 million shares at a pre-split price of $23. Buffett bought some shares, but then the price rose slightly. He thought it might come back down, so he waited. That pullback never came. In hindsight, he said paying a little more would have been inconsequential compared to the gains the stock would go on to deliver.

Buffett did try to fix this mistake. Berkshire built a new Walmart stake starting in 2005, which was worth nearly $6 billion by 2016, the year it sold most of it.

Berkshire's missed gains on Walmart show why anchoring can be so costly. If you think a $50 stock has the potential to gain 50% over the next few years, paying 5% more ($52.50) than your desired price would yield a 43% return. That's not a bad return. Waiting for the price to come back down to your target, on the other hand, risks the entire upside if it never does. A small premium costs you a little in the long run, while a missed position costs you the whole gain.

The lesson here isn't to pay any price for a stock. Buffett's Walmart mistake served as a valuable lesson because Walmart's an exceptional business, so paying a few percentage-point premium was small relative to its upside. However, chasing a stock that has already run up, or paying a premium for an average business, are different mistakes. It's fine to pay a little bit more than you'd like for a high-quality business, but don't go overboard.

Patience and price discipline are good habits, but Buffett's Walmart miss shows how they can backfire when a small price gap keeps you out of a great business. Decide what a stock is worth before you buy, set your purchases in advance, and weigh any premium against the upside you expect. That way, your discipline protects you from overpaying without costing you the opportunity.

How Much Does An Airbus A380 Engine Cost?

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ask what an Airbus A380 engine costs, and the most honest answer is that the sticker price barely tells the story. Neither Rolls-Royce nor Engine Alliance publishes a catalog figure for the Trent 900 or the GP7200, and the biggest numbers attached to them in public are billion-dollar contracts that cover much more than the engines themselves. According to analyst estimates reported by AviationPros, a single GP7200 once carried a list price of roughly $7 million to $10.5 million. Yet one engine's share of Emirates' 2015 Rolls-Royce deal works out several times higher, and that difference points to where the real cost lies.

This article traces that difference back to the published estimates for the contracts airlines actually signed. Two engine families sit at the center of the story. Rolls-Royce builds the three-shaft Trent 900, while Engine Alliance, a 50-50 venture between GE Aerospace and Pratt & Whitney, builds the GP7200. Both powered the A380-800, the only member of the A380 family to reach service, but neither was ever simply sold as a piece of hardware.

There is no published list price, so every answer is necessarily an estimate. The analyst range quoted above came from an older report written while the A380 was still being delayed, and Pratt & Whitney declined to confirm it, noting that airlines usually receive discounts on large orders. After two decades of inflation and engine upgrades, it likely understates today's replacement cost. More importantly, it describes a bare engine, which is rarely what an airline actually signs for, and says nothing about spare engines, tooling, or the support network required to keep an engine flying.

The contracts signed by major A380 operators show why the simple number can be misleading. According to Emirates' own announcement, its April 2015 deal with Rolls-Royce for Trent 900 engines and a long-term TotalCare package covering 50 A380s was worth $9.2 billion, although a footnote put the order-book value at $6.1 billion. That figure included the engines and long-term support rather than representing a simple purchase price. Etihad's GP7200 deal was structured similarly: GE Aerospace said in its original announcement that the airline selected GP7200s for ten A380s and signed a multiyear Fleet Management Agreement covering maintenance, repair and overhaul, with a total value of more than $1.3 billion. A subsequent GE Aerospace announcement put the value at up to $1.5 billion. Rolls-Royce described the Emirates order as the largest in its history in its press release.

These figures therefore cannot be treated as straightforward engine prices. Emirates' $9.2 billion package covered 200 Trent 900 engines plus TotalCare, while Etihad's $1.3 billion-plus GP7200 agreement covered engines for ten A380s together with long-term fleet management and maintenance. Dividing the headline contract values by the number of installed engines would produce roughly $46 million per engine for the Emirates package and more than $32.5 million per engine for Etihad's original $1.3 billion figure, but those calculations include services and should not be mistaken for the price of the hardware itself.

The reason these numbers diverge is straightforward: an A380 engine contract is rarely only an engine purchase. The Emirates and Etihad packages bundled hardware with years of servicing. TotalCare covers overhauls, spare parts, and health monitoring, while Etihad's agreement included long-term fleet management. Engine makers can heavily discount the hardware and recover part of their investment through the aftermarket, a model established in the commercial engine business for decades.

The size of those discounts can make a published list price almost theoretical. As AirInsight reported from Rolls-Royce's patent lawsuit against Pratt & Whitney, Rolls-Royce said it had to discount the Trent 900 by more than 87%, compared with a normal discount of 77%. That makes it difficult to treat the nominal price of an engine as a reliable measure of what an airline actually pays.

The service contract is a crucial part of the economics. Aircraft Commerce explains that most Rolls-Royce engines are maintained under TotalCare packages, with services selected from a menu and billed per engine flight hour, giving airlines a predictable cost. AirInsight also wrote that Engine Alliance said it offered its engine at cost in the Emirates competition, only to be undercut by Rolls-Royce. For an A380 operator, the real purchase is consequently a package of hardware, spares and years of support rather than four isolated engines.

Simple Flying makes the same broader point about A380 ownership in its comparison of A380 and 747 costs: the purchase price is only the first part of the aircraft's financial story. For the engines, that distinction becomes even more important once they begin accumulating flight hours.

A closer look at the combination of GEnx strengths and Trent 1000 weaknesses that have led many airlines to opt for the GEnx.

The maintenance figures show just how quickly the original purchase price becomes only one part of the equation. According to Aircraft Commerce's owner's and operator's guide to the Trent family, published in 2012, a Trent 900 first shop visit typically cost $5.0 million to $5.3 million, while a second visit cost $6.2 million to $6.5 million. The first removals were expected after 23,000 to 28,000 engine flight hours.

The same guide put the Trent 900's life-limited parts at $4.9 million for disks and shafts plus another $1.9 million for fan blades and related parts, or about $6.8 million altogether. That is roughly the same order of magnitude as the old analyst estimate for an entire GP7200. These components also have defined service lives. The Trent 900's 18 disk and shaft items were certified for anywhere from 1,000 to 12,500 flight cycles against a 15,000-cycle target, meaning early-build engines could face heavier and earlier work.

Aircraft Commerce translated those maintenance costs into reserves of $245 to $291 per engine flight hour through the first shop visit, rising to $320 to $407 through the second. Across all four engines, that means an A380 could carry roughly $980 to $1,160 in engine reserves per flight hour at the first interval alone, in 2012 dollars. Because those figures are now more than a decade old, they should be treated as a historical floor rather than a current forecast. Newer estimates run higher: aircraft broker Jettly puts full Trent 900 overhauls at $10 million to $15 million each.

The maintenance cycle also explains why the support business remains important even though A380 production has ended. As Aviation Week's data tool forecasts, the Trent 900 fleet will see 548 overhaul shop visits over the next decade. Those visits involve much more than simply removing and reinstalling an engine, and the engineering behind the bill helps explain why the original hardware is so expensive.

The scale of the A380's powerplants is the first clue to their complexity. Each engine swings a 116-inch (296-cm) fan and produces enormous thrust. Aircraft Commerce gives the Trent 900 a maximum thrust rating of up to 84,000 lbf (374 kN), while Engine Alliance lists the GP7200 at 70,000 to 81,500 lbf (311 to 363 kN). The GP7200 also has an 8.8 bypass ratio and a pressure ratio of more than 36, while its 116-inch fan uses 24 swept, hollow titanium blades.

The GP7200 is also a genuinely multinational engine rather than a simple GE-Pratt & Whitney combination. Engine Alliance's current component breakdown assigns the fan and accessory gearbox to Pratt & Whitney, while Safran supplies the low-pressure compressor and booster and the high-pressure compressor. GE Aviation is responsible for the combustor, high-pressure turbine, and FADEC, while MTU Aero Engines supplies the low-pressure turbine. The arrangement combines technologies and manufacturing expertise from several major engine companies in a single powerplant.

The Trent 900 follows a different route with its three-shaft architecture. Aircraft Commerce notes that hollow, wide-chord fan blades reduce weight and drag across the Trent family. It describes the Trent 900 as a scaled-down three-shaft design with 24 swept fan blades, a tiled combustor that cools its inner walls with a film of air so it can burn hotter and cleaner, and a contra-rotating turbine that allows smaller guide vanes to perform the same work. Each feature contributes to fuel burn, noise, or performance, but each also requires precise manufacturing and inspection.

Rolls-Royce said in its 2015 announcement of the Emirates order that the Trent 900 had powered the first commercial A380 in 2007 and was already in service with eight operators across more than 70 aircraft, having accumulated over four million flight hours. The same Rolls-Royce announcement, reproduced by AviationPros. This was therefore a mature engine program by the time Emirates selected it for another 50 A380s.

All that engineering had to be scaled for an exceptionally large aircraft. According to Simple Flying's look back at 20 years of the A380, the A380-800 weighs up to 1,234,600 lb (560,000 kg) at takeoff and spans 261 feet, 8 inches (79.75 meters). That scale makes the engines technically impressive, but technical efficiency alone could not solve the A380's larger economic problem.

Its connection to the Airbus A380 reveals how planemaker reshaped its flight decks.

The Trent 900 and GP7200 were designed to make a four-engine aircraft as efficient as possible, and they achieved meaningful gains. Aircraft Commerce's 2012 comparison of Trent-powered aircraft found that an A380 with Trent 970 engines burned 35,319 US gallons (133,700 liters) of block fuel on a Frankfurt-New York flight. That equated to 0.0196 US gallons per seat-mile (0.046 liters per seat-km), the best result among the four-engine aircraft examined, although it remained slightly worse than the twin-engine A330s and 777-200ERs on unrestricted routes. The analysis assumed 486 seats on the A380.

Engine Alliance presents the GP7200's economics in more favorable terms. The manufacturer says the engine can deliver up to $500,000 in annual fuel savings per aircraft and a payload-range advantage worth up to $5.5 million a year. In a 2010 press release, the company also said Airbus credited the engine with a 0.5% fuel-consumption improvement and that dispatch reliability stood at 99.9% after nearly two years in service. These are manufacturer claims rather than independent cost calculations, but they show what Engine Alliance considered the engine's economic selling points when airlines were choosing between the two A380 powerplants.

The numbers illustrate the problem the A380 ultimately faced. The engines could be remarkably efficient for their size, but four engines still meant four sets of maintenance requirements, four nacelles, and four powerplants consuming fuel. Airbus built its last A380 in 2021, shifting the economics of the engines once again. With production over, their value increasingly depends on the aircraft that are already flying.

The end of A380 production has changed what makes a Trent 900 or GP7200 valuable. No new engine will be built for a new A380, so future revenue increasingly comes from overhauls, replacement parts, upgrades, and the engines already installed on aircraft that remain in service. The engine has effectively moved from a new-aircraft product to a long-term support business.

The size of that installed fleet still matters. According to Simple Flying's report on why Airbus offered two engines, Airbus took 251 A380 orders, 130 for GP7200-powered aircraft and 121 for the Trent 900. That left both engine families with a substantial installed base, and both manufacturers therefore have a long-running aftermarket opportunity even though the production line is closed.

Utilization will determine how long that opportunity remains attractive. The same report found Dubai hosted 29,626 A380 flights in 2026, followed by London Heathrow with 6,974 and Singapore Changi with 5,684. Concentrated flying keeps engines accumulating flight hours and reaching shop visits on a predictable schedule, exactly the type of activity that long-term service agreements such as TotalCare are designed to support. At the same time, the concentration of the remaining fleet around a relatively small number of airlines and hubs means that one operator's fleet decision can affect the economics of an entire engine family.

That makes the future value of the A380 engine more complicated than its original price tag. Airbus has not completely closed the door on the aircraft itself. As Aeroflap reported, Airbus commercial chief Christian Scherer said in July 2024 that the door was closed, "but it is not locked." Air Rifles Are Still Underrated for Big Game Hunting. Here’s Why They Shouldn’t Be Overlooked

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 14, 2026 10:00 AM EST

The latest for gun hunters and competitive shooters.

I lived in Europe for several years and was introduced to quality airguns for competitive shooting and hunting while there. In the late 90’s I obtained my first PCP rifles, a .177 Daystate Huntsman followed by a .22 Webley Raider. It did not take long to appreciate the advantages these guns offered for small game hunting. When I returned home I found there was a small but enthusiastic community of airgun hunters testing the limits of the existing technology.

Eventually, a growing number of airgun manufacturers released larger caliber and more powerful rifles to market. It was only a matter of time before we started to think about using air rifles for larger game like deer and hogs. It was about this time that I first heard of Dennis Quackenbush and the big bore airguns he was producing, initially in .308 and .50 calibers, but eventually the .457 became his flagship model. I purchased the .308 in early 2002 for predator hunting, using it to bag fox and coyote. About the same time the idea of using these rifles for larger game really took hold with me.  But why would anyone want to use an airgun, rather than a conventional firearm?

Quite simply, because it’s fun, challenging, and effective. In the following overview, I’ll give a brief history of the big bore airgun, the motivations for hunting with them, the power they are capable of generating, hunting regulations, ammunition used for hunting, and how to keep the rifle filled with air and ready to hunt.

There are several advantages to airguns in general: they are safer to use in more developed and densely populated areas, they are far quieter than even a rimfire, and ammunition is less expensive. But in my opinion these advantages are less significant when discussing large caliber rifles. Why use them then? For many it comes down to the challenge of the hunt, the requirement for the hunter to exercise the discipline to get closer, be more selective in choosing the shot, and be more precise in shot placement. At least for me, hunting is more about how you do it, than just the outcome. Many hunters find this challenge through hunting with handguns, archery, or muzzleloaders.

In this respect hunting with a big bore airgun is closer to the ethos and field craft of bowhunting but based on the core skill set of the rifleman. And this is what many enjoy most about hunting big game with an airgun, it forces a hunter to close the range and be very precise in shot selection, with the discipline to work for the optimal shot and hold back until that is achieved.

The first commercially available rifles that were applicable for larger quarry, were the Quackenbush big bores. With the advent of this new generation of big bores, the power outputs that could be achieved with an airgun jumped from the 30-40 foot-pound of energy (fpe) range obtained with a standard .22 to over 400 fpe. And just like that, the potential for using them on deer or other large game became a reality.

If like me, you grew up hearing that a deer rifle needs to generate over 1,000 fpe to be effective, this 400 number seems very low. Well, It turns out the 1,000 fpe threshold is simply wrong. I have killed many hogs with airguns generating 100 fpe, and hogs are harder to kill than deer, in my opinion. And many times, when hunting alongside others using centerfires, my kills have been cleaner and more decisive. This is because I get in closer and place the shot more precisely. The best air rifles produce a wound similar to an arrow in that there is no hydrostatic shock generated, but rather direct injury to the vital organs. The simple fact is that 300 fpe is plenty of power to kill deer-sized game but you have to be selective with your shooting and be capable of hitting your target. You cannot be a sloppy or lazy in airgun hunting.

Today’s big bore airguns can be very powerful, and there is a wide selection of calibers and power outputs to choose from. An important factor to understand, is that the caliber is only one element contributing to the power an air rifle can generate. A .50 caliber slug in one rifle design may generate 250 fpe, but that same slug in another design can produce more than 800 fpe. The power is defined by the gun, the pressure it charges to, the pressure and volume of air it releases, and the efficiency of air flow and the guns valving system.

When talking about the more powerful rifles such as the Hatsan Piledriver, Umarex Hammer, Air Force Texan, and Western Bushbuck, you should expect to get over 600 fpe in a .457 and over 700 in the .50 caliber versions. One of the newer players in the big bore space is the AEA Zeus, which is a .72 caliber that comes in various barrel lengths and can generate between 1,000 fpe to 1,500 fpe.

Getting the rifle optimized for a hunting application is a balancing act between power, accuracy, and shot count. Some big bore airguns are regulated, for instance the Umarex Hammer has a factory set regulator adjusted to operate at 3,000 psi, and since every shot uses the same air pressure and volume it is very consistent. Some rifles, such as the AirForce Texan allow the user to adjust the power, which is usually achieved by increasing or decreasing the tension on the hammer spring. And others, such as the Western Bushbuck, have a two-step cocking position that allows either a full power or reduced power shot to be achieved. In many ways, a power adjustment on an airgun is an analog to hand loading for a powder burner.

Initially, the problem big bore airgunners faced was finding game that could be legally taken with their guns. Regulations were the barrier, and in the early days there were very few places that allowed airguns to be used for anything other than small game and pest control. Most jurisdictions have separate deer seasons for firearms, muzzleloaders, and archery that are very specific in defining the criteria for each method. And up until a few years ago, none of them specifically allowed airguns for big game. But there were a few workarounds, Texas for instance did not allow any (big or small) game animal to be taken with an airgun. However non-game species such as feral hogs, predators, and exotic species, could be taken in any number, at any time, and by any means.

Texas became ground zero for early big game airgun hunting in the U.S. The state has large populations of feral hogs, and there are also populations of feral Spanish goats, axis deer, and aoudad that could be hunted. I started flying down to the Texas to meet up with my buddy Eric Henderson, and we would roam the state hunting hogs and exotics during the day, then go after predators at night, with our Quackenbush air rifles.

We experimented with our guns, trying different fill pressures and hammer springs, to optimize power, accuracy, consistency, and the highest shot count. Somewhere along the way, big bore airguns gained visibility and more people started shooting them. This led to grassroot movements lobbying state wildlife management authorities to introduce provisions for airgun hunting, and new regulations came on the books.

At the time of this writing, several states including Michigan, Indiana, Alabama, Missouri, Georgia, Virginia, Tennessee, Kentucky, Idaho, North Dakota, and a growing number of others allow deer hunting with big bore airguns (airgun hunting law resource). Texas and Arizona not only allow deer to be taken with airguns, but most other species of big game can be taken with air powered PCP rifles as well. Some states place a caliber and/or power restriction on the guns that are permitted, so you need to check local regulations. Under these new regulations, airguns have taken whitetail and mule deer, elk, bison, bear, mountain lion, javalina, and pronghorn.

Along with the North American hunts, I’ve been traveling to South Africa for almost two decades to hunt plains game with air rifles, after being granted permission to do the first sanctioned airgun hunts there in 2003. Many of my big bores have been used to take kudu, wildebeest, blesbok, impala, springbok, warthog, bushbuck, and several other species over the years.

Many of the manufacturers producing big bore airguns offer ammunition as well. These may be produced inhouse or on an OEM basis, but in general there is a wide selection of ammo available in all big bore airgun calibers (.308-.72). Hatsan offers their own line of big bore “pellets” in .457 and .50 caliber in a variety of weights. Not surprisingly, they work very well in the Hatsan guns. Established muzzleloader companies such as Hunters Supply have an extensive line of big bore airgun slugs and pellets as well, and even the heavy hitters like Nosler have stepped in with a line of airgun ammo.

One of the companies whose product I use a lot is Mr. Hollowpoint, which is a small operation run by Robert Vogel. He was one of the early big bore airgunners and knows airguns inside and out. The slugs he produces are mostly (but not all) hollow points that are specifically designed around the individual guns.

Airguns can be notoriously finicky about the ammo they prefer, and even guns out of the same manufacturing run might have different preferences. When I get a new rifle, I will try a few slug configurations and weights until I find the one that’s most accurate—then I stick with it. Of course, if you want to use a flatter shooting 86-grain .308 slug for coyote and switch to a heavier 126-grain for better penetration on feral hogs, that’s fine. You just need to find pellets in both weights that work for your gun and commit to those.

Here’s another important consideration for all air rifle hunters: How will you keep the rifle filled? There are a couple options, but in my experience a handpump is not a viable one. The volume of air a big bore requires, the high pressures they usually fill to, and the high-volume air usage all make a handpump ineffective. This leaves you with either getting an air tank that you have filled at a paintball or dive shop, or getting your own compressor.

Tanks are a good approach; you will want to opt for a large-volume carbon fiber tank that can be filled to 4,500 psi. These tanks are connected to the rifles on board storage using a high-pressure hose with either a female quick release connector or a proprietary fill probe. The air flow circuit is opened so that the air flows from the high-pressure tank to the low-pressure onboard storage, and when the gun reaches the required pressure, the tank’s valve is closed, the bleed valve opened, the gun disconnected, and you are ready to go.

Tanks are moderately priced, fast and easy to operate, and are probably the most convenient means of filling a rifle. There is a downside though, first you need to find a place with a compressor that will fill your tanks (look for paintball shops or dive shops, which will usually do this for a fee). A bigger problem is that these shops are often unable or unwilling to charge the tank up to 4,500 psi.

Another option, especially for a high-pressure high-volume rifle is to get a portable compressor. Up until a few years ago this was cost prohibitive, unless you shot a lot or had a group of shooters going in together, as a compressor could easily cost more than $3,500. But there are several compressors (both AC and DC models) on the market today that can fill an individual gun to a 4500 psi, and they can cost less than a carbon fiber tank. The downside is they take longer to charge the gun than a tank does, they are loud, and they require some upkeep. Personally, my preference is to have both a CF tank and a portable compressor to use in combination, using the tank to charge as high as its residual pressure will allow, then topping off with the compressor.

Each of the topics we’ve covered could fill multiple chapters on their own, but now you have an honest overview big game hunting with an air rifle.

I quit hunting with anything but air-powered guns almost 15 years ago, but before that I had moved to handgun hunting and bowhunting to increase the challenge. The airgun is an effective, efficient, and ethical method of take for deer and other big game, but you need to use the gun appropriately. If you just want to go out and shoot a deer at long range and call it a season, a big bore airgun is probably not a good fit for you. However, if you want the challenge of closing the range and dropping the slug precisely on target, you need to give hunting with an air rifle a try. Now more than ever, big bore airguns are readily available. There’s a broad selection of models with a range of features and price points, and there are more places to hunt with them than ever before.

Rinnai has launched a new lineup of electric heat pump water heaters that adds wifi controls and more installation options to an appliance that can cut the energy needed for hot showers.

The REHPW series comes in 50-, 65-, and 80-gallon sizes. It builds on Rinnai’s existing REHP lineup, with changes designed to make the units easier to install, monitor, and maintain.

Heat pump water heaters pull heat from the surrounding air and transfer it into the tank. Moving that heat requires less electricity than producing it with conventional electric resistance elements. There’s no fuel combustion at the appliance, although emissions from its electricity supply depend on the grid.

Rinnai lists a Uniform Energy Factor, or UEF, of 3.75 for the 50-gallon model and 4.0 for the two larger versions. Higher numbers mean greater efficiency under standardized testing; actual bills depend on electricity rates, hot water use, and installation conditions.

The lineup delivers up to 91 gallons of hot water in the first hour, depending on tank size. That rating includes both stored hot water and what the unit can heat during that hour – useful context when several people take consecutive showers.

There are five operating modes, 4.5 kW backup heating elements, and a variable-speed fan designed to keep noise down. The heat pump can operate with ambient air as low as 37°F.

Installation is where some of the more practical upgrades show up.

The new Rinnai heat pump water heater units have water connections on both the top and side, giving installers more options for connecting existing plumbing. Both the air intake and exhaust are on top, allowing zero side clearance where Rinnai’s installation requirements permit.

An optional ducting kit adds flexibility for tighter spaces. Carrying handles help with moving the tank into place, and the air filter slides out horizontally from the side for cleaning.

Rinnai has also added a third tank temperature sensor to the 50- and 80-gallon models to help manage hot water delivery.

Wifi adds remote monitoring and control. Rinnai’s updated Central app supports the REHPW series, including scheduling and tools to use time-of-use electricity rates to manage energy use.

The units also carry CTA-2045 AC Level 2 certification for demand response. That gives compatible utility programs a way to communicate with the water heater and adjust its electricity use. Rinnai says the certification can help qualify homeowners for local and state utility demand-response rebates; availability depends on the program.

The series is Energy Star certified, meets NEEA Tier 4 requirements, and complies with California’s Title 24 requirements, including JA13, according to Rinnai. Warranty coverage includes 10 years for the tank and other parts, plus one year for labor.

Rinnai directs buyers to an installer for pricing, so the upfront cost – and how much a local rebate might offset it – will require a quote. However, the REHP series price on Amazon hovers around the $2,000 mark.

Painter Sascha Braunig Pushes Through the Void

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Sascha Braunig’s paintings have a tendency to produce visceral reactions: a pit in the stomach, an eyebrow raise, a need to move closer. This is not unintended.

“I want the viewer to have a bodily experience,” Braunig says.

The paintings are bright, smooth, candy-colored—lighthearted contrasts to the psychologically intense shapes: luminously rendered heads and torsos often bound by wiry tentacles. At times the figures recede into their matching optic backgrounds, as if hiding in plain sight.

Braunig, who is 43 and splits her time between New York and Maine, has been painting with this funky verve for close to two decades. Not long after she got her MFA from Yale’s painting program in 2008, she was having solo exhibitions of her small paintings of heads at the now-defunct Foxy Production in Manhattan. Inclusions at the New Museum Triennial and MoMA PS1 soon followed. (Attention from critics came quickly as well; one called her an “inspired reanimator of Surrealism,” another a “phenom.”)

Braunig is excellent at capturing the inherent tension of living in a body—especially one that is subjected to societal expectations of femininity. She’s playing with the ways women have been contorted and cinched and squeezed for time immemorial, asked to fit into archetypes that seem at turns glamorous or sinister. Often they are both.

“Our aesthetic, our personhood is formed in relation to these cultural forces and imagery,” she says. “You can’t separate yourself out from what you’ve seen throughout your whole life.”

These themes of gender performance and corporeal control permeate “Wrist Painter,” Braunig’s most extensive exhibition to date, which opens November 13 at the Portland Art Museum. The show brings together some 45 works, including paintings, sculptures, works on paper, and sketchbooks. It will be her first solo institutional show in Maine. (A special occasion, as almost all of the pieces in the show were made a block and a half away, in her studio at the Portland arts nonprofit SPACE, over the last 15 years.)

“Sascha really stands out here in this arts ecosystem. She’s doing something really singular,” says Sayantan Mukhopadhyay, a curator at PMA who organized the show. When people think of Maine painters, they usually think of landscapes. But Braunig leans toward the cerebral, with a bit more bite. “The paradoxes are what really excite me about the work,” Mukhopadhyay says.

And the dichotomies are many. “Sascha has a way of presenting an abstraction that can read both soft and strong, feminine and violent,” says Olivia Smith, whose New York City gallery, Magenta Plains, now represents Braunig. “I think that’s what I’ve always loved: that she offers a way for the viewer to re-associate, which ends up telling them more about themselves than about Sascha.”

One of the earliest artworks in “Wrist Painter” is Sequins, from 2010. It’s from Braunig’s head series, which she began in grad school. Her process starts with a drawing, then she brings her sketch to life by making an embellished three-dimensional model. Then she’ll light the model and paint what she observes. She applies her lustrous oil paints with such precision that these strange bodily shapes start to look “real.”

She continued this system as she moved on to her bodies series a few years later, using clay resin, wire, and paper to make her maquettes. This haptic process is why works like Warm Leatherette, a 2015 painting at PMA that looks like a snatched Michelin Man encased in chartreuse padding, seem so tactile. (She has often described her choice of colors as “seasonal” or “dietary”—she finds a palette that works for one show and then cycles to a new one for the next.)

Her maquette process also lends a certain theatricality. She is literally setting up a scene, as if the entire idea of femininity were on a velvet-curtained stage, the whole specter aglow under klieg lights. What is gender if not a performance?

Braunig, who grew up in British Columbia, Canada, liked drawing and painting from a young age. Her father (a dentist) and mother (a nurse) enjoyed creative pursuits outside of work, and the house was full of art books. She loved watching Pee-wee’s Playhouse, which she credits as being just as formative, even more so, than going to museums.

Her parents encouraged her to attend an intensive summer art program at Skidmore College in upstate New York when she was 16. It was there that she first learned about Cooper Union, the New York City school where artists Lee Krasner and Wangechi Mutu got their start. Braunig decided it was the place for her.

New York City was the perfect place for the budding artist. “I was totally in love. It maybe ruined me forever,” she jokes now. She went to Yale right after Cooper, and then she moved to Maine in 2010, in her mid-20s. “I was really fortunate to have a New York gallery as I left the city, because I was able to have shows and have my work represented here even though I wasn’t here.”

Living somewhere far less expensive was also a game changer—she didn’t need to pile on jobs to make ends meet, and could instead focus on building her deeply personal style. “I had been exposed to so many influences coming to New York. And maybe those first five years in Maine were a time when I was able to process them alone.”

Braunig knows her art history, and she gets why people connect her style back to Surrealism. But her artistic comrades are more like French self-portraitist Claude Cahun or even Frida Kahlo, she says, “who’s really making work that is influenced by events of her own life.” Her art might feel dreamy (or perhaps like an acid trip), but “it’s not a dream space. I really am thinking about actual forces in the actual world.”

For many years Braunig used her work to illustrate broader ideas around culture and gender, but her most recent output has taken a turn toward the autobiographical. A few years ago she went through a creative block that left her stranded from her art practice, unable to make anything. As she emerged from this period last year, she started making her most personal paintings yet.

That work went into her radiant show “Cauldron” at Magenta Plains this past spring, its title a reference to the messy, transformative stew of midlife. Its anchor painting was My Death (2025), which, along with several others from the show, will travel to Portland for “Wrist Painter.”

Sascha Braunig, My Death, 2025, oil on linen over panel.

She calls My Death an “explicit self-portrait” about her own reanimation—an emergence through the void of creative block. It’s juicy and haunting and beautiful. She employed those tentacles again, but this time they are like a life force attached to a sleeping Braunig. A mysterious figure made from strips of vermilion orange pulls on them like marionette strings, bringing the artist back to life.

“I’ve come to understand that painting is a survival practice,” she says. “That can be a framing practice where you’re using it to talk about ideas in the world, or it can be a practice where you’re directly processing your own experiences. Maybe those two things aren’t that different.”

For the PMA show, Braunig has made one brand-new work, titled Torsion. At 78 inches tall by 60 inches wide, it is the largest painting of her career. She previously used the title for a museum show in Norway, but realized she’d never given it to an individual painting. The term, in physics, refers to the twisting of an object around an axis: a towel wrung out to dry.

This new Torsion is a painting of a fleshy pink figure with a tiny, twisted torso standing on tiptoes within a gray box. The figure’s muscular back is to the viewer, its knees fanning out in a plié. Dramatic tentacles in orange and yellow trace its exaggerated outline.

The figure’s head is cut off by the box’s ceiling, and its arms stop around the elbows, where they encounter the side walls. Maybe the figure’s been blocked, held back by the confines of this shadowy encasement. Or maybe, somehow, it has broken free.

“Sascha Braunig: Wrist Painter” is on view at the Portland Art Museum in Maine from November 13, 2026, through March 14, 2027.

HBO’s 5-Part Crime Drama Has One of the Best Seasons in TV History

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Wire’s fourth season provided a far deeper look at Baltimore’s problems

The Wire’s fourth season provided a far deeper look at Baltimore’s problems

At the turn of the 21st century, HBO was the place for prestige crime drama. The launch of The Sopranos in 1999 paved the way for the 2002 premiere of The Wire, which focused on drug dealers in Baltimore and the police officers trying to disrupt their operations. The series is still one of the best shows of all time, and provided breakout roles for huge stars like Idris Elba and Michael B. Jordan. But while it was compelling from the beginning, The Wire truly achieved perfection in season 4 when the scope expanded beyond the drug trade to examine the cycle of institutional failure.

Season 1 of The Wire was tightly focused on the drug empire of Avon Barksdale (Wood Harris) and his business-savvy second-in-command Stringer Bell (Elba). Showrunner David Simon quickly established a brilliant mix of humor and pathos. The show could find comedy in something as simple as cops struggling to get new furniture into their office, and then gut audiences with the death of a relatable drug dealer. Season 2 shifted the focus to the city’s port system, a jarring change as many characters faded into the background to make room for the tragic tale of the Sobotka family. Season 3 was the most tightly linked to the show’s first season, largely completing the story of the Barksdale gang.

That made season 4 perfect for a soft reboot, a shift that was defined by star Dominic West all but disappearing after his relentless detective, Jimmy McNulty, was demoted for pissing off his bosses too many times. The detectives he previously worked with in the major crimes unit continue their work, investigating a vicious new gang that’s filling the void Barksdale left behind. Lester Freamon (Clarke Peters) effectively steps into the McNulty role of pursuing the truth, even when his supervisors would love for him to be a little less dogged, bringing a new methodical nature to the investigations instead of McNulty’s brash confidence. But while the cops vs. criminals beats are familiar, they become just one part of a far more ambitious season that fully capitalized on Simon’s knowledge of the city. (Simon worked as a journalist at The Baltimore Sun for 12 years before creating The Wire.)

The season’s largest secondary plot involves the students at a Baltimore middle school where former cop Roland Pryzbylewski (Jim True-Frost) begins teaching. The young actors do a phenomenal job establishing likable characters in terrible circumstances. You want to root for all of them, from the ambitious Randy Wagstaff (Maestro Harrell) to the fiercely protective Michael Lee (Tristan Wilds). By the end of season 4, the system has profoundly failed almost all of these kids, driving them to lives of addiction and violence. The Wire was always good at making most of its characters sympathetic, but seeing children trapped in such a brutal cycle really drives home the show’s themes of how crime thrives when institutions fail.

That theme also plays out in the mayoral campaign of Tommy Carcetti (Aidan Gillen). Carcetti was introduced in season 3 as an ambitious city council member, but he really shines in season 4 as he runs on reforming the Baltimore Police Department — and then quickly breaks his promises. Gillen’s oily performance as a political survivor who would doom his city to satisfy his own ambitions was what landed him the role of Littlefinger in Game of Thrones. Carcetti is even more compelling a character because he feels like a man who could have done good if he’d been willing to make sacrifices rather than feed his own ego.

The Wire season 4 is able to build so many new arcs because of the strong foundation established in the previous three seasons. The show is dense with rich characters who have grown significantly over the years even as they continue to struggle with many of the same problems. The cops and drug dealers alike feel world-weary as they deal with a more violent new generation represented by Marlo Stanfield (Jamie Hector) and his vicious enforcers Snoop (Felicia Pearson) and Chris Partlow (Gbenga Akinnagbe). Combined with the dark fate of most of the kids and Carcetti’s broken promises, the season creates the bleak feeling that everyone is trapped in a cycle of misery and decay.

Season 5 gave most of The Wire’s characters satisfying ends to their arcs and delivered a scathing critique of the downfall of American newspapers, commentary that has only become more profound 20 years later. But overall, it’s a big step down from season 4, spending too much time on a goofy fake serial killer plot and not enough on the nuanced character-driven stories that made season 4 feel so authentic. There are no bad seasons of The Wire, but season 4 perfectly achieved the show’s goals. It’s an alternately funny, tragic, and profound look at the complex ways that people are betrayed by systems that never really give them a chance to succeed.

FAA grounds departing flights nationwide, citing air traffic communication system outage

📅 2026-11-13  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Federal Aviation Administration halted thousands of departing flights nationwide on Friday afternoon, a rare measure, blaming communications issues that affected "multiple" air traffic control centers. More than 10,000 flights were disrupted.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said Friday at the White House that there was no indication that the issues were a threat to the United States.

The FAA said in post on X that there was a "temporary telecommunications outage" and that it halted flights out of an "abundance of caution." The issues were related to communications and radar problems, according to an internal message from Alaska Airlines, which was seen by CNBC.

Airlines said en route flights would continue, but carriers were preparing to cancel flights as the disruption wore on. JetBlue Airways told staff it would cancel the remainder of the day's flights, aside from European routes, as well as others scheduled for Saturday so that crews and planes are in place to resume operations, according to a memo seen by CNBC.

FAA administrator Bryan Bedford had a call with airline CEOs around 6 p.m. indicating that it was unclear when the situation would be resolved, according to people familiar with the matter.

As of 5:56 p.m. ET, some outbound international traffic was in the air, according to Flightradar24, flight-tracking site. That included a Lufthansa flight from Boston to Frankfurt, Germany, that departed at 5:21 p.m. ET and an ITA Airways flight from Boston to Rome that left at 5:24 p.m. ET, according to the site.

As of 6:50 p.m. ET, cargo and passenger flights were also departing the West Coast for Asia and Hawaii.

Flights will be allowed to depart as en route traffic facilities recover, "through coordination with the air traffic control system," the FAA said at about 6 p.m..

Airlines said flights in the air would continue.

When it issued the ground stop at about 4 p.m., the FAA said the communications issues were "impacting multiple Air Route Traffic Control Centers." These facilities guide airplanes at high altitudes along their routes.

The FAA's technicians are investigating, the agency said.

A ground stop halts flights at their departure point. Disruptions tend to snowball if ground stops drag on as flight crews reach their maximum number of duty hours for a day.

There were 11,000 flights scheduled to depart U.S. airports after 4 p.m. ET on Friday, according to flight-data firm Cirium. There were about 600 diverted flights on Friday, 104 of them sent to other airports, Cirium said.

Nationwide ground stops are extremely rare.

In January 2023, the FAA halted flights because of issues with a pilot alert system. Before that, traffic was halted nationwide after the Sept. 11, 2001, terror attacks.

Technical issues have repeatedly snarled air traffic in the U.S. in recent years.

In September, hundreds of flights into Newark, New Jersey, and Philadelphia were canceled, delayed or diverted after a Verizon fiber cable was accidentally cut. Officials said it occurred during construction in New Jersey, severing a backup feed of key data for air traffic controllers.

In 2025, air traffic controllers lost contact with aircraft heading to and from Newark Liberty International Airport, an equipment failure that led to massive flight delays and raised more concerns about aging U.S. aviation infrastructure and staffing shortages.

It wasn't immediately clear what caused Friday's problem.

Inbound international flights will be managed on a case by case basis, the FAA said.

The major U.S. airlines issued statements saying they would work with customers who were affected by the ground stop and encouraged travelers to check for updates.

Iraq's oil output climbed to its highest level in seven years, the IEA said

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Gulf crude exports reached 18.4 million barrels a day in October, still 26% below pre-war levels, the IEA said

Iraq pumped 4.6 million barrels of crude a day in October, an increase of 540,000 barrels a day from the previous month, the International Energy Agency said in its monthly oil market report on Friday.

That was the highest level since September 2019, according to Bloomberg.

Iraq's output ran 170,000 barrels a day above its implied target of 4.43 million, the IEA said. The agency put the country's sustainable capacity at 4.87 million barrels a day.

Gulf producers excluding Oman exported 18.4 million barrels a day in October, which is 26% below pre-war levels, the IEA said. Those exports rose by 950,000 barrels a day from the prior month, according to Bloomberg. Producers in the region are sending some cargoes on low-profile tanker runs across the Strait of Hormuz and are shipping other crude by alternative export routes that never touch the strait.

Global oil supply rose by 2.3 million barrels a day to 105.4 million in October, the IEA said. That followed a 3 million barrel-a-day increase in September. The agency said the gains came from high Gulf exports despite continuing attacks on energy infrastructure and tankers.

Saudi Arabia produced 9.99 million barrels a day in October, up from 8.93 million in September, the IEA said. Kuwait produced 2.03 million barrels a day, up from 1.58 million.

The IEA's estimates differ from figures that Gulf producers submitted to OPEC. Iraq told OPEC it produced 4.888 million barrels a day in October, above the IEA's 4.6 million. Saudi Arabia told OPEC it pumped 10.563 million barrels a day, compared with the IEA's 9.99 million.

The recovery in Gulf output follows disruption from the U.S.-Israeli war on Iran, which began in late February.

Four ideal signings for Mauricio Pochettino’s Man Utd in January

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Mauricio Pochettino is set to take the reins at Manchester United and they’ll be hoping for an immediate boost in their fortunes – but they might have to add to their squad at the next opportunity to give themselves the best chance.

United’s season so far has been a disaster. They’ve gone into the international break sitting in the Premier League’s relegation zone. It’s cost Michael Carrick his job as manager, but quite rightly, the blame is more widespread.

United’s owners, directors and players have all come in for criticism, but if anything’s going to change, it’s probably the latter.

It’d be wrong to expect a mass overhaul in January, a notoriously difficult transfer window to do big business in, but it’d be equally daft to expect United to just sit around and do nothing.

A new manager coming in gives them a chance to bring in new ideas and players to suit those ideas.

So, keeping it semi-realistic, who’d be a good signing for them under Pochettino?

It’s tempting whenever a manager gets a new job to look at who’s at his former clubs that he might like to reunite with.

Ironically in this case, the one familiar player that might most appeal from Tottenham isn’t someone Pochettino worked with at Tottenham – it’s been a while, after all – but at Chelsea instead.

Gallagher was Pochettino’s most-used player during his sole season at Chelsea, playing in 48 of his 51 games in charge.

Sure, United already did a lot of work on their midfield over the summer, but they still seem to be missing runners there. And if there’s one thing Gallagher can do, it’s run. And if there’s one thing Pochettino wants his midfielders to do… you get the picture.

On average, Gallagher has covered 12.2km per 90 minutes when playing in the Premier League this season. It’s the highest average in the squad of anyone with three or more starts.

Pochettino used to rave about Gallagher at Chelsea. Exhibit A, this ringing endorsement in February 2024:

“He is a player with a great commitment to the team. He is always trying to compensate in every situation, in offensive and defensive situations. It’s priceless to have a player like him.

“‘He is in the starting XI nearly every game and one of the captains. For me, he is the type of player that the club need to have.

“He runs, he plays, he fights. He does everything to win. He is a really important player that the coaching staff want to have on the team.”

Gallagher hasn’t exactly been a regular starter for Spurs this season, but he only joined them in January, so he might be tricky to prise away.

But hypothetically, he’d get more of a look in from Pochettino in a midfield where Kobbie Mainoo is a top prospect, but Carlos Baleba has had injury issues, Youri Tielemans hasn’t convinced with his energy and Andrey Santos remains an unknown quantity.

Santos spent the 2023-24 season out on loan away from Chelsea rather than as part of Pochettino’s squad.

Gallagher would add to United’s homegrown quota too, but for a Pochettino team, he wouldn’t just be making up the numbers.

Pochettino got the best out of Luke Shaw when they worked together at Southampton, but Luke Shaw then was different to Luke Shaw now.

Back in those days he was a talented teenage prospect. Nowadays, he’s United’s tired only natural option at left-back. Quite simply, they need to freshen it up.

Pochettino will probably be happy to keep Shaw around as a senior, trustworthy figure. But he’ll be wanting more energy from the full-back positions and that means fresh legs.

Some of Pochettino’s best teams thrived when their full-backs bombed on to offer width while the wingers cut inside. Shaw can’t really do that anymore.

One player who can get up and down the flank is Palace’s Mitchell, who might not be the most inspiring choice but could be the best of what’s available.

Palace’s precarious position, coupled with the fact that Mitchell is out of contract at the end of the season, could make January his ideal time to escape.

His attacking output hasn’t always been cited as a strong point, but he can cover plenty of ground up and down the channel. A Pochettino team don’t necessarily need their full-backs to be the ones getting goals and assists, just to offer support and threatening runs.

Mitchell also has a good record in duels and could offer United more stability in defence, as they desperately need. There might be a signing with a higher ceiling to make, but that’s the kind of thing that might have to wait until the summer.

It’s not just at left-back United need to upgrade. The battle between Diogo Dalot and Noussair Mazraoui on the other side is hardly awe-inspiring, but unless they can shift one or the other, finding an upgrade might be a summer job.

At centre-back, though, they might not be able to afford to wait. Getting Harry Maguire back from injury will be a boost when the time comes, but with Lisandro Martinez suffering a sharp downturn in form, a brand new centre-back would help.

We reckon Nottingham Forest’s Murillo would be a pretty smart signing for United. He’s strong and imposing but also good on the ball, which is perfect for Pochettino.

Is he gettable in January, though? Probably not. He signed a new contract in the summer and we know what Forest are like when it comes to selling players. Or stopping them from being sold.

File this one under the dream signing category. Murillo has Premier League experience, plenty of good years still ahead of him and the attributes that Pochettino values highly in his defenders.

Look, we can safely say Pochettino would love to work with Harry Kane again. We know there have been numerous times when United would have loved to sign Harry Kane. It’s just not happening, though, is it?

Ink dry on his new Bayern Munich contract, Kane is off the menu. But a striker is still high on United’s wish list.

By January, Benjamin Sesko will be just about ready to return from injury, but who knows in what state. In his absence, United don’t have great alternatives. Joshua Zirkzee has had enough chances now and not taken them, while Matheus Cunha and Bryan Mbeumo are probably better out wide.

The summer transfer window is usually the more likely option for big striker moves. United could still do with someone to put them on, though.

The right kind of middle-ground between luxury signing and mere stop-gap could be someone like Borussia Dortmund’s Guirassy.

He’s the same height as Kane at 6’2 and boasts some similar strengths, such as his link-up play and chance conversion.

With eight goals from 14 games this season, including four from four in the Champions League, he’s in solid form. It’s no anomaly, looking at his last few seasons: 22 goals last time out, 38 the season before, 30 in the one before that.

At 30 years old, he’s not necessarily the long-term solution. But Kane is 33 and you’d sign him, wouldn’t you?

Guirassy might be one of the best options out there in terms of value for money.

Canada is threatening to sue Stellantis over Brampton plant sale

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Industry Minister Mélanie Joly said Stellantis has 30 days to address a notice of default tied to a federal funding agreement

Canada warned it may take Stellantis $STLA to court over the automaker's plan to sell its shuttered factory in Brampton, Ontario, giving it 30 days to resolve Ottawa's objections. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly told a Toronto news conference on Thursday that Ottawa had formally notified Stellantis it is in default.

"We sent a notice of default and, basically, they have 30 days — or else, of course, we will sue them," Joly told reporters, according to CNBC.

Joly said Stellantis broke the contractual promises it made to Ottawa and to Brampton's workforce when it revealed it had struck a deal to sell the facility to Roshel, which makes armored vehicles. She said the company is "on the hook" for several hundred million dollars.

Stellantis, the parent of Jeep, Dodge, Fiat, Chrysler and Peugeot, announced on Monday a deal to sell the plant to Roshel, though it did not disclose the price. The company said it recognizes what it owes the facility's employees and is ready to negotiate a settlement for union-represented workers in talks with Unifor, Canada's largest private sector union.

Stellantis Canada CEO Trevor Longley said on Thursday that the automaker will give back C$24 million in subsidies it received from Ottawa, according to CNBC. That figure falls short of what the government estimates Stellantis owes.

"We recognize the disappointment and uncertainty surrounding the future of the facility. While our decision to sell the plant represents a change from the original plans for Brampton, the agreement we have advanced with a Canadian manufacturer provides a path to return the facility to productive industrial use," Longley said, according to CNBC.

A Stellantis spokesperson was not available for comment on Friday morning, according to CNBC.

Prime Minister Mark Carney has called the situation at the plant “unacceptable” and a “betrayal” of its workers, according to CNBC.

Stellantis had dropped its plan to build the Jeep Compass at Brampton, moving future output to an Illinois factory as U.S. auto tariffs and other pressures in the market weighed on the company, according to CNBC. The U.S. and Canada are in a trade dispute, and President Donald Trump has urged carmakers to shift their vehicle manufacturing to the United States.
