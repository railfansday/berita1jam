A Guide to NYC’s Little Italy—and the Culinary Gems Hidden Among the Tourist Traps

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Contrary to other tourist-focused neighborhoods in New York (looking at you, Times Square), Little Italy boasts an innate charm that resonates with visitors and locals alike. Sandwiched between SoHo and the Lower East Side, this jewel box area is home to long-standing, family-owned restaurants and authentic Italian bakeries, the majority of which have been neighborhood staples for decades. Yet despite its touristy atmosphere, a unique feeling of old school New York magic hangs in the air; beyond the personality-less stands and souvenir shops, framed family photos on restaurant walls and structural imperfections of tenement buildings speak to a bygone area of the city’s past, evoking a sense of nostalgia that so many neighborhoods lack. Pop down to Mulberry Street and see for yourself—though be sure you know which spots are actually legit (that’s where we come in).

A note on how we’re defining the area: Over the years, Little Italy’s boundaries have shrunk to focus on the touristic area of Mulberry Street and its immediate surroundings. While the southern and western boundaries of Canal Street and Lafayette Streets remain unchanged, the historic easterly Bowery boundary has arguably shifted to Elizabeth Street; similarly, the northerly boundary of Kenmare has also condensed down, according to many, to Broome Street. For the sake of this piece, we’re using the boundaries of Lafayette Street, Canal Street, Elizabeth Street, and Broome Street as a happy medium.

Located in the heart of Little Italy, Umberto’s Clam House is best known for its—you guessed it, all things clams. The restaurant quickly garnered attention shortly after opening in 1972, when mobster Joe "Crazy Joe" Gallo was shot in the restaurant’s dining room. Like its mafia-related history, the food at Umberto’s is as Italian-American as it gets. Baked clams and fried calamari are the appetizer list’s biggest show-stoppers, while superbly chilled clams on the half-shell pair exquisitely with equally cold (and generously sized) cocktails from the bar. Seafood-focused mains are the name of the game here, including the garlicky linguini and clam sauce and recently resurfaced scungilli with pasta served with piping hot red sauce.

Address: 132 Mulberry Street, New York, NY 10013

For Italian-American eats in a less stereotypically Little Italy ambiance, Il Cortile is the way to go. Contrary to the old school Italian vibes found in the majority of the neighborhood’s restaurants, Il Cortile’s atrium-style glass ceiling and plant-filled room feel more like dining al fresco in Trastevere than the heart of Mulberry Street. The family-owned restaurant’s neighborhood roots date back to 1975, with a solid focus on Northern Italian cuisine. The homemade cheese ravioli and wood-fired pizzas are always a safe bet, though for meat lovers, springing for the Fettuccine di Parma made with sweet sausage and wild mushrooms won’t disappoint—and be sure to save room for the pillowy soft tiramisu made with creamy mascarpone and imported Italian ladyfingers drenched in espresso.

Address: 125 Mulberry St, New York, NY 10013

Original Vincent’s, often called The Original Vincent’s Clam Bar, is home to one of the richest restaurant success stories in Little Italy. Founded in 1904 by Giuseppe and Carmela Siano, the business began as a sidewalk street cart serving up fresh seafood and jars of homemade red sauce, available in mild, medium, and hot. Today, the restaurant sits on the corner of Mott and Hester Streets, and is still known for its signature gravies, baked clams, and linguini-based pasta dishes (don’t skip the frutti di mare). As in its earliest days, Original Vincent’s still sells jars of their red sauce to-go, making replicating the restaurant’s pasta dishes at home a breeze—simply grab a bottle of Chianti, cue up your Louis Primo playlist and voila.

Paesano of Mulberry Street is home to one of the coziest interiors in Little Italy, offering a peaceful refuge from the neighborhood bustle. Rustic twig bunches and leafy plants strung with twinkle lights hang from the ceiling, while traditional Italian frescos and framed family photographs adorn the whitewashed brick walls. Originally opened in 1961, the restaurant remains owned by the same family and continues to be known for their Southern Italian recipes, including linguini with clam sauce, wood-fired pizzas, and savory chicken marsala drenched in sautéed mushrooms. Meat lovers, don’t skip the restaurant’s signature Veal A La Paesano, featuring a juicy and tender veal base layered with thin slices of prosciutto, steamed broccoli, and gooey mozzarella cheese finished with succulent brown gravy.

Address: 136 Mulberry St, New York, NY 10013

For serious pizza lovers, a trip to Lombardi’s is a no-brainer. Tucked on the corner of Mott and Spring, this neighborhood institution is credited as America’s first pizzeria, originally founded by Gennaro Lombardi in 1905. Lombardi quickly rose to fame for his large, heavily-topped pies, which he baked in an on-site, coal-and-gas oven. The result? Smoky crusts topped with perfectly balanced tomato sauce and gobs of whole milk mozzarella—along with sweet Italian sausage and various other accoutrements. The interior oozes with classic New York charm, featuring tables clad in red-and-white checkered cloths and framed family photographs strung along the red-hued walls. Head’s up, the spot is cash only, and while there’s an ATM on site, we recommend coming prepared to avoid the withdrawal fee.

Address: 30 Spring St, New York, NY 10012

Pasquale Jones-12_2. Brussels Sprout Pizza with Red Onion, Smoked Mozzarella & Lemon

In a sea of mediocre neighborhood wine lists, Pasqule Jones offers a breath of fresh air for Little Italy exploring wine lovers. Situated on the cusp of the neighborhood’s boundaries (and easily identifiable by its rainbow neon lights), this Italian-American pizzeria first opened its doors in 2016 and quickly became a staple for industry folks and visiting oenophiles alike. Beloved for its wood-fired New York style pizzas (don’t miss the signature clam pie) and thick, homemade pastas, the restaurant’s warm-yet-casual ambiance is ideal for posting up with a pie and a bottle—especially if you’re after great Burgundy, Champagne, or Italian reds. Expect classic picks from Pierre-Yves Colin-Morey, Domaine de l’Arlot, and Vietti, as well ample fun and fresh selections from Sandlands, Chacra, and Domaine Gramenon.

Address: 187 Mulberry St, New York, NY 10012

Address: 195 Grand St, New York, NY 10013

Although less famous than nearby Ferrara, Caffé Palermo features the innate Italian charm one looks for when wandering the neighborhood. Owned and operated by Baby John DeLutro (the self-proclaimed “Cannoli King”) since 1973, the establishment lives up to its owner’s moniker, offering flavored iterations—including pistachio and hazelnut made with sweet ricotta cream, the former of which is one of the joint’s standouts—in perfectly crisp shells. What Palermo does have over Ferrara is a solid amount of outdoor seating, ideal for posting up with a cappuccino and people watching on Mulberry.

Address: 148 Mulberry St, New York, NY 10013

If you’re looking for a wine break between pizza and pastries, this is just the ticket. Located on Little Italy's western limits (Centre Street between Broome and Grand), this French-inspired wine bar offers extensive by-the-glass options and shareable bites, including cheese and charcuterie boards to larger main courses, including beef tartare and lemon spaghetti. The comprehensive wine list, which features anywhere from 750 to 1,000+ references at all times, leans unsurprisingly French, though ample selections from Italy, Spain, and the USA are also available. Our suggestion? Post up with a bottle of Grower Champagne and relish in the perfectly executed Parisian bar à vin ambiance.

Address: 249 Centre St, New York, NY 10013

Address: 409 Broome St, New York, NY 10013

Technically located on the cusp of Little Italy’s historic boundaries (Kenmare and Broome Streets), La Esquina offers a flavor-packed reprieve from the classic red-sauce joints synonymous with the neighborhood. Situated in a former counter deli, this multi-level Mexican hotspot is best known for its casual taqueria (located in ‘The Corner Deli’ space) serving tacos, tortas, and ceviche. However, for those looking for something a bit stronger, head through the secret door and down the stairs to find the restaurant’s underground speakeasy. Lit by candle and adorned with Lucha Libre posters, the bar’s high-energy ambiance is prime for enjoying queso fundido and smoky Mexican street corn (elotes callejeros) washed down with expertly crafted agave-based cocktails.

The Trump administration has proposed a slew of changes to Medicare Advantage and Part D designed to "revitalize care" in these markets.

For one, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services said late Friday that it will update application and network adequacy requirements to make it easier for new entrants. The agency proposed to require organizations to demonstrate network adequacy during their first year in operation, rather than during the application process.

This shift would eliminate a significant barrier for new entrants, driving greater competition in the market.

CMS has also proposed combining three specialty areas in behavioral health — clinical psychology, clinical social work and outpatient behavioral health — into one specialty group for the purposes of network adequacy. The changes will give plans greater flexibility in building out behavioral health networks.

CMS will also make adjustments that seek to boost competition for special needs plans.

In addition, the agency said it will make adjustments to the star ratings program, focusing its ratings on 15 measures. The proposed measure set would emphasize "clinical outcomes and patient experience while retaining select process measures in high-priority areas where performance remains low."

CMS is also aiming to update the calculation methodology that will "raise the bar for achieving high ratings," and thus quality bonus payments.

"People with Medicare should be able to choose coverage based on what matters most to them — quality, affordability, and access," said CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., in the announcement. "These proposals are about creating a Medicare Advantage marketplace that rewards better care and better value while empowering beneficiaries with the options and knowledge they need to make informed decisions about their health."

The proposed rule also includes provisions that align with Department of Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy's Make America Healthy Again agenda by setting a "healthy food" standard that MA plans should follow for supplemental benefits.

The goal, CMS said, is to ensure that Medicare dollars are spent on food benefits that align with MAHA.

"Medicare food benefits should pay for nutritious food that helps people improve their health and manage chronic disease," Kennedy said in the announcement. "This proposal would require those benefits to meet the Dietary Guidelines for Americans while preserving access to foods and nutritional products that providers recommend for patients’ specific medical needs."

"Under President Trump, we are putting prevention at the center of health care and demanding more from every Medicare dollar," Kennedy said.

15 Best American Movies Of All Time Ranked, Including Once Upon A Time...In Hollywood

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

There have been approximately 500,000 feature-length films released to date, spanning several genres including action, drama, comedy, sci-fi, and more. This total includes titles released in Hollywood, along with other major cinema markets like India (Bollywood) and China (with its ever thriving box office). Of this total, around 50,000 of these are major American movies (which includes studio films, independent projects, and more).

The United States is often considered to be the hub for cinema, with the longstanding presence of this business in cities like New York, Chicago, and Los Angeles (especially the latter). Some of the biggest movies ever made have been in the U.S., with franchises like Star Wars and the Marvel Cinematic Universe both being funded and made by Americans.

Not only does the United States have some of the biggest movies, but the country is also home to some of the very best movies made as well. Titles like Citizen Kane, The Godfather, and the original Star Wars trilogy are all products of American auteurs from U.S.-based studios. Not only that, but these successful projects span nearly a century, with films like the aforementioned Citizen Kane debuting roughly 85 years ago (and with plenty of amazing movies still being made in modern times, too).

When looking at the best American movies ever made, other titles come to mind as well. Films from directors like Steven Spielberg, Quentin Tarantino, and the Coen brothers, along with classics like The Wizard of Oz and It’s a Wonderful Life, all deserve to be in contention for this prestigious list.

Once again, one of the very best films ever made is Citizen Kane, which was released in 1941. Although it may have made its debut before the United States even officially entered World War II, and despite its lack of color, this Orson Welles masterpiece is the kind of movie that is way ahead of its time in virtually every aspect.

Citizen Kane, which was written, directed by, and starring Orson Welles, tells the story of the life of Charles Foster Kane. Kane’s life, from childhood to old age, is portrayed in the film, and includes his rise to owner of the New York Inquirer and an ambitious run for Governor. All of this is told within the framework of a mystery presented in the film’s very first scene, which shows Kane’s death, and his very famous final words: “Rosebud.” Citizen Kane then explores that mystery and teases the cryptic meaning behind them.

There are many reasons why Citizen Kane is such a classic. Welles’ acting is exceptional, as is his innovative and industry-changing direction. There’s also the usage of non-linear storytelling, which had seldom, if ever, been done in mainstream movies up until this point. Citizen Kane popularized this tool, thus paving the way for countless classic non-linear movies (Christopher Nolan’s movies come to mind here, as the director is known for his affection for this kind of narrative).

Stanley Kubrick was an English director, but one of his most famous films, 2001: A Space Odyssey, was made by an American studio and stars American actors. As such, 2001 more than belongs on this list, and deserves its place among the best American movies ever made. This movie is truly extraordinary, with visuals that are still impressive decades later. Additionally, Kubrick’s hard-to-wrap your head around story deserves great praise, too, as it paved the way for other filmmakers, like David Lynch, to explore the abstract in their own projects.

Some movies on this list were overlooked upon their initial release, only to receive acclaim in the years that followed. Others, meanwhile, were praised right away, and 1991s The Silence of the Lambs belongs firmly in that category. The film, which stars Jodie Foster and Anthony Hopkins, won the “Big Five” awards at the Oscars — Best Picture, Best Actor, Best Actress, and Best Adapted Screenplay.

And, just like many other films on this list, the story of The Silence of the Lambs is likely very familiar. The film follows FBI trainee Clarice Starling as she’s tasked with meeting the infamous serial killer Hannibal Lecter, with the hope that Hopkins’ character will offer some insight that may lead to the capture of another killer, Buffalo Bill, who’s still on the loose.

This movie remains one of the best American films ever made, even 35 years after its release, in large part because of the performances by Foster and Hopkins. Both give some of the best turns in recent Hollywood history, and performances that have greatly influenced the future of the industry, too. For example, Hopkins’ portrayal of a serial killer has become the basis for actors playing murderers ever since.

Halloween is one of the simplest films on this list, with the story of a masked killer returning to his hometown and wreaking havoc among the residents, who fight to stay alive amidst his murder spree. Yet, that simplicity doesn’t go against John Carpenter’s film, which is not just one of the best horror movies ever, but one of the best films ever, too.

The straightforwardness of Halloween’s narrative is actually one of the film’s greatest strengths, as it allows the characters, like Michael Myers, Dr. Loomis, and Laurie Strode, the chance to truly shine. In turn, the suspense and terror of the film’s story is allowed to grow, too. Ultimately, Halloween (1978) is a masterpiece in horror storytelling, with expertly executed scares and a main villain that remains one of the best movie characters ever.

Steven Spielberg has made so many classic films in his career, but one that stands near (or at the very top) of the list is E.T. The Extra-Terrestrial. The 1982 film focuses on a young boy who discovers a gentle alien stranded on Earth, and the two then bond as they evade government officials and attempt to send E.T. home.

This movie truly has it all — it’s funny, relatable (with an excellent portrayal of kids at this age), and, above all else, extremely emotional. The film’s ending, where E.T. goes home on his spaceship, is simultaneously heartwarming and heartbreaking at the same time, even all these years later.

Actor James “Jimmy” Stewart served in the United States military during World War II and, just one year after the end of the conflict, starred in what proved to be the biggest film of his career (and one often watched during this time of year) — It’s a Wonderful Life. The film, which is loosely based on Charles Dickens’ A Christmas Carol, is essentially the perfect holiday movie.

The story follows George Bailey, a down-on-his-luck man who plans to take his own life on Christmas Eve. However, his plans are thwarted when he’s visited by a Guardian Angel, who shows him why life is still worth living. It’s a Wonderful Life is one of the most emotionally touching movies ever made, making it one of the best movies ever made, period, in turn.

Workplace bullying damages health and drains productivity. For autistic employees, it perpetuates the cycle of abuse that starts much earlier. A 2026 review confirmed that autistic students experience disproportionately high levels of bullying, with most estimates falling between 40% and 70% (Heredia-Oliva et al., 2026), and it is a major source of stress for them.

The painful pattern then follows people into work. And many work environments prove fertile grounds for both overt (threatening, shouting) and especially covert (backstabbing, sabotaging,

In experiments, neurotypical people reported liking autistic people less than neurotypical people, with vague explanations like perceived “awkwardness” (Alkhaldi et al, 2021). Workplace stressors have the potential to magnify the dislike. Autistic employees, with their spiky profiles of abilities, are more likely to be on extreme ends of performance. High performers often experience veiled violence like sabotage or having crucial information withheld due to coworker envy; low performers face overt hostility, which makes their performance decline rather than improve (Jensen et al., 2014). Add autistic tendencies of direct communication, including possibly challenging inefficiencies and misconduct (Hartman & Hartman, 2024), along with a focus on work over socializing, and the target is set.

Autistic people's nervous systems often have less capacity to recover from social stress (Cheng et al., 2020), and autistic adults are already at elevated risk of developing mental and physical health issues due to a mismatch between their needs and the environments, sensory stress, effortful masking, and other forms of chronic stress (Botha & Frost, 2020; Dhanasekara et al., 2023). Past bullying also leaves a psychological trace: 87.4% of autistic adults showed signs of fear of being laughed at, compared with 22.6% of non-autistic adults (Leader et al., 2018).

Multiple vulnerabilities create compounding effects. Years of mistreatment result in hypervigilance and keep the body's threat response switched on. Each new injury lands on a system that never fully recovered from the last. To make things worse, the lack of resources often associated with unemployment, with only about 3 in 10 working-age autistic people in the UK employed (Buckland, 2024), leaves fewer options for recovery, seeking help, or leaving a toxic job.

Reporting bullying at work is generally a thankless task. Reports are often ignored or dismissed: in a nationally representative U.S. survey, 60% of employers reacted negatively when bullying was reported (Namie, 2021), and targets describe HR as regularly siding with bullies (Boddy & Boulter, 2025). Moreover, skillful manipulators, which many bullies are, can present themselves as victims.

Autistic people, however, face additional barriers to successfully reporting bullying and being heard.

Most organizations say they are committed to healthy workplaces, but their methods for handling bullying often make things worse. Those methods fail autistic employees and others dealing with trauma or overwhelm in at least three ways.

They are reactive. Action typically begins only after bullying is proven. But the first line of defense against bullying and any other hazard at work is prevention (Praslova et al., 2022). Preventing harm is both more ethical and more practical than repairing it.

They put the burden on the target. Targets are told to document what was said and done, when, and who was present, then discuss it calmly with a supervisor. How many people can realistically do this while doing their jobs, under stress, and dealing with trauma? The process appears to be designed to make it easy to reject the complaint because of the “tone” or the target “being emotional.”

They try to fix personalities. Bullies may act out their entitlement, fragile egos, and poor self-control, but bullying flourishes where the environment lets it go undetected and rewards it with resources or advancement (Praslova et al., 2022; Salin, 2003). Change the environment, and you are more likely to change the behavior.

Remove the rewards for bullying. Avoid internal competition for scarce resources. Distribute resources fairly and transparently, and balance individual rewards with team rewards. Realistic workloads reduce bullying used to offload work onto others, and transparency may take some of the fuel out of envy. Transparent decision-making makes rumors and backstabbing less likely to pay off (Praslova et al., 2022).

Set standards and train for them. A clear policy should state that bullying is not tolerated, and name its covert forms, not just its overt ones (Praslova et al., 2022). Train everyone in respectful communication, and train new managers specifically for complex interpersonal situations. Ideally, facilitate professional support from psychologists trained in workplace well-being.

Build civility and voice together. Some people mistake authenticity for a license to be cruel. Others try to buy “calm” by silencing people. Neither works. Bullying of autistic people is a predictable outcome of systems that fail to check assumptions, reward sameness and conformity, and make targets prove their own reality. Workplaces that are healthy by design, with transparency, civility, and voice, on the other hand, not only support autistic people, but make work better for everyone (Praslova, 2024).

When It's Always All About "Me"

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

We’re living in the age of “me.” We see most things through the lens of “self”: How’s this experience going for me, and how am I doing? What does this mean for and about me? How’s this person perceiving me? Does my “me” like this or not? Our culture teaches us to be the protagonist of our life, make something of ourselves, be “somebody”—to actualize and optimize ourselves. We should be exceptional, the “star” of our own story, hero of our own journey.

From the moment we can gaze into a mirror, those around us start saying, “That’s you!” “Look at you!” “There you are!” We start being recognized (and celebrated) as a “me,” a “someone” who’s her own unique identity, the one doing this life. A few years later, we learn to advocate for and protect that “me,” snatching toys from other little people, saying “That’s mine,” “Give me my…” We build a sense of possession, of being a “me” who has things or doesn’t.

Soon after (and for the rest of our life), we take on the project of that “me” and start working on behalf of our “me.” We become our own protector, PR rep, agent, and biographer, the narrator of our self-story—the story of “me.”

So too, the message we learn is that this “me” must be moving forward in life, continually progressing towards some better future version of ourselves, a new-and-improved, happier-and-better “me.” We can never rest because we must continue stacking up evidence of our advancement. As we are and where we are, we’re not enough—our “me” still hasn’t done enough. There’s some place, or so we’re taught, at the end of all our progress, where we will have progressed far enough and become enough to make the project of “me” complete, and set it down. Then we’ll be able to experience life directly without our “me” in the way.

We don’t just nurse at the bottle of progress; we also feed on the narrative of materialism, which offers us a system of values and ideas about what “counts” as progress. As the narrative goes, money, success, power, and lots of shiny things will earn us the elusive enough-ness we seek, and the right to stop focusing on our progress and the project of “me.” The problem is that this promised place of enough-ness, where we can finally stop protecting ourselves and progressing on our hero’s journey, never comes. No matter how much we achieve and prove about our worth, enough-ness eludes us, so we must continue pursuing the advancement of our individual “me.”

The self-help market is built around this same premise: that the individual is a project to be continually improved. The message is that our “me” needs to be fixed, or at the very least, needs to be better than she is. We’re supposed to be living more authentically, manifesting the life we deserve, putting ourselves first, distinguishing ourselves, and always becoming our best self (which is definitely not this self).

Self-help teaches us to relentlessly focus on our own experience and how we’re doing in every moment—how our life is going, and, most importantly, how the project of “me” is going. We learn to gauge and evaluate the present moment by whether it gives us what we want and need, whether it’s “good” for us. While paying attention to our own experience and how the present moment is affecting us is profoundly important and necessary for a healthy person, as is the ability to honor one’s own wants and needs, the problem is that this self we’ve learned to place at the center of our attention and indeed existence becomes the operating system through which we experience all of life. We don’t experience life directly, what’s actually happening; we experience what our life says about us, which we also invent and create in our mind.

Does this situation, interaction, or opinion make me feel good about me? Do I feel safe, secure, liked, important as a result of it? Does it support my self-image, the story I tell myself about myself? Am I getting what I need to shore up the version of myself I need to function? We encounter, experience, and react to life through a self-referencing lens, as a reflection of and commentary on who we are.

At the same time, our self-focus is motivated by our most basic instinct: survival. On an evolutionary level, we’re always trying to protect our existence as a species—to stay alive, continue being. Long ago, when we lived in the forest, we had to focus on threats: other animals that might eat us or our offspring, branches that could land on our heads, a dark sky that meant our nest would be flooded. Seeing reality through the lens of how it would impact our survival—through the lens of “me” and mine has deep biological roots.

And indeed, statistically, it made more sense to pay attention to what could kill us than what could delight us, like finding a great berry to eat. This now translates into “negativity bias,” our tendency to focus on what’s negative over what’s positive. But threats in modern times are generally not branches or other animals, but rather threats to our ego, self-esteem, and the grand story of who we are. In everyday life, it’s the psychological front on which we now feel continually endangered. It’s our “me” that has to constantly be on the lookout for danger.

All this to say, we have many good reasons to put ourselves at the center of the universe, and to see life through a personal lens of what it means for and about “me.”

The irony, however, is that our constant self-focus and the effort we put into shoring ourselves up, protecting and proving our self, promoting the “story” of our self, is actually an attempt to make ourselves feel happy and secure. And yet, it ends up making us feel unhappy and insecure. In the system we’ve designed, the relentless defense and hyper-vigilance to the self’s survival can never end. But in fact, it can… we just need to step out of the system.

In part 2, I’ll illuminate the subtle and not-so-subtle suffering that our “me” lens creates. I will also propose a radical solution. Spoiler alert: it will go against everything you’ve ever learned.

President Trump may have initially accepted the results of his party's midterm losses, but data released Friday shows there is still broad distrust in the elections process among Republicans.

Less than half of Republican voters nationwide considered the midterm elections "free and fair," according to new polling by the University of California San Diego. Just 52% of Republicans said they trusted votes were counted accurately.

In comparison, after the 2024 election that Trump won, more than 80% of Republicans said they had confidence in the vote-counting process.

While trust in elections often dips for voters of the losing party, the GOP has seen bigger swings in confidence in recent years, as Trump has falsely claimed that U.S. elections are corrupt.

"There are still a lot of people who remain unconvinced that they can trust elections," said Thad Kousser, a co-director of UC San Diego's Center for Transparent and Trusted Elections. "And they're predominantly located in one [political] party."

Overall, a wide majority — 66% — of Americans said they think the midterms were fair. That represents an improvement from before the election, when less than half of U.S. adults said they expected free and fair contests.

But that post-election increase in trust was buoyed by Democrats and independents — many of whom worried before Election Day about potential federal interference at the polls that didn't come to pass.

"It would be a mistake to [look at the 66%] and think, 'Oh, everyone has a pretty good view of the election,'" said Lauren Prather, also a co-director of UC San Diego's election center. "It really buries the lede a little bit about the partisan gap."

The widespread doubt among Republicans may be a reality check for those wondering whether Trump's brand of election denial has run its course in American politics.

On the one hand, there will be fewer politicians who deny or question election results in office next year. And the vast majority of election-denying candidates running for positions involved in overseeing statewide elections lost their races this year, according to tracking by the advocacy group States United Action.

Election experts have also celebrated as Trump and high-profile losing candidates on the ballot this year conceded their losses.

"Sure, I'll accept [the results]," Trump told Fox News' Bret Baier on election night, as it became clear Democrats would retake the House and Senate. "Why wouldn't I?"

But just a few days later, he began conspiracy theorizing about those same results.

"Does anybody really believe that this 'Election' was honest," Trump posted online.

The new polling from UC San Diego shows that among Republicans, there still may be a receptive audience to such claims. When asked if politicians are discussing voter fraud too much, too little or about the right amount, just 15% of Republicans answered "too much."

"There is both demand among the Republican base for continued focus on this issue, and as we saw even after the election, politicians are continuing to supply it," Kousser said. "As long as those two dynamics are there, there's still going to be a market for these concerns."

To be clear, in most elections — domestically and internationally — supporters of the losing party usually see a dip in trust.

"It's sort of like asking people after an overtime basketball game, do you trust the referees?" Kousser said.

But in an ideal world, he said that oscillation would be more like 10 percentage points in either direction. Not the 30-point swing seen among Republicans between 2024 and 2026.

The numbers do still represent an improvement from 2020, however. At that time, leading up to the Jan. 6 riot at the U.S. Capitol in 2021, just 22% of Republicans said they had confidence in the nationwide vote count, according to similar polling done by MIT's Election Data and Science Lab.

Türkiye is one of the world’s most visited countries, straddling the vibrant crossroads of Europe, Asia, and the Middle East. With sun-soaked Mediterranean beaches, Ottoman-influenced gastronomy, historic mosques, and bustling bazaars, it’s no surprise that the country attracted nearly 64 million visitors in 2025. But if your love of the country has you considering a prolonged vacation (or perhaps a second home), the government’s new golden visa might be a good fit—so long as you have $150,000 to spend.

On November 17, Türkiye's president Recep Tayyip Erdoğan signed a decision to enact a new foreign investor visa. The program offers two pathways: a $150,000 investment for a three-year residence permit, or $200,000 for a five-year stay (both of which can extend to the applicant’s spouse and dependents).

As for where your money goes, you can deposit your funds in a local bank, buy government debt instruments, contribute to a pension plan, or invest in real estate or venture capital funds. No matter which pathway you select, you must hold your investment for at least half the visa term (as in, 1.5 years for the three-year visa or 2.5 years for the five-year visa). That means you could potentially withdraw your investment as early as after 18 months, according to IMI Daily, an investment migration trade publication.

The president’s announcement doesn't detail when or how foreigners can begin to apply, or the specific documents required. However, applications for the country’s other residence-by-investment permits require documents such as proof of financial means and health insurance, according to the government’s Investment and Finance Office.

If you don’t want to shell out $150,000, Türkiye offers other (renewable) relocation pathways. You may qualify for a two-year Short-Term Residence Permit for reasons such as tourism and university-sponsored research. Or, if your spouse is Turkish, you could apply for the three-year Family Residence Permit. Once you’ve lived in the country continuously for eight years, you’ll be eligible to apply for the indefinite Long-Term Residence Permit, which grants you almost all the same perks as citizenship, minus civic duties such as the ability to vote and mandatory military service.

It's important to note that the new golden visa does not provide permanent citizenship. If you’re hoping to find your way into a passport, get ready to spend more. Türkiye’s citizenship-by-investment pipeline will require you to buy a home worth at least $400,000, invest at least $500,000 in sectors such as government bonds or venture capital funds, or, alternatively, help create at least 50 jobs.

Over the last few years, the influx of new residency-by-investment programs has made it easier for wealthy travelers to relocate internationally. In New Zealand, you can invest $1 million New Zealand Dollars (about $567,300) in exchange for a three-year work-to-residency track. For Mediterranean living, Greece offers a five-year permit for a 250,000 euro (about $291,120) investment in a startup. Or, for a more affordable option, Paraguay has a permanent residency program for those who can invest $150,000 in “tourism projects” or $200,000 in stocks or real estate—putting it in line with Türkiye as one of the more affordable relocation options.

Prediction markets are facing new limits on Capitol Hill. The Senate passed a bill Thursday evening that would bar platforms from offering contracts tied to terrorism, assassination, an individual’s death, war or U.S. military operations–the first legislation targeting the burgeoning industry to clear either chamber of Congress.

Sens. Adam Schiff (D-Calif.) and John Curtis (R-Utah) introduced the bill, formally called the DEATH BETS Act, to prevent exchanges and clearinghouses overseen by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission from offering or processing those contracts. It would also ban contracts whose outcomes are reasonably expected to depend mainly on someone’s death, even if the wording does not mention death.

The bill passed the Senate by a voice vote and must still pass the House before heading to the president’s desk.

“They are offered with no regard for national security and in far too many cases, even basic morality,” Schiff said on the Senate floor. “These markets are operating in a wild West, desperately in need of regulation and guardrails.”

As platforms like Kalshi and Polymarket have grown in prominence over the past two years, they have offered contracts on seemingly anything, drawing scrutiny over bets tied to war, violence or human suffering. They have also raised concerns about insider trading, as users can trade without publicly revealing their identities, making it harder for outsiders to spot potential conflicts of interest.

Several cases have drawn politicians’ attention to these issues. One involved a Polymarket trader who made more than $400,000 betting on Nicolás Maduro’s removal from power before U.S. forces captured the Venezuelan leader in January. Kalshi also drew scrutiny over a market on whether Iranian Supreme Leader, Ali Khamenei, would leave office, which sparked a dispute after his killing when Kalshi declined to pay traders who had bet he would leave office, citing a rule that excluded death as a qualifying outcome.

In his speech, Schiff pointed to a Polymarket bet on whether NASA’s Artemis II mission would explode, raising concerns about wagers on astronauts’ lives. He also cited a bet on whether Russian forces would capture the Ukrainian city of Myrnohrad.

The companies’ leaders have previously pushed back on these criticisms. “We don’t list markets directly tied to death,” Kalshi CEO Tarek Mansour wrote on X in February. Speaking at a conference in March, Polymarket CEO Shayne Coplan called conflict-related markets a “complicated question” but defended their value as a source of useful information.

Kalshi and Polymarket did not immediately respond to Fortune’s request for comment.

Other platforms, such as Novig, avoid these contracts altogether, limiting their offerings to sports markets and restricting trading to users 21 and older.

The bill’s passage comes amid growing political scrutiny of prediction markets, particularly after Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress in this month’s midterm elections. Over the past week, several Democratic lawmakers have criticized the platforms on social media.

Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-Mass.) pointed Wednesday to Kalshi’s decision to pause trading on contracts tied to President Donald Trump’s public remarks, which have drawn concerns about insider trading. “The CFTC needs to do more to stop insiders from cashing in,” she wrote on X.

Rep. Ro Khanna (D-Calif.) focused on younger users, calling Wednesday for an investigation and a ban on sports prediction markets. He criticized Kalshi for allowing 18-year-olds to bet on sports and said users under 21 had wagered $3.9 billion on the platform. “They call it a ‘prediction market.’ It’s gambling,” he wrote.

Though Democrats have been the most vocal, the scrutiny has crossed party lines. Besides cosponsoring the bill that passed Thursday, Curtis joined Warren last month in questioning Kalshi and Polymarket about potentially improper election-related trading. The Utah Republican has also backed separate legislation to ban sports and casino-style prediction contracts, citing concerns about young users’ exposure to gambling.

Sen. Ted Cruz (R-Texas) also announced plans to introduce legislation that would bar prediction market platforms from offering sports contracts without state or tribal approval, according to Bloomberg Law.

10 worst prisons in the world: crowding, isolation and gang rule behind the list

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Security Journal UK ranks 10 prisons as the world's worst. See which conditions still hold today and who has answered for them

Krzysztof Popławski / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)

Ask most people to picture the world's worst prison and they will describe a single building, a fortress with a cruel warden and a name everyone has heard. The reality is usually broader than that. In the Indian state of Maharashtra, home to Mumbai, the prison system as a whole holds far more people than it was built for and runs at nearly 173% of capacity, according to Mumbai Live. In 2010, almost half of all inmates in Russia were ill with diseases such as HIV and tuberculosis, according to federal prison service figures reported by Reuters. Neither problem was confined to one notorious jail: the crowding in India spans a state's whole prison system, and the illness in Russia was counted across the country's inmates. So when a single prison becomes infamous, it is usually the place where a nationwide shortage of space, staff and medical care is easiest to see. It is rarely an exception. That changes the question worth asking. How bad one jail is matters less than what keeps it that way.

Crowding is not simply a measure of how much crime a country has, because courts, budgets and government policy decide who stays inside and for how long. Money decides a good deal of it. Mumbai Live reports that in Maharashtra, many people held while they await trial stay locked up for long periods because they cannot afford a lawyer or bail. Many of the people in those cells are there because they are poor, not only because of what they are accused of. That is why officials who build new space or move inmates between jails rarely end the problem, since courts keep sending new people in. Not every harsh prison is a crowded one, though. Some governments impose isolation or detention without end on purpose. Those prisons raise a different question: who made that decision, and can anyone challenge it?

Security Journal UK set out to identify the prisons with the most inhumane, overcrowded and abusive conditions in the world, looking at places as far apart as an overflowing jail in Mumbai, Butyrka Prison in Moscow, a supermax in the Colorado Rockies and the U.S. detention camp at Guantanamo Bay, Cuba.

Crowding at Mumbai Central Jail is a daily condition that runs through the whole jail, not one bad stretch or a single notorious event. By Security Journal UK's count, more than 2,000 inmates live in a jail built for around 800, so they share beds, toilets and guards with far more people than the building was meant to hold. Crowding is one of the reasons the jail is ranked No. 10, along with disease, bribery and violence. Officials have few good places to move some of those inmates.

Mumbai Live reports that nearby prisons are overcrowded too and that options for relocating inmates are limited. Officials hold nearly 2,936 inmates in Thane Central Jail, near Mumbai, which was designed for 1,086. Sending inmates there would trade one overfilled jail for another. The state has allocated an 11-acre plot in Mankhurd for a proposed new prison. A plot of land offers the people inside Mumbai Central Jail no relief until a prison stands on it.

A packed jail is most dangerous because of what spreads and festers inside it. Tuberculosis and COVID-19 are among the illnesses recorded at the jail. Mumbai Live reports concerns about sanitation, water, healthcare and disease outbreaks across Maharashtra's prisons, along with a shortage of manpower. In a jail this crowded, inmates face those diseases with few defenses. The bribery and violence cited against the jail also take place in a system with too few staff to run it. The worst violence on record came in 2015, when fights between inmate groups left people injured and dead. Disease is the more recent mark against the jail.

Mumbai Central Jail's problems start with too many people in too little space, and disease and violence are harder to control in a jail so full.

Mendoza Prison in Mendoza, Argentina, is known for holding far more people than it was built for, and that reputation was earned. Security Journal UK includes the prison because it is filled to triple its capacity, with malnutrition and gang violence among its inmates. An outside count found crowding on the same scale: in May 2005, more than 1,600 inmates were registered in a prison built for 600, according to Amnesty International. The crowding is the most solid part of the case against Mendoza, though the only dated figures behind it come from 2005.

The most vivid detail about Mendoza is also the hardest to measure. The prison is on the list partly because a cell built for four sometimes holds more than 20 inmates. That claim covers only some cells, and no count says how many. Readers can hold the 20-to-a-cell image loosely, because the documented conditions are damning without it.

Provincial authorities knew how bad Mendoza was. Mendoza Province declared a prison emergency in 1999, 2000, 2001, 2003 and 2004, according to Amnesty International, so the emergency had become the ordinary state of affairs. Declaring the problem took the place of solving it. When the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights reviewed the prison in 2005, the damage went beyond the headcount to the building itself. "We couldn't work out where the toilets were, there's no water or electricity and the sewers have collapsed; it is in a total state of neglect and isn't even fit for animals," said Florentín Meléndez, a commissioner with the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights.

The last change on record was a reshuffle rather than a repair. Later in 2005 fewer inmates were held there, and officials made more room by using units left empty after transfers, according to Amnesty International. Nothing on the record describes the prison after that year. Mendoza's place among the world's worst is therefore a verdict on a crisis documented in 2005, and whether the prison fits that verdict today is unknown.

Federal Bureau of Prisons / Wikimedia Commons

The harm at ADX Florence, the federal supermax in Florence, Colo., comes from isolation rather than crowding. Each inmate is locked by himself in a cell for 23 hours of every day, according to Security Journal UK. That confinement is the prison's ordinary routine, not a penalty for breaking its rules. The prison ranks eighth among the world's worst largely because that near-total isolation causes mental illness among the men held there. The isolation is the purpose of the prison, not a side effect of a shortage of money or space.

When isolation is the purpose of a prison, the decision about who goes there is itself a punishment. Biden spared the men on federal death row from execution in December 2024, and Trump, once back in office, signed an order instructing Attorney General Pam Bondi on how they should be housed, according to The Associated Press. The push to move some of those men to ADX came from that order, which treated them as one group rather than one prisoner at a time. U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly, whom Trump nominated, temporarily blocked the transfer of 20 of them, which makes his ruling hard to dismiss as partisan. Kelly drew his line at the process, not at the prisoners' crimes, even though ADX's inmates have included Ted Kaczynski, Dzhokhar Tsarnaev and Joaquín "El Chapo" Guzmán. "But the Constitution requires that whenever the government seeks to deprive a person of a liberty or property interest that the Due Process Clause protects — whether that person is a notorious prisoner or a law-abiding citizen — the process it provides cannot be a sham," Kelly said.

The ruling is a temporary block, and it makes no finding about what life inside ADX does to people. A class action filed in 2012 challenged the effects of the isolation. The Associated Press reports that Kelly based his ruling on the Fifth Amendment's guarantee of due process. The legal fight over ADX now centers on who can be sent there rather than on what its cells do to prisoners. The two sides still disagree about the cells themselves. The inmates' lawyers argue their clients face years in a small cell cut off from other people, while government lawyers argue the conditions are not unusual for prisoners such as these.

ADX clearly meets the ranking's test for inhumane isolation imposed on purpose, but whether the law will ever call that isolation cruel remains open. So far a judge has only paused the transfer of 20 men until the government gives them a fair process.

Bang Kwang Central Prison in Thailand is known for its chains, but its lasting problem is the number of people inside it. Security Journal UK reports that as many as 50 people have been packed into some of its cells, and it names that crowding as one reason the prison ranks among the world's worst. That crowding is also the part of Bang Kwang's reputation that the prison's recent reforms left untouched.

The prison's treatment of inmates has changed in ways its crowding has not. Prisoners at Bang Kwang have spent months in leg irons, another reason the prison is counted among the worst, but Thaiger reports that recent reforms ended routine shackling and introduced video calls. Those changes show that Thai prison authorities can alter how inmates are treated when they choose to. They changed the rules inside the walls, not the number of people living within them. Bang Kwang is best judged today by its crowded cells and by the prisoners waiting to learn whether they will be executed.

For the condemned, the heaviest burden is not knowing what comes next, the third reason the prison is ranked among the worst. Thailand does not announce executions in advance, according to Thaiger, so a prisoner on death row has no way of knowing which day might be the last. That fear is concentrated at Bang Kwang, where Thailand sends people facing death, life behind bars or a term of at least 25 years. The country's most recent execution took place in June 2018, and the years without one since then have stretched the waiting out rather than ended it. Most of the hundreds of people Thailand has condemned to death were found guilty of drug crimes.

Whatever has changed about the chains, Bang Kwang's prisoners are left with the crowding and the waiting.

Stanislav Kozlovskiy / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)

Butyrka Prison in Russia has been crowded on a scale that no cell was built for. Cells designed for 10 prisoners frequently hold as many as 100, according to Security Journal UK. At Butyrka, cells packed that tightly are a regular condition rather than a rare emergency. Tuberculosis and reports of torture are also among the charges against Butyrka, and the case rests on all three together: too many people, sickness and abuse. With crowding, disease and abuse in the same cells, the obvious question is what happens to a prisoner who falls ill there.

The Russian state has taken part of the blame for the death that defines Butyrka's reputation. Sergei Magnitsky, a lawyer, died in custody there in November 2009, and by 2010 Russia's federal prison service had admitted partial responsibility for his death, according to Reuters. Critics did not have to prove this charge, because the agency that ran the prison accepted some of the blame itself. Once officials concede that the system failed one prisoner, the reports about packed cells and disease are harder to dismiss. An admission is not a repair, however. The real test is what the prison did next.

Within about a year of Magnitsky's death, Butyrka's management was promising comforts rather than space. "We are developing additional medical services... and even sunbeds will be put in place," Sergei Telyatnikov, Butyrka's head, said in 2010, as reported by Reuters. The sunbeds were due by the end of 2010, and the plan also called for ultrasound systems, possibly mud baths, and permission for inmates to use Skype. Better medical equipment may help sick prisoners. Still, none of these additions creates a single extra place to sleep in a cell built for 10.

Butyrka shows a state that admitted failure and then answered with amenities, while the crowding stayed in place.

Kamiti Maximum Security Prison in Kenya is accused of failing its inmates in nearly every way at once. Security Journal UK ranks it fifth among the world's worst prisons, citing malnutrition, rampant disease, abuse by guards, bribery and torture cases. These harms come from many directions, so fixing any one of them would leave the prison largely as it is. The hunger comes from the daily routine rather than from an occasional shortage, with maize porridge as the typical meal. An outsider could check that charge by looking at a plate. Only someone who gets inside could confirm the gravest charges, torture and abuse by guards.

The UN official whose job was to investigate torture tried to see Kamiti and could not get in. Nigel Rodley, the UN Special Rapporteur for Torture, moved freely through Kenya's police stations on his 1999 mission, The New Humanitarian reports, but the prison stayed closed to him on Sept. 29 of that year. The refusal applied to this prison in particular, not to his mission as a whole. "It is hard to imagine that if the authorities did not have something to hide they would have denied me access," Rodley said. Those words came from the UN's own torture investigator, and they turned a refused visit into a suspicion about what the prison held.

Kenya's government gave an innocent explanation. Joshua Terer, permanent secretary of Kenya's home affairs ministry, blamed the refusal on a failure of communication, according to The New Humanitarian. In his account, the refusal was a mix-up, not an attempt to hide anything. Neither account was proven, and under either one, no independent inspector got inside. Kamiti's reputation therefore rests partly on what its accusers describe and partly on the inspection that never took place.

Kenyan officials kept the UN's torture investigator out of Kamiti, so nobody independent has been able to test the worst accounts of the prison.

Prisoners at the prison in Muhanga, Rwanda, have slept standing up, and the reason is the number of people inside. Security Journal UK includes the prison because its inmates sleep on their feet for lack of room. With 6,441 people held in a building designed for 3,063, according to IGIHE, many inmates have no floor left to lie on. Once people have to sleep upright, every other problem inside gets worse with the crowding, from the spread of disease to fights between inmates to the guards' job of keeping order.

Muhanga's crowding is not just a leftover from the genocide. The cells first filled when Rwanda jailed suspects after the 1994 genocide, and the country's prisons have not emptied since. Rwanda's prison system has an overcrowding rate of 124%, according to the latest Human Rights Commission figures reported by IGIHE, and Muhanga's own rate is far higher. Crowding is a problem across Rwanda's prisons, but Muhanga carries much more of it than the system as a whole. The genocide explains how Muhanga filled up, but it does not explain why the prison is still full.

Abroad, Muhanga is better known for claims that inmates ate one another. IGIHE reports that the claim first ran on the Indian outlet Zeenews in 2015 and resurfaced in the British tabloid The Mirror in 2022. The story has been repeated rather than proven. "Prison overcrowding did exist, it is true, but no prisoner has ever eaten another," Sheikh Abdul Karim Harelimana, Rwanda's interior minister in 1996, said. Even while denying the rumor, the former minister did not dispute the crowding. The rumor remains in dispute, while the crowding does not, so the crowding is the part of Muhanga's record a reader should weigh.

Rwanda's government treats the crowding as a problem of how many people it locks up. Its plans include new facilities, alternative sentences and electronic bracelets, according to IGIHE. Only the first of those adds room. The other two would keep some people out of prison altogether.

The real test for Muhanga is not the next rumor but whether the number of people inside ever falls toward what the building was made to hold.

Diyarbakır Prison in Turkey owes its notoriety to the torture of the political prisoners held there, not to crowding. Security Journal UK ranks it third in the world, largely for the systematic torture of political prisoners that began in the 1980s. Most of those prisoners were Kurdish, according to bianet, so the abuse fell on a political and ethnic group rather than on inmates chosen at random.

Turkey's military kept its own count of the dead. In April 1984, the General Staff put the number of deaths inside the prison at 53, according to bianet. That count came at the end of a period of torture that ran from 1980, the year the prison was built, through 1984. The abuse went on for several years rather than in a single episode.

The charge that overcrowding and violence continue at Diyarbakır fits the prison's recent past better than its present. That charge rests on the years after the 2016 coup attempt, when many people detained in its aftermath were held there. Authorities have since emptied the prison, which held 270 prisoners at the end, according to bianet. The government has also placed the site under Minister of Culture and Tourism Nuri Ersoy to become a museum. With no one held inside, the building can no longer be the scene of the crowding and violence it is charged with.

Survivors tried to have the torture judged as a crime, and no court ever ruled on it. Over a thousand people once jailed there for political reasons brought criminal complaints, according to bianet. The investigation ran out under the statute of limitations, and none of those responsible for the torture was ever put on trial. Torture that went on for years has produced no defendant. "You cannot confront the past by closing a prison, you are trying to deceive the people," Mithad Sancar, co-chair of the HDP, said.

The U.S. detention camp at Guantanamo Bay, Cuba, is notorious for how long it holds people and on what grounds, not for how many people it holds. Security Journal UK ranks the camp second among the world's worst prisons because it detains people indefinitely without charge. Some detainees have been confined there for more than a decade without any court finding that they committed a crime. For those men, the waiting itself serves as the punishment.

The camp has shrunk to a small group of men, and the people running it have not closed it. Since 2002, roughly 780 detainees have been held at the camp, PBS NewsHour reports, and by June 2023 just 30 were left. Sixteen of those 30 had already been cleared for release, yet they stayed in custody. President Biden had set the goal of shutting Guantanamo before he left office. As of February 2025, the camp was still operating with fewer detainees.

The torture charge against Guantanamo concerns the camp's present conditions as well as its early years. The allegations behind its ranking go back to the camp's opening, and for most of its existence no independent United Nations investigator went inside. In 2023, Fionnuala Ní Aoláin, the UN Special Rapporteur on Human Rights and Counterterrorism, made the first such visit in the camp's history, PBS NewsHour reports, and she found detainees shackled whenever they moved within the facility. In her view, little now separates the torture many of these men endured in earlier years from the conditions they live under today.

The U.S. government disagreed with many of her findings in 2023. It said its handling of detainees met the requirements of international and U.S. law. Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch have condemned the camp's practices as torture, and the dispute over how the remaining men are treated is unresolved.

Inmates at Ciudad Barrios Prison in El Salvador face two harms at once: far too many people share the space, and a gang controls much of it. Security Journal UK ranks the prison first among the world's worst for that combination of overcrowding and gang rule. Its builders planned for around 800 inmates, and well over 3,000 people now live inside. Staff cannot keep that many people apart, supervised or cared for in a space designed for so few. Every routine set up for the original population breaks down. The failure at this prison lies in how its problems combine, not in any one of them alone.

The identity of the inmates turns a crowded prison into a different kind of prison. Most of them are members of MS-13, so they share one set of loyalties and one chain of command, and they keep that structure intact behind bars. When one gang makes up most of an overfilled prison, its members are in a position to run daily life, and the guards are not. That is the gang rule behind the prison's ranking. The crowding and the gang make each other worse. The more inmates the staff must handle, the less control the staff keep, and gang members take up the authority the staff lose.

Fixing Ciudad Barrios would take more than easing the crowding. Officials who move a few inmates elsewhere will not fix a prison this far past its limits and this dominated by one gang. Whoever takes responsibility for Ciudad Barrios faces a problem of control as much as one of space.

For Years, Doctors Blamed My Mysterious Symptoms on Running. A Simple Blood Test Proved Them Wrong.

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

All along, running actually helped me feel better.

This article originally appeared in the Spring 2025 issue of Runner’s World.

Running and menstruation entered my life around the same time, at age 13. I got hooked on running the mile, and, swayed by a persistent and impactful track coach, I eventually left soccer behind to join the cross-country team. My first period arrived right before my first track season; however, while the training cycles progressed, my menstrual cycle didn’t—my second period wouldn’t come until five months later.

Throughout my years on the high school cross-country team, this irregular pattern stuck: I only got about four periods a year and never knew when they would arrive. I felt confused and left out whenever my teammates asked me for a menstrual pad I had no use for, like my body couldn’t do what it was supposed to do.

When it came to running, though, my body felt strong, capable of carrying me through interval workouts and lifting heavy weights during weight room sessions. On the track, I felt more equal to my teammates. The training cycle was one I could control.

At my yearly physical exams, I expressed concern that my periods weren’t occurring monthly, and the feedback was the same every time: I was still young, and sometimes it takes time to become regular. That I accepted. What didn’t sit well with me was what followed: “You’re missing periods because you run a lot. Try eating more.”

Neither felt like an answer to the problem. My 20-something-mile weeks kept me physically fit and helped me deal with anxiety, especially when my dysfunctional home life felt out of control. Not only did the exam show I kept a healthy weight, I had been putting on muscle mass from deadlifting 200 pounds. But hoping the doctor was right, I took a year off from distance training.

At 19, I started seeing a new primary care physician. My doctor was surprised when I recounted my prior experiences. “So you’ve never had any blood tests done?” they asked. I underwent the test and a pelvic ultrasound, and it didn’t take very long to get an answer: The likely culprit was polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).

PCOS is the most common endocrine disorder in people assigned female at birth of reproductive age, yet so much about the condition is still unknown, including the cause of PCOS, according to the American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology. The National Institutes of Health requires only two of the following symptoms for PCOS diagnosis (all of which I’ve experienced): ovulatory dysfunction (irregular, heavy, or missed periods), high androgen levels (often manifesting as excessive body hair or acne), and/or one or more cysts on the ovaries. Besides irregular menstruation, PCOS can cause, for example, infertility and weight gain. Although there isn’t a permanent cure, some patients are able to treat their symptoms by managing their lifestyle: eating well, exercising regularly, and getting enough sleep.

The diagnosis came at a turbulent time in my life. I was swept up in another cycle: emotional abuse stemming from controlling relationships. I escaped this abuse through schoolwork and decided to take extra classes over the summer so I could graduate from college a year early and find my own place.

But I also got back to running, which has always helped me build a sense of agency and played a big role in my postgrad transition—I spent that summer running across the empty campus and habitually relying on a few miles to calm me when faced with chaos. I started taking an herbal supplement that others with PCOS raved about. I moved in with roommates. I found a therapist to help me unpack the trauma I’ve experienced.

And at 21, I finally started to get a regular monthly period.

Every menstruation since has felt like Christmas morning…followed by some gnawing cramps.

What I am only now starting to understand is just how much my environment affected my mental health and how that in turn impacted my physical health. I recently read research studies linking PCOS and emotional abuse, and that’s when I found what felt like the last missing link.

I still mourn for the runner I was in high school, who felt lost and frustrated without any answers, dealing with conflicting feelings around her body image. I remember lying on cold, uncomfortable tables, intimidated by informational pamphlets—none of which covered hormonal health—all while doctors were delivering confusing medical advice or dismissing my concerns with oversimplified notions. A 2022 health survey found that 29 percent of women and 21 percent of men felt doctors disregarded their worries during visits. I was told my problem existed “because I ran.” In reality, it’s far more complex than what many people are told, and I am just one example.

I’ve since run two half marathons while maintaining regular periods throughout each training cycle. Running was never the problem. On the contrary, the very thing doctors blamed my symptoms on brought me relief all along—and I will never give it up again.

She previously coached high school girls cross country and currently competes in seasonal races, with more than six years of distance training and an affinity for weightlifting. You can find her wearing purple, baking cupcakes, and visiting her local farmers market.

None of that puts competitive pressure on anyone, because the engine has no rival to fight. GE holds the 777X exclusively, as it has held every 777 variant since the 777-200LR.

Where the engine matters commercially is on an aircraft it has never powered. By most accounts, GE has not shown much appetite for it.

The headline dimension is the front fan, which measures 134 inches across and carries 16 fourth-generation carbon fiber composite blades inside a composite fan case. For service on the 777-9, the engine is certified to 105,000 pounds. The record figure therefore describes what the architecture can produce at its limits, not what a flight crew will ever command.

Efficiency is where the design earns its reputation. GE puts the GE9X at approximately a 10:1 bypass ratio and a 60:1 overall pressure ratio, with specific fuel consumption up to 10% better than the GE90-115B. Ceramic matrix composites appear in the combustor liner and the high-pressure turbine shrouds, parts that tolerate higher temperatures with less cooling air than the metal components they replace. GE says the engine received FAA certification in September 2020, after 27,000 cycles and 17,000 hours of testing.

Every A350 ever built flies behind the same engine. Rolls-Royce supplies the Trent XWB as the family’s sole powerplant across a thrust range. Reuters reported in October that industry sources say Airbus has toyed with using a GE Aerospace engine for the roughly 400-seat A350-2000, but that the engine maker has so far shown limited interest in joining. Airbus, in other words, has considered the option and GE has not pursued it.

Nothing about the underlying arithmetic has changed since 2008. GE holds the 777X exclusively, and a 400-seat A350 would compete directly against the 777-9 rather than against the aging 777-300ER. Supplying an engine for that aircraft would mean competing with a program GE already owns outright, at a moment when the 777X backlog represents years of engine deliveries and decades of aftermarket revenue. AIN noted the politics of an Airbus-GE widebody partnership as a complication in its own right, separate from the engineering.

Practical constraints sit on top of the commercial ones. The GE9X has no application outside the 777X, which means every development dollar spent adapting it for an Airbus wing would serve a single prospective customer on an aircraft that has not been launched. GE is also still closing out problems with the engine it has. AIN listed a variable guide-vane issue and, more recently, a mid-seal durability issue among the technical challenges the engine has encountered during 777-9 flight testing. An engine maker working through durability findings on its newest product is not an obvious candidate to open a second airframe program.

The Boeing 787 comes with both GE and Rolls-Royce engine options, although GE's engines have become more popular in recent times.

A Common Heart Failure Warning Sign May Actually Be Causing Dangerous Blood Clots

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A hormone used to detect heart trouble can also promote dangerous blood clots in experiments, pointing scientists toward a possible way to prevent blockages without disrupting normal healing.

Heart disease remains the leading cause of death in the United States, claiming a life nearly every 34 seconds. For people living with heart failure, a condition in which the heart struggles to pump enough blood throughout the body, doctors often rely on blood tests to detect signs of cardiac stress. Now, researchers have discovered that one of these familiar warning signals may also contribute to a dangerous complication: blood clots that can block circulation and trigger life-threatening events.

The substance is a hormone called B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP). Doctors routinely measure BNP and related protein fragments because elevated levels can indicate heart failure or other cardiovascular problems. Until now, BNP has largely been viewed as a marker of an overworked heart rather than a potential contributor to disease.

Researchers at the Texas A&M University Irma Lerma Rangel College of Pharmacy, part of Texas A&M Health, have found that elevated BNP can directly activate blood platelets and accelerate clot formation. The study, published in Blood, identifies a molecular pathway that could eventually offer a more targeted way to prevent dangerous blood clots.

“We thought that BNP might not only act as a passive marker but actively contributes to the pathogenesis and progression of cardiovascular disease,” said Dr. Zhenyu Li, a professor of pharmaceutical sciences who led the research.

Platelets normally protect the body from blood loss. When a blood vessel is damaged, these tiny blood components gather at the injury and stick together to help stop bleeding. Problems arise when platelets become overly active and form clots inside blood vessels where they are not needed. Those clots can obstruct circulation, depriving tissues and organs of oxygen, and contribute to emergencies such as heart attacks and pulmonary embolisms, which occur when a clot blocks blood flow in the lungs.

Li’s team traced BNP’s effects to a receptor on the surface of platelets called natriuretic peptide receptor-A (NPRA). Receptors allow cells to respond to specific chemical signals, and the researchers found that BNP activates platelets by binding directly to NPRA.

“It was previously unknown whether platelets even expressed this receptor,” Li explained. “We are the first team to investigate whether BNP directly activates platelets through the NPRA receptor.”

Once BNP attaches to NPRA, it increases the amount of a signaling molecule called cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) inside platelets. This sets off a chain of cellular responses that makes platelets more likely to stick together and promotes clot growth. In experimental studies involving blood-vessel injury and pulmonary embolism, elevated BNP markedly accelerated clot formation.

The researchers also examined what happened when NPRA was blocked or its gene was removed. Under those conditions, BNP could no longer increase cGMP levels or activate platelets, and the experiments showed protection against abnormal clotting.

“Knocking out the NPRA gene protected against these complications, proving the clotting process is mediated by the NPRA receptor,” Li said.

Blood samples from patients with heart rhythm disorders provided additional evidence. In these patients, higher circulating BNP levels were associated with greater platelet activity. The finding supports a possible connection between BNP and clotting risk in humans, although the association alone does not establish that BNP directly causes blood clots in patients.

People with elevated BNP are already known to face a higher risk of serious cardiovascular events, including heart attacks and pulmonary embolisms. The newly identified pathway offers a possible biological explanation for that relationship. Li emphasized that the discovery emerged from years of work examining platelet activity and cardiovascular disease.

“It has been an ongoing, gradual progression—not a sudden breakthrough, but a logical continuation of our research over the past two decades,” he said.

The findings could have implications for how dangerous blood clots are prevented. Existing antiplatelet medications reduce the ability of platelets to form clots, but their effects throughout the body can increase the risk of serious internal bleeding. By targeting the interaction between BNP and NPRA, researchers hope to develop treatments that interfere with harmful clot formation while preserving the normal clotting needed to heal injuries.

Such treatments remain experimental. Li suggested that, in the meantime, physicians may need to monitor patients with elevated BNP more closely and consider whether antiplatelet therapy could help reduce their risk of clot-related complications.

Several questions also remain about the mechanism. Although platelets appear central to BNP-driven clotting, the researchers still need to establish how much of the effect comes specifically from NPRA receptors on platelets rather than receptors on other cells. Future studies will selectively remove NPRA from different cell types to clarify their respective roles.

“Platelets appear to be major contributors, and to confirm this further, we need to develop tissue-specific models,” Li said.

The team is now working with Dr. Wenshe Liu, Regents Professor and Harry E. Bovay, Jr. Chair in Chemistry at the Texas A&M College of Arts and Sciences, to identify small molecules capable of blocking the BNP-NPRA interaction. Promising compounds will undergo further testing to determine whether they can prevent BNP-induced platelet activation and thrombosis without broadly disrupting healthy clotting.

“Because patients with high BNP levels face the greatest risk, targeting this pathway opens new opportunities to reduce thrombotic complications while minimizing standard side effects like severe bleeding,” Li said.

WASHINGTON, D.C. — On Thursday, Sierra Club submitted two comment letters opposing the Securities and Exchange Commission’s (SEC) proposal to rescind Rule 14a-8, which allows shareholders to put proposals addressing climate risks and other corporate issues before fellow investors for a vote, and amend Rule 14a-4(c), which governs how companies may exercise discretionary voting authority over shareholder proposals. The Sierra Club submitted both a technical comment and a grassroots comment, reflecting concerns from over 1,100 of its members and supporters.

Together the submissions urge the SEC to withdraw its proposed changes and preserve investors’ ability to raise concerns directly with corporate management and press companies to address material risks, including climate-related risks, that can affect their long-term financial interests.

Jessye Waxman, Campaign Advisor on Sierra Club’s Sustainable Finance Campaign, released the following statement:

“Climate change and other systemic risks have real consequences for companies, investors, and the broader economy. Shareholder proposals give investors an important way to bring those risks to management’s attention, push for meaningful action, and hold companies accountable when they fail to act. The SEC’s proposals would weaken those tools, making it harder for investors to protect their long-term financial interests and leaving corporate management with less accountability for the risks its decisions create. The SEC’s proposals would shield corporate management from meaningful shareholder scrutiny, while undermining the very investor protections the Commission is charged with upholding.”

Rule 14a-8 establishes when public companies must include qualifying shareholder proposals in the materials they send investors ahead of shareholder meetings. The SEC’s proposed repeal would leave decisions about shareholder proposals to state law and individual companies’ governing documents, eliminating a federal framework that investors have used for more than 80 years.

Sierra Club’s technical comments explain that material risks, like climate change, can both have significant financial consequences for companies and create systemic financial risks that extend across investment portfolios and the broader economy. Shareholder proposals give investors a way to bring these concerns to company leadership and fellow shareholders, including by surfacing risks that management may overlook or have limited incentives to address. The letter emphasizes that, for decades, this process has served the broader market by helping investors collectively identify and address financially material risks.

The letter warns that replacing a consistent federal framework with varying state laws and corporate bylaws could make shareholder engagement less predictable and push disputes over proposals into costly litigation. It points to the increase in shareholder lawsuits challenging proposal exclusions during the 2026 proxy season as an indication that disputes do not disappear when the SEC steps back from administering the process; instead, they can shift to more costly and uncertain avenues.

The submission also opposes proposed amendments to Rule 14a-4(c), which would expand company management’s discretionary authority to vote shareholders’ proxies on proposals excluded from company proxy materials, unless shareholders affirmatively opt out. Sierra Club’s letter warns that these changes could allow management to vote on proposals that investors have not had an adequate opportunity to evaluate.

Thrillers can fool readers with speed. Hide one fact, delay one reveal, add a killer, and the pages will probably turn. A masterpiece has a harder job. The tension has to survive after the secret is known, and the characters must stay interesting after the danger passes.

That is the standard here. Every book on this list can be reread without losing its grip. The shock may change, but the psychology, fear, moral pressure, and human damage become clearer. These are stories people finish quickly and continue thinking about for years. That’s also perhaps why most of these were later adapted into books as well.

The Day of the Jackal should have no suspense. Frederick Forsyth is writing about a fictional attempt to assassinate Charles de Gaulle, and readers already know de Gaulle was never killed. Forsyth turns that limitation into the smartest part of the book. The question shifts from whether the Jackal succeeds to how close an unknown professional can get before the French authorities identify him.

The preparation is incredibly detailed without becoming lifeless. The assassin builds false identities, commissions a custom weapon, changes his appearance, studies security, and removes anyone who threatens the plan. On the other side, investigator Claude Lebel has almost no useful description and a deadline that keeps shrinking. Forsyth respects procedure enough to make paperwork, border checks, phone calls, and hotel records genuinely tense. The Jackal remains frightening since he treats murder as a technical assignment rather than a personal cause. You understand every step he takes, yet you never really know him. That distance makes the hunt colder and much harder to shake.

Most plot twist-driven novels become smaller once the answer is revealed. Shutter Island becomes crueler. U.S. Marshal Teddy Daniels arrives at Ashecliffe Hospital in 1954 to investigate the disappearance of patient Rachel Solando. A hurricane cuts the island off, the staff answers questions carefully, and Teddy suspects that the hospital is conducting experiments on people who cannot defend themselves.

Dennis Lehane lets Teddy’s investigation operate as both a detective story and a personal escape. His memories of Dachau, grief over his wife Dolores, anger toward the doctors, and obsession with a patient named Andrew Laeddis all influence what he sees. The clues are real, but Teddy has arranged them into a version of events he can survive. That is much more painful than a cheap reveal. The doctors are also divided over treatment, punishment, and whether a damaged mind can be reached without violence. Once the truth is known, every earlier conversation changes. The staff was never hiding only one secret. They were trying to find out whether Teddy could face his own life.

In Misery, Stephen King traps bestselling novelist Paul Sheldon in a farmhouse with Annie Wilkes, the nurse who rescued him after a car accident. Paul initially thinks he has been saved. Then Annie reads his latest book, learns that he killed off her beloved character, Misery Chastain, and decides that he owes her a new novel.

Annie is terrifying since her violence comes with rules that change whenever she needs them to. She calls herself Paul’s number-one fan, but she has no interest in Paul as a human being. She wants ownership over his imagination. The captivity also forces Paul to confront his own contempt for the books that made him successful. Writing becomes survival, punishment, and eventually a source of control. King understands the physical misery of the room too. Paul’s injuries, painkillers, thirst, typewriter, hidden pills, and attempts to move around the house make every small decision dangerous. Annie later amputates his foot, and even that horror does not end the deeper fight. Paul has to finish a story for the person destroying him and somehow keep part of it for himself.

The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo follows Harriet Vanger, who vanished from a family gathering decades ago, and her uncle Henrik still receives a pressed flower every year on his birthday. He believes her killer is taunting him. Disgraced journalist Mikael Blomkvist accepts the investigation, partly to escape the damage caused by his libel conviction. Lisbeth Salander enters from another direction, first hired to investigate Mikael before becoming the person he needs most.

Stieg Larsson takes his time connecting the Vanger mystery to corporate corruption, misogyny, financial crime, and generations of protected violence. That slow build has a purpose. The respectable surfaces of Swedish business and family life begin to look worse with every document Mikael and Lisbeth uncover. Lisbeth is also far more than a brilliant hacker attached to a male detective. Her distrust has been earned through institutional abuse, and her methods come from knowing official systems rarely protect people like her. The investigation succeeds through journalism, research, memory, and technology working together. Then Lisbeth allows herself one small hope outside the case, only to have it crushed. The crime is solved. Her loneliness is not. This movie based on this book is worth watching before or after reading the novel, too.

Four criminal masterminds. One of them was always going to be yours. Here is who comes for you, why they picked you out of everyone available, and what they want from the encounter.

Your nemesis does not want to beat you. He wants to know you completely, and he is patient enough to wait for it. The frightening part is how good the conversation is.

Your nemesis is not after money, territory or revenge. He is after a demonstration, and you are the person he has chosen to prove his point on.

Your nemesis respects you, which sounds flattering until you work out what it means. He chose you because nobody else was worth his time.

Your nemesis has no personal interest in you whatsoever, and that is precisely what makes him unsurvivable. You are not a rival. You are a stop on the way.

The first Mrs. de Winter is dead before Rebecca begins, yet the unnamed narrator cannot find a single room at Manderley that seems free of her. She marries Maxim de Winter after a rushed courtship and arrives at his estate painfully aware of her youth, lower social position, and lack of confidence. Everyone appears to know how the new Mrs. de Winter should behave except her. Daphne du Maurier turns insecurity into genuine suspense in this book.

Mrs. Danvers protects Rebecca’s memory with frightening devotion and knows exactly how to make the new bride feel like an intruder. Maxim responds to questions with anger or silence, which leaves his wife guessing what kind of marriage she has entered. The later revelations change Rebecca from an idealized dead wife into something far more complicated, but they also expose Maxim in ways romance cannot clean up. The narrator gains confidence while becoming involved in his secrecy. That moral shift is easy to miss if the book is treated only as a Gothic love story. Manderley was never simply haunted by Rebecca. It was built around people choosing which version of her helped them live with themselves.

The Talented Mr. Ripley follows Tom Ripley, who wants Dickie Greenleaf’s money, confidence, clothes, friends, and freedom. He also wants Dickie himself in a way Tom cannot separate from envy. When Dickie’s father pays him to travel to Italy and persuade his son to return home, Tom treats the assignment as entry into the life he believes he should have had.

Patricia Highsmith puts the reader dangerously close to Tom’s thinking. His lies are quick, his shame is intense, and his ability to copy other people becomes more impressive as his crimes become worse. Dickie’s rejection humiliates him, but murder is never presented as one sudden break from normality. Tom has been studying how to disappear into other identities long before violence becomes necessary. The truly disturbing choice is Highsmith’s refusal to build the story around punishment. Readers start worrying about whether Tom will be exposed, even though exposure would be justice. That uncomfortable loyalty is the whole trap. Tom does terrible things, and Highsmith makes us anxious on his behalf. The Talented Mr. Ripley would go on to inspire a movie that doesn't quite live up to the novel.

Gone Girl turned a missing-wife plot into one of the nastiest books ever written about marriage. Amy Dunne disappears on her fifth wedding anniversary, and Nick quickly becomes the husband everyone suspects. He lies badly, smiles at the wrong moment, has an affair, and cannot control how television turns his marriage into public entertainment.

Then Amy’s account tears up the story readers thought they understood. This is written by Gillian Flynn, and she uses the competing narrators to show two people who spent years performing ideal versions of themselves. Amy created the Cool Girl Nick wanted. Nick created the attentive husband Amy expected. Resentment grew once both stopped enjoying the act. Amy’s revenge is extreme, brilliant, cruel, and built around a precise understanding of how gender shapes public sympathy. Nick is a victim of her plan, but he is never rewritten as innocent. The book refuses that easy comfort. They know each other too well, hate each other too deeply, and still find a reason to stay together. Murder would have been a cleaner conclusion. Marriage becomes the real life sentence.

Hannibal Lecter gets most of the cultural attention, but Clarice Starling is the reason The Silence of the Lambs belongs at number one. She is still an FBI trainee when Jack Crawford sends her to interview Lecter, hoping the imprisoned psychiatrist can help identify the serial killer known as Buffalo Bill. Lecter immediately notices her accent, cheap shoes, ambition, and discomfort. He understands how badly she wants to escape the life she came from. Thomas Harris makes every conversation between them a contest.

Clarice needs information, while Lecter demands personal memories in exchange. She has to remain honest enough to interest him without surrendering control. Outside the cell, Buffalo Bill’s latest victim is still alive, which gives the psychological games a real deadline. Harris also fills the investigation with forensic detail, institutional pressure, sexism, and mistakes that cost time. Clarice earns progress through observation rather than sudden genius. Her fear never disappears, especially when she enters Bill’s house alone. She continues anyway. In a book filled with men who study, test, dismiss, or hunt women, Clarice refuses to become someone else’s object.

America is in a turkey slump

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

One of the most quintessential American things is having roasted turkey on the table at a Thanksgiving feast. While that remains the norm, Americans are eating much less of this kind of poultry today than in the past. Annual consumption has fallen by more than 25% over the past three decades, while demand for chicken has soared.

I’m a business school professor who has written about the economics of various holidays. I find turkeys fascinating, because even though I live in an urban area, a large group of wild turkeys has taken over my backyard.

And I can offer several good explanations for the turkey slump.

Receding since the late 1990s

Domesticated turkeys, unlike their wild cousins, are a big business.

More than $5 billion worth of the birds were sold in 2025. That’s no chicken feed.

However, it’s the latest sign of the industry’s decline. U.S. consumers are drifting away from eating turkey. Back in 1995, the average American consumed 17.7 pounds of turkey each year. By 2025, consumption had fallen to just 13.2 pounds.

While turkey consumption started to recede in the late 1990s, it began accelerating around 2008, when the Great Recession was underway. Occasionally, turkey production ticks upward a little, as happened in 2026 as demand for niche products like turkey jerky rose, but the long-term picture is one of steady decline.

It’s not like Americans are eating less poultry overall. Chicken consumption has steadily grown during this turkey slump, rising about 50% from 69 pounds in 1995 to 104 pounds in 2025.

In other words, we’re eating almost eight times more chicken than turkey. Well, at least the 95% of Americans who aren’t vegetarian or vegan are.

More costly at every step in the supply chain

Government agencies, researchers, and some pundits have identified two possible explanations for why Americans are eating less turkey.

First: pocketbook concerns. Turkey often costs more than chicken, except when it goes on sale just before Thanksgiving.

Over the past 25 years, wholesale turkey prices per pound averaged about 10% higher than chicken, according to U.S. Department of Agriculture data. That price, which is what big retailers typically pay, affects what you owe at checkout.

For example, just before the 2026 Thanksgiving sales began, a Bell & Evans fresh whole turkey was going for $4.99 a pound, while a Bell & Evans fresh whole chicken cost $3.49 a pound at my local supermarket. That’s $1.50 per pound difference, which is significant in an economy where many people feel economically pinched.

Nationally, you will see similar price differences, with fresh turkey being more expensive than fresh chicken.

So, why is turkey pricier than chicken?

Both turkeys and chickens are fed a diet of corn and soybeans. However, turkeys consume a higher percentage of soybeans. And soybeans currently cost about twice as much as corn.

The latest data shows turkey feed costs are up almost 80%, without accounting for inflation, since 2000, while chicken feed costs have climbed only 60%.

Too big for more families

The second reason has to do with the size of today’s families.

The typical Bell & Evans whole chicken my grocery store sells weighs approximately 4 pounds, while its fresh turkeys tend to range between 10 and 12 pounds.

Big birds are handy if you have a lot of mouths to feed, but American families have shrunk over time. Today the average household includes only 2.5 people. The extra half-person does not represent children who are picky eaters or people who don’t like eating meat. It just means the typical family has between two and three people.

That means cooking a 10-pound turkey results in 4 pounds of food per person, which is far more than almost anyone who is not a competitive food eater can consume.

Cooking a 4-pound chicken, however, results in a more manageable 1.2 pounds of poultry per eater after getting rid of the bones, which make up about a quarter of a chicken. In simple terms, buying a whole chicken often results in one or two meals per person. Cooking a whole turkey often yields enough meat to produce meals for an entire week.

There are two more factors to consider. First, a 10-pound turkey costs a lot more than a 4-pound chicken. A 10-pound fresh bird at $4.99 a pound sets you back almost $50, while a $3.49-per-pound chicken that weighs 4 pounds costs under $14.

Last, bigger birds take longer to roast. Cooking a 10-pound turkey in an oven takes about three hours, while a 4-pound chicken takes about two. That extra hour is a big burden in an increasingly time-pressed society.

In case you were wondering, the world record for eating a turkey is held by Joey Chestnut. He gobbled down 9.35 pounds of turkey off the bone in 10 minutes in 2014. Please do not try that at home.

What about wild turkeys?

If you need to save money on turkey this holiday season, you should consider buying a frozen one. They are much cheaper.

But can you save even more by going turkey hunting?

Given all the large wild turkeys roosting in my backyard, I looked into what it would take. I live in Massachusetts, where hunting wild turkeys before Thanksgiving is legal.

For much of the fall, hunters are allowed to use only bows and arrows. Beyond needing to be a proficient archer, they also have to purchase a sporting license, a turkey permit, and an archery stamp for $95 each year.

Having learned about that, I’m sticking to buying my turkeys at the supermarket.

Iran relies on China and Russia as US pressure grows, but both powers are pursuing broader interests

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As Donald Trump seeks to tighten economic pressure on Iran, China remains its biggest oil customer and Russia a key partner. But both powers are balancing support for Tehran against broader interests with Washington.

As Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin meet in Shenzhen, Washington wants to tighten its blockade of Iran, but China remains Tehran's main oil buyer and Russia offers it political and logistical support.

And when Trump, Xi and Putin sit down together, Iran will not be at the table, even though Washington's pressure campaign against Tehran is expected to be among the issues discussed.

For Tehran, relations with both China and Russia are routinely described as strategic. That relationship looks more complicated from Beijing and Moscow.

Both countries oppose Washington's efforts to isolate Iran and maintain political and economic ties with Tehran. But both have broader interests in their relationships with the United States and may see Iran partly through the prism of their own negotiations with Washington.

That raises a central question: Can Trump's strategy of economically isolating Iran succeed if China and Russia refuse to fully cooperate?

China is currently the most important destination for Iranian oil.

In 2025, it was widely reported that Chinese buyers took more than 80% of Iran's seaborne crude exports, much of it through independent refiners and at steep discount because of sanctions.

And as China provided Tehran an economic lifeline, Iran also became increasingly dependent on Chinese manufactured goods, machinery and industrial inputs.

Mohsen Solhdoost, an assistant professor of international relations at Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, says China is important to Washington's strategy but not decisive on its own.

"If the objective is to reduce Iranian export earnings, restrict access to equipment and make economic and military recovery more difficult, the United States can achieve substantial results without Chinese cooperation," he told DW.

But forcing Iran to accept Washington's preferred nuclear or regional policies is another matter.

"The ability to interrupt trade is much easier to demonstrate than the ability to determine another government's political choices," Solhdoost said.

That distinction has become more important as US sanctions enforcement and maritime blockade efforts have increased pressure on Iranian crude oil exports.

In November, Washington announced Task Force Iron Gate, an initiative aimed at disrupting the movement of sanctioned goods, military equipment and revenue through Iran's land borders and the Caspian Sea.

Even so, these trade links have not disappeared. The commercial and security interests of countries including Iraq, Pakistan and Turkey mean that goods continue to move across the region, albeit under greater scrutiny.

As a result, China's role in Iran's economy remains significant, but Solhdoost argues that demand alone cannot sustain Iranian exports. The oil must be able to reach international markets.

"A Chinese buyer cannot keep Iranian export revenues flowing simply by remaining willing to buy if newly loaded tankers cannot reach the market," Solhdoost said.

This economic pressure is shaping Tehran's financial planning.

A senior official at Iran's Plan and Budget Organization recently said the government was preparing its 2027-2028 budget under several scenarios, including one in which oil revenue falls to zero.

That does not mean Iran has adopted a zero-oil budget, but considering such a scenario illustrates how seriously Tehran views the situation.

At the same time, Chinese trade can preserve critical parts of the Iranian economy even if overall commerce shrinks.

Machinery, electronics, spare parts and industrial components can still reach Iran by rail and road. Such trade cannot replace maritime commerce at comparable scale or cost, but it can keep selected factories and infrastructure operating.

Such Chinese support could help Iran endure pressure without preventing economic deterioration, says Solhdoost.

But even for Beijing supporting Iran economically does not mean supporting every Iranian policy.

Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi has maintained support for cooperation with Tehran while calling for negotiations, safe passage through the Strait of Hormuz and stable energy supplies.

A prolonged conflict around Hormuz threatens wider energy supplies and global shipping. China therefore benefits from continued access to Iranian oil but also from regional stability.

Russia creates another obstacle to Iran's isolation. The two countries share access to the Caspian Sea and have expanded military, transport and economic cooperation.

Iranian officials have discussed ways of using Russian and northern trade routes to reduce the impact of American restrictions. There has also been speculation about oil swaps and deeper Russian assistance, although these claims remain difficult to verify.

Ahmad Vakhshiteh, director of the Moscow-based Center for Strategic Research on Developing Relations with the Middle East, believes the summit should be viewed within a larger attempt to manage the wars in both Ukraine and the Middle East.

"Russia, China and the United States appear to be seeking constructive dialogue and results that could shape a new security architecture in the international system," Vakhshiteh told DW.

The summit could be significant if it establishes a continuing format for dialogue rather than producing symbolic statements, Vakhshiteh says, while cautioning against expecting a dramatic settlement from a single meeting.

Solhdoost also warns against overstating Russian support for Iran.

The strategic partnership between Moscow and Tehran provides for security cooperation and efforts to counter unilateral sanctions, but it does not require Russia to enter a war on Iran's behalf.

Since Russia is deeply engaged in Ukraine it likely has its own priorities in negotiations with Washington, says Solhdoost.

"Russia would prioritize its own national interests in negotiations with the US over Ukraine and Iran," he said.

Moscow could support Iran while simultaneously using its relationship with Tehran as leverage in talks with Trump over Ukraine.

For Iran, ties with China and Russia remain essential. They provide alternative trade routes, export markets and diplomatic support that complicate Washington's efforts to isolate Tehran.

United Airlines Coming Back To A Hub It De-Hubbed Over A Decade Ago Could Be Its Biggest Domestic Bet In Years

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Wikimedia Commons, Wirestock Creators | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

United Airlines has buoyed its presence at Cleveland Hopkins International Airport (CLE) recently. Several route additions this year have helped expand the US-based legacy carrier’s footprint in Northern Ohio to its largest in over a decade. They have also left locals with ample choice of United hubs and leisure destinations, making Cleveland an ever more important part of the airline’s network.

But there is a sense of irony in the move. United largely turned its back on the airport, unceremoniously winding down operations 12 years ago. This cut what was a major hub in itself from United’s network. So with the carrier now appearing to bet on the site once again in what looks to be a full-circle moment, the question is: What has changed?

United’s move back to Cleveland is particularly noteworthy because of its historical relationship with the airport. After a merger with Continental Airlines in 2010, Cleveland became one of nine hubs for the expanded carrier, which retained United’s name. Unfortunately for the airport, this would turn out to be just one of a host of factors that would see it fall out of favor with the new, combined entity.

Not only was Cleveland the smallest of the nine hubs, but it was also relatively close to United’s existing gateways at Chicago O'Hare International Airport (ORD) and Washington Dulles International Airport (IAD), as well as another former Continental base: Newark Liberty International Airport (EWR). While these fit nicely into the larger network and remain in use as hubs by United to this day, Cleveland failed to fill a significant role in the wider operation. Not least was Chicago—a huge airport in comparison, serving a far greater market—being only 315 miles (275 km) down the road.

Behind all of this was the fallout from the 2008 financial crisis, which had hammered profitability across the industry. After United and Continental joined forces to cut costs, the unviable, loss-making Cleveland base became an easy target in the now-unified United and Continental brand’s drive towards efficiency. This came in 2014, with its de-hubbing (which was actually the second time in its history that United moved away).

The de-hubbing did not mean United left Cleveland altogether, though. Rather, the carrier drastically scaled back on departures and rerouted connecting services elsewhere. Under plans laid out at the time, United said average daily departures would be cut by around 60%. Some 72 peak-day flights serving 20 destinations nonstop were to be retained, it added, down from 200 and 61 beforehand, respectively.

These fell further with time, too. By 2025, just 30 or so United flights were departing Cleveland daily. That is, until the carrier opted to expand its offerings from the base once again this year. Las Vegas’ Harry Reid International Airport (LAS), which United last flew to from Cleveland in 2024, would be served year-round with five flights a week starting in September, an announcement said in May. A winter service, running between December and April next year, would then see Miami International Airport (MIA) reappear on United’s list of destinations from the airport for the first time since 2014.

* Myrtle Beach: 11 flights from June 4, 2027.

** San Juan: 26 flights from December 17, 2026, to January 11, 2027.

*** Sarasota: 4 flights from March 13 to April 3, 2027.

Owing to the additions, United’s winter schedule for this year going into next is set to cover 14 nonstop routes from Cleveland. This will be the most among any of the 10 passenger airlines that regularly operate out of the airport during the period. It will also leave United flying out of Cleveland almost 40 times a day in the meantime, taking capacity of the airport to its highest in over a decade. No doubt, this is in response to strong local demand.

The carrier is betting big on Cleveland's travel market with the announcement of two new nonstop routes to Miami and Las Vegas.

As Scott C. Carr, a City of Cleveland Department of Port Control assistant director, commented on the news of United’s route additions in May.

“Strong demand for leisure travel continues to create opportunities to grow service and connect Northeast Ohio travelers to destinations they want most.”

This is by no means new, however, with the airport having enjoyed progressively stronger footfall for some time now.

In fact, the airport’s own data shows a clear trend of improving traffic in almost every year since United’s large-scale departure in 2014, aside from an obvious slump during the pandemic. Though passenger numbers naturally dipped as United slashed services, it only took a few years for other airlines to move in, pick up the slack, and push the figures higher once more.

Among the flurry of airlines to begin or expand operations in response were low-cost carriers Frontier Airlines and Spirit Airlines, as well as close United rivals Delta Air Lines and American Airlines, to name a few. Simply put, demand was always there, but capturing it without losing money required a different approach than the one United had committed to thus far.

Prior to 2014, Continental — and later United following their merger — primarily used Cleveland as any other airline would: to funnel passengers originating elsewhere onto flights headed elsewhere. But such an operation is complex and can come with caveats.

A major consideration is whether what are known as banked connections can be sustained. Here, airlines effectively sort their arrivals and departures into waves so that passengers have time to disembark one flight and board another. The downside is that all aircraft involved have to remain grounded for a period to give travelers time to get through the airport, which can be costly for a carrier. Alongside this, airport infrastructure and ground crews must be in place to support sudden spikes in activity as planes come and go in waves.

Clearly, the practice was ultimately deemed unsustainable at Cleveland by United post-consolidation with Continental. Instead, a scaled-back, arguably simpler operation targeting those originating in or traveling to the area has taken its place. The bonus for United is that the fewer moving parts in a point-to-point system, compared to a hub-and-spoke model, make reacting to demand simpler — especially when the personnel and infrastructure to accommodatechanges are already in place.

Newark is the main United hub, while it has other major hubs in other airports across the country.

Indeed, United drastically cut back on services from Cleveland, but it did not fully scrap them. In fact, the airline remained its largest by passenger traffic, which gives some context into the weight of the de-hubbing decision on the airport. Either way, that meant United retained everything it needed to fly from the site — including a pilot base, cabin crew domicile, and maintenance facility.

But despite having the means in place to easily expand its schedule from Cleveland, United has found itself in a far less dominant position there today. Yes, it is still the airport’s primary carrier, but that is by no means as clear-cut as before the de-hubbing.

United accounted for a 28.84% share of Cleveland’s passengers over the year to August 2013, according to the US Bureau of Transportation Statistics. ExpressJet was the airport’s next-largest operator at the time, with a 14.60% share. Come the 12 months to August this year, United’s share was a more moderate 23.77%, while the gap to the second-largest - now Frontier with 21.47% - had narrowed drastically.

In other words, competition has heated up, and a string of nonstop routes passengers can choose from in Cleveland are serviced by more than one carrier — hence the risk United has had to accept in moving back in. But another factor may have contributed to the decision.

Spirit took the axe to its network prior to its collapse this year. This involved closing its base in Cleveland, where it had historically held a near 10% market share and from which it had even flown to the likes of Miami and Las Vegas. How much that has influenced United’s decision-making is debatable. Regardless, it has no doubt left a gap to be filled.
