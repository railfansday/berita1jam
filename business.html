Does the calendar matter for Dutch Bros investors? Not really, but the price might.

I can't quite shake Dutch Bros (BROS -2.16%). The stock has spent most of 2026 sliding downhill. On Nov. 17, it sits near $35 a share, about 43% below where it started the year. Every time Dutch Bros' stock looks like a bargain, it finds a new floor to fall through. Meanwhile, the drive-through coffee shop chain keeps opening locations like it gets paid per ribbon-cutting.

So, should you buy before the end of November? Let me spare you the suspense: the calendar doesn't care. There's no earnings report due before the month runs out, and the next one isn't expected until February 2027. If you buy Dutch Bros stock, you should do it because of the price and the business, not because November is running out of days.

The third-quarter report gave Wall Street plenty to grumble about. Revenue jumped 30%, which sounds great until you lift the lid. Systemwide customer visits grew a whisper-thin 0.4%. Nearly all the same-shop sales growth came from people paying more per order, not from more cars pulling up to the window.

In other words, Dutch Bros' famous round-the-block car lines stopped growing longer in recent months.

Management also trimmed its full-year outlook. It now expects at least $2.1 billion in revenue and roughly $380 million in adjusted earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA), after calling for up to $2.13 billion and $390 million in August.

Here's what keeps me interested. Dutch Bros ended September with 1,273 shops and plans to open about 250 more in 2027. Management says it has already lined up the real estate for 2,029 shops by 2029.

I appreciate punny business goals, but it's also a downright ambitious target. Staying on track to meet it is an impressive feat, and Dutch Bros did it again in Q3 2026.

After the 2026 sell-off, Dutch Bros trades at about 30 times forward earnings. That's a steep multiple for a restaurant chain, but a more modest one for a company growing revenue near 30% a year.

If you have a long investing horizon and a sturdy stomach (more coffee might help), a 43% price drop for a business growing this fast deserves a closer look. November isn't a deadline for buying Dutch Bros stock, but it does look tempting right now.

Economic Headwinds Drag Homebuilder Confidence to New Low

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Homebuilder confidence in November’s National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index (HMI) report slumped four points to 25, a new 14- year low amid low consumer sentiment and significant pressure on the housing sector.

Last month’s homebuilder confidence score, a measly 29 points, was already a 14-year low. This month’s new slump sends homebuilder confidence even further back in time to April 2012.

NAHB blamed a variety of causes, the most important of which were “high mortgage rates, ongoing inflation pressure, elevated energy and material costs, (and) rising labor shortages and hesitant buyers.”

“Builders are confronting multiple challenges in today’s economy,” said NAHB Chairman Bill Owens in a statement. “Stepped-up immigration enforcement is keeping legal workers off job sites, deepening labor shortages and driving up costs. Higher fuel prices are prompting subcontractors to raise their rates sharply, and with fewer buyers in the market, more builders are scaling back construction.”

Robert Dietz, NAHB chief economist, focused on the housing market’s supply-side challenges, homing in on “building material costs pushed higher by tariffs, an ongoing shortage of construction workers and excessive regulations that undermine housing affordability.”

Dietz also added that “(m)ortgage rates above 7%, softer consumer confidence and an uncertain economic outlook are holding buyers back.”

The NAHB/Wells Fargo HMI, an ongoing monthly survey of more than 40 years, asks builders to rate their perceptions of current single-family home sales, potential single-family home sales in the next six months and prospective buyer traffic. Builders’ perceptions on each of these three component indices is scaled onto a range of zero to 100, which is then used to calculate a general homebuilder confidence level.

The three component indices this month were, unsurprisingly, also down. Current sales conditions dropped six points to 28, while future sales conditions dropped two points to 30.

Prospective buyer traffic ticked down two points to 16, another new low for this report. The last time prospective buyer traffic reached these troughs was 15 years ago (excluding a brief period at the start of the pandemic in 2020).

The NAHB/Wells Fargo HMI this month also reported that 46% of builders reduced prices, up 3% from last month. This is the highest share of builders cutting prices in the post-Covid period.

Almost seven-in-10 (68%) builders offered incentives, also up 3%, representing a post-Covid high. November 2026 is now the 20th consecutive month in which at least 60% of builders utilized sales incentives.

Regional HMI scores were similarly gloomy. All four regions experienced declines, but the Northeast and the Midwest experienced the largest declines point-wise at four and five points, respectively. The Northeast now sits at 34 points, the Midwest at 35, and the South and West both tumbled three points to 27 and 23 points, respectively.

The Whites were finally beaten by Valencia in the last four of Europe's elite club competition in 2001, having beaten AC Milan, Lazio and Deportivo La Coruna on their way to the semis.

The Yorkshire outfit also had many players coming through their academy system looking to become the next Alan Smith or Robbie Keane in the first team, with Aaron Lennon and James Milner two players who emerged into the first team in the years following.

Rav Matharu was in the age group with Aaron's older brother Anthony, signing a professional contract at 17 years of age, having been born in Leeds and then progressed through the ranks as a Leeds-mad boy.

There were inevitably extra eyes on him as a young Asian player, as both he and Harpal Singh reached the fringes of the first team at the same time. But he says he never got close to making a first-team appearance for Leeds, having also failed to appear for the reserve side, which often consisted of first-team players too.

Matharu said: "I don't think I got close. Maybe I'll tell people differently in the pub after a few drinks, but I don't think I was near to breaking through to the first team at Leeds.

There were internationals playing in the reserves, it was hard to progress. I remember being on the bench for the reserves against Sheffield Wednesday, and that was probably the closest. Chris Waddle was playing for them and I was watching him, remembering idolising him at the 1990 World Cup.

Speaking about his next steps, he said: "I always used to draw. I was into clothing and footwear and designing. It seemed like something I could do. I really wanted to learn more.

After moving to London to pursue a dream of working in fashion, he was let go from his role as Head Designer at House of Billiam, which forced him to set up his own business.

He added: "When I moved to London I always had the intention of starting my own brand. Clothsurgeon has gone from strength to strength, with Matharu explaining exactly how the process works.

"We concentrate on quality fabrics, quality production. You can come in and create a wardrobe of quality clothes.

Now, a new report from Realtor.com® has found that buyers of new homes are also seeing reduced mortgage rates.

The report found that new-home buyers saw an average starting mortgage rate of 5.01% in Q3, compared with 6.15% for existing-home buyers. This translates to about $258 less per month in interest during the first year of homeownership (on a $400,000 home purchase with 10% down).

While some of these rate reductions are only temporary (via rate buydowns), the report also noted that the fixed-rate mortgage also saw a lower rate for new-home buyers. Specifically, new-home buyers saw an average rate of 6.19%, compared with 6.68% for existing-home buyers. This translates to about $117 less per month in interest for the 30-year loan term (on a $400,000 home purchase with 10% down).

“Builders have adjusted to a market where affordability is still a major hurdle, but that adjustment isn’t always visible in the listing price,” said Realtor.com Senior Economist Joel Berner. “Reduced mortgage rates can help make a new home more affordable for buyers while allowing builders to hold asking prices relatively steady.”

“We're not saying they're perfect” says architect fighting to save Melbourne's public housing blocks

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Photography by Ben Hosking, Michael Pham

Architect Simon Robinson has been leading calls for the Victorian state government to rethink its plan to demolish all of Melbourne’s public housing towers. In this interview, he sets out why he believes retrofit is the smarter approach.

Announced by the Labor-run state government in 2023, Homes Victoria’s High-Rise Redevelopment Program (HRRP) will see all 44 of Melbourne’s public housing towers and many other lower-rise blocks razed and replaced with new housing by 2051.

Officials argue that the buildings, constructed between 1962 and 1975, have reached the end of their useful lives. They claim the towers do not meet modern standards and cost too much to maintain, and that the redevelopment programme will triple housing capacity and improve the quality of homes.

But the policy has proved highly controversial, with critics particularly angry about a lack of transparency or consultation with residents, who are being relocated.

It is now subject to an ongoing legal dispute, has prompted concern from the UN’s special rapporteur on housing, and become a political issue in the build-up to the Victorian state election later this month.

Among the voices leading calls for a change of course is Melbourne not-for-profit research and design practice Office.

Office has worked pro bono to make the case for refurbishing the towers, after learning from residents that the government has not published feasibility studies for keeping any of the condemned estates.

“We thought that was pretty odd that you wouldn’t do a refurbishment study on these estates,” Office director Simon Robinson told Dezeen.

“So we took it upon ourselves to start to do these feasibility studies, along with public housing residents.”

“We’re not saying that they’re in perfect condition,” he added. “We’re also not saying that all of them should be refurbished – perhaps some are beyond repair. All we are saying is that these rapprochement studies need to occur.”

In fact, feasibility studies have been conducted by the government but not been made public, despite requests by the Victorian Legislative Council.

By avoiding the relocation of residents, Office estimates that a retrofit and infill approach would cost around $300 million less than demolition and rebuild.

“As all the estates are occupied, the cost of relocating and housing the residents during construction work needs to be included in the project cost; this is consistently excluded and never disclosed,” said Robinson.

“We have estimated that these costs are very significant, with reports indicating that the government is spot purchasing or head-leasing properties from the private market.”

The practice has put together a counter-proposal based on a strategy that it calls Retain Repair Reinvest (RRR).

RRR focuses on retaining buildings without displacing residents, carrying out renovation work to improve housing comfort and energy efficiency while reducing construction waste and carbon impact.

Robinson agrees that most of Victoria’s existing public housing buildings are currently not fit for purpose – buildings are rundown, cold in winter, and sweltering in summer. But he believes comfortable living standards can be achieved through renovations.

Office has applied its RRR strategy to three Melbourne estates in hypothetical case studies – Ascot Vale Estate, Barak Beacon Estate and the Flemington Estate.

Formerly home to approximately 10,000 people, Flemington Estate’s four towers make up about 10 per cent of Victoria’s public housing stock.

Only about 32 households remain, though an injunction prevents Homes Victoria from serving further eviction notices while legal challenges are pending.

Office has proposed retaining the estate’s 720 dwellings and building an additional 577 infill social housing units to increase density.

New facades and private balconies would be added, with other upgrades including integrated underground parking, a central communal green space, new heating and cooling systems, improved insulation, new kitchens and bathrooms.

Energy efficiency is another key focus. Double-glazed windows, photovoltaic cells, and water conservation systems would provide a 55 percent reduction of global warming potential compared to the demolition approach, according to Office.

Despite the seemingly obvious benefits, the Victorian government has so far been unmoved by the studies presented by the practice.

Robinson believes the reason lies in the fact that the state government no longer wants to manage public housing.

“When the state government manages public housing, they are responsible for maintaining the buildings and organising new tenants – all the admin that comes with owning a building,” he said.

As well as the demolitions, also attracting concern is the type of housing proposed for redevelopment under the HRRP.

Rather than like-for-like public housing, the new homes will be “community housing” – developed by private developers, and split between below-market and market-rate rental homes.

Despite still being classed as social housing by the state government, community housing is slightly more expensive, and the tenancy is less secure from eviction.

Robinson believes the term was created to misdirect the public.

“What they’re building is community housing, not public housing, so there’s no longer any public housing being rebuilt in Victoria,” he said.

“The government is now calling everything social housing, because they don’t want the public to know that it’s no longer public housing,” he continued.

“Victoria has the lowest percentage of public housing stock in Australia, and we’re currently demolishing it all and turning it into private and community housing.”

The issue is particularly charged in the context of Australia‘s housing-affordability crisis. There are currently over 60,000 people on the waitlist for social housing in Victoria, where social housing accounts for three per cent of all dwellings, below the national average of 4.2 per cent and the OECD average of 7 per cent.

“Public housing serves as foundational social infrastructure that treats shelter as an essential human right,” said Robinson.

“By offering rents permanently capped to income and delivering unmatched security of tenure, it provides an unconditional safety net for society’s most vulnerable households.”

Office’s work has gained political attention. In November 2023, the Victorian Legislative Council debated the merits of the RRR approach, with the council’s Legal and Social Issues Committee urging the government to reconsider demolition and explore all refurbishment options.

The team can already claim a small victory. At Ascot Valley Estate, the first RRR case study released by Office, the development was quietly removed from the planned demolitions list on the Homes Victoria website and part of the estate has since been refurbished.

The estate is a collection of 57 three-storey brick housing blocks, built in 1947 and designed by renowned modernist architect and public housing advocate Best Overend during his time with the Housing Commission.

This gave Robinson and his team hope that the government would consider the alternatives if presented with the option.

“We have presented to Homes Victoria, we’ve presented to different ministers as well, and we’ve kind of heard that they are starting to look at retrofit some of their existing building stock,” said Robinson.

“It kind of suggests that perhaps they’re gonna prefer we show all of them. So we released the following studies on Barak Beacon Estate and the Flemington Estate.”

However, Barak Beacon Estate has since been demolished, and the construction of the redevelopment is due to complete by the end of the year.

Now the fate of Flemington Tower awaits a ruling from the High Court of Australia following a hearing last month.

A class action filed by Inner Melbourne Community Legal on behalf of public housing residents in North Melbourne and Flemington is on its last appeal, after the Supreme Court of Victoria and Victorian Court of Appeal ruled in favour of demolition.

Meanwhile, the Liberal opposition has pledged to pause the HRRP if it wins office at the upcoming state election.

Robinson hopes he and his team have done enough, and continue to raise awareness about the issue.

“We’ve gotten to a point where we feel we can’t just keep putting out these reports,” he said.

“The papers we produced are quite dense and not for the general public. So what we’re now doing is translating that work into a series of publications, putting it in bookshops, and making it more accessible for the general public.”

“We continue interviews with different residents, different advocates, different professionals, speaking with journalists and doing lectures. The idea is to bring awareness to what’s occurring, rather than us doing more reports.”

He is also calling for a frank discussion in the architecture community about the redevelopment projects, which many of Australia’s prominent studios are working on.

“All the big-name architects are working on these estate renewals, some have even won awards as exemplar projects for these architects to promote their practice in terms of doing public good,” said Robinson.

“It’s really hard to find anyone who’s willing to talk about why they’re doing it, or is it good, the displacement and environmental issues. We’re alone.”

Dezeen put Robinson’s claims to the Victorian state government, but was told it could not respond in the lead-up to the election.

The FDA granted accelerated approval to neladalkib, sold as Nelkarmi, for ALK-positive non-small cell lung cancer

GSK won accelerated approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration on Tuesday for neladalkib, a lung cancer drug sold as Nelkarmi.

The FDA cleared the kinase inhibitor for adults with locally advanced or metastatic anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)-positive non-small cell lung cancer. Eligible patients have received one prior ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitor consisting of alectinib, or two or more prior ALK inhibitors and platinum-based chemotherapy, the agency said.

GSK gained neladalkib through its $10.6 billion acquisition of Nuvalent, according to Bloomberg. The deal closed in July, according to Reuters.

The acquisition also brought Jideytro, which the FDA approved on July 22 for adults with locally advanced or metastatic ROS1-positive non-small cell lung cancer who have received at least one prior ROS1 inhibitor. Nelkarmi is the second of the two lung cancer drugs from the deal to win approval, according to Reuters.

The FDA granted the approval on an accelerated basis because an early-stage trial found notable anti-cancer effects and a safety profile resembling that of current ALK-targeting drugs, according to Bloomberg. A later-phase trial meant to establish how much the drug ultimately helps patients is still ongoing, Bloomberg said.

The approval gives patients who have run through the available targeted therapies another option, according to Reuters. For those patients, the principal fallback has frequently been chemotherapy.

"I think we're making great progress with the oncology portfolio," Hesham Abdullah, GSK's head of oncology R&D, said in a briefing, according to Bloomberg. He said GSK is assembling a franchise of lung cancer drugs, and that it has additional medicines in development for blood and gastrointestinal cancers.

GSK reported higher third-quarter sales and core operating profit last month, with oncology sales up 23% to £600 million ($786 million). CEO Luke Miels cited Jideytro among three new U.S. specialty medicine approvals in recent months, alongside Hibsago for chronic hepatitis B and Jemperli for rectal cancer.

Oncology is a business GSK exited a decade ago, according to Reuters. Miels is leading its return to the field, and the Nuvalent purchase marks the boldest step GSK has taken so far toward re-entering oncology.

The CFTC has stayed certification of some Kalshi mention contracts, citing possible manipulation, two months after a staff advisory

Kalshi halted trading on several contracts centered on President Donald Trump's remarks late last week after the Commodity Futures Trading Commission asked it to, according to CNBC.

The paused contracts invite traders to bet on Trump's use of nicknames ahead of 2027, his word choices in November, and the countries and companies he mentions before this month closes, according to CNBC.

Brooke Nethercott, a CFTC spokesperson, told CNBC the agency has put a hold on certifying several of the mention markets Kalshi has proposed. She pointed to a contract on which key words Trump would utter ahead of a set deadline, one that Kalshi self-certified in February 2025.

"The CFTC stayed the certification of these contracts because they may be susceptible to manipulation, which is consistent with the staff advisory the CFTC put out two months ago," Nethercott said, according to CNBC.

Staff of the CFTC's Division of Market Oversight issued the advisory on Sept. 22. It covers contracts that settle on whether an individual will say certain words, attend an event or interact with another person. The staff said they may view such contracts as presumptively readily susceptible to manipulation and expect exchanges to make a heightened showing to list them. The advisory states it represents only the views of staff and does not create new obligations.

The advisory says those closest to the outcome often have advance knowledge, such as access to scripts or prepared remarks, and can be targeted by others seeking to influence the result.

Kalshi and the White House did not respond to CNBC's requests for comment.

The CFTC was looking into mention markets internally in August, according to CNBC. The agency asked Kalshi to pull its mention markets tied to sports; CNBC describes the company as the sole U.S.-regulated exchange that lists this kind of contract. Kalshi complied.

A mention market tied to Trump came under scrutiny in July, when a White House teleprompter operator was found to have traded on the president's comments, according to CNBC. In a settlement, the CFTC ordered the operator to stop trading for three years and pay a penalty of more than $172,000.

A mention market on what Vice President JD Vance will say at the New York Times DealBook Summit on Wednesday was active as of Tuesday morning, according to CNBC.

Cologne Auction House Moves Forward with Sale of Gothic Paintings as Jewish Dealer’s Heirs Claim They Were ‘Stolen’

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Cologne-based Lempertz auction house said it still planned to sell two works by Gothic painter Johann Koerbecke after the heirs of a German Jewish art dealer who was persecuted by the Nazis claimed the pieces were “stolen.”

Dealer Max Stern held the paintings in Düsseldorf between 1936 and 1937, the year the Nazis shut down his gallery. Per a provenance supplied by Lempertz, both paintings were then held by Düsseldorf’s Galerie Hans Bammann, which appears to have then sold them. On Monday, the Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation issued a release in which it called on Lempertz to stop the sale of the two works planned for Saturday. According to the foundation, such a sale should not occur “until two distinct ownership claims of different victims are justly resolved according to the Washington Principles on Nazi-Confiscated Art.”

Both paintings—The Annunciation and The Vision of Saint Bernard—are likely from Marienfeld high altarpiece, which was completed in 1457. They are being auctioned in a sale of Old Masters art. They carry high estimates of around 300,000 euros each, or about $329,000 apiece, making them the auction’s most expensive works. “For art historical reasons,” the Lempertz lot entries read, “the two important panels (Lots 1005 and 1006) will first be offered individually and then together.”

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation pointed out that both works appear in several databases of missing artworks, including the Interpol Database of Stolen Art since 2021. On the German Lost Art Database, both are reported as “Nazi-confiscated property”; the foundation registered that claim with the organization in 2018.

The foundation has demanded the return of multiple artworks belonging to Stern over the past decade, and in some cases, it has been successful. In 2017, for example, the FBI returned a painting by Jan Franse Verzijl that had been on view at an US art fair two years earlier.

Potentially complicating the matter is the fact that, prior to Stern’s possession of the Koerbecke paintings, they were owned by Ellen Funke, who, during the Nazi era, was classified as a Mischling, or “mongrel,” because she had Jewish ancestry through her mother. She sold her collection in 1936 to fund her emigration from Germany, and works she once owned have also been the subject of restitution claims—two of which happen to involve the Koerbecke works.

In 2025, Bavaria’s state museums restituted Koerbecke’s Vision of Saint Bernard to Funke’s heirs. At the time, the Bayerische Staatsgemäldesammlungen noted that The Vision of Saint Bernard and The Annunciation were at one point part of a single work, with both images appearing on either side of the same panel, and that Stern had taken them apart with the hope of selling them both. Funke’s heirs are listed as the sellers of The Vision of Saint Bernard on the auction house’s website.

Similarly, Funke’s heirs are also listed as the sellers of the The Annunciation, which entered the Kolumba Art Museum of the Archdiocese of Cologne as a donation in 1996. The museum restituted the work in 2025.

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation’s release notably does not mention Funke by name. But it does state that the foundation “maintains that the rights and histories of both Jewish victims must be taken into account, and neither claim should be privileged at the expense of the other.” In an email to ARTnews, a spokesperson for the foundation said “the museums restituted the paintings to Ellen Funke’s heirs in accordance with the priority principle set forth in the guidelines established in Germany to apply the Washington Principles,” adding that that restitution ”did not result in a ‘just and fair solution’ in accordance with the Washington Principles.”

In a statement to ARTnews, Lempertz said it would not cancel its planned sale of the paintings by Koerbecke, who is widely considered one of the great painters of the Gothic era in Germany.

“We adhere to current restitution practices and follow the recommendations of the Federal Government,” a representative for the auction house said, claiming that the Stern “had the panels sawn in two” and went on to sell one of them for “double the price.”

“These magnificent paintings were not taken away from Max Stern,” the Lempertz representative said, adding, “Max Stern received compensation after the war; the heirs of the Funke family did not.”

The Stern Foundation spokesperson said the “view that the paintings were not ‘seized’ from Max Stern has long been refuted,” citing a 2019 statement from the now-defunct German Advisory Commission that the persecution of Jewish art dealers began at latest by August 1933, “so that from that point on at the latest, it can in no way be assumed that ‘proper, customary business transactions’ took place.” The spokesperson added that the claim that Max Stern was compensated for the paintings is “incorrect“ and has “no legal basis.”

Lempertz did not dispute the listings for both paintings on the Art Loss Register, but the representative also noted that the listings contain details of the works’ respective restitutions.

What We Learned When Sabastian Sawe Finally Broke the Two-Hour Marathon

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In the early 2010s, the idea of breaking two hours in the marathon went from pipe dream to attainable goal. For the first decade of the new millennium, the world’s top distance runners had been chipping away at it. Paul Tergat first breached the 2:05 barrier with his 2:04:55 mark at the 2003 Berlin Marathon. Haile Gebrselassie ran 2:03:59 at the same race in 2008, and by 2014, Dennis Kimmetto held the record with a 2:02:57, less than seven seconds per mile away from sub-two.

Like the four-minute mile, first toppled by Roger Bannister in Oxford, England, in 1954, the two-hour marathon is an enticing target. It’s an even number in a popular event. It’s easy to describe, and therefore easy to market. Perhaps that’s why Nike decided to pour millions of dollars into their Breaking2 project, starting in 2016, bringing Eliud Kipchoge within 30 seconds of the barrier on May 6, 2017, in an exhibition race on the F1 track in Monza, Italy.

After that much-watched effort, Kipchoge became the torchbearer for breaking two hours. His next attempt in 2019 came with a perfectly flat looped course, a roster of pacers blocking the wind, controlled nutrition on a controlled course, and a finishing time of 1:59:40. Kipchoge showed the world that sub-two was possible. He made history. But he didn’t do it in a race.

It would take a special set of circumstances to see the result in a record-eligible setting. An elite athlete would need the right training, the right weather conditions, the right competition. The previous nine times the world record had been set, these circumstances came together in a fall race (Berlin eight times, Chicago once). But at the 2026 London Marathon on April 26, the stage was set. Sabastian Sawe had returned to defend his title, lining up alongside 2025 Chicago Marathon champion Jacob Kiplimo and Yomif Kejelcha, an accomplished track runner making his marathon debut.

On a clear, calm day, the three runners followed pacers through the halfway mark in 60:29. To drop below two hours, they would need to run the second half at least a minute faster, cutting the pace by at least five seconds per mile from their average of 4:37. By mile 18, Kiplimo was falling off the back, leaving just Kejelcha stubbornly hanging on to the shoulder of a hard-charging Sawe. Sawe continued ratcheting the pace down, running the eighth 5K of the race (from approximately mile 21 to 24) in a blistering 13:42—4:25 per mile. But Kejelcha hung on, staying on Sawe’s shoulder until the final four minutes.

When he crossed the line in a time of 1:59:30, Sawe became the first runner to break two hours in a record-eligible marathon. Eleven seconds later, Kejelcha became the second. Kiplimo’s third place time of 2:00:28 was also faster than the previous world record. The two-hour barrier wasn’t broken by an exceptional individual in a one-off performance. Ultimately, a true race with fierce competition and constant pressure was what it took to bring Sawe into the history books.

Inside the rise and evolution of Ohio State's club football team

It was a 90-degree September evening in Columbus, and the setting sun was relentlessly beating down on the Lincoln Tower Park recreational fields next to Ohio Stadium. Unlike the Buckeyes who play in Ohio Stadium on Saturdays, though, nobody there was getting paid. The athletes weren't getting NIL money. They weren't on athletic scholarships. The coaches were volunteers.

What drives the Ohio State club football team is simply a love of football.

"I think it's what this program should have always been," coach James Grega Jr. said before practice. "I hope we haven't peaked."

Former and current players will tell you that Grega, who spends around 30 hours a week outside of his day job working with the club team, is a major reason for that success. By day, Grega is a content specialist at OSU's College of Arts and Sciences. In his free time, he has poured his energy into Ohio State club football, which has dominated its 15-team league, the National Club Football Association, with four championships in the past six seasons.

During the Division I varsity team's bye week, the club team went viral for beating Wright State 105-0. It set NCFA records for most points and widest margin of victory. The previous points record was 91, set by the Buckeyes two years earlier. On Nov. 15, the club team won 65-8 over Oakland to get to an 8-0 record and advance to an eighth straight conference championship.

Along with their success and massive blowout victories, six players since 2020 have gone from the Ohio State club team to the Division I team as walk ons.

Born and raised in Chicago, Illinois, Kellyn Gerenstein played quarterback from age 6 through high school. He considered a couple of Division II offers before deciding to pursue academics over athletics, enrolling in Ohio State's engineering program.

When he arrived at OSU in 2018, one of the constants in his life − playing organized football − vanished. He went through the motions at school but felt like he lacked structure. The pressure mounted. At one point, Gerenstein considered leaving Ohio State.

To kill time, Gerenstein would take a duffel bag filled with his high school football cleats and a couple of footballs from home and walk to Lincoln Tower Park late at night. He practiced throwing footballs into the available soccer nets.

"It was the only way for me to stay sane," Gerenstein said.

The kicker for the club football team was playing pickup soccer and saw Gerenstein whipping footballs around. He told Gerenstein the team needed a quarterback. Admittedly skeptical, Gerenstein showed up to the first practice and met Grega, who was embarking on his first year as coach.

Grega played on the club football team in 2012 and became the starter in 2013. Instead of playing the next year, he became the assistant sports editor of The Lantern and pursued journalism. After graduating, Grega missed being on the sideline. He had done some coaching at Westerville South High School while bouncing around writing for various sports publications.

OSU's club players reached out for Grega, hoping he would come coach them. Not only did he agree to this, but he developed a plan to reform the team's structure.

"It was very bare bones," Grega said. "So we've kind of built it. When we took over in 2018, unfortunately, the program was on the verge of not collapsing, but folding."

Grega spent time with the club sports office to ensure the team had field reservations in-season and at Ohio State's indoor recreation center. With a background in marketing and communications, Grega created a website for recruiting purposes and attempted to bolster the team's social media presence.

Along with structure, Grega wanted to instill a culture of accountability.

"It's kind of like any other team," Gerenstein said. "Like, guys have talent, but if one guy shows up more and works harder than you, we'll win with that guy. And for the most part, we didn't really run into that. All the culture and the demand changed."

"It felt like high school ball, just playing with your friends, enjoying practice, enjoying the game, enjoying every part of football that I grew up loving," Booker said. "That was something I lacked that I got back with the club team."

"It was very emotional when I was able to get back on the field again," Booker said. "It was kind of second nature for me to get back out on the field and to be able to have him in spirit with me out there."

Many leaders decide alone which days staff come into the office. Fewer people quit when managers also ask when their workday should end

Some of the country's largest companies have spent the past few years ordering people back to their desks. At the end of 2024, Amazon $AMZN's chief executive, Andy Jassy, told corporate staff to come in five days a week. In the throes of the COVID-19 pandemic, Goldman Sachs $GS CEO David Solomon said working from home was "an aberration that we're going to correct as soon as possible." Many workers protested. But both men were determined to have employees working the same way they had before the pandemic.

Millions of office workers spent more than a year working from home. Many of them stopped working at the same hour every day and stopped answering messages late at night. Many more found meaning in their jobs. Once they were ordered back to the office, though, they had to decide whether they could maintain the same schedule without looking like they were slacking off.

Employees put up with return-to-office mandates if their manager accepts the schedule they built at home, according to Harvard Business School's Grace Mele-Cormier and colleagues. The problem is less about the time they have to come in and more about settling what time people can leave.

Mele-Cormier and his team found that employees tend to quit less often and earn slightly higher performance reviews when they're allowed to maintain the same working schedules as before. But many employees still fear that their boss will think they care less about the job if they go home at the same hour every day. They become less tense once they know their manager is on board, according to HBR's researchers.

The problem is that many managers never tell employees which hours they're expected to work. Their silence on the topic forces staff to assume they have to continue answering emails and messages at all hours of the day and night, just as they had done before the pandemic. Some employees end up leaving their roles for ones that have a clearer schedule.

Executives see the resignations, but they rarely learn that employees quit over their working hours. To executives, some teams seem to lose people for no reason while other teams don't.

Many managers never ask employees what time they need to leave each day. They assume people coming back to the office want one fixed schedule from their boss, with the same start and end time for everyone. If a manager asks each person what hours they need, the team ends up with different schedules for different people, and many managers worry they'll lose control of the team's day.

They also fear their staff will think the boss has no plan for how the team should work. Asking, though, actually leaves a manager with a better plan, according to Dan Cable, a London Business School professor. By hearing employees out, managers can spot the people most likely to quit over their working hours while there is still time to change their schedules. But Cable warns against giving employees decision-making powers. "You get a voice, not a vote," Cable told an audience at the school. "Listening shapes better decisions. It does not remove the leader's responsibility to decide."

Acting on what employees say doesn't always mean giving them what they asked for. That applies in almost every situation. After Jennifer Dulski and her leadership team laid off employees at Change.org, Dulski, then its president and chief operating officer, met with the remaining employees in several countries, according to the Society for Human Resource Management. She couldn't bring back the jobs the company cut. So her team started showing employees the company's finances instead. Once employees saw the numbers, Dulski said, they understood why their leaders had cut staff.

Other leaders change a plan or cover a cost after employees speak up. Geno Cutolo spent his first 90 days as president of staffing firm Adecco North America meeting employees across the business. He then redesigned a reorganization Adecco had already planned, to try to remove many of the frustrations and obstacles employees described to him. "It's important to act on what you hear," Cutolo said.

At Safeguard Global, the HR team heard that European employees were worried about winter heating bills, so the company paid them a monthly stipend through the winter, chief people officer Katherine Loranger said. In each case, employees could point to something that changed because they spoke up.

Employees expect the same proof after a manager asks about their hours. A manager can approve working hours, move team meetings to times everyone can make, or explain why a schedule can't change. If a manager asks and then changes and explains nothing, employees conclude the manager never had any intention to listen in the first place. Employees then stop telling managers what they need to know, and managers can't possibly tell which employees are thinking about leaving until they hand in their notice.

The Homeownership Gap Between Young and Old Americans Has Doubled—These Cities Reveal Why

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The homeownership gap between younger and older Americans has more than doubled over the past four decades, with some of the nation's most expensive cities seeing the sharpest increases, according to a new report.

The analysis, from the Urban Institute, finds that nationally just 43% of households headed by adults aged 25 to 35 owned their homes in 2024, compared with 79% of those headed by adults 65 and older. The 35.8-percentage-point difference is more than twice the 17.5-point gap recorded in 1980.

But that divide pales in comparison to the gaping chasm in some of America's most expensive metropolitan areas. Between 1980 and 2024, the homeownership gap grew by 29 percentage points in Denver, 23 points in New York City, and 22 points in San Francisco, compared with 18.3 points nationally.

But those widening gaps also expose a deeper economic trade-off: The cities offering young Americans the greatest opportunities to earn more may be the very places preventing them from building wealth through homeownership

"The cities with the strongest labor markets tend to be the least affordable, so some will take the better job and rent indefinitely, giving up equity accumulation," explains Hannah Jones, senior economist at Realtor.com®. "Others will pass up higher-paying roles or move to cheaper, lower-opportunity markets in order to buy."

In 1980, younger households in Denver were nearly as likely as their older counterparts to own a home.

At the time, 55% of households headed by adults aged 25 to 35 owned their homes, compared with 67% of households headed by adults 65 and older.

By 2024, the younger group's homeownership rate had fallen to just 37%, while the older group's rate climbed to 78%, expanding the divide from 12 to 41 percentage points.

But the decline in young homeownership may reflect something besides worsening affordability: Denver's success in attracting young workers.

"Two things are likely at work. One is the affordability decline itself. The other is Denver’s growth as an economic hub that appeals to young workers, many of whom arrive as renters," says Jones. "That adds renters to the mix and dilutes the young homeownership rate, even for people who never intended to buy."

There, younger households were already far less likely to own homes in 1980, when their homeownership rate stood at 28%. By 2024, it had slipped only slightly, to 26%.

Older New Yorkers, meanwhile, saw their homeownership rate climb from approximately 43% to 63% over the same period.

"In New York, homeownership has always been expensive and out of reach for most young households, so there was little room to fall," Jones explains. "Young homeownership slipped only slightly, which suggests the current rate is close to a floor."

She suggests low COVID-19 pandemic-era mortgage rates may have helped some older households that had been waiting to purchase finally enter the market.

And regardless of how the divide developed, entering homeownership later can carry lasting financial consequences.

Separate research from Realtor.com, co-authored by Jones, found that purchasing a home by age 30 was associated with 22.5% higher net worth—approximately $119,000 more—by age 50, compared with buying in one's 40s, even after accounting for income, education, and marital status.

Those advantages can extend across generations, too. Children raised in homeowner households were 18.4 percentage points more likely to become homeowners themselves by age 35, the same research found.

One of the Urban Institute's most striking findings concerns a milestone that comes before homeownership: establishing an independent household.

In 1980, young adults in high-cost metropolitan areas were slightly more likely than their counterparts nationally to head their own households. By 2024, that relationship had reversed.

Just 41% of young adults in expensive markets were household heads, the lowest share among the study's housing cost categories.

Instead, more are living with parents, other relatives, or roommates. Research from Realtor.com backs this up, finding that a third of young adults lived with their parents across the country in 2025.

Yet expensive cities haven't lost their appeal. The share of young households living in high-cost markets actually edged up from 28% in 1980 to 31% in 2024, partly reflecting a broader shift toward urban areas.

What's changed is their housing tenure. In those markets, the homeownership rate among younger households fell from 40% to 32% over the same period.

So young adults are continuing to pursue opportunities in expensive cities, but fewer are establishing households of their own once they get there. And another finding suggests that rapidly rising prices may be changing the age composition of metropolitan areas, regardless of how expensive those places were to begin with.

Between 2014 and 2024, metropolitan areas experiencing faster home price appreciation generally saw declines in young adults' share of the population, the Urban Institute found.

The relationship held across high-, moderate-, and low-cost markets. But Jones cautions that the findings don't necessarily mean young people are being priced out.

"That can come from young people leaving, older people moving in, or both," she says. "Florida metros on the included chart may be an example. Their older populations are large and grew significantly during the pandemic, which could erode the young share on its own."

Indeed, the relationship between home prices and demographic changes could run in either direction.

"Young people may be fleeing places where costs are climbing, or older, more financially equipped buyers may be moving in and competing for homes," Jones says. "That pushes prices up, and the newcomers then make up a larger share of the population."

Determining whether younger residents are actually leaving would require examining changes in the number of young adults, not just their share of the population.

Still, other economic research suggests that housing costs can constrain where workers pursue employment.

A May 2025 analysis from the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond found that geographic mobility has declined across all age groups since the 1990s. Among the contributing factors, researchers identified restrictions on housing supply in economically productive cities, where high costs can discourage workers from relocating to pursue better opportunities.

The researchers also identified another potential obstacle: Older homeowners have become less responsive to rising local housing costs, making them more likely to stay put even as prices climb. In supply-constrained markets, that can make it harder for newcomers to move in.

Meanwhile, a February 2026 analysis from the St. Louis Fed found that home prices had risen approximately 207% from 2000 to 2024, outpacing the roughly 155% increase in per-capita incomes.

Together, the findings suggest that the costs of entering high-opportunity housing markets could affect younger Americans' earnings prospects as well as their ability to build wealth.

There may be a way around that trade-off for some.

"Remote work may be loosening the link between where people live and where the jobs are, which could enable more households to achieve homeownership without giving up job opportunities," Jones says.

But that flexibility is not available to everyone. Workers whose jobs require them to be physically present may face a more difficult choice between buying a home where they can afford one and pursuing careers in the cities where their skills command the highest wages.

And unlike the wealth accumulated by longtime homeowners, the career opportunities they pass up may be difficult to recover later.

This article was first reported by The Examination, a nonprofit newsroom that investigates global health threats.

The latest product from the tobacco industry is a flavorful nicotine tablet that melts in your mouth, allowing users to get a buzz as discreetly as popping a mint — and amping up concerns that products to replace cigarettes are too appealing to kids and teens.

In the United Kingdom and Switzerland, Philip Morris International recently introduced Zyn Melts, mint-flavored tablets that provide a nicotine hit as quickly as smoking a cigarette. Japan Tobacco International launched a similar product called Perlz in the U.K. in May.

And in the U.S., Nic Nac Naturals, a nascent nicotine company based in Spokane, Wash., has been selling flavored lozenges called Nic Nac since 2022; they’re now available in about 3,000 stores nationwide.

Users tuck these tablets next to their gums, similar to how they use nicotine pouches. Like pouches, the new products come in colorful packaging and flavors such as mint and berry. But with tablets, there’s no wet sachet to discard.

That makes them more appealing than pouches, said Erwin Henriquez, a global analyst for the marketing research firm Euromonitor. “It’s much more friendly to chew gum or a dissolving tablet than to put something in your gums,” he said. “You have vitamins and supplements that consumers are already used to.”

Public health advocates and researchers are concerned about nicotine tablets for a similar reason. Their resemblance to mints or lozenges “may make them appear harmless and more appealing to children and adolescents,” said Magnus Lundbäck, a cardiologist, nicotine researcher and professor at the Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm.

Tobacco companies have introduced e-cigarettes, heated tobacco, and nicotine pouches as less harmful alternatives to cigarettes. Tobacco-related diseases are the leading preventable cause of death worldwide, responsible for 7 million deaths a year.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration, which influences tobacco policy worldwide, has authorized these products because they’re less harmful than cigarettes and could help smokers quit.

But nicotine is tied to health problems even when it’s not inhaled through smoke, and the long-term health risks of these new products aren’t clear. Public health advocates say there’s little evidence that smokers are switching to pouches and heated tobacco entirely. More often, they say, people use those products in addition to cigarettes.

“The tobacco and nicotine industry is continuously developing new products that may attract new generations of users, rather than reducing nicotine dependence,” Lundbäck said. “The ultimate public health goal must be fewer people addicted to nicotine, not simply more ways to consume it.”

Henriquez, the Euromonitor analyst, said dissolvable tablets are a “natural evolution” of nicotine pouches and serve consumers’ growing appetite for stimulants as they “feel stress all the time.”

Nicotine pouch sales reached $9.2 billion globally in 2025, according to Euromonitor. The volume of pouches sold has grown 50% annually for the past five years and is expected to continue at 20.5% a year, according to a July report by Henriquez.

On its U.K. site, Philip Morris International tells consumers that Zyn Melts “offer a completely new way for adult users to enjoy tobacco-free and smoke-free nicotine moments on the go.” It compares tablets to pouches, saying tablets are for a “fast nicotine experience” while pouches have a “longer lasting nicotine and flavor experience.”

Japan Tobacco sells Perlz through Flavour Warehouse, a U.K. vaping company it controls. Its website states, “First came vapes. Then pouches. Now there’s Perlz — the ingenious new nicotine experience for existing adult nicotine users.”

Tablets contain less nicotine than most pouches, with Zyn Melts and Perlz available in 1 and 2 milligram doses.

Nic Nac lozenges are available in 3 and 6 mg doses. Nic Nac Naturals co-founder and Chief Executive Officer Nicco Magnotto said the lozenges are different from Zyn Melts and Perlz because they release nicotine over about 30 minutes, more like a pouch.

The most popular nicotine pouches, in comparison, range from 3 to 17 mg. Philip Morris International recently introduced a 1.5 mg pouch for users “new to nicotine pouches.”

Public health officials have been engaged in a yearslong balancing act with new nicotine products, weighing the potential benefits to adults looking for less harmful alternatives to cigarettes against the potential for youth to abuse or accidentally ingest nicotine.

Vapes exploded in popularity in 2018, causing the FDA to declare teen use an epidemic. Then nicotine pouches caught on, with surveys showing increased use among teens over the past few years. The industry, however, says the percentage of youth using pouches remains low.

Meanwhile, poison control centers in the U.S. reported that calls involving nicotine poisoning, many involving pouches, nearly doubled from 2024 to 2025. Three-quarters of cases last year involved children under the age of 6.

Nicotine tablets represent a new area of concern.

The industry says it’s not looking to sell to kids. In an email, a Japan Tobacco spokesperson said the company does not “design, market or promote Perlz to appeal” to minors and that the containers are child-resistant, requiring three buttons to be pressed simultaneously to be opened.

“We take steps to ensure that our advertising is directed towards adult tobacco and nicotine users only and does not have particular appeal to minors,” the spokesperson said.

Magnotto said his company does not advertise or market Nic Nac to youth and that the company’s research indicates they don’t find lozenges appealing. “It comes across as medicinal,” he said. “Pouches are the rage. Kids aren’t even aware of us.”

Asked about the potential for abuse by kids, Magnotto said his company’s container has a scientifically tested, child-resistent seal and will soon have a more secure mechanism.

Phillip Morris International did not respond to a request for comment. The company says on its U.K. website that Melts are meant for adults.

The U.K. doesn’t require regulatory approval for nicotine products, making it an ideal test market. The tablets were introduced shortly before regulations imposed by the Tobacco and Vapes Act 2026 became effective in October. That law imposes stricter rules on tobacco and nicotine products, including prohibitions on giveaways and big discounts.

Philip Morris International is looking beyond the U.K. and Switzerland. It has filed for trademark protections for Zyn Melts in the U.S. and Europe, both as a consumer nicotine product and a pharmaceutical aid to stop smoking.

The trademarks are among many the company has registered this year for Zyn, including “House of Zyn” and “Zyn Club” — signs that the company is positioning Zyn as a lifestyle brand.

Nicotine tablets have been available in the U.S. for years as smoking cessation aids, which requires a special FDA approval as a pharmaceutical product. The agency would need to do a separate review in order to authorize the sale of tablets to consumers.

Nic Nac Naturals submitted an application in 2023 to sell its nine mint and fruit-flavored tablets to the general public in the U.S.

Mitch Zeller, former director of the FDA’s Center for Tobacco Products, said nicotine tablets “could possibly be helpful” to smokers looking for alternatives in a “properly regulated market.”

First, he said, the product needs to undergo a complete scientific review, with studies showing how different users respond to it. He noted that a new FDA rule allows nicotine products to be sold while their application is pending, which means Zyn Melts and Perlz could be on shelves without any research on their appeal to adolescents.

That’s not how it should work, he said: “All of the intended and unintended consequences of something like this need to be sorted out on a pre-market basis.”

The Great American Recovery Initiative, co-chaired by Kennedy and Kathryn Burgum, extols the power of faith. Treatment and policy experts largely agree that faith communities — and a belief in something bigger than oneself — can play an important role in a person’s recovery. But some are concerned that federal grants will be directed to faith-based initiatives that don’t provide evidence-based treatment.

Sheepgate, for example, explicitly prohibits those who join its yearlong programs from using methadone, a drug approved by the Food and Drug Administration for opioid use disorder. They can only use another addiction medication, Suboxone, if they’ll be fully tapered off of it within the first three months, according to the center’s student guidelines — a quick timeline that doctors generally recommend against. It’s not alone: Research has found that faith-based treatment centers are less likely than secular ones to offer medications for opioid use disorder, and more likely to refuse clients who may be taking them.

Refusing to accept people on FDA-approved medications “is so irresponsible,” said Ayana Jordan, an addiction psychiatrist at NYU Langone who works with faith organizations on recovery efforts. “I would not want to provide federal funds to that type of recovery center because they’re actively working against things that we know can save lives.”

But Michael Hunsberger, Sheepgate’s regional president, said the issue isn’t so black and white. Sheepgate would accept people taking addiction medications, he told STAT, if the center received more funding and was able to build out the medical staff. He argued that faith-based organizations are often excluded from public funds regardless of the level of care they provide.

“We could literally allow multiple of those prescriptions and bring on different medical staff and still be excluded from certain dollars due to our faith-based beliefs,” Hunsberger said.

Medications for opioid use disorder are just a few on a long list of drugs that Sheepgate doesn’t have the capacity to distribute or monitor the use of, Hunsberger said. When somebody isn’t a good fit for Sheepgate due to any medication use, the staff tries to find another organization where they could seek help.

While the Trump administration has continued to broadly support medications for substance use disorder — which can be highly effective at preventing overdose deaths — Kennedy has repeatedly cast doubt on existing systems for treating addiction. He’s also expressed support for unconventional (and scientifically dubious) approaches to treatment, including “wellness farms” where people reside in nature and engage in manual labor. Kennedy’s own path to long-term recovery from drugs and alcohol leaned strongly on groups like Alcoholics Anonymous and his Catholic faith.

But what worked for Kennedy won’t work for everyone, experts say. As Trump’s tax-cut bill goes into effect and more people lose health coverage, “what really concerns me is that there seems to be an expectation from the Trump administration that these faith-based groups are going to fill those gaps,” said Libby Jones, associate vice president of the Global Health Advocacy Incubator’s Overdose Prevention Initiative.

The administration has not made official regulatory changes to the federal grant process, but it has gestured that the bar may be lowered for faith-based groups, Jones said. A “Dear Colleague” letter from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration stressed that faith-based groups should receive “equal treatment” in the grants process. The Justice Department has deemed unconstitutional Biden administration regulations that aimed to prohibit the use of federal funds for “explicitly religious activities” and protect people from discrimination on the basis of faith.

Experts believe there should be clear stipulations that faith-based organizations receiving federal funding follow evidence-based care, and that federal agencies should ensure that funding goes to a diverse array of religious groups, not only to Christian organizations.

“I am always heartened to know that there is money that is being put into faith-based institutions,” Jordan said. “But we have an obligation to taxpayers — this is taxpayers’ money — to say what actually works.”

Sheepgate would welcome any oversight and regulations that may come with federal funding, Hunsberger said, as long as it does not interfere with the organization’s core identity.

“If those federal dollars change our identity as a faith-based organization, then that would change us accepting those dollars,” Hunsberger said. But, “the faith community — the majority of it — is not against the medical side. It’s just, a lot of times, we’re pitted against each other.”

The Department of Health and Human Services did not respond directly to STAT’s questions about how it may assess the efficacy of faith-based recovery organizations for future funding opportunities. “For generations, these organizations have provided hope, accountability, mentorship, housing, workforce opportunities, and the relationships that help people sustain long-term recovery, often with limited resources and little government support,” HHS deputy press secretary Lizzie Harvey said in a statement.

This year, HHS has given supplemental funding awards of $500,000 each to Alabama, Kansas, and West Virginia to pursue faith-based prevention work. Kennedy has also said that SAMHSA’s STREETS grants — which send $12 million over four years to communities in support of work on the intersection of homelessness and addiction — will also have a “new emphasis on faith-based recovery,” according to the Iowa Capitol Dispatch.

Both of those grant notices included language directing recipients to employ evidence-based practices. But another “Dear Colleague” letter written by SAMHSA leadership earlier this year warned against relying on medications alone for addiction treatment, so as to avoid what it calls “a default sentence to life-long medication use.” That guidance, which has now been baked into SAMHSA’s terms and conditions for all grant recipients, created some confusion about the agency’s support of FDA-approved medications.

“There’s an ambiguity here that just needs to be clearer,” Jordan said, adding that the administration appears to be focusing largely on white, Christian organizations.

“The methods by which we define ‘faith-based’ and ‘evidence-based treatment’ need to be clear,” she said.

In the past four months, Kennedy and other HHS leaders have visited or hosted roundtables with at least half a dozen other Christian recovery centers. It’s unclear how many encourage or allow the use of FDA-approved medications for substance use disorders.

Without strict guidance from federal agencies, funding decisions may be left to states or more local jurisdictions. The Montgomery Alcohol, Drug Addiction and Mental Health Services (ADAMHS) Board, a county-level authority in Ohio, was tapped last month for one of eight federal STREETS grants. For years, the board has funded and coordinated with both faith-based and secular recovery homes, a housing agency, behavioral health facility, street outreach and medicine teams, and peer-support services.

The Montgomery ADAMHS board only funds recovery homes that are certified with the state, the board’s housing coordinator, Kaylyn Drodge, told STAT. That includes six Christian recovery homes and two secular ones. (They’re open to non-Christian faith groups, but have not received any such applications, Drodge said.)

Ohio law requires certified faith-based recovery homes to accept people with different religious backgrounds, as well as those who do not want to engage in any faith activities. But there are no requirements about accepting people on medications for opioid use disorder. Out of the six Christian recovery homes the board funds, two turn those people away.

Recovery homes in Ohio are not allowed to provide medical care, Drodge said. But in many places, those lines are blurred.

“For the general population, they are equating ‘recovery’ with ‘treatment,’” Jordan said. She believes organizations that don’t provide clinical treatment directly should still be eligible for federal funding, but the government should consider how they encourage or support patients to receive proven medical care.

Astronaut Loads 35mm Film Into Analog Camera While in Space

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Astronaut Loads 35mm Film Into Analog Camera While in Space

German astronaut Rabea Rogge flew on the Fram2 spaceflight mission in April 2025, bringing her trusty second-hand analog camera.

Rogge is an Arctic roboticist, currently studying for a PhD at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology in the Department of Marine Technology. She is also Germany’s first-ever female astronaut.

Last week, Rogge shared a video on Instagram as she loaded up a roll of 35mm film while onboard the SpaceX Crew Dragon Resilience spacecraft.

While many celluloid shooters have encountered difficulties loading film on terra firma, Rogge deftly loaded a roll of Kodak Portra 160 into the camera that she calls a “sturdy companion.”

A post shared by Rabea Rogge (@rabearogge)

“Analog pictures from space,” Rogge captions the post. “My old second-hand analog camera has been a sturdy companion on all my expeditions from under -20°C [-4°F] in the high Arctic to the humid climate at sea around the equator. On Fram2 it got to launch on a rocket as well – and it still lives on so far!”

PetaPixel identified Rogge’s camera as a Minolta X-700 with a 45mm lens attached. The X-700 was the flagship SLR for the now-defunct Japanese company for the best part of 20 years. In 1982, it won the European “Camera of the Year” award, and it is still popular in the second-hand market today.

With the film loaded in the camera, Rogge was afforded stunning views of Earth thanks to the Crew Dragon’s 360-degree cupola. At 2,000 square inches, it was the largest single window ever flown to space. Rogge, along with her crewmates Chun Wang, Jannicke Mikkelsen, and Eric Philips, were also the first people to ever orbit over Earth’s north and south poles.

“Can’t imagine the face of the people from the film lab when they scanned your roll,” comments Thibaut Everaerts on Instagram.

Rogge’s camera is far from the first Minolta to ever fly from space; in fact, the first American to ever orbit the Earth, John Glenn, took a Minolta Hi-Matic (repackaged as an Ansco Autoset) with him into space.

Despite buying the automatic camera for just $40 from a drugstore in Cocoa Beach, Florida, NASA engineers modified the camera with a pistol grip so that Glenn could use the camera while wearing his bulky astronaut gloves.

The photos that Glenn took in 1962 and their resulting popularity are credited with helping NASA realize how important it was to document Earth from space.

Why scammers try to keep you on the phone (and how to spot their tactics)

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If something feels off, hang up and call the company back on their official number.

That unexpected call from someone claiming to be from your bank can be worrisome, especially if they're calling to alert you about a suspicious money transfer. Your savings are at risk, but fret not, the caller who somehow got hold of your number says they can stop it — as long as you stay on the line.

But more than the emergency itself, their instruction to stay on call is the real catch. Scammers are counting on you to not hang up, as that valuable time helps them earn your trust. It then leads to turning up the pressure, which eventually leads you toward a decision you otherwise wouldn't take. That's when they might ask for a verification code, or straight up ask you to make a transfer. Keeping you occupied just long enough is the plan, so you don't end up calling the bank for a second opinion.

Dedicated scammers are known to stay on the line as the victims login to the accounts to transfer funds, buy gift cards or make a visit to the nearby ATM to withdraw cash. Some even go as far as to school you on what to tell bank employees when questioned about that odd transaction. When the person "helping" you protect your savings is deterring you from speaking to anyone else, that's a pretty obvious sign that something is off.

One possible way in could be a compromised bank account. A creative scammer could inform you about open accounts under your name or even scare you with an arrest warrant. The plan is to make you feel that following their instructions is the only way out, so hanging up doesn't feel like a plausible option.

Sometimes, the whole script includes more than just one scam artist. Back in August 2026, the FBI reported that scammers posing as bank employees told their victims that their information had been used to open an account to purchase illegal firearms. The scammers then transferred the victims to someone posing as an FBI agent, but disconnected after the victims were encouraged to verify the number.

Shortly after, they received a call displaying the FBI's Boston office number, which had been spoofed. These "FBI agents" could then ask the victims to continue the conversations using an encrypted messaging app. The FBI didn't specify how many people were targeted by this scheme.

But the threatening villain persona is not the only way these scammers are out to get you. Some of them act professional, stay patient and even go a step further to sound sympathetic. Now, when these friendly voices start to discourage you from reaching out to the organization directly or getting a second opinion, that should get your alarm bells ringing. After all, con artists don't want all of their efforts to result in failure.

Once the small talk is done, call handlers may switch to a dictating tone in order to get the outcome they want. This is a clear warning sign. When the caller demands you stay connected or instructs you to keep the conversation private, be sure to trust your instincts and end the call.

When they ask you to move funds to a "safer" account, it's time to hang up. Any request for passwords or even one-time verification codes should also be met with a call termination. Since scammers are often savvy enough to spoof authentic phone numbers, caller ID isn't all that reliable either.

Once you hang up or end that text conversation, reach out to the organization, but only using its official app or the number on your bank card or statement. Steer clear of contact numbers that the caller gives you, and trusting the customer-service numbers from sponsored search results isn't safe either. If it's a bit late to heed this advice and you've already shared sensitive information or worse transferred money to the scammer, contact your bank or payment provider right away.

Did you know your phone can filter out some of these unwanted calls? On iPhones running iOS 26 or newer, head to Settings > Apps > Phone > Screen Unknown Callers to enable extra incoming call checks, which include silencing unsaved numbers or asking callers to state who they are before they're connected.

If you own a supported Samsung Galaxy phone, try out spam filtering under Phone > Settings > Caller ID and spam protection, and Google Pixel owners can set up Call Screen using the Phone app's settings on supported devices in eligible regions. The downside is that legitimate callers can sometimes get caught by these filters.

Companies like Meta, Microsoft and Starlink have already worked to help take down over a million scam accounts. If you want to help save other unsuspecting consumers, it's best to report suspected scams to the FTC.

When U.S. President Donald Trump touches down in Shenzhen, China, this week for a three-way summit with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin, he will be embarking on the highest-level diplomacy he has ever undertaken at his weakest point in power.

The problem is not merely the devastating midterm election losses for his Republican Party, in which he urged supporters to turn out and cast their votes as if he were on the ballot. In recent weeks, Trump has displayed startling new levels of incoherence and evasion of responsibility for a sitting president. This seemed to reach a crescendo last week with two national crises for which Trump had no suitable answers.

The first of these crises brought news that Jensen Huang, the head of Nvidia, at once one of the world’s most valuable and strategically important companies, had had an affair with a suspected Chinese intelligence asset. Trump pleaded ignorance, denying well-sourced reports that he had been briefed about the matter as long ago as May, including by his national security advisor and secretary of state, Marco Rubio. The only thoughts the president seemed able to summon reflected credulity about Huang’s claim that he had never suspected the woman could have been a spy and personal sympathy for a man who had, until his sudden resignation from Nvidia last week, belonged to a club that means more to Trump than any other: the exclusive circle of the world’s richest and most powerful men. Perhaps for that reason, on Monday, Trump told reporters he “would not mind” if Huang was reinstated.

For all of Trump’s envy of powerful authoritarians, if the situations were reversed and Huang were a Chinese national leading a company as important as Nvidia, it is impossible to imagine Xi treating a man under such suspicions this way, including welcoming him on a flight to his country’s No. 1 competitor and adversary. But Trump’s need for this kind of validation is an ongoing matter and not something he has learned a lesson from. Witness his decision to invite the world’s richest man, Elon Musk, to join him on this trip, if not in the meetings with his fellow heads of state.

For a U.S. president heading off to a summit such as this, the week’s second crisis may have been even worse. The image the United States presented to the world in the wake of a breakdown in the nationwide air traffic control system on Friday was one of utter dishevelment and incompetence. It put the spotlight on two of arguably the least qualified members of a cabinet resolutely hostile to meritocracy: Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, a former congressman and Fox News personality, and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, a former congressman whose formal education culminated with an associate’s degree in plumbing and construction.

With thousands of flights canceled and a country desperate for answers and reassurance, they bumbled their way through an almost incoherent news conference that offered no real explanations. Instead, they cast blame on others, including Congress and past administrations, while exalting Trump’s leadership as if they were members of a personality cult and the public should be too. During the first 36 hours of the crisis, Trump for his part chose to lie low, making no statements about the matter, apparently fearing that doing so would mean taking responsibility for something bad that happened on his watch, which he has always been deeply allergic to.

These displays of unaccountable behavior and cluelessness are bad enough for the United States in their own right, but Americans should be worried about their implications for the upcoming summit. Even in ordinary times, Trump’s narcissism and emotional neediness make diplomacy like this dangerous because he craves fellowship and approval from men such as Putin and Xi, whom he all but openly idolizes. But with last week’s crises and domestic scandals sprouting in many directions as Democrats prepare to take over Congress, Trump is liable to make large and unwise concessions to his imagined peers in order to secure political coups that he hopes can restore prestige and momentum to his badly weakened presidency. The cliché holds that weakened or trapped animals are liable to strike out to defend themselves. With Trump, the danger is the opposite—given his self-centered view of the world, where his own ego gratification is paramount, he could give away the store.

What could that mean? Here, one feels that Trump is so easily flattered that the sky is almost the limit. He has reportedly already forsworn raising the Nvidia crisis with Xi as well as Russia’s ongoing war of aggression against Ukraine. If true, such self-imposed restrictions themselves emphasize the weakness of the U.S. president’s hand and preemptively vitiate the utility of such an unusual gathering.

What does this leave on the table for discussion? The Trump administration has said it hopes to make progress on limiting nuclear weapons armament at a time when the United States and Russia still have enormous legacy arsenals whose maintenance and modernization are costly and China is belatedly racing to catch up. Trump officials have also said they hope to engage leaders of the world’s other two big powers on the United States’ stop-and-go war against Iran, which by now has turned into a quagmire.

But what could the latter possibly yield? That Trump has already displayed weakness and enormous gullibility on Iran in his dealings with both powers does not bode well. Russia has supplied intelligence and munitions support to Iran throughout the war, enabling that country to target U.S. military bases throughout the Middle East with devastating effect. Consistent with his general enthrallment to Putin, Trump has publicly downplayed the importance of this, making clear that maintaining warm relations with Moscow is his priority. China, if more cautious than Russia, has also hewed to a line that has enhanced the Iranian state’s position. It has done this by continuing to purchase as much Iranian oil as it can while also reportedly supplying electronic components essential to that country’s missile and drone production. Genuine progress toward administration goals on Iran would require concrete commitments from both powers to stop what they are currently doing. Trump appears unlikely to demand this, and even if he did, neither is likely to follow through.

Putin has already mesmerized Trump into treating Ukraine as if it were the faulty party in its defensive war against Russia. That conflict may not officially be on the agenda when the three leaders sit down together on Thursday, but the specific danger in Shenzhen is that Putin will offer Trump a vague, unverifiable promise to limit support for Iran—something that Trump can present as a win—in exchange for Trump’s deeper complicity in ending the Ukraine war on Russian terms. It would be a trade of something real and irreversible for something hollow and unenforceable. Because of Trump’s unique weakness for glad-handing and grandiosity, it may take little more than that. A more lucid and competent U.S. president would never allow such confusion between the two crises.

What might Xi seek, and how do Trump’s growing vulnerabilities play into this? Beijing’s biggest goal is progress toward exerting control over Taiwan, if not taking over the island altogether by the eventual end of Xi’s longtime hold on power. Toward this end, Trump has already gifted Xi in important ways without receiving anything in return. At the level of official rhetoric, Trump has weakened America’s traditional support for a peaceful resolution of the dispute between Beijing and Taipei over the island’s future. In fact, he has come close to saying the United States would have no business defending Taiwan in case a war broke out between the two sides.

This by itself is an immense gift, and it would be enhanced further if Trump delayed arms sales to Taiwan to ingratiate himself with Xi. Already, the U.S. military posture in East Asia has been dramatically weakened by the prolonged deployment of aircraft carrier battle groups near Iran and by heavy depletion of advanced weapons systems during the war with that country. Trump has also weakened U.S. alliances in Asia through tariff demands and unpredictable behavior. By nature, Xi seems much more cautious than Putin. But sensing Trump’s weakness and need for some kind of foreign-policy achievement, however illusory, he could try to induce concessions through displays of strongman camaraderie and vague pledges of cooperation in areas of U.S. need.

If we cannot know in advance what these meetings will produce, we should know one thing already. Trump will emerge from them making great claims about how well he gets along with the world’s two other most powerful heads of state. In fact, nothing more might publicly come out of the meetings beyond this. But the world should be wary even of any quiet understandings that Trump, at this moment of extreme weakness and vulnerability, believes he has reached. The likelihood is that he is being played due to his personal and political need, and if the details are unknowable, the costs could be great.

The Stock Market’s Biggest Risk Isn’t a Crash. It’s What Investors Might Do When One Arrives.

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A market decline hurts. An improvised response can make it worse.

Imagine seeing your nest egg lose 30% of its value. In this scenario, the market has already delivered a painful setback. Selling may seem like the obvious way to regain control, but selling also creates a new problem: When will you get back into the market?

In reality, panic selling -- and delaying reentering the market -- can lead to enormous opportunity costs for investors. Let's explore why.

Seeing your portfolio lose value is never fun. Indeed, psychological studies have shown that investment losses have a greater psychological impact than equivalent gains, a phenomenon known as loss aversion. Therefore, it's completely understandable that many people seek immediate relief when the market enters a sell-off, correction, or crash.

However, the psychological relief of liquidating investments creates a new problem: determining when "it's safe."

In practice, most investors simply wait too long. Many wait for recessions to end or for bullish headlines to appear. But bullish headlines often lag positive price action. As a result, investors on the sidelines often miss out on massive recovery rallies. As we'll see next, that can have a significant impact on long-term growth.

Consider a $10,000 portfolio. If it drops 30%, its value will fall to $7,000. To get back to $10,000, the portfolio will need to gain $3,000. Since the overall value has fallen, it will need to generate approximately a 43% return on $7,000 to reach $10,000. So to wipe out a 30% drop, it needs to gain 43%. These amounts differ because the starting amounts differ.

Beyond the arithmetic, there are other costs. Transaction costs, lost dividend payments, and taxes have to be considered. In short, selling might ease one type of headache but create several others.

Despite understanding the math and the other implications of selling, many investors can't resist the urge to sell when the stock market is falling. One way to train yourself not to sell is to imagine a similar situation in a different market.

For example, let's say you own a house. Now imagine that another house in your neighborhood sells for half of what might normally be expected. That sale will decrease the value of all homes in the neighborhood, perhaps significantly. But even if the price of your own home falls 10%, 15%, or even 25%, that doesn't mean one of the bedrooms in your home disappears.

Stock portfolios work the same way. Prices can fall, but that doesn't necessarily mean that the businesses behind the stocks have deteriorated to the same degree.

For a buy-and-hold strategy to work, investors must build a portfolio that can withstand periods of volatility. Leveraged positions and meme stocks can wreck a portfolio during a stock market correction or crash. Therefore, savvy investors know to limit their exposure, or, better yet, avoid such investments altogether. By building a diversified portfolio based around well-run businesses, an investor can avoid much of the worst effects of a market downturn.

Market crashes are terrible, but thankfully, they're also rare. However, to prepare for them, it's best to have a plan. Writing it down can help you manage your emotions, think carefully, and avoid panic. At the end of the day, the math and the studies are clear: It's best to ride out market downturns rather than sell and try to time reentry perfectly.

If you can keep a cool head and remember this in the moment, you'll likely come through the next financial downturn in better shape than many other investors.

8 Soulslikes With Insane Difficulty Spikes

The Soulslikes genre offers some of the greatest challenges in gaming. With difficult bosses, enemies that demand patience, and many obstacles throughout a level, meeting success in any Soulslike can be incredibly rewarding.

However, there are times when a game takes it too far and includes a harsh difficulty spike that seemingly comes out of nowhere. This could sometimes lead to areas of the game feeling a bit unfair.

It's not the genre's area of expertise, but some of its representatives are more daring, offering fascinating movement mechanics.

While the games listed in this article are some of the best entries in the genre, they do have pacing issues when it comes to difficulty. Here are eight Soulslikes with brutal difficulty spikes.

Sekiro is already a demanding game from the get-go, but its difficulty reaches a whole other level in the mid to late game. Enemies become more aggressive, minibosses hit harder, and all encounters will constantly remind players that they need to master the parry system to stand a chance.

Boss fights also become considerably more demanding, requiring players to learn lengthy attack patterns and maintain constant pressure rather than relying on dodging. Just when you thought you were getting good at the game, Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice raises the bar.

The expansion of Elden Ring, Shadow of the Erdtree, is known for its difficulty spike right from the start. As soon as you enter the Land of Shadow, you’ll realize enemies deal way more damage, and your character is much weaker overall.

With the introduction of Scadutree Fragments, items that raise attack power and defense, players essentially have to start from scratch and engage with a new progression system. In other words, even OP base-game builds get a wake-up call when starting the DLC.

Overall, Lies of P is generally on the easier side for a Soulslike. However, the game has brutal difficulty spikes in a handful of its bosses. For example, Laxasia the Complete, Simon Manus, and King of Puppets are ten times more challenging than anything else in the game.

Each of these bosses is a true test of reflexes and endurance, where even the smallest mistake can prove costly. What makes these encounters particularly jarring is the stark contrast in difficulty, as the areas leading up to them are relatively easy to breeze through.

There’s no denying that The First Berserker: Khazan is a very challenging game right from its first level. Still, despite it being a very tough experience through and through, it still features an insane difficulty spike in its second half that can sometimes feel pretty unfair.

Late-game bosses are some of the most demanding encounters the genre has to offer, featuring relentless combos that leave very little room for mistakes. Khazan’s combat is super satisfying, but players who haven’t mastered its mechanics will quickly discover just how unforgiving it can be.

If you're looking for the heir to Soulsborne games, look no further than this list of ten Soulslikes that are unexpected masterpieces.

While the 2023 version of Lords of the Fallen never reaches the difficulty heights of 2014’s, it’s definitely not an easy experience. The game reaches its peak in difficulty during Bramis Castle, an area filled with the hardest enemies in the entire game.

Bramis Castle has been heavily regarded as unfair by many players due to the absurd amount of elite enemies. It was so hard that there were multiple post-launch patches to make it feel more reasonable. Still, despite the patches to make it easier, Bramis Castle remains an insane difficulty spike.

The original Hollow Knight was a fairly well-balanced experience from beginning to end, and while Silksong is a phenomenal game, it’s not as consistent as the original. For example, Bilewater increases the difficulty tenfold due to poisonous waters and brutal platforming.

Additionally, Act 3 of Silksong introduces an entirely new set of challenges as the Void corrupts Pharloom, making enemies considerably more dangerous while changing areas and levels. Fortunately, Act 3 is completely optional, meaning players who aren’t interested in tackling this difficulty spike can simply ignore it.

Wuchang: Fallen Feathers features several notoriously sharp difficulty spikes, mainly in its boss fights. Wuchang has the tendency to make the player feel incredibly powerful while exploring a level and then completely ripping that feeling away during some of the main bosses.

One of the earliest examples of this is the Commander Honglan boss fight, the first brick wall players will hit in a playthrough. All earlier bosses are a walk in the park compared to Commander Honglan, and everything that follows involves bosses that demand fast reflexes and perfect performance.

Nioh 3's difficulty spikes can be particularly unforgiving, especially when players enter the Heian Period. This period involves some of the most challenging locations and bosses in the game, such as Ibaraki Doji, Kajiwara Kagetoki, and many more.

Pretty much, once the player enters the Heian Period, the real Nioh 3 experience begins, and the rest of the adventure is a true test of skill. Bosses hit hard, are faster, and the importance of leveling equipment increases dramatically.

David Duchovny on Books, Band and ‘The X-Files’

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

David Duchovny has been famous for as long as I have been alive. From 1993 to 2002, The X-Files made government conspiracies a staple of primetime television. Duchovny’s FBI special agent Fox Mulder, with his skeptical counterpart, Gillian Anderson’s agent Dana Scully, personified the imminent breakdown of consensus reality in the coming century, delivering the paranoid fringe into 20 million American living rooms. The show belongs to a world of monoculture that no longer exists, and it launched Duchovny into a realm of visibility that’s hard to imagine now, when famous people are produced and consumed at an industrial rate. A few weeks ago, as my boyfriend shopped for a suit jacket at Crowley Vintage in Brooklyn and I edited this story on my phone, I watched a college-age boy put on a long, gray trench coat and announce to his girlfriend: “Mulder mode.”

Duchovny went on to star in Californication, a raunchy dramedy about a divorced novelist navigating the sexually and professionally parasitic Hollywood studio system of the 2000s. Many viewers interpreted Californication as a kind of autofiction, although Duchovny did not write for the show. The actor has written a number of novels, but it’s apparent to anyone who has spent 30 seconds with him that this soft-spoken, reluctant celebrity has nothing to do with the character Hank Moody. Still, Californication did anticipate something about art, celebrity, and self-commodification: Anybody who writes today must contend with marketing an artistic persona for a public that does not, as a rule, read.

In addition to his six books, Duchovny has released four records, and written and directed a number of feature films. He greets the degraded state of American arts with a charming blend of rigor and self-deprecation. “I’m not Jonathan Franzen,” he says of his fiction-writing process. “But if I work on something every day, it deepens. And eventually, hopefully, I can achieve one tenth of what Jonathan Franzen achieves.” But he has an aversion to the sort of publicity that might make his various artistic ventures more lucrative. He tells me he finds it “spiritually impoverishing.”

I meet Duchovny with his band—millennial slackers half his age—at their practice space in Carroll Gardens, where they’re preparing to tour their fourth LP, Prince of Pieces. He wears a white Hanes T-shirt, tight underneath his athletic arms, and he has a tattoo on his ankle—a compass pointing west, something he got when his daughter, West, was hospitalized as an infant. (She’s 27 now, an accomplished actor. “She wanted to be a doctor,” he says. “I was so happy when she told me she was going to pursue acting. Thank God.”)

The albums are composed of subdued, easy-to-listen-to folk-rock tracks, not dissimilar from early Kurt Vile. It’s the band’s first rehearsal in ages, and one of their only chances to prepare in advance of a European tour. In person, they play the songs harder and heavier, sprinkling in crowd-pleasing covers: “Godzilla” by Blue Öyster Cult, “The One I Love” by REM, and—“sadly only one time,” Duchovny says—“Hotline Bling.” The energy in the room is high. These men like one another. Duchovny’s manager offers me a beer from the cooler I’m perched on. I gesture to the tallboy in my purse. He looks disappointed. “You didn’t trust us,” he says. Duchovny steps aside to take a sip of water and picks up the microphone.

“You can sing along,” his keyboardist, Colin Lee, says.

At 66, David Duchovny remains a sex symbol. He’s in better shape than he was on The X-Files. He’s in better shape than the boys in the band. The night after practice, during a Q&A for a screening of his 2023 film Reverse the Curse, he mentions that the wardrobe department had to fashion a “BDSM harness” for him to wear under his clothes to remind him to hunch like an old man. He plays a Red Sox fan who bonds with his estranged son over baseball, marijuana, and their shared lust for a beautiful, young death doula. Duchovny’s character has terminal cancer. “I’m in my dying era,” he tells the crowd. “I die in every film. ”

For the most part, the audience at Westchester County’s Irvington Theater is old enough to have watched The X-Files as it aired. At least two esoteric-looking old men carry David’s poetry collection like a prayer book. There are a handful of young people. A college-aged girl in a KN95 mask raises her hand to ask about Bucking Fucking Dent, the novel adaptation of the Reverse the Curse screenplay, which Duchovny wrote while the movie was in production purgatory. “In the book, Ted and Marty’s last name is Fullilove. In the movie you change it to Fullaker. Why did you do that?”

“Good question,” Duchovny says, stalling. “Full of love. Full of shit. It’s all the same.”

Inevitably, someone asks about the new X-Files. Ryan Coogler is rebooting the show for Hulu. Duchovny will later suggest to me that Chris Carter, the show’s original writer, has designs on a reboot of his own—“He has plans, and wants to end with the original show.” As for Coogler’s version, he says, “I know he said he wants me to come do something, but we haven’t figured out what yet.” During the audience Q&A, Duchovny seems to have misheard the question, and spoils the ending for The Exiles, his forthcoming Taiwanese crime film: “I die in that one too.”

Duchovny’s latest book, About Time, a collection of poems published last year by Akashic, is also largely about dying: the death of his father, the death of his first marriage, the ambient death that hung in the air at the beginning of the decade. He started writing the poems when his therapist encouraged him to talk to dead people. “He was talking about Rudolph Steiner’s belief that you can talk to the dead. And he said: You can talk to your father and you should. My dad and I didn’t connect like we might have,” Duchovny says. “When you’re younger, you think you have all the time in the world to get the relationship you want. But the relationship doesn’t end.” In conversation, he is cerebral without being annoying. “There’s a power struggle when you read a really good novel,” he says. “Somebody’s mind is taking over your mind. I think about language a lot and how we take it for granted as being an accurate signifier. When I was younger I thought communication was possible. I’m not so sure now. But it’s fun to try.”

Duchovny’s humility complicates the sexual charisma that still lives at the center of his public persona. His introduction to About Time begins, “I know what you’re thinking: Just what the world needs now—a bunch of poems from an actor.” There’s a sense that he wants to get ahead of the reader’s skepticism. But there’s also plenty of perceptive lines in the poems themselves: “You dreamt you wrote something so good, / so ambivalent and destructive, so creative, / so lacking in morals, decency, or instruction, / so sad and so funny, / so useless and old, / so familiar and new, / so full of theft and power / that they had to name a god after it.” I ask him if good writing is necessarily amoral. He says, “Probably. Which is why any politically correct work of art is usually super successful and really bad. Usually artists are difficult people, and I have no problem separating the art from the artist. But people do now.”

In college, Duchovny wanted to be a poet. “I thought it was an actual occupation you could have,” he says. When an underclassman turned up in his workshop who was better than him, he quit writing poems on the spot. “It was actually Walter Kirn,” he says. “He showed up at 16-years-old. He was a genius. I was like okay—I’m not a poet.”

The two men have become close in the last decade. Kirn, who wrote Up in the Air and founded the newspaper County Highway, tells me what Duchovny was like at Princeton: “He was golden. He had an aura. I was a little starstruck by him.” Duchovny has agreed to play paranormal radio host Art Bell in American Nights, a biopic that Kirn is developing alongside director Alex Lee Moyer. Bell’s Coast to Coast, a cult AM show during the ’80s and ’90s, was beloved by long-haul truckers and compound preppers alike, who called in to report UFO sightings, cattle mutilations, and crop circles. “I, like the rest of my generation, grew up on The X-Files,” Moyer tells me. “It made a massive impression on me, and I’ve been a fan of David ever since. The synchronicity of him being a part of this is not lost on me.”

I ask Duchovny if he always needed to be the best. “I at least needed to be the best in my class,” he says.

In LA, I meet Duchovny in a quiet restaurant to talk about his books. He describes his first novel as “an act of cowardice.” Holy Cow is narrated by an anthropomorphized barnyard animal who uncovers the conspiracy theory that is factory farming. “I thought: Well, they’re not gonna cut me off at the knees on this. Because I’m clearly not that serious. It was calculated. I didn’t want to try for the great American novel the first time as an actor.” The books that followed are more ambitious, each one better than the last. By the time he was promoting his fourth book, 2021’s Truly Like Lightning, he made his ambition plain. “I want to be taken seriously,” he said on Marc Maron’s podcast. “That’s my weakness. I want that book to be taken seriously.”

“You don’t want people to be like the guy from The X-Files wrote a book,” Maron said.

“That’s what they’re gonna say,” Duchovny said, “but I hope and I believe that eventually people will see that I mean business.”

Truly Like Lightning centers on a Mormon polygamist family forced to enter the godless world of Los Angeles when their homestead is upended. “When I started to read about Mormonism, it struck me as super optimistic. Everyone’s gonna get their own planet and everyone’s gonna be able to propagate souls. As opposed to a European perspective, which was always about the end of something, being 2,000 years removed from this thing. The Mormons said no, this is still happening.” The novel takes cues from the literary critic Harold Bloom, who wrote that Mormonism was an “American original” and called its founder a “religious genius.” Duchovny studied under Bloom at Yale, where he earned some of an English PhD. “Everything goes back to Bloom,” he says. “And you never really leave grad school. I’m not sure I’ve left grad school. They might still be waiting for my dissertation.”

But Duchovny did leave grad school. He signed with a talent agent one summer to try to earn some money acting in commercials. His agent told him that if he wanted to go out for theater or film roles, he’d have to get into an acting class. “I said: I’m not doing anything. When I stepped into acting class, something happened. I wasn’t a demonstrative person—I was an intellectual person. So it was like: Yeah, be unreasonable. Be irrational. It opened up a different world, a world of fiction, a world without consequences. I’m doing a scene, and I’m yelling at this person and I’m going to murder them, but I don’t have to go to jail.”

For someone with half a dozen good books and an enormous public profile, it’s surprising there haven’t been more reviews. Johnny Temple, who published Duchovny’s two most recent books on Akashic, tells me, “It’s certainly frustrating that there hasn't been more review coverage of David’s books. For any well-known actor, musician, or other type of public figure, there’s always going to be obstacles to being taken seriously in a different medium.” Kirn tells me, “That makes writing fiction a much purer achievement in my mind. You’re doing this because you love it, and you know that two thirds of your fans will only remember you as a TV character. It’s a brilliant, Sisyphean task. Also, he’s a superb writer.”

I ask Duchovny whether he still thinks about being the best. “I think now I can acknowledge that it’s a hollow designation,” he says. “Especially in the arts. Even though we give awards, which is silly. Holy Cow made the Times bestseller list. Ever since, everything I write sells less and less.”

“It’s hard for someone to champion it,” he adds. “If I wrote a memoir, I’m sure it would get reviewed in the Times.”

Talking to Duchovny, one is reminded that wanting to be taken seriously and being in on the joke aren’t mutually exclusive. As band practice winds down in Brooklyn, the guys are excited to cover Lou Reed’s “Perfect Day.” Duchovny crosses his arms in front of his lap like a schoolboy as he sings. To my ear it sounds just fine, but Lee, the keyboardist, asks the mixer to lower the reverb on David’s voice.

“Maybe lower David’s mic in general?” the bassist, Mitchell Stewart asks.

“Lower than zero,” Duchovny says. “Less than zero.”

A few days later, he texts me. He just screened an early cut of Soapbox, a Hollywood satire he wrote and stars in opposite Laverne Cox. At first, he’s bummed that the test audience has decided that his character shouldn’t get killed in the final moments of the movie. But a couple hours later, he writes me again: “People don’t want me to die. I guess I should be flattered.”

Antalya, Türkiye — The COP31 Leaders’ Summit concluded today, with several new announcements and speeches from heads of state and the UN Secretary-General. Developments included country endorsements of the COP Presidency’s 35×35 electrification pledge, growing support for the transition away from fossil fuels, and new pledges from philanthropies to the Tropical Forest Forever Facility, among others.

Following is a statement from Ani Dasgupta, President and CEO, World Resources Institute:

“One message came across loud and clear from world leaders: climate action has shifted from an environmental movement to a core economic and security imperative. The conversation is moving beyond high-level commitments to the practical challenges of how to transition away from fossil fuels while supporting workers and communities and how to put entire economies on a cleaner, more resilient path.

“Electrification and the transition away from fossil fuels rightly took center stage. These are two vital, intertwined goals, and progress on one must reinforce the other. Early support for the 35×35 electrification pledge is encouraging, but many more countries need to endorse it and turn their commitments into credible national plans backed by concrete policies and investment.

“President Lula deserves credit for keeping the transition away from fossil fuels at the top of the international agenda and delivering a global roadmap that countries can adapt to their own circumstances. Australia and Türkiye now have an opportunity to lead by example, putting forward their own transition plans and encouraging others to follow.

“Vulnerable countries delivered an equally clear message: access to affordable finance is critical to them implementing their climate ambition. High borrowing costs and unsustainable debt are holding back investment in the very countries that need it most. With the $1.3 trillion finance goal established, the focus is shifting to how all sources of finance can work together to deliver investment at scale and how we ensure affordable capital reaches those who need it most. There are encouraging signs, including an influx of pledges from philanthropies for the Brazil-initiated Tropical Forests Forever Facility.

“Adaptation and early warning systems received much-needed attention after a year of crushing heatwaves, fires, and floods. But that attention now needs to turn to the investment in systemic resilience as the best way to make economies and societies stronger and improve people’s lives. Building more resilient cities was a major focus and will take deep collaboration between national governments, cities, and states. Cities are on the front lines of delivering the climate solutions and essential services that make people’s lives better, and demonstrating these benefits is critical to accelerating the transition. Local leaders are on the front lines of climate impacts and the delivery of essential services that make communities safer, healthier, and more resilient, yet too often lack the resources to respond. Cities and local communities especially need greater access to adaptation finance.

“It was disappointing to see no dedicated ocean event at the Leaders’ Summit, despite over 90% of countries including ocean action in national climate plans. The ocean is on the front lines of the climate crisis, but it could also be one of our greatest allies in tackling it. Ocean-based solutions can provide up to one-third of the emissions cuts needed by 2050 to keep 1.5°C within reach. This ambition for ocean action must be reflected in any COP31 cover decision and translate into concrete progress, especially for vulnerable coastal and island nations.

“Some leaders also delivered a resounding defense of multilateralism, even amid deep geopolitical tensions. International cooperation on climate may be changing shape, but it is still alive and needed now more than ever. Even in a fractured world, leaders recognize that no country can secure its economic future, build resilience, or prosper without working together on shared climate and environmental challenges. That spirit of cooperation must now carry through the negotiations and deliver a strong, ambitious outcome at COP31.”

Grazing sheep in fields with solar arrays

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Two new research papers shed light on the growing practice of grazing sheep in fields with solar arrays. One demonstrates that, in the right numbers, sheep grazing increases populations of beneficial insects like bees and lady beetles compared to mowing around solar panels. The other research describes a decision-making support tool to help farmers profitably grow their solar grazing herds.

A team from the College of Agriculture and Life Sciences (CALS) found that when two sheep per hectare graze around solar arrays, the abundance of wild bees, honey bees and lady beetles jumped by 38%, compared to a control plot that was mowed. The increase was driven primarily by an 83% increase in honey bees alone, according to the team’s findings in “Low solar-grazing densities support beneficial insects in agrivoltaic systems,” published Oct. 7 in Environmental Research Letters.

“Sheep grazing and beneficial insect habitat are two prominent conversation topics around solar development right now,” said Leana Zoller, the paper’s first author and a postdoctoral associate in natural resources in the Cornell CALS Ashley School of Global Development and the Environment. “But what’s been missing is research that treats them as parts of the same system. When we talk about sustainable solar development, integrating these discussions into a single framework is really helpful to farmers and community decision makers.”

For three years, Zoller and a team of entomologists, ecologists and animal and plant scientists tested various stocking rates – how many sheep per hectare are allowed to graze a certain plot of land – using Cornell’s teaching sheep grazing at the university-owned Cascadilla Community Solar Farm in Dryden, New York. At four sheep per hectare, they found beneficial insect abundance was similar to the mowed control site, while vegetation management effectiveness was greater than at two sheep per hectare. At eight sheep per hectare, vegetation management was maximized and sheep flock health remained high, but honey bee abundance dropped by 45%.

“We zeroed in on stocking rates because that’s a concrete number that sheep farmers understand and work with every day, and we want this research to be useful,” said Steve Grodsky, paper senior author and assistant professor of natural resources in the Ashley School. “With solar grazing, people, energy, agricultural animals, plants and insects are all part of this ecosystem. We’re trying to understand the connections between those pieces so that when solar developers, municipalities looking at solar development, or farmers are faced with a decision, they can have scientific information about potential trade-offs and synergies.”

Grodsky is also assistant unit leader of the U.S. Geological Survey New York Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit. Additional co-authors are sheep farmer and solar grazing consultant Nikola Kochendoerfer, Ph.D. ’20; Antonio DiTommaso, professor in the School of Integrative Plant Science; John Losey, professor of entomology; Scott McArt, Ph.D. ’12, associate professor of entomology; Paige Muñiz, formerly a technician in McArt’s lab; and Todd Ugine, Ph.D. ’04, research associate in Losey’s lab, all in CALS. Sarah Carson, director of Cornell’s Campus Sustainability Office, was key in coordinating site access between researchers and the solar company that manages the Cascadilla solar farm.

Another Cornell research team, from the Cornell SC Johnson College of Business, has developed a tool that helps farmers whose sheep graze in solar arrays to make better decisions about their operation. The team described the tool in “Flock Management for Solar Grazing,” which published Sept. 28 in Manufacturing & Service Operations Management.

After a chance encounter with Caleb Scott, Ithaca-based sheep farmer and founder of United Agrivoltaics, Vincent Slaugh, assistant professor at the Cornell Peter and Stephanie Nolan School of Hotel Administration and first author of the paper, began thinking through the economics and logistics of solar grazing. He recruited Linwei Xin, associate professor of operations research and information engineering, and Shane Henderson, Charles W. Lake, Jr. Professor in Productivity, both in the Cornell Duffield College of Engineering, to work on the topic.

Together, they developed a spreadsheet-based model that helps sheep farmers to optimize procurement and sales decisions, based on factors like target flock size, costs, anticipated revenues, and expected vegetation in particular solar sites; if a farmer doesn’t have enough sheep to keep the grass down, they’ll have to cut it some other way. It also helps solar developers understand growers’ needs and develop contracts that are more profitable and sustainable for all parties long-term. United Agrivoltaics has already incorporated the tool into its onboarding process.

Slaugh sees opportunity for U.S. shepherds to grow their operations – especially with solar contracts offering a steady source of income and animal feed. Current domestic sheep production for meat and wool doesn’t come close to meeting domestic demand; about half of all lamb and mutton consumed in the U.S. is imported, primarily from New Zealand and Australia.

“If we grow the sheep industry, will the American diet eventually include more lamb? Could that be a side effect of the solar energy revolution? I think the answer is very likely yes,” Slaugh said. “And then when that happens, we’d expect processors and supporting industries to come back, for banks to be willing to give more loans, and for supply chains to follow. All of that grows the economy, and rural economies in particular.”

Kochendoerfer, who grazes 700 ewes on solar installations in New York and Vermont, said that economy is already growing.

“I work with a lot of farms that are now scaling up because of solar,” Kochendoerfer said. “There is a new wave of sheep farmers who are capitalizing on this access to good land. With solar grazing, you get paid to graze as opposed to having to pay to graze. That leads to sustainable, meaningful economic income that these farmers can use to expand, build a barn, buy equipment or hire someone. It’s enabling a lot of folks to build sound, viable agricultural businesses.”

Funding for these research projects came from the Cornell Atkinson Center for Sustainability, the National Institute for Food and Agriculture and the National Science Foundation.

Robert Pattinson cast as next James Bond

📅 2026-11-16  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Robert Pattinson has been cast as the next James Bond in the upcoming film from director Denis Villeneuve. It will be the 26th James Bond film and the first since Amazon MGM took over the iconic franchise.

Pattinson will be the seventh actor to play the role. Monday's announcement — which came in part via an Instagram post — ends a long period of speculation about who would take over as "007" after Daniel Craig.

"James Bond is one of the most legendary characters in cinema, and to be entrusted with 007 is the honour of a lifetime," Pattinson said in a statement. "I couldn't be more excited about this partnership and the work ahead with this fantastic team."

Pattinson has appeared in several big-ticket films this year, including Christopher Nolan's "The Odyssey" and psychological thriller "Primetime," and he'll soon appear in "Dune: Part Three," also directed by Villeneuve. Pattinson, who rose to fame with the Twilight movie series, is also in production for Matt Reeves' "The Batman Part II," a follow-up to the 2022 film based on the DC comic.

The upcoming Bond film will have a script by Steven Knight and will be produced by Amy Pascal, known for her work on the Spider-Man franchise, and David Heyman, who produced all eight Harry Potter films.

"We were surrounded by extraordinary talent, but there can only be one 007. I am overjoyed to welcome Robert Pattinson as our new James Bond. He has the timeless soul of 007 and brings a spirit that is completely new. I cannot wait to begin," Villeneuve said.

In 2022, Amazon bought MGM Studios, which had long produced the Bond movies, for roughly $8.45 billion. Amazon MGM then acquired creative control of the James Bond intellectual property rights in 2025, when it closed a deal with longtime producers Barbara Broccoli and Michael G. Wilson.

The film is still untitled, and Amazon MGM hasn't announced a projected release date.

The lost years of Lamar Jackson -- and the greatness still within reach

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Some years, Lamar Jackson's thumb hits another player and is fine. Some years, his ankle just gets hit rather than rolled up on. And those years, he has been -- and will be -- a player who can win an MVP and perhaps a Super Bowl.

Jackson's latest injury was almost too cruel to believe. After missing the previous three and a half games with a high ankle sprain, the two-time MVP was finally back. So, too, was the Ravens' hope. Jackson had looked phenomenal -- MVP-level phenomenal -- in new offensive coordinator Declan Doyle's system. Not only was he back, but he was back in a big spot: against fellow MVP Josh Allen in a crucial matchup vs. Buffalo on the first day of November, with Baltimore trying to end a two-game losing streak.

And then, on the Ravens' first play, Jackson hit his thumb on Greg Rousseau's arm. Jackson looked down at his injured right thumb, trotted toward the sideline and then took a knee.

"Oh boy," NFL on CBS analyst Ross Tucker said.

Medical personnel surrounded Jackson, draped a towel over his hand and brought him down the tunnel, off the field.

What more was there to say? Lamar Jackson, one of the defining players not just of the era but of NFL history, was hurt. Again. And it was serious. Again.

Jackson officially landed on IR with a thumb injury that will require surgery. It is "potentially" season-ending, and with the Ravens 4-5 and losers of four straight, it's looking like a lost season in Baltimore. This is the fourth time in the last six years the 29-year-old has missed at least four games. It has robbed the Ravens of their Super Bowl aspirations. It has robbed the NFL of a superstar. And it has robbed Jackson, a Heisman Trophy winner and twice an NFL MVP, of even higher levels of all-time greatness.

On the positive side, the thumb injury is the first ailment to land Jackson on IR. He has never had an ACL tear, an Achilles tear, etc. On the negative side, Jackson has racked up so many injuries of so many different kinds that it seems like something is constantly getting in the way, whether it's nagging at him or keeping him out of games. He even said the ankle pain entering the Bills game last week was merely "bearable," not gone.

Since the start of last season alone, Jackson has had hamstring, ankle, toe, knee, back, and thumb ailments. Not all of those kept him out, but they add up. He admitted he came back from last year's hamstring injury too early, impacting his play all season.

And yes, for what it's worth, it's bad luck. If Jackson's hand hits Rousseau at any other angle, he might not hurt his thumb enough to need surgery. If a diving Jermaine Johnson II doesn't awkwardly trap Jackson's ankle to the turf in Week 3, Jackson might not miss the next three games. Josh Allen and Patrick Mahomes have both had hundreds of similar plays and, with the exception of Mahomes' torn ACL last year, avoided injury for the most part. Sometimes, it's simply human anatomy, something Jackson cannot change.

For as prolific a runner as Jackson is, he does an excellent job avoiding hits on those plays. None of his injuries have been on runs. It has just been a constant parade of misfortune. Unfortunately for Jackson and the Ravens, the NFL rolls on, whether you're lucky or not.

Perhaps time and injuries have taken their toll and blocked our collective memory from how incredible Jackson has been. That would be truly unfortunate.

In 2019, Jackson's second year of the league, he threw 36 touchdown passes and ran for 1,206 yards. In NFL history, there are ...

In fact, only one other player ever has posted 1,200 rushing yards and merely double-digit passing touchdowns in a season: Spec Sanders of the New York Yankees (football version) in the All-America Football Conference in 1947. But he only threw 14 touchdowns. Jackson did things modern football has never known.

Jackson became the youngest quarterback to win MVP and joined Tom Brady as the only unanimous MVPs in NFL history. There are many ways to measure individual season greatness, but Pro Football Reference's "Approximate Value" is as good as any. Jackson had an approximate value of 25 in 2019, tied for the second-best season on record; LaDanian Tomlinson's 2006 season, in which he scored an NFL-record 31 touchdowns, is the only one better.

Jackson was healthy in 2020 and had another strong season, including a second straight 1,000-yard rushing effort. He has as many 1,000-yard rushing seasons as all other quarterbacks in NFL history combined (Michael Vick and Justin Fields both have one).

Then the injuries started. In 2021, Jackson was on pace for another 1,000-yard rushing season, and the Ravens led the AFC North when Browns linebacker Jeremiah Owusu-Koramoah dove at Jackson's ankles after Jackson released a pass. Jackson got carted off and didn't play in any of the final four games. The Ravens lost all four and missed the playoffs.

The next year, Jackson sprained his PCL when his left knee got driven into the ground on a sack by the Broncos' Jonathan Cooper. He ended up missing six weeks -- the final five of the regular season and a wild card game, which Baltimore lost.

This was a strange one. John Harbaugh initially wondered if Jackson could be back the next week and added "If not, it'll be sometime after that shortly." Then, Ravens officials were confident Jackson would return for the playoffs, per ESPN, leaving Jackson to essentially rule himself out -- and give his own medical update -- via social media.

In 2023, though, Jackson was healthy and marvelous once again. Under new offensive coordinator Todd Monken, Jackson set career highs in completion percentage (67.2%), passing yards (3,678) and yards per attempt (8.0). He was still excellent on the ground with a team-high 821 yards. He won MVP and was one vote short of doing so unanimously.

He was somehow even better in 2024, with 4,172 passing yards and 8.8 yards per attempt. He threw 41 touchdowns and just four interceptions. He ran for 915 yards. He had already reached unprecedented levels. Now, he was reaching new ones, as the first player with:

His 10.25 touchdown-to-interception ratio was also the best ever in any 30-touchdown season.

Somehow Jackson didn't win MVP. He was a first-team All-Pro, but MVP went to Allen, an extreme rarity. Jackson would have been the youngest three-time MVP in NFL history. That's how good the start was.

From 2019 to 2024, Jackson ranked third in passer rating and touchdown-to-interception ratio and fourth in yards per attempt. He was also fourth in touchdown passes despite missing significant time in two seasons. He was also eighth in rushing yards and first in yards per carry.

He was the Shohei Ohtani of football. No one can be this good at two different aspects, right? Wrong. Jackson was truly exceptional, and he seemed to be getting better.

He had nine touchdowns and zero interceptions on an absurd 9.6 yards per attempt through three weeks of 2025. Then came the hamstring injury, which started an avalanche of injuries. The hits haven't stopped coming since.

If two things stand out as Jackson's career shortcomings, it's injuries and playoff performance. The two are somewhat intertwined. In 2021, Baltimore missed the playoffs. In 2022, Jackson missed the playoffs. Last year, his injuries derailed the Ravens, but they somehow got a Week 18 win-and-in game against the Steelers. Tyler Loop missed a game-winning field goal attempt. It's looking like he will not add to his postseason ledger this season, either.

Had Jackson been healthy in even two of those four lost seasons, is he a Super Bowl champ? Or does he at least have more postseason wins?

Jackson is one of nine quarterbacks to win multiple MVP awards since the 1970 AFL-NFL merger. Of those nine, Jackson has the second-fewest playoff wins (two) before his 30th birthday, only ahead of the famously late-blooming Steve Young.

Wins, it is said, are not a quarterback stat, and that can be true. Unfortunately for Jackson and the Ravens, both his play and his team's have declined in the postseason. The Ravens are 3-6 in the playoffs since drafting Jackson in 2018 (3-5 with Jackson as the starter).

Jackson lost in his playoff debut in 2018, but the real disappointment came after his 2019 NFL season. With the Ravens as the AFC No. 1 seed, he had 365 yards and a touchdown passing and 143 yards rushing against the Titans, but he also had two interceptions,  lost a fumble, and failed on two fourth-and-short runs.

The next year, Jackson earned his first playoff win, getting a measure of revenge on the Titans in the wild card round. But one week later, Baltimore went quietly in a 17-3 loss to Buffalo, most remembered for Jackson throwing a terrible end-zone interception that Taron Johnson returned 101 yards for a touchdown.

They have also been truly unlucky with injuries. He was healthy in 2023 and 2024. Last year's hamstring and this year's ankle were unfortunate, but the thumb was a freak injury. Jackson may never reach the heights he was on the path to after being on the precipice of becoming the youngest three-time MVP ever in 2024. That's the sad truth about a player playing hurt and/or missing significant chunks of three years of his prime. Would he have another MVP? Two? A Super Bowl ring? We'll never know, and that's tough to swallow.

But there's a version of Jackson -- a little luckier, a little better in the playoffs, a lot better of a passer than he was his younger years -- that can still be a superstar for many years and get him back on a path that ends with him as an all-time great.

Saylor’s Strategy Keeps Buying Bitcoin, Adding 'A Little Orange' Through the Dip

Strategy kept buying Bitcoin through a brutal market stretch, adding 485 BTC worth roughly $33.4 million and repurchasing $228 million of its preferred stock, as chairman Michael Saylor urged the market to rally with a characteristically cheeky nudge.

The company disclosed the purchase in a filing Monday. The modest addition, small by the standards of a firm that once bought tens of thousands of coins at a time, lifted Strategy’s total holdings to 850,849 BTC as of Nov. 15, alongside $4.9 billion in U.S. dollar assets.

Strategy, the largest corporate holder of Bitcoin, remains far ahead of any other treasury company.

The buy caps a stretch of smaller, steadier accumulation paired with stock buybacks. Strategy recently added $82 million in Bitcoin while repurchasing $188 million of its STRC preferred shares, part of a shift toward what it calls a capital-management approach, buying back stock when it views the shares as undervalued rather than relentlessly stacking coins.

Bitcoin has slid sharply in recent days, trading well below the six-figure highs it touched earlier in the cycle, and the downturn has squeezed Bitcoin treasury firms whose valuations rise and fall with the asset.

Saylor, undeterred, posted the holdings update to X alongside the firm’s trademark Bitcoin tracker, and followed it with a quip aimed at the slumping market: “This dip could use a little orange,” a nod to Bitcoin’s signature color.

The purchase lands during a precarious moment for the treasury-company model Strategy pioneered. Index provider MSCI has finalized rules that could eject Bitcoin treasury firms like Strategy from its widely tracked equity indexes, classifying companies whose balance sheets are dominated by a single asset as “non-operating” and therefore ineligible.

Removal would force index-tracking funds to sell the affected shares, potentially pressuring prices and shrinking the premium these firms have long commanded over the value of their Bitcoin.

For now, Strategy is doing what it has always done, buying Bitcoin and talking it up, even as the market tests its conviction.

The combination of a small purchase and a sizable buyback underscores the balancing act: keep accumulating, defend the stock, and weather a downturn that has turned the once-unstoppable treasury trade into a far more fraught proposition.

How Are Hotels Paid When You Redeem Points, And For Elite Perks?

There’s a real thrill to redeeming points for stays at luxury properties, and getting outsized value. I’m sure many people have wondered about the economics of that. How much is the hotel getting reimbursed for your stay? Who is paying for any elite perks that you receive? Does the hotel care if you redeemed points or not? Let’s talk about those various points in this post…

The major global hotel groups (Hilton, Hyatt, IHG, Marriott, etc.) don’t own a vast majority of their properties. Instead, the companies have an asset light strategy, and have management or franchise contracts for the individual properties.

In some cases, large investment firms own hotels, and they may have properties belonging to all kinds of brands. In other cases, a hotel may even be family owned.

The way the major hotel groups make money is primarily through fees (they’re also increasingly directly monetizing their loyalty programs, but that’s a whole different topic). There’s typically an initial fee to open the hotel, and then on top of that, there are fees on ongoing revenue. Now, one of the reasons that consumers flock to certain hotel groups is because of their loyalty programs, which are massive marketing machines. A big part of the value proposition of these loyalty programs is the ability to redeem points.

As you’d expect, those points are redeemed centrally through the program, and then the loyalty program has to “buy” that room from the hotel. After all, the hotel owner wants to be compensated for each head in a bed, especially since they’re already paying a system fund fee, to fund these perks. That brings us to the question of how hotels are reimbursed…

As you’d expect, hotel groups don’t publicly disclose how they reimburse their properties when members redeem points. However, over the years we’ve been able to get some insights into this.

Note that the number of points you redeem for a stay doesn’t directly determine reimbursement for a hotel. In other words, a hotel doesn’t get paid X amount per redeemed point. Similarly, if a program offers a fifth night free on award redemptions, the hotel is getting reimbursed for all five nights, as it’s the loyalty program incurring the cost there.

Instead, points redemption rates are set by the loyalty program, and are intended to reflect the cost incurred by the program. That’s why in many cases you’ll find that redemption rates don’t correlate directly to how much a property would cost in cash, but rather also factor in average occupancy at a property.

A hotel loyalty program would probably rather you redeem your points at a $1,000 per night hotel that’s 50% full, rather than a $500 per night hotel that’s 95% full, because the latter redemption will cost the program more. Hotel loyalty programs use historical data to determine points pricing, and the costs they incur.

Let me actually give a real example from a recent stay. I recently redeemed points for a two night stay at a Wyndham Rewards property, at the cost of 30,000 points per night. The folio I was sent accidentally had the amount Wyndham had to pay, rather than the amount I had to pay.

Even though I was paying the same number of points both nights, the hotel was being reimbursed around $87 for one night, and $184 for the next night. Funny enough, while sitting in the lobby working the first morning, I overheard one front desk agent tell the other that they were fully sold out the following night, so that tracks. The first night was being reimbursed at a reduced rate, while the second night was being reimbursed based on something closer to the average daily rate.

Officially, you should expect exactly the same treatment as if you paid cash, and you absolutely shouldn’t feel sheepish about the fact that you didn’t pay cash for your stay.

Now, in practice that might not always be how things play out. There’s sometimes a back and forth between hotel owners and hotel groups. Hotel owners often want it both ways — they want the power of the major hotel group distribution channels and loyalty programs, without actually incurring the costs associated with it, whether it’s complimentary upgrades, points redemptions, or a decent breakfast.

Will you sometimes find a hotel that’s perhaps a bit condescending if you redeem points and have an issue? Absolutely, but it’s not how it should be, and it’s the exception rather than the norm. Some may remember my stay at the Aegon Mykonos, at the time a Marriott Autograph Collection property (the hotel has since… mysteriously disappeared?).

Long story short, I was downgraded despite using a confirmed upgrade, and the manager was completely unapologetic about it, and was unwilling to do anything meaningful. One of his defenses was that I was staying “complimentary,” and therefore shouldn’t be complaining.

The hotel industry is complex, given the various parties involved, from the hotel group to the individual hotel owners. Hopefully the above is a useful rundown of how reimbursement works when redeeming points. The important thing to understand is that hotels get paid a lot more when you’re redeeming points if the property is full.

When redeeming points, you definitely shouldn’t feel sheepish about it. You earned your stay, and the reason hotels belonging to major loyalty programs are able to command a revenue premium is because of the points and loyalty perks they offer.

Unlike many tokenized stocks, which give holders a claim on a share, Bullish's issuer-sponsored token "is itself the share," Jupiter COO Kash Dhanda said in a presentation at Solana's Breakpoint conference in London.

Users will be able to borrow against them with fully onchain liquidations in an "institutional-grade lending market," and integrations with qualified custodians, he said. Bullish acquired Equiniti, one of the world's largest transfer agents, earlier this year, Dhanda noted.

Dhanda framed the announcements around what he called the "post-tokenization era." Most tokenized assets today are "parked, not productive," he said.

Of the roughly $460 billion in tokenized assets tracked by data platform RWA.xyz, only about $39.5 billion is actually onchain and can be moved off the issuer's platform, and just $3.4 billion is used in DeFi, according to DeFiLlama, Dhanda said, a "utilization gap" of 99%.

The Jupiter Global Passport lets users verify their identity to buy permissioned assets, while keeping their information as private as possible using zero-knowledge data sharing, Dhanda said. Jupiter Liquidity Services, meanwhile, will help asset issuers design their markets, seed initial capital, find market makers and integrate with Jupiter's routing so that their tokens are available across the platforms that use it. "You integrate once, you will be available everywhere," Dhanda said.

"Shallow credit markets don't drive liquidity on chain," he added. "If you cannot do more with your onchain asset than you can do with the off-chain version of it, you're probably not going to switch."

Jupiter says it has handled more than $500 billion in trading volume so far this year and paid out $162 million in yield.
