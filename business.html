One of the world’s biggest zinc mines is getting Alaska’s largest solar farm

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Red Dog Mine in Alaska burns about 40,000 gallons of diesel a day to keep one of the world’s largest zinc mines running, and now, an 8.8-megawatt (MW) solar farm and battery storage will help cut that fuel use.

The Alaska Energy Authority’s board unanimously approved a nearly $5 million loan for the project through its Power Project Fund. Tugliq Red Dog1 will build, own, and operate the roughly $35 million solar project and sell the electricity to mine operator Teck Alaska. The state’s loan review says site work began in June and expects the solar and storage to come online in October 2028.

Red Dog sits about 105 miles north of the Arctic Circle, on land owned by NANA, an Iñupiat-owned Alaska Native corporation. The array would be larger than any solar farm operating in Alaska today, as KOTZ’s Desiree Hagen reported for Alaska Public Media. Hagen reported that the plans call for more than 15,000 panels across around 30 acres.

The mine gets all its power from on-site diesel generators. In summer, it can draw so much electricity that Teck has to cut power elsewhere at the site. The solar array and the 8.8 MW, 15 megawatt-hour (MWh) battery will give the operator another source of power during those crunches.

The state of Alaska expects the solar and storage project to cover about 5% of the zinc mine’s electricity needs under its current operating plan. The project could do even more after mining ends, covering about 90% of the site’s annual electricity needs.

Teck expects the mine’s existing deposits to support operations through 2031, and it’s studying whether to develop two nearby deposits. If that expansion goes ahead, the state projects the solar and battery project would cover about 3% of the larger operation’s electricity needs. If mining ends, demand will drop sharply, but the site will still need electricity for cleanup and long-term water treatment.

Why Ultra-Long-Haul Flights Are Easier On An Aircraft Than Short Hops

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Services like Qatar Airways' flight from Doha to Auckland and Singapore Airlines' route from Singapore to New York are among the most challenging flights in the world. They push modern aircraft to their limits, testing their range, performance, and fuel burn like no other. There's been a noticeable increase in ultra-long-haul flights in recent years, driven by the arrival of new aircraft like the Airbus A350 and Boeing 787 Dreamliner, which are more capable and efficient than earlier widebodies.

However, while these flights push planes to their technical limits, they aren't particularly physically demanding on the aircraft. Longer flights are actually much less stressful on the airframe and the engines than operating multiple shorter services in a given day with few breaks in between. Naturally, multiple factors go into how stressed an aircraft is during a flight, and time between flights also matters.

The lifespan of large transport aircraft is really determined by two measurements: cycles and hours. The hours metric is as the word implies, while cycles measure the number of times an aircraft is pressurized and depressurized. This roughly corresponds to the number of flights the aircraft takes, although airliners do occasionally operate flights without fully pressurizing the cabin (these are almost always ferry flights flown when the aircraft has a pressurization issue).

Widebody aircraft operating long-haul and ultra-long-haul flights tend to rack up hours very quickly, while short-haul airliners rack up cycles quickly. However, while hours are one measure of an airframe's lifespan, the number of cycles/flights operated is a more important metric for determining how stressed the aircraft is overall. Operating multiple flights means the landing gear and brakes are used for multiple landings, while the fuel and hydraulic systems also wear with each flight.

In addition, each flight involves the engines being pushed to takeoff power and, typically, the reverse thrusters being activated upon landing (this doesn't occur with every landing, but it occurs onalmost every landing). This puts strain on the engines, whereas an aircraft operating a long-haul or ultra-long-haul flight only performs one takeoff and one landing. The number of hours an aircraft flies certainly stresses the airframe, but its cycles and the number of flights it takes physically stress the plane even more.

Hawaiian Airlines currently operates 19 717-200s. These planes are used exclusively to operate between the Hawaiian islands, which means that they routinely operate eight, nine, 10, or even 11 flights per day. Most of these flights last under 40 minutes, so they're not racking up hours as quickly as a typical widebody. However, despite the short length of these routes, the Hawaiian interisland operations are considered some of the most demanding in the world.

The aircraft's engines are routinely pushed to takeoff power, then reverse thrust is used on the runway, and then this process is repeated multiple times per day. The engines are constantly being used and rarely have time to cool down. The landing gear and brakes are used frequently, and short turnaround times mean the plane rarely rests. What's more, the environment exposes the aircraft to salt particles in the air, raising corrosion concerns and increasing stress on the airframe.

One of Hawaiian's biggest challenges was determining how these aging planes would be replaced. The engines on newer types like the A321neo and 737 MAX would not take kindly to the constant stress, while their fuel efficiency would provide little benefit. Ultimately, Alaska Air Group (which now owns the Hawaiian brand) opted to use older 737-800s from Alaska's fleet to replace the 717s, presumably scheduling extra downtime due to the aircraft's size and to allow the planes to rest more.

The A350's reputation as a 'passenger favorite' masks a more complex truth for flight crews, whose rest experiences can vary widely.

Hour and cycle limits are mainly used for pressurized aircraft because of the stresses pressurization introduces on the airframe. While other components of the aircraft also get stressed, pressurization is one of the largest stress factors. Each time the aircraft is pressurized, the fuselage expands slightly as the high pressure air inside the cabin pushes the fuselage against the low-pressure air outside. Over time, this can fatigue the fuselage, potentially resulting in microscopic cracks.

Regularly scheduled maintenance inspections uncover any potential fatigue cracks before they become a safety issue, but the pressurization cycle nonetheless puts significant stress on the fuselage. The pressurization cycle and these stress forces were a major factor in two mid-flight breakups of the de Havilland Comet in the 1950s, the world's first jet airliner. While it was not the first pressurized aircraft, no other airliner flew so high, and the intense forces from pressurization cycles fatigued the fuselage far faster than anticipated.

Metallic fuselages are particularly susceptible to the stress forces caused by pressurization, while newer carbon-composite designs are much stronger. This has allowed manufacturers to lower cabin altitudes by using higher-pressure air, which would ordinarily require a much heavier metal fuselage due to the added strengthening needed. Meanwhile, some business jets have even lower cabin altitudes while flying higher than airliners, which is justifiable as weight and fuel burn are less of a concern in private aviation.

Aircraft are designed to serve for upwards of 30 or even 35 years, so aircraft engineers design them with a specific service life in mind that covers typical usage across multiple decades. Manufacturers perform fatigue analysis to establish safe limits and inspection intervals for the aircraft, while working with regulators to determine the aircraft's lifespan. This is designed around the plane's intended mission, with widebodies and narrowbodies having vastly different hours-to-cycles ratios.

In addition to the aircraft's overall lifespan, various components may have their own maintenance intervals, with engines in particular largely on their own schedules. Overall, aircraft designers set goals for the expected service life of the aircraft and its components, and then work diligently to achieve them. In addition, the original limits set for an aircraft are often increased by the manufacturer later in the type's service life, usually via an upgrade that can be retrofitted onto pre-existing examples.

The A320 was originally designed with a service limit of 60,000 flight hours or 48,000 cycles, whichever occurs first. Later on, Airbus was able to increase these limits to 120,000 hours or 60,000 cycles, whichever occurs first. The 767, meanwhile, is designed with a limit of 50,000 cycles, fewer than the A320, but more hours. In addition, some aircraft types are converted to freighters later in their lives, and these projects often come with post-conversion limits as well.

An aircraft's age is often quoted in terms of years, but this doesn't have much of an impact on its actual remaining lifespan. While age can determine factors such as reliability and which parts need to be used, planes can easily operate for upwards of 40 years if they don't exceed their hour and cycle limits. Instead, however, planes are largely retired for economic reasons, as maintaining an old aircraft that has operated for decades is possible but rarely cheap.

As aircraft become older, they become less reliable, and maintenance costs tend to rise. In addition, airliners require multiple types of maintenance overhauls, including what are often dubbed 'D checks', where basically the entire aircraft is taken apart, which come at a huge cost. Older aircraft also burn more fuel than newer ones, which makes it much more expensive to operate aging planes. Oftentimes, airlines will retire their oldest planes right before their next major overhaul, and then they get sent for scrapping.

Because aircraft are retired for economic reasons, it's actually rare to see them near their service limitations. What's more, aging planes tend to be used less often towards the end of their lives due to their high operating costs, which further slows the pace at which the aircraft approaches its service limits. As such, some aircraft remain in service for upwards of 40 years with small cargo operators and in nations such as Iran, as well as older 737s and A320s that are nearly 40 years old, since they remain within their hour and cycle limits.

A former Ohio State University graduate student could not convince a federal appeals court to overturn a jury’s verdict in favor of the school in her sexual harassment lawsuit because she failed to show that her trial was unfairly prejudiced, according to a Tuesday decision.

The plaintiff in Huang v. The Ohio State University alleged that a faculty advisor subjected her to unwanted touching and that, when she resisted his advances, he retaliated by manipulating her candidacy exam for an engineering doctorate. She brought claims under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act as well as the 14th Amendment’s due process right to bodily integrity.

A district granted summary judgment for Ohio State on the plaintiff’s Title VII claims, holding that she was not an employee of the university within the law’s definition and therefore could not bring those claims. At trial, a jury held for the plaintiff’s advisor. She appealed to the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, which reversed and ordered a new trial.

At the second trial, the jury determined that the plaintiff was not an employee because Ohio State and the advisor did not have the “right to control the manner and means by which [the plaintiff] accomplished her work.” The jury also ruled in favor of the advisor on the plaintiff’s 14th Amendment claim.

The plaintiff again appealed to the 6th Circuit, alleging that the district court incorrectly instructed the jury on what facts were relevant to its determination of whether she was an employee. She also claimed that the court abused its discretion in excluding certain evidence and that it improperly prohibited her from requesting economic damages in her closing argument.

Each of these claims failed, the 6th Circuit held.

The plaintiff forfeited her challenge to the court’s jury instructions because she failed to raise it, despite the district court specifically asking whether the parties had objections or edits to its instructions. To grant a third trial on this point “would be unfair to the court and the defendants,” the 6th Circuit said.

In her evidentiary argument, the plaintiff claimed that the district court improperly prevented her from submitting portions of a slideshow containing the results of an Ohio State campus climate survey on sexual assault. She argued that the presentation would have made her allegations more plausible.

However, the plaintiff failed to include the relevant slides in her appeal, leaving the 6th Circuit unable to conclude whether they had sufficient weight to affect the jury’s verdict. The appeals court also could not determine whether the slides offered anything “above and beyond” other testimony in the case.

In all, the plaintiff failed to show that any of her allegations amounted to unfair prejudice warranting a new trial.

The case represents one in a handful of recent jury decisions to favor employers facing Title VII lawsuits. In March, a jury held for Haribo of America in a former employee’s discrimination and retaliation case, determining that the plaintiff’s dismissal was a mutually agreed-upon separation between the parties rather than retaliation.

And in a 2025 case, a jury sided with Levi Strauss in a lawsuit alleging that a former executive did not receive a promotion because of her pregnancy, gender and age.

Flipping a House Isn’t Paying Like It Used To. Is Renting the Better Bet?

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The typical home flip generated just $60,526 in gross profit in the second quarter of 2026, down from $71,000 a year earlier, according to new data—before accounting for renovation costs or other expenses.

It's a shrinking cushion for investors who have long relied on a simple formula to fuel their business: Buy low, apply elbow grase, and sell high. But with elevated borrowing costs and renovation expenses eating into margins, there's much less room than there used to be for anything to go wrong.

The report, from real estate data analytics company ATTOM, found that flipping returns have been declining for two years, with the typical gross return falling to 21.5%, from 27.6% a year earlier.

"Flippers are still making money in most markets, but the typical return continues to narrow," Rob Barber, CEO of ATTOM, said via press release. "The second-quarter numbers continue the general downward trend in profit margins and gross profits we have seen over the past two years."

So, why not skip the resale altogether and rent out the property instead?

After all, the promise of passive income—collecting rent every month while building equity—helped fuel a wave of small investors buying up homes earlier this decade when borrowing costs were low and rents were on a meteoric rise.

But the economics have changed for landlords, too.

Potential rental yields declined in more than half of U.S. counties with comparable data between 2025 and 2026, according to separate ATTOM research. That's to say nothing of the work involved in managing a rental property, which, as Realtor.com® previously reported, has proved to be anything but passive.

In a market defined by high carrying costs and shrinking returns, the better strategy may come down to which risks an investor can afford to weather—and for how long.

Despite shrinking profits, an index of fix-and-flip market conditions remained in expansion territory in the third quarter of 2026, according to a November survey of roughly 280 investors from John Burns Research and Consulting and Kiavi.

Some 66% of respondents reported selling most of their properties over the previous 12 months in line with their initial after-repair value (ARV) estimates—the amount a property is expected to fetch after renovations.

“Flipping can be profitable in a hot market with limited inventory, where homes sell quickly and buyers pay a premium for updated properties,” explains Realtor.com economist Jiayi Xu.

By purchasing below market value and making improvements that buyers look for, investors can boost the home's after-repair value and pocket the difference. In the right conditions, that means faster returns than holding the property long term.

And the slowdown may be creating opportunities to buy at a deeper discount.

Competition for fixer-uppers has eased in some markets, particularly Texas and Florida, where 44% and 37% of surveyed investors, respectively, reported less competition than usual, according to the November survey.

"Fewer bidders doesn't make every deal a good one," cautions Charles Goodwin, vice president of investor mortgage sales at Figure, Kiavi's parent company. “What it does is give well-capitalized investors with a defined exit strategy more room to be patient and negotiate. That's where experienced operators tend to separate themselves."

The trouble is, it's becoming harder to accurately predict what that exit will pay.

Four in 10 surveyed flippers reported closing sales at lower prices than a year earlier, according to the November John Burns–Kiavi survey. Meanwhile, 20% said most of their properties sold below their initial ARV estimates.

Among those who fell short of their targets, 84% said they had overestimated the eventual sale price, while 57% reported underestimating their costs.

Goodwin warns against basing resale projections on comparable sales from a stronger market.

"If a deal only works with last spring's comps, it probably doesn't work," he says.

And even when investors get their resale estimates right, renovations can consume a substantial share of the proceeds.

The average renovation cost was approximately $72,000 per flipped home in the third quarter of 2026, down from $80,000 a year earlier. Yet renovations still consumed a larger share of the average sale price, rising from 16% to 17%, according to the survey.

Financing adds another layer of risk, particularly when a project takes longer than expected.

The average effective interest rate reported by surveyed flippers who obtained new financing was 9.3% in the third quarter. At that rate, an investor carrying a hypothetical $320,000 interest-only loan would spend roughly $2,480 in interest for each additional month before selling.

Just one extra month would wipe out more than three times the $800 saved over a six-month hold by securing a loan at 9.3% rather than the previous year's 9.8% rate, according to the survey's calculations.

And even when a flip does succeed, Xu notes that taxes can reduce the final profit. Properties acquired primarily for resale as part of a flipping business generally generate ordinary income rather than qualifying for preferential long-term capital gains rates.

With strong headwinds to flipping a property, renting out a fixer-upper can offer a better risk exposure, especially for investors prepared to hold the property long term, according to Xu.

“If you prefer steady income, long-term wealth building, and lower risk, renting is generally the wiser choice,” she says.

Over time, owners may also benefit from property appreciation and tax deductions associated with operating a rental, making the rental path more attractive for those focused on building wealth.

And while asking rents were in decline for more than three years, the market is beginning to stabilize. The median asking rent rose 0.1% year over year to $1,692 in October 2026, its first annual increase since June 2023, according to the latest rental report from Realtor.com

Of course, renting out a fixer upper isn't entirely without risk.

Potential gross rental yields for three-bedroom properties declined in 54.8% of U.S. counties with comparable data between 2025 and 2026, according to ATTOM's annual single-family rental report.

"Even though rents and wages are rising in many markets, record-high home prices are compressing yields," says Barber.

And those projected yields don't account for the ongoing costs of owning and maintaining the property.

Katie Lyon, a rental property owner and host of the Landlord Diaries podcast, previously told Realtor.com that even profitable long-term rentals can leave landlords with little room for unexpected expenses.

"If the water heater goes out, you're back three years of cash flow," she says.

A simple benchmark can help would-be investors gauge whether a rental deal makes sense. The “1% Rule" suggests that monthly gross rent should equal roughly 1% of the purchase price.

When it comes to fixer-uppers, seasoned investors rely on a similar guiding principle. The "70% Rule," advises paying no more than 70% of a property's after-repair value, minus estimated renovation costs.

Ignoring this benchmark can quickly erode profits, especially in today's market where flip margins are already thin, warns Ian Hart, a seasoned housing investor and founder of Barndominium.org.

And the latest returns suggest that buying at the lowest possible price isn't necessarily the answer.

Homes purchased for $100,000 to $200,000 generated a typical gross flipping return of 28% in the second quarter of 2026, according to ATTOM. By contrast, properties acquired for $50,000 or less generated a typical gross loss of $15,000 before renovation expenses.

The November John Burns–Kiavi survey offers another measure of how cautious investors have become: About two-thirds of respondents said they wouldn't pay more than 70% of a property's estimated ARV, while the average maximum was just 67%.

Perhaps most importantly, though, buyers should look for exit flexibility. The best fixer-upper deals are those that work in more than one scenario—whether as a flip in a hot resale market or a rental in an area with strong tenant demand.

Medical associations and providers notched a legal victory over the federal government in a court ruling vacating federal funding requirements for Ryan White clinics involving gender-affirming care.

Those restrictions would have required recipients of Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program funds to not acknowledge the identities of transgender patients or use funds for gender-affirming care.

It was challenged by the HIV Medicine Association of the Infectious Diseases Society of America (HIVMA), the American Academy of HIV Medicine and the International Association of Providers of AIDS Care in June (see that coverage below), who argued that the policy was contrary to Congress' intent when establishing the program under law in 1991, lacked sufficient explanation and discriminated against transgender people in violation of the First and Fifth Amendments.

The Nov. 19 decision from Judge William G. Young, of the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts, prohibits the government from opposing its conditions in regard to all federal funding and permanently blocks any similar policies from being implemented by associational plaintifs' members, "which covers virtually every HIV care provider in the United States," said Lambda Legal, which acted as co-counsel for the plaintiffs.

"The ruling is a decisive and important victory for transgender people with HIV and protects the ability of physicians and other health care professionals to offer evidence-based care without political interference," Philip Bolduc, M.D., chair of HIVMA, said in a statement reacting to the decision.

Young's opinion and order opened with an acknowledgement that there are "sincere and deeply felt views" regarding transgender identity, and that the issue at question was of legal regulation rather than "the propriety of individual gender expression."

Still, the judge concluded his opinion by writing that the administration's conditions "are based in animus, loathing and discrimination. They do not stand for justice—in fact, they defy it. They are contrary to the laws of this nation and cannot continue to stand."

A group of medical associations and providers has filed a lawsuit challenging new federal funding requirements for Ryan White clinics involving gender-affirming care.

The new requirements, issued in a recent HRSA Notice of Funding Opportunity, require recipients of the Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program funds to not acknowledge the identities of transgender patients or use funds for gender-affirming care. The restrictions undermine the very intent of the program, the plaintiffs say, and violate anti-discrimination components of the Fifth Amendment.

“The Ryan White Program was created as a public health intervention to address the needs of low-income, vulnerable populations who otherwise would not have access to life-saving care,” Melanie Thompson, M.D., co-chair of a primary care guidance panel for the HIV Medicine Association, said in a press release. “It is crucial that Ryan White clinicians be allowed to continue to deliver the most scientifically appropriate treatments for all patients, including transgender individuals, without discrimination if we are to improve health outcomes for all and end the U.S. HIV epidemic."

The lawsuit alleges (PDF) that HHS and HRSA violated the Administrative Procedure Act by exceeding their statutory authority and imposing restrictions that conflict with the Ryan White statute. The plaintiffs ask the court to block enforcement of the funding restrictions.

The case was filed by Lambda Legal and HSF Kramer on behalf of the American Academy of HIV Medicine, the HIV Medicine Association and the International Association of Providers of AIDS Care, plus two providers.

A preliminary hearing will likely be scheduled in a few weeks. Lambda Legal is “cautiously optimistic” about the future of the case, José Abrigo, senior attorney and HIV project director at Lambda Legal, told reporters on a webinar on Thursday afternoon. He called the administration’s actions “unlawful,” and said gender-affirming care in this context includes all types of care, including primary care and mental healthcare.

Specifically, the HRSA Notice of Funding Opportunity reads: “Discretionary awards shall not be used to fund, promote, encourage, subsidize, or facilitate … denial by the recipient of the sex binary in humans, or the belief that sex is a chosen or mutable characteristic.”

Some providers are already preemptively complying with the restrictions, Abrigo said, particularly in Tennessee and Kentucky. It is unclear how many other providers will also stop gender-affirming care. In certain cases, it’s up to individual states or departments of health to enforce the restrictions. It’s possible that medical records could be audited and reviewed for indications of pronouns, per Thompson. “It does matter what someone’s gender and pronouns are listed as,” Thompson said on the webinar, explaining that medical records are shared between providers and help ensure affirming care.

If a clinic is found to be non-compliant, the repercussions are steep, Thompson added. “The penalty for being found out is severe, and puts an entire program’s funding in jeopardy,” she said on the webinar.

Forty percent of American Academy of HIV Medicine members rely on Ryan White funding, of which 40% voluntarily report providing gender-affirming medical care. For low-income trans individuals with HIV, there aren’t many alternative options, per Abrigo. They may seek care through private insurance or potentially through Medicaid, but typically the population relies on Ryan White clinics for services.

Gender-affirming care restrictions under the Trump administration have been commonplace. Over 40 hospitals have reportedly stopped some gender-affirming care services for minors in response to pressure. Last week, the Cleveland Clinic promised the Department of Justice (DOJ) to commit $2 million to detransitioning services and a “decades-long” agreement not to perform gender-affirming care on minors. Delivering most of these services is currently illegal for minors under Ohio state law. Last month, DOJ struck a similar deal with Texas Children’s.

For months, experts have been sounding the alarm about the U.S. HIV response slipping into dangerous instability. In April, the International Association of Providers of AIDS Care (IAPAC) released a data brief finding that 61% of providers have reported at least one HIV service disruption and 68% have seen operational changes such as staffing reductions, service cutbacks and program closures. This was attributed to a combination of policy decisions, funding cuts and social determinants like loss of insurance coverage.

“It’s a catastrophe in the making," José M. Zúñiga, AIPAC's president and CEO, told reporters on the webinar. "It was already happening. This adds fuel."

The canyon also comes with a literary history: In 1960, Jack Kerouac retreated to a cabin here owned by Lawrence Ferlinghetti, the poet and co-founder of City Lights Bookstore. According to Hornig, the canyon has also been home to the film director Sam Peckinpah and the Disney illustrator Ron Dias.

Built in 1984, the single-level home has three bedrooms, two full bathrooms, and 1,489 square feet of living space. A wraparound porch overlooks the forest.

“Luxury doesn’t always have to be expressed through scale or elaborate architecture,” Hornig says. “Here, the luxury is the relationship between the home and its surroundings.”

Beating the national Social Security average is one thing, but whether that check actually covers retirement is a far tougher test.

By Kailey Hagen, CFP® – Nov 22, 2026 at 10:00AM EST

Where does your monthly benefit check sit compared to the rest of the country? As of October 2026, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA), the average monthly benefit for retired workers is $2,092.17. That amounts to about $25,106.04 per year. This single figure covers everyone from those who just claimed at 62 to retirees well into their 90s.

Averages depend heavily on who you are and when you claimed. For instance, the average for people aged 62 is $1,424.40 per month, while those aged 70 receive an average of $2,274.68. Gender also creates a significant gap in the final numbers.

Averages also vary between men and women across the entire retired-worker population. As of December 2025, according to the SSA, these were the monthly averages for men and women across all age groups. They reflect the combined impact of different career earnings and claiming choices.

Landing above the $2,092.17 benchmark isn't a matter of luck. The SSA calculates your benefit based on an average of your monthly wages across your 35 highest-earning years. It adjusts earlier wages for inflation and runs them through the primary insurance amount (PIA) formula. Higher lifetime earnings generally result in a larger monthly check.

Claiming age is the other primary lever for beating the average. Full retirement age (FRA) ranges from 66 to 67, depending on your birth year, and it's 67 for everyone born in 1960 or later. Claiming at age 62 can reduce your check by up to 30%, while waiting until 70 earns delayed retirement credits worth 8% per year.

While beating the average is helpful, it's rarely enough to cover a full retirement. Social Security is designed to replace only about 40% of your pre-retirement income. However, the average senior household headed by someone 65 or older spends $61,432 per year, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). That's much higher than the $25,106.04 annual average benefit.

A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027 to help checks keep pace with inflation. That adjustment adds about $75 per month to the $2,092.17 average. Even with these raises, most retirees rely on savings or other income sources to bridge the gap between their benefits and their total living costs. Beating the Social Security average is not the goal; covering your actual retirement expenses is.

If you want to increase your odds of claiming an above-average benefit, there are three things you must do. First, work at least 35 years before you apply for Social Security. This will ensure you have no zero-income years included in your benefit calculation, which would weigh down your checks.

Second, do what you can to increase your income today. The larger your average income throughout your career, the larger your future Social Security checks will be.

Third, choose your claiming age carefully. Claiming Social Security early could make sense if you have a short life expectancy or don't have much in personal savings. Otherwise, you may get a larger lifetime benefit by delaying Social Security until your FRA or possibly until you qualify for your largest checks at age 70.

“While research suggests GLP-1s could reduce brain inflammation, improve blood vessel health, and even protect neurons, much broader, randomized clinical trials on GLP-1s and brain health, including in people with Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, are needed,” she said.

GLP-1 medications like semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy, mimic the GLP-1 hormone. Tirzepatide, the active ingredient in Mounjaro and Zepbound, also targets a second hormone pathway called GIP.

These medications have been shown to produce significant weight loss and improvements in cardiometabolic health.

Choose an SBA loan over an online loan if you can wait weeks. The SBA’s guarantee comes with a capped rate and terms up to 10 years for most uses, 25 for real estate. Expect to pledge collateral on loans over $50,000, sign a personal guarantee, and, as a startup, put in at least 10% of the cost.

Owners turn to online lenders for speed. In the Federal Reserve’s 2025 Small Business Credit Survey, 60% of firms that borrowed from online lenders said their borrowing costs ran higher than expected, against 32% of large-bank borrowers.

An SBA loan is slower and cheaper. The U.S. Small Business Administration guarantees up to 85% of most 7(a) loans, which banks and other approved lenders make. In return for that backing, SBA rules cap a variable rate at the banks’ benchmark prime rate, now 7.00%, plus 3 to 6.5 percentage points, depending on the loan’s size.

Say a landscaper owes $120,000 on online term loans repaid over two years at 30% interest, about $6,710 a month. A 10-year 7(a) loan at a fixed 11% cuts that to about $1,650. The longer term costs more interest in total, about $78,000 over 10 years against $41,000 on the online loans. Extra payments shrink that, and a 7(a) loan shorter than 15 years carries no prepayment fee.

A 7(a) loan of up to $5 million can cover working capital, equipment, real estate, debt refinancing, or starting or buying a business. A 504 loan, made through a nonprofit Certified Development Company, finances buildings, land, and long-lived machinery at a fixed rate. SBA microloans of up to $50,000 come from nonprofit lenders.

Under the SBA’s lending rules, every owner of 20% or more personally guarantees the whole loan, and a loan over $50,000 needs collateral, though a lender can’t refuse you for weak collateral alone. A business open a year or less must put in at least 10% of the total cost, and so must most buyers of a business.

To get an SBA loan, start with a bank that knows your business, and ask whether it’s an SBA Preferred Lender, one allowed to approve loans without the SBA’s review, which can take up to 10 business days. Or describe your needs in the SBA’s Lender Match tool, which lists interested lenders two business days later.

Apply once you know a big cost is coming, and keep online loans for needs that can’t wait weeks.

A Guide to NYC’s Little Italy—and the Culinary Gems Hidden Among the Tourist Traps

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Contrary to other tourist-focused neighborhoods in New York (looking at you, Times Square), Little Italy boasts an innate charm that resonates with visitors and locals alike. Sandwiched between SoHo and the Lower East Side, this jewel box area is home to long-standing, family-owned restaurants and authentic Italian bakeries, the majority of which have been neighborhood staples for decades. Yet despite its touristy atmosphere, a unique feeling of old school New York magic hangs in the air; beyond the personality-less stands and souvenir shops, framed family photos on restaurant walls and structural imperfections of tenement buildings speak to a bygone area of the city’s past, evoking a sense of nostalgia that so many neighborhoods lack. Pop down to Mulberry Street and see for yourself—though be sure you know which spots are actually legit (that’s where we come in).

A note on how we’re defining the area: Over the years, Little Italy’s boundaries have shrunk to focus on the touristic area of Mulberry Street and its immediate surroundings. While the southern and western boundaries of Canal Street and Lafayette Streets remain unchanged, the historic easterly Bowery boundary has arguably shifted to Elizabeth Street; similarly, the northerly boundary of Kenmare has also condensed down, according to many, to Broome Street. For the sake of this piece, we’re using the boundaries of Lafayette Street, Canal Street, Elizabeth Street, and Broome Street as a happy medium.

Located in the heart of Little Italy, Umberto’s Clam House is best known for its—you guessed it, all things clams. The restaurant quickly garnered attention shortly after opening in 1972, when mobster Joe "Crazy Joe" Gallo was shot in the restaurant’s dining room. Like its mafia-related history, the food at Umberto’s is as Italian-American as it gets. Baked clams and fried calamari are the appetizer list’s biggest show-stoppers, while superbly chilled clams on the half-shell pair exquisitely with equally cold (and generously sized) cocktails from the bar. Seafood-focused mains are the name of the game here, including the garlicky linguini and clam sauce and recently resurfaced scungilli with pasta served with piping hot red sauce.

Address: 132 Mulberry Street, New York, NY 10013

For Italian-American eats in a less stereotypically Little Italy ambiance, Il Cortile is the way to go. Contrary to the old school Italian vibes found in the majority of the neighborhood’s restaurants, Il Cortile’s atrium-style glass ceiling and plant-filled room feel more like dining al fresco in Trastevere than the heart of Mulberry Street. The family-owned restaurant’s neighborhood roots date back to 1975, with a solid focus on Northern Italian cuisine. The homemade cheese ravioli and wood-fired pizzas are always a safe bet, though for meat lovers, springing for the Fettuccine di Parma made with sweet sausage and wild mushrooms won’t disappoint—and be sure to save room for the pillowy soft tiramisu made with creamy mascarpone and imported Italian ladyfingers drenched in espresso.

Address: 125 Mulberry St, New York, NY 10013

Original Vincent’s, often called The Original Vincent’s Clam Bar, is home to one of the richest restaurant success stories in Little Italy. Founded in 1904 by Giuseppe and Carmela Siano, the business began as a sidewalk street cart serving up fresh seafood and jars of homemade red sauce, available in mild, medium, and hot. Today, the restaurant sits on the corner of Mott and Hester Streets, and is still known for its signature gravies, baked clams, and linguini-based pasta dishes (don’t skip the frutti di mare). As in its earliest days, Original Vincent’s still sells jars of their red sauce to-go, making replicating the restaurant’s pasta dishes at home a breeze—simply grab a bottle of Chianti, cue up your Louis Primo playlist and voila.

Paesano of Mulberry Street is home to one of the coziest interiors in Little Italy, offering a peaceful refuge from the neighborhood bustle. Rustic twig bunches and leafy plants strung with twinkle lights hang from the ceiling, while traditional Italian frescos and framed family photographs adorn the whitewashed brick walls. Originally opened in 1961, the restaurant remains owned by the same family and continues to be known for their Southern Italian recipes, including linguini with clam sauce, wood-fired pizzas, and savory chicken marsala drenched in sautéed mushrooms. Meat lovers, don’t skip the restaurant’s signature Veal A La Paesano, featuring a juicy and tender veal base layered with thin slices of prosciutto, steamed broccoli, and gooey mozzarella cheese finished with succulent brown gravy.

Address: 136 Mulberry St, New York, NY 10013

For serious pizza lovers, a trip to Lombardi’s is a no-brainer. Tucked on the corner of Mott and Spring, this neighborhood institution is credited as America’s first pizzeria, originally founded by Gennaro Lombardi in 1905. Lombardi quickly rose to fame for his large, heavily-topped pies, which he baked in an on-site, coal-and-gas oven. The result? Smoky crusts topped with perfectly balanced tomato sauce and gobs of whole milk mozzarella—along with sweet Italian sausage and various other accoutrements. The interior oozes with classic New York charm, featuring tables clad in red-and-white checkered cloths and framed family photographs strung along the red-hued walls. Head’s up, the spot is cash only, and while there’s an ATM on site, we recommend coming prepared to avoid the withdrawal fee.

Address: 30 Spring St, New York, NY 10012

15 Best American Movies Of All Time Ranked, Including Once Upon A Time...In Hollywood

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

There have been approximately 500,000 feature-length films released to date, spanning several genres including action, drama, comedy, sci-fi, and more. This total includes titles released in Hollywood, along with other major cinema markets like India (Bollywood) and China (with its ever thriving box office). Of this total, around 50,000 of these are major American movies (which includes studio films, independent projects, and more).

The United States is often considered to be the hub for cinema, with the longstanding presence of this business in cities like New York, Chicago, and Los Angeles (especially the latter). Some of the biggest movies ever made have been in the U.S., with franchises like Star Wars and the Marvel Cinematic Universe both being funded and made by Americans.

Not only does the United States have some of the biggest movies, but the country is also home to some of the very best movies made as well. Titles like Citizen Kane, The Godfather, and the original Star Wars trilogy are all products of American auteurs from U.S.-based studios. Not only that, but these successful projects span nearly a century, with films like the aforementioned Citizen Kane debuting roughly 85 years ago (and with plenty of amazing movies still being made in modern times, too).

When looking at the best American movies ever made, other titles come to mind as well. Films from directors like Steven Spielberg, Quentin Tarantino, and the Coen brothers, along with classics like The Wizard of Oz and It’s a Wonderful Life, all deserve to be in contention for this prestigious list.

Once again, one of the very best films ever made is Citizen Kane, which was released in 1941. Although it may have made its debut before the United States even officially entered World War II, and despite its lack of color, this Orson Welles masterpiece is the kind of movie that is way ahead of its time in virtually every aspect.

Citizen Kane, which was written, directed by, and starring Orson Welles, tells the story of the life of Charles Foster Kane. Kane’s life, from childhood to old age, is portrayed in the film, and includes his rise to owner of the New York Inquirer and an ambitious run for Governor. All of this is told within the framework of a mystery presented in the film’s very first scene, which shows Kane’s death, and his very famous final words: “Rosebud.” Citizen Kane then explores that mystery and teases the cryptic meaning behind them.

There are many reasons why Citizen Kane is such a classic. Welles’ acting is exceptional, as is his innovative and industry-changing direction. There’s also the usage of non-linear storytelling, which had seldom, if ever, been done in mainstream movies up until this point. Citizen Kane popularized this tool, thus paving the way for countless classic non-linear movies (Christopher Nolan’s movies come to mind here, as the director is known for his affection for this kind of narrative).

Stanley Kubrick was an English director, but one of his most famous films, 2001: A Space Odyssey, was made by an American studio and stars American actors. As such, 2001 more than belongs on this list, and deserves its place among the best American movies ever made. This movie is truly extraordinary, with visuals that are still impressive decades later. Additionally, Kubrick’s hard-to-wrap your head around story deserves great praise, too, as it paved the way for other filmmakers, like David Lynch, to explore the abstract in their own projects.

Some movies on this list were overlooked upon their initial release, only to receive acclaim in the years that followed. Others, meanwhile, were praised right away, and 1991s The Silence of the Lambs belongs firmly in that category. The film, which stars Jodie Foster and Anthony Hopkins, won the “Big Five” awards at the Oscars — Best Picture, Best Actor, Best Actress, and Best Adapted Screenplay.

And, just like many other films on this list, the story of The Silence of the Lambs is likely very familiar. The film follows FBI trainee Clarice Starling as she’s tasked with meeting the infamous serial killer Hannibal Lecter, with the hope that Hopkins’ character will offer some insight that may lead to the capture of another killer, Buffalo Bill, who’s still on the loose.

This movie remains one of the best American films ever made, even 35 years after its release, in large part because of the performances by Foster and Hopkins. Both give some of the best turns in recent Hollywood history, and performances that have greatly influenced the future of the industry, too. For example, Hopkins’ portrayal of a serial killer has become the basis for actors playing murderers ever since.

Halloween is one of the simplest films on this list, with the story of a masked killer returning to his hometown and wreaking havoc among the residents, who fight to stay alive amidst his murder spree. Yet, that simplicity doesn’t go against John Carpenter’s film, which is not just one of the best horror movies ever, but one of the best films ever, too.

The straightforwardness of Halloween’s narrative is actually one of the film’s greatest strengths, as it allows the characters, like Michael Myers, Dr. Loomis, and Laurie Strode, the chance to truly shine. In turn, the suspense and terror of the film’s story is allowed to grow, too. Ultimately, Halloween (1978) is a masterpiece in horror storytelling, with expertly executed scares and a main villain that remains one of the best movie characters ever.

Steven Spielberg has made so many classic films in his career, but one that stands near (or at the very top) of the list is E.T. The Extra-Terrestrial. The 1982 film focuses on a young boy who discovers a gentle alien stranded on Earth, and the two then bond as they evade government officials and attempt to send E.T. home.

This movie truly has it all — it’s funny, relatable (with an excellent portrayal of kids at this age), and, above all else, extremely emotional. The film’s ending, where E.T. goes home on his spaceship, is simultaneously heartwarming and heartbreaking at the same time, even all these years later.

Actor James “Jimmy” Stewart served in the United States military during World War II and, just one year after the end of the conflict, starred in what proved to be the biggest film of his career (and one often watched during this time of year) — It’s a Wonderful Life. The film, which is loosely based on Charles Dickens’ A Christmas Carol, is essentially the perfect holiday movie.

The story follows George Bailey, a down-on-his-luck man who plans to take his own life on Christmas Eve. However, his plans are thwarted when he’s visited by a Guardian Angel, who shows him why life is still worth living. It’s a Wonderful Life is one of the most emotionally touching movies ever made, making it one of the best movies ever made, period, in turn.

Casablanca may be named after a city in Morocco, but it’s firmly an American film, and one of the country’s very best, in fact. The film, which was released in 1943 (at the height of World War II) and stars Humphrey Bogart, has the classic redemption arc for its leading character. Bogart’s Rick Blaine claims that he won’t risk his life for any cause (or anyone), but, over the course of the movie, changes his mind and becomes a staunch anti-fascist.

It’s the kind of character arc that has been repeated countless times since, perhaps most notably in the very first Star Wars film, which saw Han Solo go from being ambivalent towards the Empire to becoming a full-blown Rebel hero. This makes Casablanca not only an incredible movie, but very influential as well. Not only that, but the film is also beautifully made, with exceptional acting, writing (excellent dialogue in particular), and cinematography.

Bar The Godfather trilogy, Francis Ford Coppola’s most famous work is arguably 1979s Apocalypse Now. The film had a tumultuous production, but these hardships certainly paid off in the end. Apocalypse Now is, in so many ways, the perfect war movie. It shows the harrowing realities of the conflict in Vietnam, and gets deep into the psyche of the characters at the heart of the story.

The Coen Brothers’ best film, Fargo remains a masterclass in tone, suspense, and humor, even thirty years later. The film, which is set in the frozen town of Fargo, North Dakota, focuses on a world of crime that comes to the small town after a car salesman hires hitmen to kill his wife. The plot may sound dark, but the Coen Brothers inject the film with so much life that it actually ends up being very funny, emotional, and, above all else, a true whirlwind of emotions.

The Wizard of Oz had plenty of reported issues during production, including drug use, infighting among the cast and crew, and going over budget. Yet, none of that affected the final product, which remains one of the best American movies ever made. The story of Dorothy and her dog Toto traveling to Oz, meeting a group of friends, and attempting to get back to Kansas is simply timeless. It feels like a modern-day fairy tale in this regard, and this movie is so good that it’s no wonder that the franchise is still going nearly 90 years later.

Why Autistic Employees Are Targeted By Bullies

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Workplace bullying damages health and drains productivity. For autistic employees, it perpetuates the cycle of abuse that starts much earlier. A 2026 review confirmed that autistic students experience disproportionately high levels of bullying, with most estimates falling between 40% and 70% (Heredia-Oliva et al., 2026), and it is a major source of stress for them.

The painful pattern then follows people into work. And many work environments prove fertile grounds for both overt (threatening, shouting) and especially covert (backstabbing, sabotaging,

In experiments, neurotypical people reported liking autistic people less than neurotypical people, with vague explanations like perceived “awkwardness” (Alkhaldi et al, 2021). Workplace stressors have the potential to magnify the dislike. Autistic employees, with their spiky profiles of abilities, are more likely to be on extreme ends of performance. High performers often experience veiled violence like sabotage or having crucial information withheld due to coworker envy; low performers face overt hostility, which makes their performance decline rather than improve (Jensen et al., 2014). Add autistic tendencies of direct communication, including possibly challenging inefficiencies and misconduct (Hartman & Hartman, 2024), along with a focus on work over socializing, and the target is set.

Autistic people's nervous systems often have less capacity to recover from social stress (Cheng et al., 2020), and autistic adults are already at elevated risk of developing mental and physical health issues due to a mismatch between their needs and the environments, sensory stress, effortful masking, and other forms of chronic stress (Botha & Frost, 2020; Dhanasekara et al., 2023). Past bullying also leaves a psychological trace: 87.4% of autistic adults showed signs of fear of being laughed at, compared with 22.6% of non-autistic adults (Leader et al., 2018).

Multiple vulnerabilities create compounding effects. Years of mistreatment result in hypervigilance and keep the body's threat response switched on. Each new injury lands on a system that never fully recovered from the last. To make things worse, the lack of resources often associated with unemployment, with only about 3 in 10 working-age autistic people in the UK employed (Buckland, 2024), leaves fewer options for recovery, seeking help, or leaving a toxic job.

Reporting bullying at work is generally a thankless task. Reports are often ignored or dismissed: in a nationally representative U.S. survey, 60% of employers reacted negatively when bullying was reported (Namie, 2021), and targets describe HR as regularly siding with bullies (Boddy & Boulter, 2025). Moreover, skillful manipulators, which many bullies are, can present themselves as victims.

Autistic people, however, face additional barriers to successfully reporting bullying and being heard.

Most organizations say they are committed to healthy workplaces, but their methods for handling bullying often make things worse. Those methods fail autistic employees and others dealing with trauma or overwhelm in at least three ways.

They are reactive. Action typically begins only after bullying is proven. But the first line of defense against bullying and any other hazard at work is prevention (Praslova et al., 2022). Preventing harm is both more ethical and more practical than repairing it.

They put the burden on the target. Targets are told to document what was said and done, when, and who was present, then discuss it calmly with a supervisor. How many people can realistically do this while doing their jobs, under stress, and dealing with trauma? The process appears to be designed to make it easy to reject the complaint because of the “tone” or the target “being emotional.”

They try to fix personalities. Bullies may act out their entitlement, fragile egos, and poor self-control, but bullying flourishes where the environment lets it go undetected and rewards it with resources or advancement (Praslova et al., 2022; Salin, 2003). Change the environment, and you are more likely to change the behavior.

Remove the rewards for bullying. Avoid internal competition for scarce resources. Distribute resources fairly and transparently, and balance individual rewards with team rewards. Realistic workloads reduce bullying used to offload work onto others, and transparency may take some of the fuel out of envy. Transparent decision-making makes rumors and backstabbing less likely to pay off (Praslova et al., 2022).

Set standards and train for them. A clear policy should state that bullying is not tolerated, and name its covert forms, not just its overt ones (Praslova et al., 2022). Train everyone in respectful communication, and train new managers specifically for complex interpersonal situations. Ideally, facilitate professional support from psychologists trained in workplace well-being.

Build civility and voice together. Some people mistake authenticity for a license to be cruel. Others try to buy “calm” by silencing people. Neither works. Bullying of autistic people is a predictable outcome of systems that fail to check assumptions, reward sameness and conformity, and make targets prove their own reality. Never before have young people faced so many challenges. Young Germans are asking: Will I be better off later in life than my parents are now?

It is becoming increasingly difficult for young people in Germany to find a job after finishing professional training or university. And finding an affordable place to live in a big city and finally starting to live independently is nearly impossible.

Nor do young people feel that politicians are finally taking action on issues that matter to them, such as climate protection. The climate conference in Antalya, Turkey, is a case in point. It therefore shouldn't be a surprise that young people across Europe are taking to the streets this Friday to make their voices heard. In Germany, Gen Z protests took place in 73 cities, coinciding with World Children's Day.

What particularly infuriates young people is being talked about rather than spoken to. Equally infuriating is seeing their protests branded as criminal before they have even begun, all because of the actions of a small minority.

One example from the political realm was when the youth wing of the conservative Christian Democratic Union/Christian Social Union (CDU/CSU) party in Berlin-Mitte suggested deploying the German army against demonstrations.

It can also be seen in the media, such as when Bild, Germany's highest-circulation tabloid, ran the headline: "Young, male, known to the police — who rioted after the demonstrations and where chaos threatens today."

"Young people are taking to the streets because they feel they are not being heard. And, of course, that feeling is strongly reinforced when the press devotes no more than a brief paragraph to their demands while focusing the rest of its coverage on how these protests could become problematic," says Erik Sczygiol, who until recently was deputy secretary-general of the Federal Conference of Student Representatives. The report is titled "Succeeding Generations: Empowering young people to chart the future."

The United Nations surveyed 109,000 people across 73 countries. Some of the findings are encouraging. Young people worldwide are better educated than any generation before them. Today, 78% of adolescents complete at least upper-secondary education. Yet finding a job is becoming increasingly difficult, while informal employment is on the rise across the globe.

This economic uncertainty has far-reaching consequences for young people. According to the report, financial independence is often not achieved until one's mid-to-late twenties, and a stable partnership and parenthood are often not realities until one's early-to-mid thirties.

Nor do young people have a seat at the political decision-making table when it comes to shaping future reforms in the labor market, housing or climate protection. Florian Brod, 25, points out: "Almost half of the world's population is under 30, yet only 2.6% of parliamentarians worldwide are under 30."

Young people need and want change to protect their livelihoods and those of future generations. "Yet those who will have to live longest with the consequences of today's decisions are precisely the ones who have hardly any representation in the political decision-making process," says Brod.

At least Brod is somewhat closer to the political decision-making process. He has served as a Youth Delegate for Sustainable Development for almost two years. In this role, he represents the interests of young people in Germany at United Nations conferences. This is certainly an important step towards giving his generation a greater say in political processes.

Nevertheless, both Florian Brod and Erik Sczygiol, who represents students, have had similar experiences. They often participate in conferences and panel discussions and are invited to submit their opinions, but these invitations often amount to little more than symbolic gestures.

"Having access to an international conference is a first step. But without substantive briefings ahead of the conference, without involvement in preliminary negotiations and, in some cases, without access to negotiation texts, there is little opportunity for us to help shape the outcome. When young people see their demands making little headway politically while they are simultaneously excluded from the search for solutions, frustration inevitably builds," says Brod.

Sczygiol adds: "We often get the feeling at the Federal Youth Conference that we were invited simply to provide a nice photo opportunity. We may be sitting at the table, but often only once a bill has already gone through 17 different drafts and is just days away from its final vote in the Bundestag. By that stage, young people have virtually no say in changing anything, and their voices are barely heard. Their contributions are often simply dismissed with: 'Well, that's just typical youthful nonsense.' And that is something that really frustrates young people."

When It's Always All About "Me"

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

We’re living in the age of “me.” We see most things through the lens of “self”: How’s this experience going for me, and how am I doing? What does this mean for and about me? How’s this person perceiving me? Does my “me” like this or not? Our culture teaches us to be the protagonist of our life, make something of ourselves, be “somebody”—to actualize and optimize ourselves. We should be exceptional, the “star” of our own story, hero of our own journey.

From the moment we can gaze into a mirror, those around us start saying, “That’s you!” “Look at you!” “There you are!” We start being recognized (and celebrated) as a “me,” a “someone” who’s her own unique identity, the one doing this life. A few years later, we learn to advocate for and protect that “me,” snatching toys from other little people, saying “That’s mine,” “Give me my…” We build a sense of possession, of being a “me” who has things or doesn’t.

Soon after (and for the rest of our life), we take on the project of that “me” and start working on behalf of our “me.” We become our own protector, PR rep, agent, and biographer, the narrator of our self-story—the story of “me.”

So too, the message we learn is that this “me” must be moving forward in life, continually progressing towards some better future version of ourselves, a new-and-improved, happier-and-better “me.” We can never rest because we must continue stacking up evidence of our advancement. As we are and where we are, we’re not enough—our “me” still hasn’t done enough. There’s some place, or so we’re taught, at the end of all our progress, where we will have progressed far enough and become enough to make the project of “me” complete, and set it down. Then we’ll be able to experience life directly without our “me” in the way.

We don’t just nurse at the bottle of progress; we also feed on the narrative of materialism, which offers us a system of values and ideas about what “counts” as progress. As the narrative goes, money, success, power, and lots of shiny things will earn us the elusive enough-ness we seek, and the right to stop focusing on our progress and the project of “me.” The problem is that this promised place of enough-ness, where we can finally stop protecting ourselves and progressing on our hero’s journey, never comes. No matter how much we achieve and prove about our worth, enough-ness eludes us, so we must continue pursuing the advancement of our individual “me.”

The self-help market is built around this same premise: that the individual is a project to be continually improved. The message is that our “me” needs to be fixed, or at the very least, needs to be better than she is. We’re supposed to be living more authentically, manifesting the life we deserve, putting ourselves first, distinguishing ourselves, and always becoming our best self (which is definitely not this self).

Self-help teaches us to relentlessly focus on our own experience and how we’re doing in every moment—how our life is going, and, most importantly, how the project of “me” is going. We learn to gauge and evaluate the present moment by whether it gives us what we want and need, whether it’s “good” for us. While paying attention to our own experience and how the present moment is affecting us is profoundly important and necessary for a healthy person, as is the ability to honor one’s own wants and needs, the problem is that this self we’ve learned to place at the center of our attention and indeed existence becomes the operating system through which we experience all of life. We don’t experience life directly, what’s actually happening; we experience what our life says about us, which we also invent and create in our mind.

Does this situation, interaction, or opinion make me feel good about me? Do I feel safe, secure, liked, important as a result of it? Does it support my self-image, the story I tell myself about myself? Am I getting what I need to shore up the version of myself I need to function? We encounter, experience, and react to life through a self-referencing lens, as a reflection of and commentary on who we are.

At the same time, our self-focus is motivated by our most basic instinct: survival. On an evolutionary level, we’re always trying to protect our existence as a species—to stay alive, continue being. Long ago, when we lived in the forest, we had to focus on threats: other animals that might eat us or our offspring, branches that could land on our heads, a dark sky that meant our nest would be flooded. Seeing reality through the lens of how it would impact our survival—through the lens of “me” and mine has deep biological roots.

And indeed, statistically, it made more sense to pay attention to what could kill us than what could delight us, like finding a great berry to eat. This now translates into “negativity bias,” our tendency to focus on what’s negative over what’s positive. But threats in modern times are generally not branches or other animals, but rather threats to our ego, self-esteem, and the grand story of who we are. In everyday life, it’s the psychological front on which we now feel continually endangered. It’s our “me” that has to constantly be on the lookout for danger.

All this to say, we have many good reasons to put ourselves at the center of the universe, and to see life through a personal lens of what it means for and about “me.”

The irony, however, is that our constant self-focus and the effort we put into shoring ourselves up, protecting and proving our self, promoting the “story” of our self, is actually an attempt to make ourselves feel happy and secure. And yet, it ends up making us feel unhappy and insecure. In the system we’ve designed, the relentless defense and hyper-vigilance to the self’s survival can never end. But in fact, it can… we just need to step out of the system.

In part 2, I’ll illuminate the subtle and not-so-subtle suffering that our “me” lens creates. I will also propose a radical solution. Spoiler alert: it will go against everything you’ve ever learned.

The Pennsylvania 529 Investment Plan is Pennsylvania’s 529 plan open to savers in any state, and its 26 portfolios, including target-enrollment ones that shift toward bonds as college nears, are all managed by Vanguard. At an assumed 6% yearly return, saving $200 a month for 18 years in a target-enrollment portfolio costs about $1,000 in fees, against about $1,700 at the 0.30% that plans sold without an advisor averaged in 2025, Morningstar found.
