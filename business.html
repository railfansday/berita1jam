Eaton Sales Up 28%; GAAP EPS Rises Just 7%: Rapid Earnings Reaction

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Eaton's third quarter paired record demand with a widening gap between adjusted and GAAP profit. Here's what's eating into earnings and what to watch next.

Eaton (ETN -1.05%) reported third-quarter sales of $8.9 billion, up 28%, and shares rose about 1.8% in Tuesday trading. Organic sales grew 19%, orders and backlog hit records, and management raised its organic growth guidance. But GAAP earnings per share rose only 7%, to $2.77. Acquisition debt and amortization absorbed most of the extra operating profit.

Debt is the main drag. Net interest expense nearly tripled, to $198 million, as borrowings roughly doubled alongside the Boyd Thermal and Ultra PCS purchases.

Amortization is the second drag. Acquired intangible assets are expensed over time, and that charge more than doubled. Adjusted EPS excludes both charges, so it grew far faster than GAAP. Full-year GAAP EPS guidance is roughly flat with 2025, while adjusted guidance implies double-digit growth.

Orders are the real signal. Twelve-month rolling orders hit record dollars in all three segments, and Electrical backlog grew 46%.

Watch whether fourth-quarter organic growth lands in the guided 13% to 15% range, down from 19% this quarter. Also track net interest expense, which sets how much operating profit reaches GAAP earnings.

Why Amazon Stock Can Keep Rising Through 2035

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Amazon's cloud business gets the spotlight, but improving e-commerce margins is an overlooked opportunity.

Most of the attention on Amazon (AMZN +2.20%) goes to its cloud business, Amazon Web Services (AWS). But the company's online stores are quietly becoming more profitable, thanks to automation and robotics. That's a big reason the stock can keep rising through 2035.

On the third-quarter earnings call, CEO Andy Jassy said, "There has never been a business with a trillion dollars of annual revenue. Amazon is on a path to have two of them in AWS and stores." He added that some folks are "sleeping a bit" on how much progress the retail business has made.

That progress shows up in profit margins. Jassy said Amazon's North America and International segments have each expanded operating margins by nearly 10 percentage points over the past few years. The company is also on track to lower its cost to serve each unit, or deliver each item, for a fourth straight year.

Robotics is a big driver. Amazon has more than 1 million robots in its fulfillment network, and its newest facilities, including same-day delivery sites, are "very heavy on robotics." In the third quarter, shipping costs, excluding fuel and long-haul inflation, grew about four percentage points slower than units shipped. Jassy said the economic impact on its business so far is "small compared to what it is going to be in the next five years."

Lower costs on a sales base this large, with Amazon's online store generating $286 billion in annual revenue, can free up a lot more cash. Over the past 12 months, operating cash flow, the cash the business brings in before investments, grew 29% year over year to $168 billion. It has compounded about 25% a year over the past five years, and with Amazon's automation push still early, that cash flow has room to keep growing for years. And over time, Amazon's stock price tends to follow cash flow.

Yet the stock trades at about 16 times operating cash flow, based on the Oct. 29 close. That's near the lowest multiple Amazon stock has traded since the 2008 bear market -- a discount for a business whose cash flow is still growing rapidly and has room to expand.

The risk is heavy spending. Amazon expects capital expenditures of about $220 billion in 2026, mostly for Amazon Web Services, and management expects to spend "meaningfully more" in 2027. High capital spending has pushed free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital expenditures) negative over the past year.

If those investments don't earn strong returns, free cash flow could stay low and weigh on the stock. Still, with retail becoming a second profit engine and the stock trading at a discount, Amazon shares could deliver excellent returns over the next 10 years.

Rising temperatures may have a dangerous effect on mental health

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

New research reveals how extreme heat may increase suicide risk by disrupting sleep and emotional regulation, highlighting a hidden mental health threat.

Extreme heat can do more than exhaust the body. It may also disrupt sleep, weaken emotional control, and increase vulnerability to serious psychological distress.

A new study has identified temperatures at which extreme heat is associated with increased psychological distress, revealing how disrupted sleep and emotional regulation may contribute to suicide risk.

The research was led by scientists from Georgetown University and the World Bank. It draws on a nationally representative panel study of nearly 7,500 adults in Bangladesh.

The researchers found that extreme heat was associated with an increased risk of suicide-related thoughts, with disrupted sleep playing a surprisingly important role.

“Rising global temperatures threaten human mental health on a massive scale,” said lead author Syed Shabab Wahid, an assistant professor of global health at Georgetown.

“In climate-vulnerable nations such as Bangladesh, this heat stress can translate into severe psychological strain and elevated suicide risk.”

The study tracked two interconnected pathways between heat and suicidal cognition: disrupted sleep and emotional dysregulation.

The association between temperature and suicide-related thoughts became statistically significant at 28°C and was strongest at 35°C.

Temperatures this high are common in Bangladesh during the summer months.

At that upper threshold, nearly 30 percent of the total association between heat and suicide risk ran through poor sleep quality alone. Emotional dysregulation accounted for a smaller but still significant 1.8 percent.

Together, poor sleep and difficulties regulating emotions statistically accounted for roughly a third of the observed relationship between heat and suicide-related thoughts.

The study design is worth understanding, because it shaped what the researchers could and couldn’t conclude.

Participants were interviewed twice – once in winter (January–February 2024) and once at the height of summer (May–June 2024).

Suicide risk was measured using the Brief Suicide Cognitions Scale, a validated tool that captures the sense of unbearability, unlovability, and unsolvability that precedes suicidal behavior.

Heat exposure was operationalized as the number of days with maximum temperatures at or above 35°C in the two weeks prior to each interview.

The panel structure meant the same individuals answered both rounds, which allowed the team to compare the same person’s mental state across seasons.

Summer scores were meaningfully worse: average suicide cognition scores rose from 10.99 in winter to 12.96 in summer.

Sleep quality declined. Emotional regulation worsened. Both effects were consistent across age groups, though older adults and women showed the steepest deterioration.

“Imagine a smartphone with a drained battery, overheating processor, and critical apps crashing all at once. Sustained ambient heat acts on the human brain and body in the same way,” Wahid explained.

“The body cannot cool down at night, leading to broken rest and depleting mental reserves. When the emotional processor overheats without restorative recovery, coping mechanisms collapse under stress.”

The study found that risk was notably higher during summer among less educated and poorer populations.

These differences may reflect unequal access to cooling, greater exposure to outdoor heat, and housing conditions that make it harder to escape high temperatures.

Individuals with disabilities showed consistently elevated risk across seasons. Unemployed people reported higher suicide cognition scores than those in most other employment categories.

Urban residents were more susceptible than rural ones. This may reflect the urban heat island effect. Concrete and asphalt absorb and radiate heat in ways that push nighttime temperatures higher.

There’s a global dimension to these findings that goes beyond Bangladesh.

Previous meta-analyses have found that a 1°C increase in monthly mean temperature is associated with a 1.5 to 1.7 percent increase in suicidal outcomes.

Bangladesh sits in a region the IPCC projects will experience some of the worst heatwaves of the coming decades. But the mechanisms the study identified are not unique to South Asia.

“Rather than treating climate-related mental health crises as unpreventable events, public health systems can now focus on intermediate, modifiable targets,” Wahid said. Sleep is one of them.

“Health agencies can monitor heat waves alongside sleep quality disruption indicators to mobilize preventative interventions. Interventions centered on sleep preservation, household cooling access, and community-based emotional regulation training should serve as effective suicide prevention tools during heatwaves.”

The paper points toward green roofing, passive architecture, and low-cost cooling technologies as structural interventions with documented effects on indoor temperatures in Bangladesh.

A pilot study found that buildings with vegetation-covered green roofing maintained temperatures 3.5°C lower than bare rooftops.

Such reductions could help make indoor environments more comfortable during extreme heat, potentially protecting sleep and reducing heat-related stress.

The study has some important limitations.

First, it relies on ambient outdoor temperature rather than indoor measurements. That’s a known proxy problem in Bangladesh, where air conditioning is rare and indoor heat tracks outdoor heat closely.

Humidity data wasn’t available, meaning the full thermal stress experienced by respondents – particularly relevant in a country where wet-bulb temperatures can reach dangerous levels – is likely underestimated.

What the study offers is new evidence that poor sleep and difficulties regulating emotions may help explain the relationship between extreme heat and suicide-related thoughts.

These findings point toward practical ways to reduce the psychological toll of rising temperatures.

Protecting sleep, improving access to cooling, and supporting mental health could become increasingly important as extreme heat becomes more common.

The study is published in the journal Environmental Research.

I’m sure some will say you need to give directors of football time, but sometimes when something is wrong you just need to bite the bullet and move on, rather than persisting with the mistake.

Mortgage rates are constantly changing, since a major part of how rates are set depends on reactions to new inflation reports, job numbers, Fed meetings, global news ... you name it. For example, even tiny changes in the bond market can shift mortgage pricing.

Central bankers hiked the federal funds rate in September, so a relatively steady jobs report would suggest the labor market could withstand higher rates.

The Fed has a dual mandate to control inflation while supporting maximum employment, and raising the federal funds rate is their main tool for bringing inflation down. If they can work to lower inflation without hurting employment too badly, central bankers may feel more confident to act.

The September Personal Consumption Expenditures price index (PCE), the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge, was released last Thursday. The data shows inflation was 3.6% year-over-year in September, up from 3.4% in August.

September’s federal funds rate hike came in the middle of the month, so October’s inflation data will really capture its effect on inflation. The October PCE report will come out on Nov. 25.

The border wall dispute isn’t fit for the emergency docket

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

In its latest request to the Supreme Court over President Donald Trump’s approach to immigration, the Justice Department turned to the court’s emergency docket to ask for fast-track permission to construct a border barrier system along a 517-mile stretch in remote west Texas. Stymied by a federal district court’s preliminary injunction issued in October, the Justice Department argues that Supreme Court intervention is needed to prevent irreparable harm to the country. But details of the government’s legal argument reveal that the Trump administration hopes to use the emergency docket as a fast track to victory regardless of the actual urgency of the legal dispute.

Early this year, the Department of Homeland Security began taking important preliminary steps to build physical barriers through Big Bend National Park, Big Bend Ranch State Park, and nearby property along the U.S.-Mexico border. Part of the Border Patrol’s Big Bend Sector, covering approximately one-quarter of the entire border, no artificial barriers exist there. Despite its massive geographic reach, the region is sparsely populated and at immense distance from large urban centers. Unlike other parts of the U.S.-Mexico border, few people or goods cross from one country to the other in the Big Bend Sector. Indeed, according to U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone, who issued a preliminary injunction blocking DHS from carrying out its plans to erect a border barrier in the area, this is the border region with “the lowest aggregate number of apprehensions [of migrants] and the lowest number of apprehensions per mile of border.”

In Mullin v. Friends of the Ruidosa Church, a group of individuals with personal or business ties to the region, along with two advocacy groups, argued that DHS had failed to comply with required environmental reviews. Pointing to a 1996 law signed by President Bill Clinton, the Justice Department responded that Secretary of Homeland Security Markwayne Mullin and his predecessor, Kristi Noem, waived application of those environmental laws. The government also argued that the district court lacked the power to decide the lawsuit in the first place.

Cardone disagreed with the government on both counts. Congress narrowed courts’ jurisdiction in border-barrier disputes, but left room for courts to consider constitutional challenges to DHS’s conduct, she concluded. The challenge to Mullin’s waiver of environmental laws raises a constitutional question, Cardone added.

Having concluded that she could consider the challengers’ lawsuit, Cardone then turned to the criteria that Congress imposed on the secretary of Homeland Security to waive environmental review. The 1996 law directs the immigration agency “to waive all legal requirements” that could slow or impede construction of border barriers and roads. But the same law also instructs DHS to build barriers and roads “in areas of high illegal entry into the United States.” Considering the government’s data about apprehensions of people and seizures of drugs in the Big Bend Sector, as compared to other parts of the U.S.-Mexico border, Cardone concluded that the region cannot be considered to have high levels of illegal entry. “[I]f the Big Bend Sector is an area of high illegal entry, so is every other sector,” Cardone announced.

Seven days after Cardone released her opinion, the U.S. solicitor general asked the Supreme Court to intervene. Much of the government’s argument turns on its disagreement with Cardone about what constitutes an area of “high illegal entry.” Mullin determined that the Big Bend Sector constitutes an area of high illegal entry because Customs and Border Protection, the DHS agency that enforces immigration laws at and near the border, apprehended 89,000 people and “seized enormous amounts” of illicit drugs from fiscal years 2021 to 2025, the government explains. In addition, failure to build a border barrier in this region “would act as a dangerous magnet” for additional illegal entries, the solicitor general claims. Despite Mullin’s assessment that the facts on the ground met the “high illegal entry” threshold required by Congress, “the district court nonetheless issued an unprecedented injunction blocking those waivers and halting associated border-wall construction,” the solicitor general wrote to the court.

Unsurprisingly, the challengers disagree. Opposing the government’s request for relief from the justices, they describe Mullin and Noem’s waivers as “unprecedented” and claim they “carry undisputed and extraordinary political and economic significance.”

The solicitor general’s claim is fundamentally a dispute about the proper interpretation of the text of the 1996 law. The government argues that courts cannot second-guess Mullin’s decision to waive environmental laws, and that Mullin’s view of what constitutes “high illegal entry” is all that matters. Cardone disagrees and contends that courts have a role to play here.

By turning to the emergency docket, the Justice Department has dispensed with the ordinary appellate process in favor of a fast-track path to, it hopes, final victory. Wanting an answer sooner rather than later is understandable, but a party’s interest in “line-jumping,” as Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson has described the emergency docket, isn’t reason enough to circumvent the slower course of the ordinary appellate process. Jackson would prefer to limit the emergency docket to situations in which an actual emergency exists. It’s hard to see what exigency exists in west Texas, however. The solicitor general argues that drugs and people will continue to enter through the Big Bend Sector. That is undoubtedly true, but, as that stretch of land has been unfortified since it became a border on March 18, 1848, nothing is likely to occur in the near future that is markedly different from past decades.

The government’s request for fast-track intervention by the Supreme Court also does not meet the standards set by Jackson’s ideological opposites. Explaining his view of the court’s emergency docket in Trump v. CASA, the court’s fast-track decision announcing that district courts are not authorized to issue universal injunctions, Justice Brett Kavanaugh wrote that its alternative pathway is appropriate when “Congress passes a major new statute, or the Executive Branch issues a major new rule or executive order.” Neither applies to the border-wall dispute. Congress enacted the contested statute 30 years ago and the waivers issued by Mullin and Noem are neither an agency rule nor an executive order.

Nor is the border-wall legal battle the type of dispute that only the Supreme Court can resolve. In a 2021 speech, Justice Samuel Alito compared the court’s emergency docket to a hospital’s emergency room, suggesting that, in his view at least, the fast-track process is proper only when no suitable alternative exists. But an alternative does exist to decide if Cardone overstepped. The government could have appealed Cardone’s decision to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit. Like other circuit courts, the 5th Circuit regularly reviews preliminary injunctions granted by district courts. As Kavanaugh explained in CASA, “The courts of appeals and this Court can (and regularly do) expeditiously review district court decisions awarding or denying preliminary injunctive relief.” Instead of asking the 5th Circuit to correct the mistake it claims Cardone committed, the solicitor general skipped the 5th Circuit and went directly to the Supreme Court.

The reason it did this is not difficult to figure out: The administration is hopeful that it will receive a friendly audience at the Supreme Court. But this is a fast-track procedure meant for exceptional circumstances. And there is nothing exceptional about the legal fight over the administration’s attempt to build new segments of border barrier in rural west Texas. If the court grants the government’s request to pause Cardone’s preliminary injunction, allowing construction to move forward where no barrier currently exists, it should, at the very least, explain how this case meets the criteria that the justices have previously provided and if the term “emergency docket” is a misnomer.

How Syrup Became a Coffee-Shop Culture War

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For some coffee shop customers, premade syrups are now a major red flag. It presents a new opportunity for small business owners.

In a TikTok video with over 82,000 likes, the person behind the camera zooms into the shelves at a Pennsylvania coffee shop, three rows stacked with an array of Torani syrups in flavors like blueberry, brown-sugar cinnamon, English toffee, and salted caramel. “I just know my latte isn’t going to be good,” the caption reads. Across social media, similar sentiments appear. “If I see these at a cafe, I’m walking out immediately,” says one tweet, co-signed by many commenters. In a multipart video series, another creator documents her journey of replacing all of her Torani syrups with ones from small businesses instead. Last year, the niche food newsletter Unsnackable published what was described as a “Torani tirade.”

It’s not just a Torani problem, though the brand — by virtue of its market dominance — tends to get the most flack on social media. While some people seem to prefer competitor Monin, it, too, draws backlash over allegations of tasting “like chemicals.” The problem, for some drinkers of dessert-like specialty beverages, is premade syrups across the board.

For a certain sect of consumers, they’ve become a coffee shop red flag, a sign that a so-called “specialty” establishment asking them to fork over $8 plus tip for a latte is unfairly cutting corners when it comes to quality or craft. For these consumers, mass-market syrups now symbolize exactly that: They’re synonymous with coffee chains and corporations, the kind of impersonal institutions that many people are trying to get away from when they patronize a small business. The flip side: For independently owned coffee shops across the country, house-made syrup now represents a major opportunity.

When Carrie and Dennis Medina first envisioned their coffee shop, Room Service, in Ventura, California, they thought they would take an approach similar to the one at Sightglass Coffee, where they’d both gotten their start. “Very purist, with no syrups, no specialty drinks,” Dennis says. They imagined eight kinds of pour-over coffee and an emphasis on single-origin beans, since the couple also runs a roastery. “We said we’re never gonna do cream tops; we’re never gonna do these crazy drinks,” Carrie says. But in the first week alone, every customer was asking for them.

Now, Room Service offers 10 to 12 different syrups at any given time (and six different cream tops), all of them made in-house. It offers drinks such as a cinnamon roll latte topped with a cinnamon roll, and a honey cornbread latte with an ube cream top. “I hear that question all the time,” Dennis says. “‘Hey, do you guys make your syrups in-house?’” To him, a shop that isn’t trying to do at least the basics — like vanilla — in-house is “falling behind what the times are pushing for.”

The demand for syrups and other embellishments is so intense that the Medinas have brought on a full-time prep person who works exclusively on “all these things that we do in-house that make us special,” Carrie says. Room Service’s PSL, for example, starts with kabocha squash. It’s roasted, blended with spices such as cloves and cinnamon, and cooked into syrup, a process that takes two days. Echoing a sentiment that seems common among coffee shop owners right now, Carrie describes their current approach as “not necessarily the coffee shop we wanted, but it’s more like the coffee shop we have to be.” And if the market demands syrups and cream tops, then the Medinas wanted to ensure that the intention and emphasis on quality that they applied to their coffee beans also carried over.

In 2025, Michael Smith opened Trust Coffee Co. in Bay Village, Ohio, taking over, remodeling, and rebranding an older coffee shop. He, like the Medinas, imagined a more “pour-over, third-wave” style establishment. “But I’ve been a business owner for a long time,” says Smith, who also runs a restaurant and a catering business. “I’ve realized over the years that your customers tell you more about who you are than who you actually think you are.” His customers, it turned out, wanted seasonal syrups in niche flavors like maple rosemary, caramelized banana, and strawberry rhubarb.

The purist is no longer coffee’s north star; the maximalist is.

Syrups like Monin, which the shop’s previous iteration used, “serve a purpose in the industry,” Smith says, but he wanted more control over the menu. The two full-time pastry chefs from Smith’s other businesses oversee the shop’s syrups. “It’s become such a huge thing,” he says: Every time Trust’s social media manager posts a new syrup, the line is out the door for a week. “It sets us apart,” he explains.

When Sabrina Kaylor opened the first location of Bizarre Coffee in Canton, Georgia in 2020, the shop used bottled syrups. She hadn’t worked in coffee prior, and she said that approach felt like the norm at the time. “It allowed us to hone our craft and learn what we were doing,” she says. Over time, Kaylor — who’s quite active on the company’s official social media platforms — noticed that the tenor of the conversation was changing: In the immediate post-COVID years, people were making more things at home and there was more pride to be gained in “scratch-made.” With her business becoming known for lattes in flavors such as blueberry, banana, and guava, Kaylor decided to shift toward more house-made syrups. Previously, her customers didn’t ask about their sourcing, but now, they ask all the time.

As much as there is a sense of valor in the entirely from-scratch approach, Kaylor retains some skepticism about the current torrent of Torani and Monin backlash. Today, 90 percent of Bizarre’s drinks use house-made syrups, though one of its best-selling drinks still relies on the bottled stuff; Bizarre’s menu is extensive and making everything in-house would be a significant investment of labor. On the part of coffee shops, “I think a lot of [the house-made syrup conversation] is marketing, quite honestly,” Kaylor says. As for the bandwagoning that is all the anti-Torani memes, “I think it’s part of consumer behavior,” she says. And that, she acknowledges, can be really “discouraging” for small business owners.

In a sense, that’s what the premade-versus-house-made-syrup conversation boils down to: the stand between small businesses and big ones. For more consumers, the economic and social conditions of the current moment have inspired increased skepticism toward the influence of corporations and private equity in the food and beverage world.

The Minnesota coffee shop Little Joy has become famous across social media for posting the recipes of its signature drinks in detailed DIY videos. It has since launched a membership program through which other coffee shop owners can purchase its recipes, scaled up for commercial usage; gain access to training videos; and sell its drinks without even needing to attribute them to Little Joy, though the ones that would like to can pin their use of Little Joy’s recipes to a Drink Finder map.

Yes, these might just be recipes for syrups and foams, but in an interview with Eater’s industry newsletter Pre Shift earlier this year, Little Joy owner Cody Larson described the system as a way to build solidarity among independent businesses. “The big coffee chains, they’re never gonna do house-made syrups, so I think that’s where independent shops can get a leg up,” he said. “Ultimately, the chains are our competition... If independent shops can, in any way possible, raise the bar across the board, that’s good for all independent shops, including us.”

It’s hard to look at the drinks coming out of specialty coffee shops today — fluffy with foam, flavored to emulate nuanced desserts like princess cake and tiramisu— and not think of the Starbucks Frappuccino. The combination of app-ordering and social media “hack” culture have mainstreamed super-elaborate beverage requests, allowing Starbucks and coffee chains like it to become purveyors of highly complicated customizable beverages, mainstreaming the mindset of the aforementioned “hyperflavor.”

When it comes to why consumers have moved away from the single-origin pour-over approach at shops like his, “my guess is that these are a lot of people who used to be Starbucks loyalists and they’re looking for something that’s more local,” Smith says. “It’s a trend across everything where people want local, and we’re able to provide what Starbucks did, but in a more high-quality, more local way.”

Intentionality is what people are gravitating towards, especially as prices rise. If consumers are spending so much on a drink that’s undeniably a luxury, then they want to know that every part of that drink has been made carefully. And if they’re going to make the trip to a new, independent shop, they want to know that the offerings there aren’t the ones they can get anywhere, made with easily attainable, big-brand flavorings. For small coffee shops, the key to success could be as simple as syrup.

On the evening of Oct. 29, hackers stole an estimated $6.3 billion in funds from the Binance crypto exchange, marking the biggest hack in crypto history. The exchange has since promised to cover every customer loss, and it still has more than enough money on hand for that promise to be credible.

The exchange's blockchain didn't seem to be directly affected by the attack, yet its native token, BNB (BNB -1.03%), was down nearly 7% over the 24 hours since the breach. It's still up by 212% over the last three years. So is BNB worth buying, or is the Binance hack the beginning of its end?

As of Oct. 30, Binance's partners had frozen at least $3 billion of the stolen funds, and the exchange had set aside $6.3 billion to make its customers whole.

The drained wallets were among those listed in Binance's monthly proof of reserves, briefly leaving Binance's Ethereum reserves covering only about 94% of customers' ether balances. By Oct. 31, Binance said a new reserve snapshot showed ether and nine other assets fully backed again. So the damage to Binance's reserves looks small and temporary.

Furthermore, the hackers didn't do or steal anything that would touch Binance's supply of BNB.

The coin is used on the exchange's blockchain, and holding it can buy fee discounts on the exchange itself, so its value depends entirely on investors' trust in Binance, as it has no utility outside its ecosystem. And despite the theft, there is little indication that people have lost enough faith in Binance to stop using its exchange -- the same as in prior instances where it was affected by market crashes, breaches, or other problems.

In my view, there wasn't a very strong investment thesis for buying BNB before the hack, and there isn't one for buying it now, either. Here's why.

BNB Chain destroys BNB coins every quarter via its Auto-Burn mechanism, which removes coins from the supply based on its price and how many blocks are produced by the chain over the quarter. There's also another supply-reducing mechanism that destroys a portion of the BNB that's used to pay each transaction fee on the network.

The point of those two processes is to make the coin more appealing to hold by linking the chain's utilization to its supply.

So, even if the price of BNB could be much higher three years from now, I don't think it's worth buying.

From hand weakness to slurred speech: 12 things to know about ALS

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The early signs of ALS range from hand weakness to slurred speech, and these 12 points cover symptoms, diagnosis, genetics and care for the disease

A fumbled shirt button, a toe that catches on the stairs, a voice that sounds slightly thick by evening. Changes like these are easy to blame on age, stress or a long week, and most of the time that explanation is correct. In some people, though, they are the first outward signs of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.

ALS is a progressive disease of the motor neurons, the nerve cells in the brain and spinal cord that carry signals to the muscles people move by choice. As those cells stop working, the muscles they control weaken and shrink. In the U.S., the condition is widely known as Lou Gehrig's disease, after the New York Yankees first baseman whose career it ended. In the U.K. and many other countries, it is usually called motor neuron disease, a term that also covers a few related conditions.

The early signs of ALS are hard to read for a simple reason. Each one, taken alone, has many other possible causes. Muscle twitching is common in healthy people. Cramps often follow exercise or dehydration. Foot drop can come from a compressed nerve near the knee or in the lower back. What sets ALS apart is a pattern of painless weakness that persists and slowly spreads. It often starts on one side of the body or in the muscles used for speaking and swallowing.

No blood test or scan confirms the disease on its own. Neurologists build the diagnosis from a physical examination and electrical tests of nerves and muscles. Imaging and lab work rule out conditions that look similar. That process can take time.

This guide covers 12 points about ALS symptoms and the disease behind them. It describes changes people commonly notice first, including hand weakness, twitching, cramps, tripping, slurred speech and trouble swallowing. It also explains the difference between limb-onset and bulbar-onset disease. It covers how a diagnosis is reached, what is known about inherited forms and how treatment and supportive care work today. It is a selection of topics, not a complete account, and it does not replace medical advice.

No single symptom on this list means a person has ALS. Persistent or worsening weakness, speech changes or swallowing problems are reasons to see a doctor. A primary care doctor can arrange a referral to a neurologist when the pattern calls for one.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, or ALS, is a progressive neurodegenerative disease that destroys motor neurons. These are the nerve cells that control voluntary muscles. Americans often know it as Lou Gehrig's disease, a nickname taken from the New York Yankees first baseman whose diagnosis made the illness widely known.

Motor neurons come in two types. Upper motor neurons run from the brain's motor cortex down into the brainstem and spinal cord. Lower motor neurons carry signals from there out to the muscles themselves. ALS damages both groups. That combination sets it apart from many other nerve disorders.

When these neurons stop working, muscles no longer receive the signals they need. The muscles weaken, shrink and eventually stop moving on command. Affected muscles include those people use to walk, speak, swallow and breathe.

The disease's name describes what happens inside the body. "Amyotrophic" comes from Greek roots meaning "no muscle nourishment." It refers to the wasting of muscle tissue. "Lateral" points to the side regions of the spinal cord, where the affected nerve fibers travel. "Sclerosis" means hardening or scarring. It describes how that tissue looks as the neurons degenerate.

The French neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot described the disease in the 19th century. In France, it is still often called maladie de Charcot, or Charcot's disease.

Gehrig's story explains why his name stuck. He was known as one of baseball's most durable players. Then his performance declined, and he was diagnosed with ALS. In his farewell speech at Yankee Stadium, he called himself "the luckiest man on the face of the earth." The line is still widely quoted.

Outside North America, the term motor neuron disease, or MND, is common. In the U.K. and Australia, MND is the usual name for ALS. Clinicians also apply the term more broadly, to a family of related conditions that includes ALS.

ALS generally leaves the senses intact. Sight, hearing, taste, smell and touch are usually unaffected. The muscles that move the eyes and those that control the bladder are often spared until late in the disease. Some people do develop changes in thinking or behavior. ALS has a recognized overlap with frontotemporal dementia. The disease is not contagious.

Painless weakness in one hand, arm or leg is one of the most common ways amyotrophic lateral sclerosis first appears. It usually starts on one side of the body and worsens gradually. It rarely appears suddenly or affects both sides equally at first.

The cause is lost nerve supply, not an injury to the muscle itself. Muscles that stop receiving steady signals from motor neurons lose strength and, over time, bulk.

In the hands, the first changes often involve fine motor tasks. A person may struggle to fasten a button, turn a key, open a jar or pinch a small object between thumb and finger. Handwriting can become cramped or less steady. Dropping cups or utensils more often is another common complaint.

Muscle wasting in the hand is often visible. The fleshy pad at the base of the thumb may flatten. A hollow can form in the web of skin between the thumb and index finger. Neurologists describe a pattern called "split hand." In this pattern, the muscles on the thumb side waste while those on the little-finger side are relatively spared.

Weakness higher in the arm shows up differently. Lifting a bag into an overhead bin, washing hair or reaching for a high shelf can become harder. In the legs, early weakness may make it difficult to climb stairs. Getting up from a low chair without pushing off with the hands can also become a struggle.

True weakness is different from tiredness. Fatigue tends to ease after rest. Weakness from motor neuron loss persists. An affected muscle cannot produce its normal force even when it is fresh.

Sensation usually stays intact in ALS. Numbness, tingling or burning point more toward other problems. These include carpal tunnel syndrome, a pinched nerve in the neck and peripheral neuropathy. Those conditions are far more common, and many are treatable.

Weakness in one limb does not on its own mean someone has ALS. Neurologists look for weakness that keeps progressing, spreads to nearby muscles over weeks or months and comes with visible muscle loss.

Muscle twitching on its own is not a sign of ALS. Fasciculations become a concern for a neurologist mainly when they appear alongside muscle weakness or visible muscle wasting in the same part of the body.

Fasciculations are brief, involuntary contractions of small groups of muscle fibers. They show up as a flicker, ripple or jump under the skin. They usually do not move a joint and are typically painless. They can appear in the calves, thighs, arms, hands, eyelids or almost anywhere else.

Many healthy people notice them at some point. Common triggers include caffeine, intense exercise, stress, anxiety, poor sleep and fatigue. Some medications and stimulants can provoke them too. Low levels of minerals such as magnesium or potassium are another possible cause. A twitching eyelid is one of the most familiar examples. It usually fades on its own.

Persistent twitching with no other problems has its own name: benign fasciculation syndrome. People with this condition can have frequent, widespread twitches for long stretches. Their strength stays normal, and their muscles do not shrink. The condition is not dangerous, though the twitches can be bothersome.

In ALS, fasciculations have a different origin. They reflect damage to lower motor neurons, the nerve cells in the brainstem and spinal cord that connect directly to muscles. As these cells deteriorate, the muscle fibers they control can fire spontaneously. The twitches are a sign of that breakdown, not a cause of it.

Context matters more than the twitch itself. In ALS, fasciculations tend to show up in muscles that are also becoming weaker or thinner. A person might see twitching in a hand that has started to struggle with fine tasks. It might also appear in a shoulder or forearm that looks hollower than before. Twitching of the tongue, seen during a clinical exam, can point to involvement of the muscles that control speech and swallowing.

The frequency or intensity of twitching does not by itself separate a benign cause from a serious one. A doctor looks for the combination: twitching plus progressive weakness, loss of muscle bulk or changes in reflexes. During an evaluation, a neurologist checks strength and muscle bulk directly. Electrical testing can then look for signs of nerve damage beyond the twitches.

For someone with twitching and normal strength, a primary care doctor can start by reviewing sleep, caffeine intake, current medications and basic blood work.

Muscle cramps and stiffness are common ALS symptoms, and they come from damage to two different groups of nerve cells. Cramps reflect trouble in the lower motor neurons that connect to muscles. Stiffness reflects loss of the upper motor neurons in the brain.

An ALS-related cramp is a sudden, painful, involuntary tightening of a muscle. It can strike the calves, thighs, hands, feet or abdomen. Some people notice cramps in muscles that rarely cramped before, such as those in the hands. Cramps can arrive with little exertion, including at rest or during sleep.

Lower motor neurons run from the brainstem and spinal cord out to the muscles. As these cells become damaged, the nerve fibers can fire irregularly. That abnormal activity can set off a sustained contraction the person cannot stop at will.

Stiffness has a different origin. Upper motor neurons normally send signals that help keep muscle tone in check. When they degenerate, muscles can become tight and resistant to movement. This condition is called spasticity. A stiff leg may feel heavy or slow to swing forward. Walking can become effortful, and balance can suffer.

Upper motor neuron loss also changes reflexes. A neurologist tapping the knee may find an unusually brisk response, known as hyperreflexia. Stroking the sole of the foot may make the big toe curl upward instead of down. This response is called the Babinski sign. In ALS, these findings can appear alongside weakness and muscle wasting in the same limb. That mix of upper and lower motor neuron signs in one region helps distinguish ALS from many other conditions.

Cramps on their own are common and usually harmless. Pregnancy, low levels of minerals such as potassium or magnesium and certain medications, including some diuretics, can all trigger them. Occasional nighttime calf cramps in an otherwise healthy person are not a sign of ALS.

Cramps and spasticity can often be eased. Regular stretching and physical therapy help keep joints flexible and limit muscle shortening. Doctors may prescribe muscle relaxants such as baclofen or tizanidine to reduce spasticity. Treatment aims to relieve stiffness without adding to weakness. Some muscle tone can help a weak leg bear weight. For that reason, doses are adjusted with care.

Foot drop is difficulty lifting the front of the foot, and it is one way ALS can first appear in the legs. It often makes a person catch their toes and trip more than usual, typically on one side before the other.

The problem lies in the muscles along the front of the shin. The main one is the tibialis anterior, which pulls the toes and forefoot upward with each step. When the motor neurons that control it weaken, the foot hangs lower as the leg swings forward.

The resulting changes are often small at first. A toe snags on the edge of a rug, a curb or a stair. A shoe scuffs the ground. Some people hear their foot slap down on the floor instead of rolling smoothly from heel to toe.

To compensate, many people begin lifting the knee higher than normal so the toes clear the ground. This pattern is called a steppage gait. Others swing the leg outward in a slight arc. Family members sometimes notice the altered walk before the person does.

Foot drop has many causes, and most of them are not ALS. A common one is compression of the peroneal nerve, also called the fibular nerve, where it wraps around the outside of the knee. Habitual leg crossing, long periods of squatting, rapid weight loss or a tight cast can all press on it. A herniated disc pinching the L5 nerve root in the lower back can produce the same weakness. Diabetic neuropathy, stroke and multiple sclerosis are other possibilities.

The surrounding symptoms help separate these causes. Nerve compression and disc problems often bring numbness, tingling or pain in the back and leg. Foot drop from ALS is usually painless and comes without loss of sensation. The disease targets motor neurons and leaves sensory nerves largely intact.

In ALS, foot drop also tends to progress rather than hold steady. Weakness may later appear in the other leg or spread to the thigh and hip muscles.

A lightweight brace called an ankle-foot orthosis can hold the foot at a working angle. It can help reduce tripping and falls whatever the underlying cause.

Slurred, slow or effortful speech, known medically as dysarthria, can be the first noticeable sign of ALS in some people. It happens when the motor neurons that control the lips, tongue, soft palate, jaw and vocal cords begin to fail.

Dysarthria is a problem with the mechanics of speech, not with language. A person with dysarthria still knows what they want to say and can find the right words. The difficulty lies in physically forming the sounds clearly.

The changes often start subtly. Consonants that need precise tongue movements, such as "t," "d," "l" and "k," may become blurred. Speech can slow down. Some people find that talking takes more effort. Others notice that their voice tires after a long conversation or phone call.

A nasal quality is another common feature. When the soft palate weakens, it no longer seals off the nasal passage properly during speech. Air escapes through the nose and gives words a muffled or nasal sound. The voice itself may also become hoarse, strained or quieter.

ALS can damage both upper and lower motor neurons, and speech reflects that. Lower motor neuron damage tends to leave muscles weak and floppy. The result is breathy, nasal speech, a pattern clinicians call flaccid dysarthria. Upper motor neuron damage makes muscles stiff. That produces a strained, slow and harsh voice, known as spastic dysarthria. In ALS, features of both types often appear together, and clinicians describe the result as mixed dysarthria.

Family members, friends or colleagues sometimes notice the change before the person does. A frequent early clue is being asked to repeat things. Another is hearing that you sound tired, or as though you have been drinking.

Slurred speech has many causes besides ALS. Alcohol, certain medications, dental problems, stroke, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease and myasthenia gravis can all affect speech. The timing matters. ALS-related dysarthria develops gradually and keeps progressing. Speech that becomes slurred suddenly, especially alongside facial drooping or arm weakness, is a stroke warning sign and needs emergency care.

When gradual speech changes raise concern, a neurologist typically examines the tongue, palate and facial muscles for weakness, wasting or stiffness. A speech-language pathologist can assess which parts of speech production are affected and track how they change over time.

Dysphagia, the medical term for difficulty swallowing, occurs in ALS when the motor neurons controlling the tongue, lips and throat weaken. It can appear early, especially when the disease starts in the bulbar muscles. It can also develop later as the disease spreads.

Swallowing is a tightly timed sequence. The lips seal, the tongue pushes food backward, and the throat muscles squeeze it toward the esophagus. At the same moment, the larynx lifts and the airway closes. Weakness at any point in that chain can let food or liquid slip where it should not go.

Early changes are often small. A person may cough or clear their throat during meals, or feel food sticking partway down. Thin liquids such as water are frequently harder to manage than thicker foods. They move quickly and give weakened muscles less time to react. Meals may take longer, and some people start avoiding certain textures.

Drooling can also develop. In ALS, it usually reflects less frequent swallowing of saliva and weaker lip closure, not extra saliva production. A wet or gurgly voice after drinking can signal that liquid is pooling near the vocal cords.

The main medical concern is aspiration, which happens when food, liquid or saliva enters the airway and lungs. Aspiration can lead to a lung infection called aspiration pneumonia. Swallowing trouble can also make it hard to take in enough calories and fluids. That raises the risk of weight loss and dehydration.

A speech-language pathologist typically evaluates swallowing. One common tool is a videofluoroscopic swallow study, also called a modified barium swallow. It records X $TWTR-ray video as a person swallows foods and liquids of different textures. Another option is a fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing, known as FEES. It uses a thin, flexible camera passed through the nose to view the throat.

Early adjustments often focus on technique and texture. These can include sitting upright during meals, taking smaller bites, tucking the chin while swallowing and thickening liquids.

Dysphagia on its own does not point to ALS. Stroke, acid reflux, narrowing of the esophagus and many other conditions can cause it. Swallowing difficulty that persists or worsens calls for a medical evaluation to find the cause.

Neurologists often describe ALS by where its first symptoms appear. Limb-onset ALS starts in the arms or legs, while bulbar-onset ALS starts in the muscles that control speech, chewing and swallowing.

The word "bulbar" comes from an older anatomical name for the medulla oblongata, the lower part of the brainstem. Motor neurons there control the tongue, lips, jaw, throat and voice box. When those cells are affected first, the earliest changes show up in the face and throat rather than in the hands or feet.

Limb-onset ALS is the more common of the two patterns. It typically begins in a single arm or leg. The weakness is often uneven between the two sides of the body.

The labels describe a starting point, not a fixed category. ALS is progressive, and symptoms generally spread to other regions of the body over time. Someone whose disease begins in a leg may later develop bulbar symptoms. Someone with bulbar onset may later develop weakness in the limbs.

A smaller group of people first show weakness in the breathing muscles, a pattern sometimes called respiratory-onset ALS. In those cases, trouble breathing while lying flat can be one of the first clues.

The onset pattern shapes where people first go for help. Limb symptoms often lead to a visit with a primary care doctor or an orthopedic specialist. At that stage, the problem may be mistaken for carpal tunnel syndrome, arthritis or a spine problem. Bulbar symptoms may lead people first to an ear, nose and throat specialist, a dentist or a speech-language pathologist.

The pattern also guides early care planning. People with limb onset often begin with physical and occupational therapy. Braces, splints and mobility aids may follow as strength changes. People with bulbar onset tend to need earlier input from speech-language pathologists and dietitians. That input can include planning for communication devices before they are needed.

Both patterns reflect the same underlying disease. Each involves the loss of motor neurons. The same diagnostic process applies to both.

No single blood test or scan can confirm amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Neurologists diagnose it by combining a detailed physical exam with electrical tests of nerves and muscles. They also rule out other conditions that can look similar.

The exam centers on two kinds of motor neurons. Upper motor neurons carry signals from the brain to the spinal cord. Damage to them tends to produce stiffness, overactive reflexes and a Babinski sign. In that sign, the big toe extends upward when the sole of the foot is stroked.

Lower motor neurons carry signals from the spinal cord to the muscles. Damage there causes weakness, muscle wasting and visible twitching. Finding signs of both types in the same body region, and seeing them spread to other regions, is central to an ALS diagnosis.

Electromyography, usually shortened to EMG, measures electrical activity inside muscles. A thin needle electrode is placed into several muscles. The neurologist then reads the signals while each muscle is at rest and while it contracts. In ALS, the recording can show denervation, meaning muscle fibers have lost their nerve supply. It can also show reinnervation, a sign that surviving nerves are sprouting new branches to take over orphaned muscle fibers. EMG can detect lower motor neuron damage in muscles that do not yet feel weak.

Nerve conduction studies are often done during the same visit. Small electrical pulses are applied to the skin, and sensors record how quickly and strongly signals travel along a nerve. In ALS, sensory nerves usually test normal. Sensory abnormalities, or a block in motor nerve conduction, can point toward other diagnoses, such as peripheral neuropathy or multifocal motor neuropathy.

MRI of the brain and spine helps exclude conditions that mimic ALS. These include compression of the spinal cord in the neck, tumors and multiple sclerosis. Blood and urine tests can check for thyroid problems, vitamin B12 deficiency and other treatable causes of weakness. Some patients also have a lumbar puncture or genetic testing.

Early findings can be subtle. For that reason, a diagnosis may require repeat exams over several months to show that symptoms are progressing. Neurologists often refer to published frameworks, such as the revised El Escorial criteria and the Gold Coast criteria, to standardize the process. Some patients seek a second opinion at a specialized ALS clinic before treatment planning begins.

Most people with ALS have the sporadic form, which appears with no known family history. A smaller group has familial ALS, which is inherited. Changes in the C9orf72 gene are among the most common known genetic causes of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.

Sporadic ALS has no single identified cause. Its origins are not fully understood. Genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures may both play a part, but no one exposure has been confirmed as a direct cause.

Familial ALS usually follows an autosomal dominant pattern. In that pattern, one altered copy of a gene inherited from one parent can be enough to raise a person's risk. Each child of a parent who carries the change can inherit it. Inheriting it does not guarantee the disease will develop. Geneticists call this incomplete penetrance.

The C9orf72 change is a repeat expansion. A short, six-letter stretch of DNA, written as GGGGCC, is repeated far more times than usual. The same expansion is linked to frontotemporal dementia, a condition that affects behavior, language and decision-making. Some affected families include members with ALS, others with frontotemporal dementia and some with features of both.

Other genes tied to inherited motor neuron disease include SOD1, TARDBP and FUS. SOD1 was the first gene linked to ALS. TARDBP carries the instructions for a protein called TDP-43. That protein forms abnormal clumps inside the motor neurons of most people with ALS, including many who have no inherited mutation.

The line between the two forms is not always sharp. Genetic testing sometimes finds a C9orf72 expansion or another known mutation in a person with no family history of the disease. That can happen when relatives died young of other causes, were misdiagnosed or carried the change without developing symptoms.

In the clinic, familial and sporadic ALS usually look alike. A neurologist generally cannot tell them apart from symptoms or examination findings alone.

Genetic counselors help patients and relatives decide whether to be tested. They also explain what a result would mean and how to share it with family members. In the U.S., the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act bars genetic discrimination in health insurance and most employment. It does not cover life, disability or long-term care insurance.

There is no cure for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and no treatment reverses the loss of motor neurons. Care combines disease-modifying drugs such as riluzole and edaravone with coordinated support for breathing, nutrition, mobility and communication.

Riluzole is a pill taken by mouth, usually twice a day. It is thought to work by reducing the effects of glutamate, a chemical messenger that can overstimulate and damage nerve cells. The drug aims to slow the disease rather than restore strength that has already been lost. It can raise liver enzymes. Doctors typically order regular blood tests while a patient takes it. Liquid and film forms exist for people who have trouble swallowing tablets.

Edaravone is an antioxidant. It is designed to limit oxidative stress, a type of cellular damage linked to the breakdown of motor neurons. It can be given as an intravenous infusion on a repeating schedule of treatment days and drug-free periods. An oral suspension is also available. Access to each drug differs between countries. A patient in one place may be offered one medicine but not the other.

Medicine is only part of the plan. Many people with ALS are seen at specialized multidisciplinary clinics, where several specialists assess them during a single visit. A typical team can include a neurologist, a respiratory therapist, a dietitian, a speech-language pathologist, physical and occupational therapists and a social worker.

Breathing support becomes central as the diaphragm and chest muscles weaken. Noninvasive ventilation, delivered through a mask, can ease shortness of breath and improve sleep. Devices that assist coughing help clear secretions from the airways.

Nutrition is another focus. When eating becomes slow or unsafe, a feeding tube placed directly into the stomach, called a gastrostomy, can supply calories, fluids and medication.

Braces, walkers, wheelchairs and home modifications help with mobility and daily tasks. For communication, speech-language pathologists can introduce text-to-speech apps and eye-tracking devices. Some patients use voice banking. They record their own voice while speech is still clear. A computer can later use those recordings to speak for them.

As A Therapist, I Thought Caregiver Burnout Wouldn’t Come For Me. I Was Wrong.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The sandwich generation is sorely lacking in support.

Tuesday, 8:10 p.m. I’m helping my 87-year-old mother brush her teeth and change for bed. My 10-year-old calls from the room next door that he and his dad are done reading his latest Big Nate book. He asks (again) when I’m coming to his room to say goodnight. I try not to rush my mother, but I’m exhausted after a long day, and keenly aware of missing those precious minutes right before bedtime when you get the real deal from your child about everything that happened that day.

After months of juggling my work as a therapist with caring for my aging mother (who has been diagnosed with dementia), and being a present, engaged mom and wife, I had to admit that I was dropping some balls. Having all of this on my plate was clearly not working.

I knew I was not alone — I’ve been having this conversation with many friends and clients, also in their 40s or early 50s. In fact, 47% of caregivers under the age of 50 are part of the sandwich generation, those of us who are raising children and caring for aging parents at the same time. If we aren't talking about perimenopause with all its patches and pills, we’re talking about the health concerns of our parents and, potentially, our grandparents as well.

The burnout I was feeling falls particularly heavily on the shoulders of women. Even if you have siblings, most of the caregiving responsibility consistently falls on one person, and that person is typically a woman. Women shoulder the majority of caregiving responsibilities, often to the detriment of their careers. To make matters worse, some caregivers may have a complicated relationship with the parent who is now dependent on them.

Caring for your parent can be immensely rewarding and depressing. Sometimes I feel deeply honored to care for the person who dedicated her life to caring for me. Other times, my mom can’t remember why her beloved husband hasn’t come home yet — my father passed away several years ago. She really doesn’t enjoy having to be bathed by her child, for that matter.

As a mental health professional who has sat with clients going through this very thing, I expected to have more of the answers, to be able to cope and balance more than someone else due to my experience. But the truth is, nothing can truly prepare you for your induction into the sandwich generation.

No matter how we try to overcome the difficulty of caregiving as individuals, we must acknowledge that there’s minimal financial or systemic help for consistent care for the elderly living at home. Some Medicare programs can provide all your healthcare in one place, which is obviously better than driving all over town talking to doctors who aren’t talking to each other. But they often only offer last-minute daily help and, because of the high need in our country, you typically see a new caregiver each day. While better than nothing, it’s clearly not a great situation for a parent who is already confused in their day-to-day life. Some programs actually pay you, the caregiver, for your work, but that doesn’t give you back the missed moments with your own children or restore those career opportunities.

Eventually my husband and I realized that we needed help. Fortunately, we could afford to pay a nurse out of pocket, but the costs have been steep: tens of thousands of dollars annually to have carers for my mother seven days a week, and that’s with me still doing bedtime duty on the weekends. It’s simply not realistic or sustainable for most people.

And one of the challenges that we don’t often talk about is the guilt. Even if you are able to defy the odds and pay someone to come in to help, you still feel guilty about it and get caught up in the “shoulds.” I should be able to care for my parents and my children at the same time; people have been doing this forever with a lot less help. I should be grateful to be able to spend whatever time I have left with my parent. The important part missing from that is the fact that many of us don’t have villages in the way we used to — that nearby family network that would support each other during all the stages of life.

Our lack of a village came into focus for me recently when we adopted a baby into our family. She has brought us more joy than we could have imagined, and I see how much a community would change this experience for us. In my Mommy and Me classes, so many of the young mothers have discussed having their parents come and stay with them during the early weeks of having a newborn. What a stark contrast for me as a mother, in a different phase in my life, where I am taking care of my parent, rather than her taking care of me and my children.

This is a problem that is not going anywhere. Around the country and the world, people are making tough decisions every day — draining bank accounts, missing out on kids’ milestones, declining promotions and date nights with their partner — to take care of a loved one. The physical, financial, and emotional toll can cause extensive damage to the caregiver’s well-being in so many ways.

We also know what to do about this problem. Studies show that while caregiver burnout is a worldwide issue, it is less so in the countries where (surprise, surprise) there is a strong public healthcare system, flexible work environments for caregivers, and affordable private health insurance. These kinds of safeguards can be a key part of rebuilding a new kind of village and community support. The answer is there. The question is if we will take heed before we all burn out.

US President Donald Trump has famously transformed the magnificent Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool into a high profile showcase for the algae biofuel industry, and he stands firm in his faith that the tiny green microalgae can produce renewable fuels at scale. In fact, on Monday, November 2, the President held his all-important closing message for the 2026 midterm election cycle right there at the Reflecting Pool. Hey, maybe Trump knows something about algae biofuel…

The Reflecting Pool is a massive body of shallow water that extends from the foot of the Lincoln Memorial in Washington, DC. Situated on filled ground in former marshlands, the Reflecting Pool provides a golden opportunity to showcase the ability of the domestic biofuel industry to take advantage of the built infrastructure, rather than taking up valuable farmland with conventional energy crops like corn or soybeans.

Although algae cultivation was probably not the original intention of the Reflecting Pool, it has been a perennial host to algae ever since its construction in the 1920’s, with the humid environment and temperate climate of DC lending a hand.

In earlier years, the Pool was drained and refilled twice a year to control the growth. By the 1990’s, the structure was clearly sinking into the marshes, sparking calls for a major structural overhaul and the addition of a water circulating system.

The soup-to-nuts rehab was completed in 2012, and yet the algae persisted. Finally, when Trump took office for the second time in January of 2025, he lit upon a brilliant idea of turning lemons into lemonade. The Reflecting Pool would be re-made as a monument to the domestic algae biofuel industry.

By the summer of 2026, the transformation was complete and the algae were thriving, as confirmed by satellite imagery. Others mocked, but the President stood by the plan. After all, biomass is a key element in his “American Energy Dominance” policy, and where better to showcase biofuel than in the nation’s capitol, to be seen and admired by millions of tourists.

And so, that explains why Trump chose the Reflecting Pool as the seemingly incongruous scene of his Election Day eve closing message, as reported somewhat incredulously by The Guardian among many others.

“On the eve of a consequential election day, with polls indicating that most voters feel the US president has grown too focused on building projects in the nation’s capital, he stood in front of the newly opened reflecting pool to discuss – in detail – changes to the pool’s pipe, the grass surrounding the area and granite,” observed The Guardian.

Algae biofuel surfaced regularly on the pages of CleanTechnica in the early 2000’s, with the high oil content and rapid growth cycle of algae being the attractions along with the potential for developing next-generation farming and extraction systems leading to a competitive renewable energy resource (see more algae background here).

Not much progress has been made since then, and stakeholders in the field have weathered much skepticism if not downright mockery. A seal of approval from the President of the United States of America is just what the industry needs to get to the next level.

Indeed, a fresh round of activity has surfaced in recent years, with the UK firm HutanBio being one example. The startup aims to generate profitability by leveraging the carbon-capturing angle of algae farming.

Earlier this year Hutan signed an MOU (Memorandum of Understanding) with the Irish firm Utopia World Investment and the biodiesel refiner Wakud International to scale up its algae farming system, with Oman being the location. “Our unique microalgae strain, Sphaerica, is grown in seawater, on non-arable coastal land, using CO₂ and sunlight creating a genuinely low-carbon biocrude stream that the industry currently lacks and urgently needs,” HutanBio says of itself.

As described by HutanBio, Sphaerica yields up to ten times more oil than typical algae biofuel strains. Hutan expects the high level of productivity to dovetail with Oman’s high level of solar radiation, enabling it to leapfrog over its competitors.

The Oman operation is set for operation at a demonstration scale before the end of 2027, which is just around the corner. Meanwhile, Japanese automaker Mazda is also beginning to crack the algae code.

On October 7, Mazda announced that its microalgae biofuel research program has nailed down support from NEDO, the New Energy and Industrial Technology Development Organization of Japan. The Mazda program is in early stages, with scale-up expected by the 2030’s. If all goes according to plan, Mazda will use its homemade algae biofuel to factory-fill the tanks of every diesel vehicle that rolls off its assembly lines.

Evidently Mazda does not anticipate that drivers can pump out algae biofuel at any corner gas station for their next fill-up. Still, it’s another sign that that years-long research programs are finally leading to commercial application.

For a glimpse into the future, check out newly published research from the Saitama University in Japan. Where conventional systems involve cultivating algae, drying it, and processing it to remove oils, the Saitama team has developed a strain of cyanobacteria (commonly known as blue-green algae) that can express free fatty acids outside of the cell and deposit them directly into a culture medium. Among other benefits, the Saitama system does not require destroying the algae with each harvesting cycle.

As for the US, ExxonMobil was the standard-bearer for early stage algae biofuel research throughout the early 2000’s. By 2017, the company was confident enough to put up millions for an elaborate advertising blitz that showcased its efforts to the world.

Or not, as the case may be. In 2019, Massachusetts State Attorney General Maura Healey filed suit against Exxon, alleging that the biofuel ads were deceptive. “Specifically, the complaint alleges that Exxon systematically and intentionally has misled Massachusetts investors about material climate-driven risks to its business and has deceived consumers about the central role its fossil fuel products play in causing climate change,” AG Healy’s office explained.

Exxon subsequently dropped the program altogether, but the lawsuit persists. Among the details coming to light is a deposition by an Exxon scientist who worked on the algae program. Under questioning by attorneys with the AG’s office, the scientist asserted that the claims in Exxon’s ads were “patently false,” and that he said as much to the company.

It’s too bad Exxon dropped the ball before Trump had a chance to pick up the algae biofuel torch. Oh, well. We’ll always have the Reflecting Pool…

It’s the first major exhibition focused on her collection, and some of the pieces will be donated to the Bard Graduate Center’s Study Collection.

Goff’s interest in fashion design has intersected with her writing career in interesting ways. The estate of legendary costume designer Edith Head chose Goff to adapt her story for a biopic. “It’s all so surreal. “People have been fascinated by my collection,” Goff says. “It’s a bit rare to see, frankly, collectors in this space like me. I mean like me in all sorts of ways, not just as an African American woman, but an African American woman who’s not from a privileged background. It’s important to me that I’m not the last one like me in these spaces.”

I wrote a piece for The Daily Beast several years ago about the most influential Black designers in history. It was one of the most memorable pieces I’ve written. I don’t think at the time I was aware of how profoundly it impacted me, but I learned how many seminal Black American designers died of AIDS in a really short window of time. Willi Smith, one of the first Black designers to achieve major crossover commercial success in department stores, died. And so did Isaia Rankin and then Patrick Kelly. What I took away is that so much history was lost, but I also think about how different the future of American fashion might be today because these were really trailblazing and hugely influential.

They weren’t just influential Black American designers, but hugely influential American designers. Isaia Rankin basically had invented athleisure wear. It was his idea that you should be able to go from a coffee shop to a gym to the club. [He made] one of the first pieces by a Black designer I acquired online. The really cool thing is how many people I’ve met along this journey who have not only become cheerleaders but actually reach out to me about pieces. I’m from Houston, and we have the Houston Museum of African American Culture here. We’re living in a really interesting time where people are having conversations about the politics of diversity, but one of the things that’s cool about fashion is that people of different political persuasions were like, “I think this collection’s really cool. I read an article about your collection, I ended up ordering a book about Elizabeth Keckley, or I ended up going down a rabbit hole about Ann Lowe.” At this moment, to be able to do an exhibition like this in my hometown felt particularly poignant.

Melanie Lawson has been a legendary anchor in Houston for decades. I sent him a note about how much Melanie inspired me and then mentioned my collection.

It was just kismet. They’ve actually never done a fashion exhibition. It was a real gamble. But what I so appreciated is that they had the same vision that, in a time in which our world is so divided, it’s really beautiful when you can find pieces of art that people can come together around.

Inside Sophie Bille Brahe’s Danish Seaside Home, Where Every Object Tells a Story

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

It’s almost dusk in Copenhagen when jewelry designer Sophie Bille Brahe tells me over the phone that she’s about to take a quick dip in the ocean before dinner. It’s a small ritual tied to the reason she chose her five-bedroom, three-bathroom seaside home, 20 minutes north of the city center, in the first place. Designed by Danish architect Ib Lunding in 1929, the house sits on one of three small streets of similar homes in what Danes call the “flower neighborhood,” each one a slight variation on the next. For Bille Brahe, it’s this setting—and the sensory elements that come with it—that were non-negotiable. “I open up my window and I can see trees. I can smell the sea,” she says. “For me, that’s very important to have in my life every day.”

The space is a respite for the designer, who views the home as a collection of memories and a backdrop for her bustling family life, kids, and border collie included. It’s also where her namesake fine jewelry line begins. In fact, Bille Brahe often develops her designs within the home’s walls before she ever takes them to the workshop. “I sometimes catch these sensations, these small ideas, and make them a little bit more stable by doing drawings of them here at home. And then, when I have an idea of what I want to do, I’m ready to go to the workshop and start,” she explains. In those beginning stages, she works alone. “I need to be able to make really ugly things or really great things,” she says. “I need a bubble around me.”

That bubble is her workspace, an all-white room anchored by an 1870s Swedish fireplace Bille Brahe had installed when she moved in. “It has gold detailing, and it’s so beautiful,” she says. “A fireplace has a way of gathering family and speaks to a house built with love.” Nearby sits her desk, which belonged to her father’s best friend. “It’s basically where everything I make starts,” she says. “I’d loved it since I was a kid.”

Pulled up to the desk is a chair that visitors to her New York showroom may recognize: a curved stainless-steel design by Mats Theselius reupholstered in Sahco zebra fabric. The same seating appears in the showroom, a throughline between her personal and professional worlds. Marc Chagall’s Soleil Couchant, a Le Klint lamp, a neon work by Gun Gordillo, and a collection of seashells share the desk with her sketches and notes. The room opens onto the dining room, centered on a Børge Mogensen Øresund table surrounded by Gio Ponti chairs and a bouquet of sunflowers resting on it.

Of course, Bille Brahe furnished her boutiques with intention. In Copenhagen, she sought out one-of-a-kind Danish furniture and even began making her own vases in Murano. In New York, she tracked down a rare Poul Kjærholm table, square with a gray leather top instead of the round marble version many Danes grew up eating breakfast at. At home, she followed a similar rule and took her time, layering pieces slowly and making sure each one felt collected and had a story connected. “It took me many years to find a sofa, because every time I bought one I’d almost have a panic attack about the idea of buying something new, something everybody could have,” she says. “Everything I have in my home I’ve inherited, borrowed, or a friend made for me. If something doesn’t have a story, it doesn’t belong.”

Her jewel-toned Svenskt Tenn sofa is piled with pillows, each one a gift from a Swedish friend who came to visit. Beside it sits a slim side table, part drink trolley, stacked with books, a seashell, and a lamp she inherited from her grandfather. Most of the lighting in the house is inherited, except the Noguchi lanterns, which she brings back one at a time from trips to Japan. Another Japan find, the Nextmaruni Armless Chair by the architect duo SANAA (known as the Rabbit Chair for its ear-like back), sits beneath a pink painting by Danish artist Tal R.

Above all, though, it is a family house at its core. “It never feels like it’s an empty house,” she says. “I absolutely love my life with kids.” That’s particularly evident in the kitchen, where the day begins with coffee, breakfast for the kids, and, when the weather allows, the garden door thrown open. “It’s not a big house, so the kitchen needs to be efficient,” she says. “I cook a lot, so it ends up being a kitchen that can manage a lot of food without a lot of space.” Despite its small footprint, the room is warm, with kids’ art hung above the seating, embroidered tablecloths dressing the table, and an airy pendant overhead.

The same energy carries into the hallway, with its rounded window (a signature of the neighborhood’s houses) and walls in a blush pink by Farrow & Ball, the same color as the hallway in her previous home. When she moved, she changed everything else but kept the pink. “I normally don’t like colors on walls,” she says. “But I felt this was bringing something I used to love into the new house.”

Outside on the terrace, that pull toward nature continues. Every plant on the deck is one she’s had for 15 years or more, from a pomegranate tree she’s carried from home to home to the pink nerines called the “autumn flower,” and she has a story for each of them. According to Bille Brahe, there’s no need for more than this. If the trees are close enough to smell and the sea near enough to hear, she’s set and then some. “I’m not a person who needs a lot. I just need something close and clear, and if I have that, I can do what I need to do.”

— Newcomer organizations have names that resemble established groups

Several organizations have sprung up in recent years with names that sound very similar to long-standing established nonprofit public health and medical groups, whose science-based policies have withstood the test of time.

Here's a list of some of these organizations whose names, at first glance, may confuse prospective members or donors.

The American Public Health Association, founded in 1872, has some 25,000 individual members and more than 150,000 allied health professionals and advocates for public health science and spending. It publishes the American Journal of Public Health. Its CEO, Georges Benjamin, MD, has been a vociferous critic of the Trump administration's policies. The APHA has filed a lawsuit against its vaccination schedule policies and several others over grant funding.

The Academy of Public Health was founded in February 2025 by Jay Bhattacharya, MD, PhD, the Trump administration's NIH director, and others who questioned some of the ways the U.S. responded to the COVID pandemic, and who advocated the controversial policy of herd immunity. Its founders also criticized publication policies of many mainstream medical journals. It publishes the Journal of the Academy of Public Health.

The American Academy of Pediatrics was launched in 1930. It now has some 67,000 pediatricians as members. It has opposed Trump administration immunization policies as well as statements by HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. questioning vaccine schedules that have led to lower immunization rates among children, and more illness. It supports gender-affirming care for transgender youth.

The American College of Pediatricians was launched in 2002 by several pediatricians who left the AAP to protest the support of allowing same-sex couples to adopt children. It holds that life begins at fertilization. Its website says it is the "leading U.S. medical organization protecting the biological integrity of children against transgender ideology."

The American College of Obstetricians & Gynecologists began in 1951 to focus on issues specific to women's health, including family planning. It now focuses on advocating for maternal health as a national priority. It has about 60,000 members and publishes the journal Obstetrics & Gynecology. It has opposed several Trump administration policies, including warnings about use of antidepressants during pregnancy, suggesting these are linked with autism, miscarriage, and birth defects.

The American Association of Pro-Life Obstetricians and Gynecologists was an ACOG special interest group until 2013 when ACOG ended that designation. It opposes abortion, suggesting it is associated with health risks such as depression and substance abuse, and supports "the physician's right of conscience in medical decision making" regarding abortion.

The American Academy of Family Physicians began in 1947 and now says it is the voice of 124,500 family doctors. It publishes a journal, American Family Physician. It has opposed several Trump administration actions, such as its removal of an AAFP member and other members from the CDC's long-standing Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices.

The American College of Family Medicine began in the spring of 2023. It opposes mandatory abortion training in residencies and maintains that "abortion is never medically necessary and should not be offered or performed by any health professional."

The American College of Surgeons was founded in 1913 to improve surgical outcomes. It has launched numerous surgical quality improvement programs for surgical specialties to learn strategies and compare results, and its website has a surgical risk calculator. The Bureau of Securities notice, dated Oct. 30 and signed by Acting Bureau Chief Keith A. Alt, rescinds the order and the $5 million penalty that came with it. The withdrawal is without prejudice and without a ruling on the merits.

Coinbase disclosed the development in its third-quarter report filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission, which states that the regulator informed the company of its intent to rescind on Oct. 29.

Coinbase CEO Brian Armstrong said New Jersey users would regain access to staking “very soon.”

As of Monday, the exchange’s eligibility page continued to list New Jersey among the states where customers cannot stake new principal, and no reopening date has been announced.

The order traces to June 6, 2023, when the New Jersey bureau joined securities regulators in nine other states — Alabama, California, Illinois, Kentucky, Maryland, South Carolina, Vermont, Washington and Wisconsin — in a coordinated action against Coinbase’s staking program.

The states alleged the service amounted to an unregistered securities offering under state law.

California led the task force; its action covered more than 600,000 accounts. In July 2023, Coinbase agreed with California, New Jersey, South Carolina and Wisconsin to block customers in those states from staking new funds pending final adjudication. Maryland reached a similar agreement in October 2023.

Customers who had staked before the orders kept earning rewards, but could not add principal or compound the rewards they received.

The state cases ran parallel to the SEC’s June 2023 lawsuit against Coinbase, which named staking among its claims. The SEC dismissed that case in February 2025. Alabama, Kentucky, Illinois, South Carolina and Vermont dismissed, vacated or withdrew their own actions in March and April 2025. New Jersey is the sixth of the original ten states to step back.

Rescinding the order ends the restriction on new staking in New Jersey. It does not resolve the underlying legal question. The bureau made no finding on whether Coinbase’s staking program is a security, and the “without prejudice” language preserves its ability to file a new action. Coinbase’s own filing calls the state matters “preliminary” and says an adverse resolution “could have a material impact” on the business.

The filing describes a planned rescission rather than a completed one, which explains the gap between Armstrong’s statement and the unchanged eligibility page. The change for New Jersey residents arrives when Coinbase updates its systems, not when the notice was signed.

Coinbase has framed the state restrictions as a competitive disadvantage. In an April 2025 post, the company’s Ryan VanGrack argued the orders singled out one provider while residents could stake through other platforms. Coinbase put forgone rewards across the four restricted states at more than $130 million as of October 2025.

Four states remain. California, Maryland and Wisconsin continue to bar new staking on Coinbase while their cases proceed; Washington permits staking but is pursuing a $4 million fine. Coinbase estimates California residents have missed more than $89 million in rewards since 2023.

The decision lands as federal policy has shifted but not settled. The SEC has withdrawn from the registration-based cases it brought in 2023, and the CLARITY market structure bill passed the House in 2025 but stalled in the Senate. State securities regulators are not bound by the SEC’s enforcement priorities, which is why the staking cases outlived the federal one by more than a year and a half.

A future SEC, or a future bureau chief in Trenton, could revisit the question.

Infrared rays are uniquely capable of cutting through empty space and bringing warmth to the objects they strike; in fact, that’s how the sun heats the earth. Today’s infrared heaters bring warmth to objects, like clothes and skin rather than the surrounding air—an efficient mechanism in stark contrast to convection heaters, which warm the surrounding air before heating objects in that space.

Although heating the air may distribute heat more uniformly throughout a room, much energy is wasted heating unoccupied space, such as high ceilings or the outdoor air. Infrared heaters use 100 percent of the heat they produce to bring focused warmth to the objects in the path of its rays, making them much more energy efficient. An infrared heater’s energy efficiency, space coverage, and other features will vary depending on certain factors. Shoppers may want to keep the following considerations in mind while on the hunt for the best infrared heater for their homes.

When determining the right size of an infrared heater to buy, think about the square footage of the space you want to warm. Generally, infrared heaters are relatively small and portable. More powerful units for bigger areas are available, but they’re typically larger. Also, consider how much floor space an infrared heater will take up; this will vary depending on whether the unit sits vertically (in the case of a tower heater) or horizontally (like most standard heaters).

The size of the space you want to warm will also influence how powerful your heater should be. The amount of heat generated by these devices, measured in BTUs, influences the size of the space they can heat.

The BTU output of an infrared heater generally correlates with the number of watts the heater uses, which can range between 300 and 1,500 watts. (Most are capped at 1,500 watts because that’s the maximum amount a standard 110V household electrical outlet can handle.) With convection space heaters, 1 watt is equivalent to 3.412 BTUs, so it’s simple to determine the size of a space they can warm; it’s not so easy with infrared heaters.

Infrared heaters can use various heating elements, such as quartz (the most common and most efficient), ceramic, and carbon, to produce and emit infrared rays with varying levels of energy efficiency. Supplemental fans or blowers aren’t necessary, but they can provide both focused infrared heat and diffused heat through convection.

These factors impact how an infrared heater’s power rating will affect its heat output and space coverage. Just because an infrared heater boasts a higher power rating doesn’t mean it produces more heat and provides greater space coverage—much depends on the individual characteristics of the heater.

One way to calculate the general amount of power a heater should generate is to multiply the space’s square footage by 10. For example, a 100-square-foot room (10 feet by 10 feet) will need a 1,000-watt heater (100 x 10 = 1,000).

The location of an infrared heater warrants consideration due to its unique heating action.
