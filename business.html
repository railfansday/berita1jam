If you drive to work, price each commuting mile at the IRS business mileage rate, which counts what a car costs to own, not just gas. Pay for transit, vanpools, and parking with pretax money through a commuter benefit at work. Then drive fewer days with a carpool or a remote day or two a week.

Gas is the commuting cost you notice. Insurance, repairs, and the value your car loses with each mile never show up at the pump.

The IRS business mileage rate, 76 cents a mile, counts them along with the gas. The IRS sets it from a yearly study of what cars cost to own and drive. It’s an average, and the full cost of owning a car varies by model.

Say you drive 20 miles each way, 230 days a year, and count each mile at 76 cents. That’s 9,200 miles and almost $7,000 a year. If you ride transit, add up a year of fares and parking instead.

Next, pay what you can with pretax money. Under the IRS’s fringe benefit rules, a commuter benefit at work lets you buy transit passes and vanpool rides with up to $340 a month of pretax pay. A separate $340 a month can go to parking at or near work, or where you catch transit, a vanpool, or a carpool. That pay skips federal income tax and the 7.65% Social Security and Medicare tax, so in the 22% federal bracket each dollar of fares or parking costs about 70 cents.

Then cut the trips. Working from home two of your five days takes 40% off those 9,200 miles and brings the cost at 76 cents a mile down from almost $7,000 to about $4,200. Gas and upkeep on the skipped miles stop at once, and a car driven less lasts more years.

On the days you still go in, each of these cuts your driving.

Look in your benefits portal for a commuter benefit, along with other workplace perks you may be missing, then ask your manager about working from home a day or two a week.

American Airlines’ Airbus A321neo Fleet Grows To 90, Expects 2 More & 1 A321XLR By Year’s End

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

American Airlines has expanded its Airbus A321neo fleet to 90 aircraft, with two more standard A321neos and one A321XLR expected before the end of 2026. The latest arrival continues the carrier's fleet modernization program, in which Airbus narrowbodies play an increasingly important role in its domestic and international networks.

The latest delivery follows an unusually busy period at the end of last month, when American received four A321-family aircraft in just seven days. Those deliveries included three standard A321neos and one A321XLR, registered N311NY. With no further Boeing deliveries expected this year, Airbus remains central to American's near-term fleet renewal strategy.

American's standard A321neo fleet has reached 90 aircraft, with two more expected before the end of the year. The airline is also anticipating one additional A321XLR, extending its investment in long-range, single-aisle aircraft. If all three outstanding deliveries arrive as planned, American will finish 2026 with 92 standard A321neos, alongside its growing fleet of 12 A321XLRs.

American's third-quarter financial disclosures showed that it had 165 Airbus A320-family aircraft on order as of September 30, with seven scheduled for delivery during the final quarter. The subsequent acceleration in October, when four A321-family aircraft arrived within a week, highlighted the program's pace. American also expects another 27 A321-family deliveries in 2027, although the precise split between the standard A321neo and A321XLR has not been disclosed.

The A321XLR is particularly important to American's international expansion, allowing it to serve longer routes with fewer passengers than a widebody aircraft would typically accommodate.

Brian Znotins, American's former Senior Vice President of Network Planning, highlighted the aircraft's importance when announcing the carrier's 2027 international expansion.

"The A321XLR is transforming our international network, expanding it to offer destinations to satisfy every travel palate. Combined with our investments enhancing our onboard product and in-flight experience, travelers have more reasons to choose American when they fly around the world."

American received its first A321XLR in October 2025, becoming the first US operator of the type. Its original 50-aircraft commitment was subsequently reduced to 40, including five delivered by the end of 2025. Each aircraft features 20 Flagship Suite business class seats, 12 premium economy seats, and 123 economy seats, emphasizing premium revenue over maximum capacity.

The XLR restores some of the long-range narrowbody capability American lost when it retired its 757s in 2020. Its economics make thinner transatlantic routes viable without deploying a much larger 787 Dreamliner. The aircraft initially entered service on premium transcontinental flights, before expanding into international markets as more aircraft became available.

American's 2027 schedule demonstrates its ambitions, with four additional European routes assigned to the XLR. Philadelphia-Vienna, measuring approximately 4,321 miles (6,955 km), is particularly notable as it will become the world's longest narrowbody flight. The route will operate daily from May 6 through January 2028, while New York gains new services to Amsterdam and Nice.

13 new routes were planned but now only seven are scheduled.

The standard A321neo remains central to American's high-capacity narrowbody strategy. With its fleet now at 90 aircraft, it still has firm orders for 126 more airframes as the airline continues investing in the type to support busy domestic routes and replace older aircraft.

Configured with 196 seats, including 20 First Class and 47 Main Cabin Extra seats, American's legacy A321neos accommodate 41 more passengers than the XLR. It is particularly valuable on busy domestic routes connecting American's major hubs and supports the gradual replacement of older narrowbodies. Its versatility extends internationally, with Philadelphia-Reykjavík scheduled to launch in May 2027.

American's Boeing 737 MAX 8s complement the A321neos, offering 172 seats on routes requiring less capacity. The airline also has substantial outstanding Boeing narrowbody orders, including the larger 737 MAX 10, although deliveries of that variant are not expected until 2029 or later. With an eventual planned fleet size of more than 250 A321-family aircraft, Airbus narrowbodies will continue to play a leading role in American's domestic modernization and expanding transatlantic network.

Keep a log of one normal workweek, written every half hour, to see where your hours go before you try to fit more in. Then hand off or drop the work that doesn’t serve your top priorities, and book calendar time for the work that does, while your week still has open hours.

When long days still leave you behind on your top priorities, staying later only adds hours of the same mix of work.

You win time back by dropping tasks, not by doing all of them faster, and memory is a poor guide to which tasks to drop. Looking back on a week, you’ll remember the long meetings and the big deadline, not the five-minute favors, status emails, and file searches that filled the time in between.

So log the week as it happens. Pick a week without a holiday or a deadline crunch, and every 30 minutes write down what you did in the half hour before, interruptions included. A notebook works, or use Clockify, a time tracker whose free plan times each task and breaks the week down in a report.

On Friday, sort every entry into three groups: work only you can do, work someone else could do, and work nobody needs. Hand the second group to a teammate or a direct report, or automate it with an email template or a scheduled report. Stop doing the third, after you check with your manager that no one relies on it.

Take a manager whose log shows nine hours a week in status meetings, including two hour-long weekly check-ins where people only share updates. She replaces those two hours of meetings with a shared written update that takes each person 15 minutes a week to post and read, so she and every attendee get an hour and 45 minutes back each week.

Before you leave on Friday, book time on next week’s calendar for your top two or three priorities, so new meetings get scheduled around those blocks.

Jason Blum and James Wan Discuss Why Horror Is Hollywood’s Hottest Genre and How ‘Obsession’ Hit Big at Blumhouse Atomic’s Business of Fear

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Blumhouse Atomic bosses Jason Blum and James Wan took a victory lap Wednesday at their Business of Fear panel, during which they discussed horror’s banner year at the box office and why “Obsession” became the breakout hit of the summer.

But before the horror talk began, they had to address the name change. Blum’s Blumhouse and Wan’s Atomic Monster merged in 2024, and the combined labels were therefore known as Blumhouse Atomic Monster. Recently, in early November, Blumhouse Atomic Monster rebranded to just Blumhouse Atomic. Blum explained they rebranded to give a more definitive identity to the overarching banner of the two companies, while also clearing the way for both distinct labels to take credit for their work. Wan added that Blumhouse Atomic Monster was also “a bit of a mouthful.”

“As Jason pointed out, it really still is Blumhouse movies and Atomic Monster movies,” Wan added. “We’re still making the movies as one company, but still under two labels. We put out a bunch of movies, and so we have to sort of divide it. Some movies play better at Atomic Monster, some movies play better at Blumhouse. At the start of movies, even though it’s going to say Blumhouse Atomic Presents, our logos will still stand individually.”

It was then on to horror’s stellar year. Moderator Ray Subers, head of film at NRG, presented Blum and Wan with a stat that theater attendance for horror films in 2026 went up 7% from pre-pandemic levels, while attendance for all other genre films (action, sci-fi, rom-coms) decreased. Blum theorized this was because watching horror on the big screen is “participatory,” and that audiences craved a “collective experience” after emerging from COVID lockdown.

“I think that after COVID, audiences ran back to sporting events and ran back to concerts, but they had been fed movies,” Blum said. “Kids had been fed movies so much at home that it took longer to discover and remember the movies [in the theater]. And I think horror specifically, if there’s any genre that’s close to a concert or a sporting event, I think horror is more than anything else because it’s so participatory. I think movies generally, but horror specifically, are kind of joining sports and concerts as a great way to get out of your house and have a collective, fun, exciting thing to do that doesn’t involve your phone.”

The conversation then shifted to “Obsession.” Blum joined as an executive producer shortly after Focus Features acquired the film, which, from a budget of less than $1 million, grossed over $500 million worldwide. Blum said he thinks “Obsession” took off for two reasons. The first is because young people can relate to the “universal feeling” of being “obsessed or having someone obsessed with them.” The second reason was that director Curry Barker sharpened his filmmaking skills on YouTube and social media before coming to Hollywood.

“I think that he has a dialogue with his audience, which he has honed over years and years and years of putting little pieces of content on YouTube and watching his audience react and adjusting to it,” Blum said. “The best way I can describe it is it almost feels like when you’re watching the movie, Curry is like whispering in your ear little funny things or like you’re talking to this invisible hand that’s directing the movie in a way that’s very unique.”

Wan added that the success of “Obsession” has inspired a whole new generation of filmmakers wanting to make scary movies, which reminds him of when he was trying to break into the industry with “Saw.”

“That stigma is gone. Horror has crossed over from the black sheep of the household, right?” Wan said. “I really do credit the younger generation with accepting the genre in a big way and not turning your nose down on it in a snooty way. When I was doing ‘Saw’ people looked down on it, right? And it was only a certain demographic that would come out and support the films that we made. But today, like today, it is cool and hip to be making horror films.”

An analyst made a deep cut to his price target on the coal company.

By Eric Volkman – Nov 18, 2026 at 8:09PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Peabody Energy ›

Investors struggled to find the energy to be positive about Peabody Energy (BTU -1.45%) on Wednesday. An analyst tracking the storied coal company made a significant cut to his price target, which dampened sentiment toward the stock. Its shares closed the day nearly 1.5% lower.

That move came before the market open and was made by Freedom Broker's Vitaly Kononov. He now believes Peabody stock is destined for $22 per share, down considerably from his former level of $30.

Despite the deep cut, Kononov remained bullish on the coal company's future, as he maintained his buy recommendation.

According to reports, the prognosticator's new take centered around Centurion, Peabody's flagship metallurgical coal mine in Australia. Kononov noted that the production outlook for the play has been cut three times so far this year. The analyst also considers coal prices to be vulnerable.

Centurion is rightfully considered to be Peabody's most important play, so how it develops will make a major difference to the company's results. What I'd be nervous about if I were a company shareholder is the future of the energy industry, an important client base for Peabody's thermal coal business, given Peabody's continued (and deep) involvement in the segment. Why The World's Debut Boeing 747 Was Built In Half The Time Of Other Aircraft Of Its Size In 1968

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Boeing 747 has earned its title as one of the most iconic airliners ever built for several reasons. It was the first widebody aircraft, easily distinguishable by a humped second deck, a key enabler in opening up aviation to the masses, and it enjoyed roughly fifty years of production. But equally as impressive as the Queen of the Skies itself is how it came about.

Boeing took the 747 from the drawing board to rollout remarkably quickly. That is, notably faster than the Airbus A380 program took to churn out its first model after officially launching, and quicker than both Boeing and Airbus took with their latest widebodies. What is more, Boeing pulled it off decades ago in the late 1960s.

Just 29 months separated the 747 project’s official launch and the rollout of the first model. Boeing announced on April 13, 1966, that it would build the 490-seat passenger transport that would come to be known as the 747. Exactly two years, five months, and 17 days later, on September 30, 1968, the first 747-100 ceremoniously rolled off the production line at a new purpose-built assembly plant in Everett, Washington.

Less than five months later, on February 9, 1969, the aircraft took off for its maiden flight. Test pilots Jack Waddell and Brien Wygle, accompanied by flight engineer Jess Wallick, took the controls, and the jumbo jet passed the major test with flying colors, paving the way for its introduction into service just under a year later in January 1970.

All told, that saw the Queen of the Skies go from a mere concept to carrying passengers in under four years. While that is no doubt a rapid turnaround by today’s standards, the reasons behind the pace are plentiful and clear-cut.

Behind it all was keen interest from Pan American World Airways, which had set out to source a jet two and a half times larger than the existing Boeing 707 against the backdrop of an increasingly busy market. Though the industry was mulling whether supersonic flight would come to dominate in the years ahead, there remained appeal for a huge, versatile airliner that could handle cargo operations rather than passenger services in the future, if needed.

As it turned out, Boeing had already looked into something similar, having lost out in a 1965 military transport design competition to the Lockheed C-5 Galaxy. Although the 747 was ultimately largely built from scratch, research into high-bypass engine technology and the thinking behind the 747’s distinctive hump can be traced back to the military project bid. As such, when Pan Am head Juan Trippe and then-Boeing president William Allen met that same year for initial discussions about the 747, something of a basis was already there.

It was Trippe himself who is said to have pushed so heavily for the 747. He had spent years beforehand attempting to open up aviation to the masses, overseeing the introduction of the world’s first economy fare in 1958, for instance. Given that an ambitious project like the 747 would only aid in reaching that goal, Trippe reportedly told Allen in 1965: “If you’ll build it, I’ll buy it.” By April 1966, the commitment was solidified through a $525 million deal — worth almost $5.5 billion today — for 25 of the new jumbo jets. This set an ambitious deadline of 1969 for the first delivery, though.

The 747 transcended aviation and became an iconic silhouette.

Nonetheless, Boeing did not waste any time in dwelling on the task at hand. After signing the contract with Pan Am, the design team of just a few hundred workers conceptualizing and writing up requirements for the 747 ballooned to 4,500 almost instantaneously. These workers, who would later become part of a group known as The Incredibles, were led by Joe Sutter, who would similarly earn the title of the Father of the 747 over time.

Sutter himself enjoyed a 40-year-long career at Boeing, although he recalled in a 2013 interview fearing for his job as he and his team rushed to get the 747 ready. Without public backing, Boeing found itself racking up debt on the back of the project, leaving Sutter under pressure to cut jobs. Instead, he requested hundreds more engineers. “I figured that was the day I lost my job at Boeing,” he said, “but I went to work the next day, and nobody said anything, so I kept working."

According to Boeing, some 50,000 employees ultimately ended up working on the jumbo jet over the course of its development cycle. Armed with an eye-watering 75,000 drawings, physical assembly of the first model got underway in September 1967, a year prior to its initial presentation to the world and only 16 months before the first flight. All of this, of course, required a specialized facility for production to actually be carried out.

Such a factory did not exist when Boeing first unveiled plans for the 747, so it had to be built almost in conjunction with the first model. Among the battles Sutter fought in his bid to steer the project to a timely conclusion was one over where his team would be based. Faced with the proposal of being sent to California, Sutter argued against it and insisted his engineers stay close to where those who would eventually build the aircraft would work.

This turned out to be Everett, which was far from Boeing’s first choice to house the 747 program initially, but prevailed on similar fears that key personnel would not relocate in great numbers to a new facility elsewhere. Regardless of where the 747 would be built, a new building would have to be constructed to accommodate such a vast aircraft and all the equipment needed to build it.

Under the tight deadline set by the deal with Pan Am, Boeing set about clearing forest and then building the facility in 1967. Work began just before assembly of the first 747 started, but the two efforts overlapped, meaning the prototype was under construction in an incomplete factory for a period. When the former was eventually completed, it took the crown as the largest building in the world by volume — a title it retains to this day and a reflection of the mammoth undertaking by Boeing to get the project off the ground.

The original designs for the Boeing 747 were made with a different purpose in mind. Find out how the 747 came to be as we know it today.

Naturally, such an effort required vast financial backing, and it took a monumental amount of risk on Boeing’s part to get the first 747 successfully turned around at such a pace. Boeing had secured $525 million — or $21 million per aircraft — under its deal with Pan Am. This covered barely half of the program's total $1 billion costas it transpired; however, adjusted for inflation, it would exceed $10 billion today.

As a result, Boeing faced financial pressure and was forced to turn to the banks, which explains Sutter being asked to cut engineers midway through the program. He said in 2013 that “it was a big gamble,” adding, “I think the only reason that it happened is that Bill Allen was a forward-seeking guy, and Juan Trippe from Pan Am also had that characteristic.”

Despite racking up debt, the gamble paid off, and the 747 was a resounding success. Not only did the first model get built and delivered on time, but the factory Boeing built for the jumbo jet has enjoyed decades of service since. Some 1,574 747s rolled out of the site over the life of the program, culminating in the delivery of a final 747-8 Freighter variant to Atlas Air in 2023. Notably, this saw production of the 747 end just a year before that of its primary challenger, the A380, despite its initial introduction decades earlier.

The A380 is an impressive feat of engineering in its own right. The industry moved in a different direction, and while Boeing hit a sweet spot with the 747, the appetite for huge quad jets was by no means as strong as it was at the A380’s commercial entry in 2007. All told, this saw the 254th and final A380 completed in 2021.

Airbus grappled with a string of delays between officially launching the A380 in December 2000, unveiling a prototype in January 2005, and then making the initial delivery to Singapore Airlines in 2007. How severely these impacted the A380’s long-term success is up for debate. Either way, the A380’s stretched timeline offers some context for how impressive Boeing’s effort was in getting the 747 up and running so quickly.

And it is not just the A380. Boeing itself took three years and six months to show off its first 777 after launching the program in October 1990. Its 787, despite going from concept to prototype in three years and three months, then took an additional two years and five months to first take off in December 2009. As for Airbus, the A350 and its answer to the clean-sheet 787 went from launch to initial rollout in six years and five months. Similarly, the 777X project took more than five years after launching in 2013 to produce a prototype, with certification still not secured today.

Photographer Jessica Craig-Martin’s New Memoir Reveals the Life Behind Her “Drunken Lens”

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Her new memoir I Regret I Am Able to Attend is out now

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Since the early ‘90s, photographer Jessica Craig-Martin has captured high society from unusual angles. She zooms in on air kisses and other mannered gestures, cleavage in couture gowns, high heels and the floors typically omitted from pristine event documentation, straddling the line between fashion photography and art portraiture.

Craig-Martin’s memoir, I Regret I Am Able to Attend, out now, tells the story of how she got there. Craig-Martin details her bohemian childhood, wild friendships, and volatile young marriage with the effervescence that fills her compositions.

The story begins with the photographer’s parents, artists Michael Craig-Martin and Jan Hashey, who meet at the Yale School of Art. They have Jessica, then swiftly separate after revelations that they’re both having affairs, and Michael comes out as gay. From there, Craig-Martin experiences a peripatetic childhood, eventually settling into her mother’s SoHo loft. Her creative milieu includes gallerist Paula Cooper and artists Elizabeth Murray, Jennifer Bartlett, and Joel Shapiro, along with their children. Craig-Martin gets a job at high-end grocer Dean and Deluca (RIP), where Basquiat comes in to buy smoked eel for a strange supper. Artists Brice and Helen Marden hire her to babysit their daughters, and soon she’s off to Morocco and Greece with the family.

Instead of attending college, Craig-Martin receives an alternative education as she lands a gig waitressing at Brian McNally’s celebrity-filled restaurant Indochine. She flies to London to start a job as a fashion assistant at Anna Wintour’s British Vogue (they love her American enthusiasm) and, back in New York, works at Tina Brown’s Vanity Fair (they love her British sophistication). Throughout the memoir, Craig-Martin captures the colorful characters who parade in and out of her life, and around a rapidly evolving New York City.

Ahead of the book’s publication, and her forthcoming show at the Andres Thalmann gallery in Paris, Bazaar spoke to Craig-Martin about her writing process and her shifting understanding of gender in the art world. We were on the telephone, and the artist was visiting “I Am Plane Image,” a show of Brice Marden’s paintings at Gagosian in Chelsea. A guard reprimanded her for getting too close to a painting. “When I was a kid, one was encouraged to touch the art,” she told me. “A lot of art was activated by the viewer. And now someone’s always saying: ‘Excuse me!’ ‘Excuse me!’”

You’re a society photographer whose pictures conjure an entire milieu. Your book does something similar, but with language. How do you think about the relationship between writing and photography?

It’s the same brain, making different things. With photography, I never thought about what I was going to make before I made it. I’d see something with my camera, and I’d have to grab it. It’s a wonderful feeling of adrenaline, and it’s similar to looking for the next word. Everything just disappears. There’s a loss of self in the process, and you become your purest self.

In the book, your aesthetic education seems to come, in part, from painter Brice Marden.

With the camera, I felt a certain freedom. I really didn’t know much about photography, because it wasn’t a popular medium with my parents and their friends. And from Brice, I understood this freedom to put what you want in a picture. You can leave things out, push it around until it works, make it as weird as you want. If you feel like cutting off the top of a head in a photograph, try it!

I knew certain photographers, but not society photographers like Tina Barney and Larry Fink. I only knew a few pictures of Weegee’s. There was a lot for me to discover. If I’d seen more of this work, I would have felt inadequate. In this case, ignorance was bliss. Academic training can make you self-conscious. I didn’t have anything to unlearn.

Your mother has a philosophy that artists are the real aristocracy. Do you share it

She was right about everything she said. But it is extreme. I mean, we want doctors and we need other professions. But I think all artists believe this, as did all of us art brats who grew up in this kind of crazy, free-range situation. Art comes first. Everything else comes afterwards. It’s what we are in the truest form.

Good work rises to the top. Then again, not everyone whose work is good has been correctly honored or addressed. Women and people of color were left out of the discourse.

Your mother also says that Indochine is where you became a photographer.

I was raised in the very lean 60s and 70s, and then suddenly New York just was drunk on money overnight. And this job threw me into the middle of it. It was a hot seat to view all this excess. I found my subject matter there. Everything’s moving at once, and I keep that feeling when I’m taking a photograph: there’s a lot of motion in my pictures, and I’m capturing the feet or I’m above the person or I’m not where a photographer would normally be. [Gallerist] Jeanne Greenberg calls it my “drunken lens.”

The book is also a portrait of an evolving New York. It’s more sanitized now.

New York is still New York. We lose a lot with the corporatization of the city. But that’s global. It’s the same fuckers in every city making it into glass, concrete, steel with a bank in the bottom of the building and a Starbucks. It’s more painful here because New York is my city. But you can’t be too nostalgic because it’s also about speed and change. If you get stuck, you’re missing the point.

The sex appeal is now in Brooklyn. I’m an old fart now, I don’t hang out, but I know there are young people who are opening galleries, making good art. It’s just harder and harder to find a crack in the sidewalk to grow out of.

Towards the end of the book, during your marriage to artist Ashley Bickerton, you write that you made “Art Wife Curry.” That comes shortly after your mention of Rirkrit Tiravanija making curry at 303 Gallery, which he just calls “art.” You write about the covert sexism in the art world vs. the overt sexism in the restaurant world. In the writing process, did you come to think any differently about how gender had shaped you?

Yeah. When you’re naive and you’re a young woman, you’re experiencing these invisible forces pushing you around, or men are behaving in a certain way, and you’re used to it. It’s like air. You don’t see it. And not just writing the book, but getting older, you can really see the forces that pulled you this way or that, where you felt a limitation. When I look back, I think: Why didn’t I just say “fuck you?” Or why didn’t I say, no no, I’m coming through? Women second guess themselves a great deal more than men. Men push and then afterwards say oh, did I break it? And then everyone claps. That’s not the classic woman’s story. There are corrections happening, and many of them are very well-deserved.

What kinds of limitations are you thinking about?

It’s about confidence. Being an unquestioning force in the world and feeling like you have a right to be seen on the wall, have just as big a studio as your husband or partner. If you go to the Krasner Pollock home in Springs in Long Island, his studio was famously the barn, a giant empty space. And hers was this tiny room. That would be the normal order of things. That’s the way it was in my family, even though I thought of my parents as liberated. My mother was considered a star at Yale, and as soon as she left and had a baby, she was a mother first, artist second. My parents’ generation thought they had dealt with a lot of this second classing of women, and they really hadn’t.

The book ends when you're around 35, your marriage is over, and your photography career has finally begun. Why end there? Will there be a Volume 2?

Good question. There might be. But I’ve also told my friends to tie me up and duct tape me to a wall if I ever start to try, because this was so difficult to make. I’m already forgetting how dreadful I found the experience.

House keys with a house shaped keychain resting on a mortgage application form

Mortgage applications dipped slightly this week, in keeping with the average 30-year mortgage rate rising to a three-week high.

The latest Market Composite Index from the Mortgage Bankers Association (MBA)—its measure of mortgage loan activity volume and includes purchases and refinances—shows mortgage application activity decreasing 2.3% on a seasonally adjusted basis after last week’s 6.2% increase. On an unadjusted basis, the Index decreased 5% compared with the previous week.

This week’s decline follows weeks of fluctuations as the 30-year fixed mortgage rate still solidly remains over 7%–last week decreasing to 7.11% according to Freddie Mac but currently sitting at 7.46%, according to Mortgage News Daily. Freddie Mac’s next rate report will be released Thursday.

“Mortgage applications dipped as mortgage rates moved higher, with the 30-year fixed mortgage rate up to its highest level in three weeks at 7.35 percent,” said Joel Kan, CMB, MBA’s vice president and deputy chief economist. “Purchase applications were down 18 percent from a year ago, as renewed affordability challenges from elevated mortgage rates have pushed prospective homebuyers to the sidelines.”

MBA’s Refinance Index decreased 2% from the previous week and was 54% lower than the same week one year ago. MBA’s seasonally adjusted Purchase Index–its weekly measurement of nationwide home loan applications based on a sample of about 75% of U.S. mortgage activity–decreased 3% from one week earlier. The unadjusted Purchase Index decreased 7% compared with the previous week and was 18% lower than the same week one year ago, according to the report.

The refinance share of mortgage activity saw an increase this week–to 41.1% from 39.8% the previous week, with Kan pointing to a rise in FHA refinances as most notable.

“Overall refinance activity was down over the week, but FHA refinances increased almost 7 percent,” Kan stated. “The FHA rate was around 30 basis points below the conforming rate, which supported refinance opportunities for some homeowners.”

The FHA share of total applications increased to 16.1% from 15.5% the week prior, with MBA listing the VA share of total applications increasing to 12.8% from 12.4% the week prior. The USDA share of total applications remained unchanged at 0.5 percent from the week prior.

Kan also pointed to additional macroeconomic indicators as potentially weighing on home-purchase application activity into the new year.

“Treasury yields rose last week amid data showing inflation accelerated in October and Fed officials acknowledging that additional rate hikes may be needed in the coming months,” he said.

The company said customers who see a licensed provider for a clinical appointment may receive a personalized plan that includes sermorelin, NAD+, glutathione, vitamin B12 and L-carnitine. The treatments are prepared domestically, and licensed U.S. pharmacies that answer to state pharmacy regulators dispense them, the company said. Hims & Hers said its Hims brand is offering the treatments to men and its Hers brand to women.

Hims & Hers plans to add BPC-157 and TB-500 "consistent with regulatory standards," the company said. Neither peptide is approved, and the bulk of the studies so far involved animal subjects instead of people, according to CNBC.

Peptides+ is available with the company's Care+ program. Care+ covers biannual lab work, unlimited help and regular check-ins with a team of dedicated clinicians, plus around-the-clock messaging with providers who have training in peptide treatment, the company said.

The launch follows an October policy from the Department of Health and Human Services. Under that policy, the FDA has said it will hold off on enforcement against licensed pharmacies, federal facilities and physicians who compound and dispense six peptides that lack approval, for as long as formal rulemaking is being reviewed, according to CNBC. The policy does not signal that regulators have cleared those individual peptides.

"Anyone investing in their health deserves to know exactly what they're putting in their body, and to have dedicated support along the way," Anant Vinjamoori, chief medical officer of Hims, said in a statement.

Vinjamoori also acknowledged gaps in the evidence. "We don't have all of this data that we're used to having with compounds like this," he told CNBC. He said patients with serious conditions such as cardiovascular, liver or kidney disease won't qualify for the compounds at this stage.

"There is a difference between this and FDA-approved therapies," Vinjamoori told Fierce Healthcare.

Outside experts have questioned the approach. "Without actual testing, all you have is anecdotal information, and that's just not enough information to put a substance in your body," Joshua Kritzer, a chemistry professor at Tufts University, told CNBC.

It isn't easy for a huge, sprawling global company to increase its key fundamentals at double-digit percentage rates. Walt Disney (DIS +4.74%) achieved this feat with the fourth-quarter and annual fiscal 2026 results it published Tuesday morning.

All three of the entertainment giant's business units posted revenue gains in the mid-teens; arguably the most impressive of the trio was experiences, which notched an all-time quarterly record by drawing more than $10.1 billion in revenue. Let's take a brief overview of how Disney did during the quarter, and drill down into the performance of the experiences unit.

For the period, Disney's total revenue was just under $25.95 billion, a 16% improvement over the fourth quarter of 2025's tally -- although I should note that the most recent quarter included one more week. Regardless, that percentage essentially matched the growth of all three business units (entertainment and sports, in addition to experiences).

With such lift, non-GAAP (adjusted) net income also soared. It headed skyward at a 56% clip to almost $3.35 billion, or $1.77 per share.

Both numbers beat the consensus analyst estimates, albeit not spectacularly. The average prognosticator forecasts for revenue and per-share profitability were $25.21 billion and $1.68, respectively.

Experiences is a close second in terms of revenue among the three reporting units, behind entertainment. Yet it was by far the No. 1 when measured by operating income, with its $2.25 billion trouncing entertainment's $1.33 billion and sports' $1.39 billion. It also happened to be the champ in the year-ago quarter too.

I should note here that, starting in the current (first) quarter, Disney has shifted its consumer products licensing and publishing operations from experiences to entertainment. If the company had made that move before the fourth quarter, experiences would still be the runner-up in revenue. Meanwhile, the adjustment would shift consumer products' operating profit to entertainment, and make entertainment No. 1 in that metric.

Nevertheless, experiences was a crucial driver of growth for those impressive top- and bottom-line improvements. Given that, it's worthwhile to explore why, and devote a little space to whether Disney is positioning it well for further growth.

As its name implies, experiences comprises the unique, in-person Disney attractions. This includes the theme parks, anchored by the endlessly popular Disneyland in Southern California, and comprises the burgeoning Disney Cruise business and travel services (plus the consumer products operation that's now under entertainment's big tent).

What gives the unit its notably higher margins is that many of its operations center around fixed infrastructure. Disneyland and other parks are already built, and the company's cruise ships regularly travel the seas. Yes, there are maintenance costs, but for the most part much of the capex for those assets has already been spent, while the customers keep coming (and paying). In the sports division, content costs are extremely high and seem to be on the rise, so it's more challenging to make a buck. As for entertainment, we can say the same for even a mid-budget movie or a network TV show.

Happily for the company's most profitable unit, we're famously in the midst of an experiences boom, as consumers with money to spend are eager to participate in unique events. This explains the recent, sustained success of the travel industry; have you noticed how often commercial airplanes fly at near or full capacity these days?

One experience consumers long to enjoy is a cruise. Disney was happy to oblige, as its cruise days (total passengers carried times the total duration of sailings) popped by 11%. Management had smartly laid the groundwork for this rise by adding two new ships -- the Destiny and the Adventure -- to its fleet between November 2025 and this past March. The total is now eight ships, and there's clearly enough demand to keep those sailings lively.

The experience boom was gathering steam as the COVID pandemic faded in 2023. That year, Disney announced it planned to almost double its capital expenditures (capex) in the experiences segment, to roughly $60 billion over the decade from fiscal years 2024 to 2033. And part of this was the expansion of the fleet, hence the recent debuts of Destiny and Adventure.

That's still very much a central strategy for Disney. Of the capex splurge on experiences, CEO Josh D'Amaro said on the fourth-quarter conference call, "the plan will continue to expand our capacity and deliver a differentiated entertainment experience as we continue turbocharging our parks and cruise businesses."

All this, of course, leads to the question of whether Disney stock is a buy. After all, judging by Mr. Market's reaction to the earnings -- the shares closed Tuesday down by 1.5% -- a clutch of investors didn't think so. They flagged some negatives, such as the 7% slide in operating income in the international theme park segment and the anticipated 9% annual rise in spending on content in the new fiscal year.

However, as a longtime Disney shareholder and bull (with some down moments over the years, admittedly), I think the fourth quarter's stars well overshadowed the slumps, to the point where the company had scope for its very generous 33% dividend raise. Plus, its focus on experiences is already powering strong growth, and will surely continue to do so. This is one stock I'm absolutely keeping in my portfolio.

NBC’s page program dates back to 1933, during the golden age of radio, and began as a way to assist in guest relations, ushering, tour services and more at the thenbrand new Rockefeller Plaza.

The highly competitive program selects around 22 to 25 people in New York and 12 to 15 in Los Angeles three times a year for what’s become a TV business and NBC boot camp.

According to Priany Hadiatmodjo, senior director of the NBC page program, around 3,000 people apply for each cycle to get one of those coveted spots.

But once you’re here, the expectation to excel is probably harder than the actual process itself.”

After an immersive two-week onboarding where they meet with and learn from NBCU leadership, pages are assigned to different portions of the business for 18 weeks at a time. The divisions where pages can work has been greatly expanded over the past 10 years to all facets of NBCU, and pages can express an interest in which divisions they’d like to work.

“I always like to say that if my team and I are doing our jobs right, the page program serves as a microcosm of NBCU,” says Hadiatmodjo. “Some people have called it the most intense masters in media that you could get. Because the hope is that we’re finding the best and brightest out there, and that they’ll one day become leaders of this industry. So we’re trying to put them into spaces, places and environments that will really challenge them daily and give them the experience of what it’s really like to work at NBCU.”

Once an NBC page has completed a year in the program and worked in three different departments, they can start to apply for jobs inside the company.

“By then they’ve hopefully been on a path of self-discovery and figured out some things that they really love doing,” Hadiatmodjo adds. “It really is a year of self-discovery and experimentation, and really understanding what this industry is all about. Our responsibility is making sure they’re ready to hit the ground running in their next early career role. Our ultimate goal is to find that talent, cultivate it, and hopefully, see them stick around.”

And that’s come true throughout the company, with execs like Hockmeyer. CFTC: Kalshi bets may violate US ban on contracts that can be easily manipulated.

The Trump administration ordered prediction market Kalshi to stop offering event contracts that pay out based on whether President Trump will say a specific word before a particular date. Kalshi halted trading in the contracts while the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) conducts a review into whether the Trump mention contracts violate federal law.

CNBC reported yesterday that “Kalshi quietly paused trading in a handful of ‘mention’ contracts tied to President Donald Trump at the end of last week at the request of the CFTC.” Kalshi told media outlets that it is complying with the CFTC order.

The markets taken down by Kalshi allowed users to wager on whether Trump would say certain words, including nicknames and the names of countries and companies. A similar expired event contract that can still be viewed on Kalshi’s website asked whether Trump would use the nicknames “Marjorie Traitor Greene,” “Sleepy Joe,” and “Whack Job” or “Wack Job” before April 2026.

The CFTC order issued to Kalshi last week applies broadly to “the TRUMPSAY contract series.” The event contracts in this series include trades in the format, “Will [word] be said by Donald Trump after Issuance and before [date]?” The CFTC order also applied to a specific event contract that asks, “What nicknames will Trump say before 2027?”

The CFTC order came several months after the federal agency settled charges against a Trump teleprompter aide who made over $100,000 in Kalshi wagers on what Trump would say in prepared remarks. On August 28, the CFTC required the man, Gabriel Perez, to disgorge profits of $107,539.02 and pay a civil penalty of $65,000.

“In his position, Perez had access to presidential speeches prior to those speeches being delivered and Perez misappropriated that information—in breach of his duty of trust and confidence—to trade presidential mention market contracts, generating over $107,500 in profits,” the CFTC said at the time.

Senate Democrats recently said the White House hasn’t taken meaningful action against insider trading, “all while the president and his family stand to personally profit from their participation in prediction markets.” Prediction markets have flourished under the Trump administration’s permissive regulatory approach. The CFTC has allowed prediction markets to offer sports gambling nationwide and filed lawsuits against states that attempt to regulate Kalshi under their gambling laws.

Kalshi recently dropped Donald Trump Jr. as an advisor, saying it made the decision because Trump Jr.’s venture capital firm increased its investment in its competitor, Polymarket. Kalshi CEO Tarek Mansour also acknowledged that the “optics” of Trump Jr.’s advisory role weren’t good for Kalshi. Trump Jr. still stands to benefit from Kalshi’s success, because he was given equity shares in the company.

The CFTC has said that mention markets are easily manipulated but reviews them on a case-by-case basis instead of prohibiting them. Prediction markets can “self-certify” that any new event contract complies with US law and start offering it without waiting for the CFTC’s permission, but the CFTC can halt trading afterward based on reviews of specific contracts.

The Commodity Exchange Act (CEA) prohibits the listing of contracts that are readily susceptible to manipulation. CFTC staff issued a September advisory about the legality of markets on what people will say, and whether they will attend an event or interact with another person. The advisory said these contracts may be viewed as “presumptively readily susceptible to manipulation,” because they “may be controlled by a single individual, a small group of individuals, or persons with access to or influence over the individual whose words, attendance, or interaction determines settlement.”

The CFTC said this type of contract requires measures “to prevent manipulation and misappropriation of material non-public information,” and that Kalshi failed to comply with the CFTC staff advisory. About “a week after the Staff Advisory was released, Kalshi listed the Contract [on nicknames] for trading without amending the [TRUMPSAY] Contract Series’ self-certification (or submitting a new self-certification) to make the ‘heightened showing’ of non-manipulability required by the Staff Advisory,” the order said.

The CFTC said it will conduct “a proceeding to determine whether the certification of the Contracts was false—that is, whether the Contracts comply with the Act, including the Core Principles, and Commission regulations thereunder as Kalshi has certified.” So far, “Kalshi has not adequately demonstrated that the Contracts are not readily susceptible to manipulation” as required by law, the order said.

“Given the available supporting evidence, including that Kalshi has not attempted to establish that the Contracts are non-manipulable with respect to the Staff Advisory’s guidance, the Commission presumptively believes the certification is false,” the order said.

The CFTC order is not a determination on whether the Trump mention markets can meet the standard of not being readily susceptible to manipulation. Rather, it is a determination that Kalshi has so far failed to show that the contracts are not easily manipulated.

The order “resolves only that, based on the current record, Kalshi has not demonstrated that the Contracts as certified comply with the Act and that the Contracts should not trade while the Commission completes its review,” the CFTC said.

Your Favorite Coffee Shop Is Probably Streaming Music Illegally. It’s the Music Industry’s Fault, Too (Guest Column)

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A new study found 81% of U.S. businesses don't properly license music, costing creators and other rightsholders. The fix starts with education — not lawsuits.

Walk into almost any shop, gym, salon or restaurant in America, and you’ll hear music. Ask the owner whether it’s licensed and you will likely get a confident “yes.” But in most cases, that answer is, unfortunately, wrong.

Soundtrack recently surveyed 1,525 decision-makers responsible for nearly 15,000 business locations across 10 customer-facing industries to find out how U.S. businesses actually play music. Though the law is clear that businesses need specific licenses to play music in public, the results of the survey describe a market that loves music, depends on it, and, overwhelmingly, does not properly pay for it.

Ninety-seven percent of the businesses we surveyed play music for customers, and 77% do it every day. Adjusted for the size of the U.S., that means roughly 3.8 million U.S. business locations play music in their stores.

Nine in 10 of those surveyed said music boosts employee motivation and shapes the energy of their space, while more than eight in 10 called it an essential part of the business and said it has to fit their brand. Rather than merely being a background expense, businesses see music as part of the product.

Yet despite this overwhelming demand, the way music played in businesses is paid for has not evolved. A striking 79% of businesses illicitly use a consumer streaming service, such as a personal Spotify, Pandora or YouTube account, to play music for customers. Only 28% of businesses use a service built and licensed for business. This is one of the biggest failures, and also one of the biggest opportunities, for the music industry today.

Aside from the scale of misuse, it is significant how many businesses are sure they’re doing it right. Seventy-eight percent of respondents were confident the music in their business is legally compliant. Eighty-three percent said they’re familiar with the rules. Yet 81% are misusing music, and 58% believe it’s legal to stream a personal account in their business.

Even I struggle to distill the U.S. licensing rules into just a few sentences; such is the unnecessary complexity surrounding them. Put simply, businesses require both a music service licensed for commercial use and licenses with all four U.S. performing rights organizations (PROs) — ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR — that cover the music they play.

Some licensed music services bundle performance rights, while others require businesses to secure separate public performance licenses from U.S. PROs. For a business owner, working out exactly what’s included can be a job in itself.

Too often I speak to business owners who believe they’re fully licensed but are missing key PRO licenses or still playing music via their personal streaming account. The survey reflects this, finding only 5% of businesses hold licenses from all four U.S. PROs.

However, this isn’t a story about bad actors. Nine in 10 businesses said compliance matters to them, and most said it matters that artists are paid fairly. The businesses using personal streaming accounts are doing it because it’s what they know. It’s easy, familiar and often free. It’s not a problem of intent; it’s a lack of information and a non-user-friendly system.

The cost to music creators and other rightsholders is real. Combining what businesses told us they spend on music with the number of establishments recorded by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, we estimate that annual spending on music would rise by about $1.4 billion per year if every business was properly licensed through a compliant business music service. That money should flow to songwriters, artists, publishers, labels and the services that license them legally.

The music industry shares the blame. No one proactively educates the market or gives a business owner a clear answer about what they need to be fully covered. Consumer platforms offer free tiers, teaching a generation of shop owners that music costs nothing. And business music services, mine included, have not explained the difference loudly or simply enough.

Enforcement alone won’t close a gap this wide. You can’t sue three million locations into compliance, and you shouldn’t want to. These are the customers who say music is essential to them. The answer is a coordinated push across trade organisations, PROs, publishers, labels, consumer platforms and business services to create one plain-language message about what is legal, provide simpler routes to full licensing and build services businesses actually want to use.

That last part matters. Businesses told us what they look for in streaming services designed for businesses: ease-of-use, price and good playlists (the main complaint about business music services is that their music selections are limited and repetitive). The music offered by these services has to be at least as good as the consumer app on the owner’s smartphone — or the consumer app will keep winning.

Streaming rebuilt the recorded music industry by making the legal option easier for individuals than piracy. Now, the same needs to happen for businesses. The demand is already there, and most want to do it right. What’s missing is clarity, and a legal option that’s simple to use and offers better selections.

That $1.4 billion isn’t lost. It’s waiting for the industry to make doing the right thing the easy thing.

The U.S. Department of Labor proposed new policy this week that would end fast-track green cards for healthcare professionals like nurses and physical therapists—the latest in a string of policies from the Trump administration raising the bar for foreign-born workers and students across healthcare and other industries.

Currently, nurses, physical therapists and other workers “with exceptional ability in the sciences or arts” are granted an exemption from an administrative test in the permanent labor certification program (PERM), a months-long administrative process in which an employer must prove there are no able, willing, qualified and available U.S. workers for a permanent job ahead of sponsoring a foreign worker.

The exemption, referred to Schedule A, is given to occupations where the DOL recognizes a nationwide shortage of U.S. workers. Nurses and physical therapists have been listed under Schedule A for more than 30 years.

The Department of Labor, in its newly proposed rule, said it wants to eliminate Schedule A as it cannot “demonstrate that the occupations currently listed in Schedule A are still shortage occupations.”

It cited in the proposed rule a late 2023 request for information published under the Biden administration seeking input on potentially expanding Schedule A. Despite more than 2,000 responses, DOL now says it didn’t receive the information it would need to establish a methodology for regularly reviewing and updating Schedule A occupations, and questioned whether it has the statutory authority to make such occupational exemptions in the first place.

The proposed rule, which is open to public comment for 60 days, also includes requirements for employers to advertise their open jobs more broadly and for a longer period of time to demonstrate a good-faith effort to recruit American workers before turning to a green card sponsorship. These and other proposed changes would be the first “comprehensive” reforms to the PERM program in almost two decades, the Department of Labor said.

If finalized as-is, employers who want to sponsor professional nurses, physical therapists and other affected roles “would be required to go through the lengthier and costlier PERM labor market testing process, adding time, cost and administrative work to a pathway that hospitals, nursing homes and rehabilitation providers have historically relied on to fill persistent shortages,” Caroline Tang, an Austin shareholder at labor and employment law firm Ogletree Deakins, told Fierce Healthcare.

The mortgage industry was built to help Americans buy homes. An aging America needs more from it.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Survey of 2,000 homeowners 55 plus shows 88% prioritize staying home and 90% prioritize independence

For decades, the mortgage industry has helped Americans achieve one of their biggest financial goals: buying a home and building equity over time.

It’s a model we know well. Homeowners purchase properties, make mortgage payments, refinance when it makes financial sense and gradually accumulate wealth. Much of the mortgage industry is structured around supporting that journey.

But what happens after decades of building that wealth?

As homeowners approach retirement, the financial equation shifts. Instead of focusing primarily on acquiring a home and paying down debt, the question becomes how to use the wealth they’ve accumulated to support the years ahead. Housing presents a unique opportunity but also a challenge because, for many Americans, their largest financial asset is also the place they call home.

You can sell a stock without moving. Selling a home is another matter. Trading down and moving to a less expensive home to tap home equity is both expensive and difficult. This might be why our recent research shows that the desire to remain at home continues to grow stronger with age.

In a recent Longbridge Financial, LLC (NMLS #957935) study of more than 2,000 U.S. homeowners ages 55 and older, 90% said maintaining their independence was extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. Nearly as many (88%) said the same about remaining in their homes. Both priorities ranked above paying for health care, paying off debt, long-term care and providing financial support to family members.

The desire to age in place isn’t surprising. What’s particularly revealing is how strongly that sentiment differs across age groups.

Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 85% rated remaining in their homes as extremely or very important. That figure rose to 88% among those ages 65 to 74 and 93% among homeowners 75 and older. The importance of maintaining independence followed a similar pattern, climbing from 87% in the youngest group to 96% in the oldest.

These are differences between age groups at one point in time, rather than a measure of how individual preferences change over time. Still, the pattern points to an important finding in understanding how homeowners think about retirement.

Over decades, homeowners accumulate substantial equity through mortgage payments and increases in property values. Yet the very home that holds the wealth is also where many want to spend their later years. Its financial value tells only part of the story. Giving a homeowner a choice to move to a less expensive, and thus less attractive, home to use their home equity may not be much of a choice at all.

Consider two retired households with the same net worth. One holds most of its wealth in savings and investments. The other holds much of it in home equity.

Their balance sheets might look similar, but their financial flexibility is different. The first household may be able to draw from liquid accounts to cover an unexpected expense. The second has wealth, too, but accessing it may involve borrowing against the home or selling it altogether. Both options come with trade-offs, particularly when remaining in the home is a priority.

For an industry accustomed to measuring property values, loan balances and equity, this is an important distinction. These figures describe a homeowner’s financial position, but not necessarily how easily they can access that wealth — or what they might have to give up in the process.

The same survey reinforces why this distinction matters. Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 73% rated paying for health care as extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. That figure climbed to 85% among those 75 and older. While these findings don’t tell us how much homeowners will spend or whether they’ll tap into their home equity, they do highlight how financial priorities can shift across different stages of life.

Mortgage finance has traditionally centered on transactions: purchasing, refinancing, servicing and eventually paying off a loan. Those functions remain essential. But paying off a mortgage doesn’t end a homeowner’s financial relationship with their home.

For some older homeowners, selling or downsizing may be the right choice. Others may have sufficient income and savings to remain in place. Still others may consider home equity loans, lines of credit or reverse mortgages, depending on their financial circumstances, eligibility and the associated costs and risks.

The opportunity for our industry extends beyond any single product. It’s about understanding the role home equity can play in supporting homeowners through retirement, while recognizing the personal value they place on remaining in their homes.

It also requires recognizing that homeowners’ priorities differ. In our study, 93% of women rated independence as extremely or very important, compared with 88% of men. Similar five-point gaps appeared for remaining at home and paying for health care. While the research doesn’t explain why these differences exist, it underscores the importance of understanding homeowners as individuals rather than treating homeowners 55 and over as a single market with identical needs and priorities.

Housing has always been both an investment and a place to live. As homeowners age, the balance between those roles deserves greater attention from the industry that helped them achieve homeownership in the first place.

We’ve spent decades helping Americans build wealth through homeownership. The next opportunity is helping them understand how that wealth can support the years ahead, without losing sight of what they value most: independence, financial flexibility and, for many, the ability to remain at home.

GTA 6 Review - An Astounding, Impossible New Bar For Video Games

The most anticipated video game of all time is a triumph on every level that exceeds years of unbelievable expectations. You're not ready for Leonida.

Throughout much of my nearly 100 hours of playtime, reviewing Grand Theft Auto 6 felt like a paradox. How do you possibly begin to talk about something that had whole studios built just for its NPCs?

GTA 6 is the biggest video game of all time, but we really only knew the basics. Something that dawned on me less than an hour in when it became clear just how much had been held back. Even with the weight of 13 years of anticipation on my shoulders, I’m also well aware that nothing I say about Jason and Lucia’s felonious tour of Leonida will influence the minds of anyone even half interested in it.

The great irony of this herculean task is that, more often than not, Grand Theft Auto 6 left me completely speechless, pointing at the screen and gibbering like an idiot at what Rockstar has accomplished. Following the narrative heights of Red Dead Redemption 2 and its beloved Arthur Morgan is an unenviable task, but Grand Theft Auto 6 doesn’t disappoint. It follows Jason and Lucia, two small-time criminals who reunite and are quickly forced to rise through the ranks of Leonida’s underworld after a failed job puts them up against Vice City’s most dangerous forces.

So far, so GTA, but there’s more to the plot than the rags-to-riches setup would have you believe. Unsurprisingly, I can’t tell you much about the overarching story and what sets it in motion, but Jason and Lucia’s involvement has far more emotional and mature stakes than any other game in the series before it. Rockstar takes the pair to places I never would have predicted (including some surprisingly dark moments), and it all lands because of how expertly written and characterised both protagonists are.

Jason is more stoic and guarded than any of the more out-there characters we followed throughout Los Santos, but he’s got a heart of gold and lots of charm underneath the gruff exterior, which mostly comes out when he’s with Lucia. He’s harder to read and takes time to warm up to, but by the end, Jason stands comfortably alongside the series' most memorable protagonists, Niko, CJ, and even Red Dead’s revered gunslingers.

Lucia, on the other hand, is without a doubt the best protagonist in Grand Theft Auto history. She’s fiercely loyal, emotionally mature, witty, and doesn’t take crap from anyone, almost to a fault, as her passion and eagerness to prove herself often land her in trouble. As the series’ first female lead (no, I don’t count the pixel blobs from the first game), there’s a lot of pressure on Lucia, but she easily reaches the lofty bar set by Arthur Morgan.

“We believed in this idea of putting a relationship at the center of the game and trying to make a GTA that was still as funny, crazy, shocking, and fun as the previous games but with added heart. But yeah, the process of making something like this you always question things. As you saw with the 2022 hack, game development isn’t pretty. It can take a long time and a lot of iteration to make good on an idea and to get things working. But I think simply the idea that the relationship would be something that the player needed to work at an earn (or ignore if they want to!) felt like exciting new territory.” - Rupert Humphries, Senior Vice President of Narrative

Of course, as great as Jason and Lucia are on their own merits, they come as a pair in GTA 6 and are together for nearly all of the story’s biggest moments. I had some doubts about the Bonnie and Clyde-style romance, as it's not exactly familiar territory for Rockstar. But after seeing their chemistry and working to give them an unbreakable bond through hand-holding, dates, clubbing, and, of course, doing crime as a pair in my playthrough, I simply can’t imagine the two not being madly in love. Some of my favourite moments in the whole game were simply taking Jason and Lucia on a date, grinning as they flirted, and getting unhealthily invested in their relationship.

It’s not just a win for the story, either, as Jason and Lucia’s cooperative gameplay is a huge step up from GTA 5, with far more options than just swapping, from letting your partner drive you to waypoints and having them cover you in firefights to asking them to fence your stolen goods or hang back as a getaway driver. In GTA 6, having a partner in crime finally means exactly that.

That romance might be the focal point and main standout of GTA 6’s story, but there’s plenty to love about the excellent supporting cast as well. Whether you’re supposed to love them like the cool-as-ice mentor figure Raul or hate them like some characters I won’t dare mention, there’s not a single weak link. Though the writing can occasionally be slightly less biting than previous games, Jason and Lucia’s tale tops GTA 4 as the series’ best narrative.

Perhaps my favourite character of the whole game is Karim, the hacker in Raul’s group who provides some surprising Watch Dogs 2 vibes.

Sony is right - Grand Theft Auto 6 is probably going to play best on PlayStation.

It would have been easy (or at least easier) for Rockstar to take GTA 5’s lucrative formula and just polish the graphics a bit, but one of the biggest strengths of Grand Theft Auto 6 is how it completely rebuilds the main tenets of the whole series. For starters, core mechanics like movement, shooting, and driving all feel far better than ever thanks to extensive improvements and tweaks.

Take GTA’s infamous mash X to sprint controls, for instance, which have been completely reworked for a more standardised and smoother experience. Jason and Lucia are both far more agile than previous protagonists and have more vertical options at every moment, which makes the simple act of getting around on foot much more enjoyable. The only downside to the rework, and spoilers for the rest of this review - the game as a whole - is that the controls can sometimes be a little overcomplicated due to how many interactions you have at your disposal.

It did take me a good few hours to adjust to the new control scheme, but it’ll be incredibly tough to go back now. There is an option for the classic version if you really struggle, though.

The experiment kind of stalled mid development due to other competing priorities but was revitalized by Rob (Head of Development). After that, we all strongly felt it should be the default control scheme as we’d had consistently good feedback from the team and user research. Shooting, meanwhile, takes cues from Red Dead Redemption 2 and its weightier weapons, which already felt inspired by Max Payne 3’s stellar gunfights. There’s even a Dead Eye/Bullet Time slow-motion focus mode for both protagonists, rather than being locked as a special ability for one character, and it becomes an essential tool as you go up against the gangs and cops of Leonida. It’s the best combat has ever felt in a Grand Theft Auto game by a country mile.

The same goes for driving, which sits somewhere between GTA 5 and GTA 4 thanks to a greater focus on vehicle weight, physics, and damage while somehow still retaining the buttery smooth controls and flow. Driving is also the most immediately obvious example of Rockstar not just improving how Grand Theft Auto 6 plays, but recontextualising every mechanic’s implementation with a greater focus on worldbuilding and player involvement.

Unlike previous games in the series where you could grab a fast car and bomb it down the city streets without so much as tapping the brakes, Leonida’s denser traffic and narrower streets (including a ton of painfully realistic roadworks) mean you need to be paying attention at all times. It’s only when you start to master the new systems and put the effort in that you can weave through traffic like a pro, which makes it infinitely more rewarding. Couple that with cars requiring far more work to attain thanks to higher security and a ridiculous amount of upgrade and modding options, and vehicles become so much more than just something you steal, park in your garage, and don’t give a second thought to.

On the other hand, gunfights are no longer about just aiming for the head with whatever arsenal you’ve got in your back pocket, as you now need to incapacitate enemies with clean, targeted shots to the knees and arms if you want to keep a professional criminal profile. You also have a more limited choice of weapons, forcing you to plan ahead and keep your trunk stocked before a firefight. Even crime, the main thing Grand Theft Auto is known for, is completely different in GTA 6. Not only are the police more advanced than ever before, with multiple stages of realistic awareness of your actions that incentivise masks, stealth, and being as efficient as possible, but there’s far more effort that needs to be put in for even the simplest of sins.

Having an unknown wanted state created lots of great gameplay moments, where you could nonchalantly leave a crime scene undetected. We added threat indicators to give the player more feedback around how they were being perceived by cops/enemies in game. This opened up some cool stealth gameplay opportunities, but was also crucial seeing as we removed enemies from the radar. Once you are spotted, we now rely on cops calling it in before you fully known. This gives the player a window of opportunity to deal with the situation before things kick off.  But yeah, it’s been a massive effort. Fueled by constant tweaks and iteration over many years." - David Hynd, Vice President of Technology

Gone are the days of completing a few story missions and having millions in the bank without anything to spend it on, as money is incredibly hard to come by in Vice City. You really need to work for it, whether through simple but low-reward gas station stick-ups and jewel store heists, or storming gang hideouts for drugs you can sell on to fences. What’s most impressive about all of the crime opportunities is that they’re completely emergent and up to the player, a massive leap forward from Los Santos story-specific heists and very basic convenience store shakedowns.

As if being able to pull off story mission-esque heists at any moment wasn’t enough, GTA 6 has also made some notable improvements to the main campaign. When they’re not trying to make Jason and Lucia feel like real people with quieter moments, story missions are energetic, bombastic, and, unlike some criticisms of Red Dead Redemption 2, give the player more freedom and control than before. While I’m sure some players will prefer the classic, more thoughtless approach, for my money Grand Theft Auto 6 is a better and more considered game on every single front, pushing the series formula to impossible new heights.

In a game that’s all triumphs, the biggest of all is Grand Theft Auto 6’s meticulously crafted world, which is so unbelievably impressive it single-handedly justifies the long wait. From the murky swamps near Leonida Keys to the neon-lit upper echelons of Capri, Leonida is jaw-dropping at every turn (fittingly for Vice City, especially at night) and a downright feat of engineering to get it all working without any issues on PS5 Pro. Some things, especially the weather system and frankly ridiculous lighting, need to be seen to be believed.

I’ve never stopped to smell the roses so much in a game, but it’s impossible not to soak in all of the work and love that’s gone into every explorable interior, every beach packed with NPCs you can interact with or just hear have detailed conversations, every neighbourhood with potential things to steal, and every flea market with functioning shops. Almost all of the effort feels necessary to deliver on the promise of Leonida, but you can often feel Rockstar flexing its penchant for the little details (such as food being realistically eaten and the unique games that are playable on the PlayStation parody) that make these worlds feel so real.

Grand Theft Auto 6 also easily has the best radio selection the series has seen since Vice City. Justice’s original soundtrack is also incredible and punctuates major story moments perfectly.

Rockstar’s foray into heightened realism in Red Dead Redemption 2 has clearly left its mark on GTA 6, which focuses heavily on immersion and realism. You can shower, work out at the gym to build muscle, eat food to put on weight, get tired from not sleeping enough, need to keep vehicles filled with fuel, and have more hairstyle and clothing options than you know what to do with. I was a little worried it’d go too far and end up a little intense like RDR2, but Rockstar has taken the right amount of inspiration from that game while still making sure GTA isn’t getting too close to a life-sim.

“I personally love going into a gas station, buying a hot dog and a slushie and walking out alternating between eating and drinking and walking down the street doing that, then throwing the empty cup in the trash. I love getting a six pack of beers and walking along drinking them one at a time, throwing them around!”- Rob Nelson, Head of Development

Beyond being so detailed and immersive, Leonida is also just an incredibly varied and handcrafted county that feels unique no matter where you go. It’s a far cry from GTA 5’s handful of city areas mixed with vast wilderness that ends up getting ignored, and it helps that it’s stuffed with side missions, minigames, and emergent events that keep the water-cooler spirit Rockstar’s games are so well known for.

It might feel like I’ve said a lot about GTA 6 (it certainly does to me, anyway), but even with close to 100 hours, there are still whole areas I’ve barely explored, mechanics I’ve only glanced at, easter eggs to find, and activities that are begging for more of my time - it’s an absolute juggernaut. There’s also so much I can’t talk about or simply don’t want to get into to keep the sense of discovery for everyone else.

Without a shadow of a doubt, Grand Theft Auto 6 is the best open-world game I’ve ever played, and one of the best games full stop, with only incredibly minute nitpicks barely worth mentioning that do nothing to drag it down from masterpiece status. Its jaw-droppingly detailed world, completely revamped and recontextualised mechanics, and heartfelt narrative with two stellar leads put it far above the rest of the incredible series and set an impossible new bar for the medium as a whole. I'm sure we’ll never see another game with such unreal hype that actually lives up to and exceeds expectations, but you can rest easy knowing that the long road to Leonida was worth walking.

Just a few miles up the road earlier that afternoon, Zidane's former club were playing their fifth match of the season in the sixth-tier Regional 1, in a north-western suburb of the city.

Thousands of Bordeaux fans had made the short away outing to Saint-Medard-en-Jalles' 3000-seater ground to watch their team seal a fifth win in five this season.

Girondins de Bordeaux, the six-time French champions, who were once regulars in European cups, now find themselves facing amateur opposition on a weekly basis.

Since 2022, the club has plummeted from Ligue 1 to the sixth tier through a combination of on-pitch struggles, mismanagement at all levels, and sanctions due to their financial troubles.

The supporters turned out in their numbers for the first home match of the campaign, which had been delayed amid the uncertainty over whether the club could continue running, against Stade Montois.

More than 11,000 tickets sold, a record crowd for that level of French football.

"140 years of history, we won't be stopped," read one of the banners held up by the Ultramarines fan group, who also made it a point to thank the seven first-team players who had stayed despite the club's double relegation.

The team is centred around a core of players with Ligue 1 and Ligue 2 experience. Steve Shamal, who scored a hat-trick in that 4-1 victory over Stade Montois, previously turned out for the likes of Caen and Annecy in the second tier.

10 ways wildfire burn scars turn heavy rain into flood danger

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Wildfire burn scars and flash floods are closely linked, and these 10 points explain how fire changes soil, runoff and the risk to communities downstream

For people living below a burned hillside, the danger can arrive long after the flames are out. It can come with the first hard rain. Ground that once soaked up a storm can send water racing downhill. That water can carry ash, soil, rocks and charred wood toward homes, roads and streams below.

The link between wildfire burn scars and flash floods comes down to how fire changes the way land handles water. A healthy forest slows rain at almost every step. Leaves and branches catch it. A layer of needles, leaves and decaying plant matter absorbs it. Porous soil lets it sink in. A severe fire can weaken or remove several of those defenses at once.

Fire also leaves things behind. Heat can create a water-repellent layer a short way below the surface. Fine ash can clog soil pores and form a crust. Loose soil and burned wood sit on slopes with little to hold them in place. Together, these changes mean a burned watershed can shed water faster than it did before the fire.

Government agencies treat this as a distinct hazard. The National Weather Service issues flash flood warnings that name burn scars. The U.S. Geological Survey assesses post-fire debris-flow hazards after major fires. Burned Area Emergency Response teams from the USDA Forest Service assess burned land and recommend treatments to help stabilize it.

The effects can reach well beyond the fire perimeter. Sediment and debris can travel downstream into channels, roads, reservoirs and water intakes. Communities well outside the fire's path can still face damaged infrastructure or problems with drinking water.

The 10 slides that follow are a selection of key points, not a complete or ranked list. They explain what fire does to soil and vegetation and why a brief, intense storm can overwhelm burned ground. They also cover how runoff can become a debris flow, how far the effects can reach and how the danger changes as plants return. The final slide looks at the tools land managers and forecasters use to reduce the risk, and at their limits.

A wildfire can leave a water-repellent layer a short distance below the ground surface. Rain that would normally soak into that soil stalls at the layer and runs off instead.

The process starts with the fuel. Leaves, needles, twigs and other plant litter contain organic compounds, including waxes, oils and resins. As fire moves through that material, the heat turns some of those compounds into gas.

Not all of that gas escapes into the air. Some of it is driven downward into the soil, where temperatures are lower than at the burning surface. When the vapor reaches cooler soil particles, it condenses and coats them.

The coated particles repel water rather than absorbing it. The condition is known as soil water repellency, or hydrophobicity. The layer forms where the vapor condenses. It typically sits beneath a thin band of surface soil instead of right at the top.

That placement matters. The loose soil above the layer can still take in some water. Once that thin band fills up, the water cannot move deeper. It flows downhill along the top of the repellent layer. The saturated surface material can then wash away with the runoff, carrying sediment into small channels on the slope.

Some soils repel water to a degree even without fire. This is common under shrubs and trees that drop waxy or resinous litter. Fire can strengthen that existing tendency or create it where it was absent.

Field crews check for the condition with a method called the water drop penetration test. A drop of water is placed on exposed soil, and the crew notes whether it soaks in or beads up on the surface. Repeating the test at different depths helps locate where the repellent layer sits.

The layer is rarely uniform across a burned slope. It can be patchy — strong in one spot and weak or absent a few steps away. Where it is continuous, water has fewer places to soak in. On those stretches, more of each storm becomes surface runoff rather than groundwater.

A burned forest canopy can no longer catch and slow falling rain. Storm water strikes the ground directly and arrives faster. That change removes a watershed's first layer of protection against a downpour, before the soil or the forest floor even comes into play.

In a living forest, leaves, needles and branches intercept rainfall. Some of that water clings to foliage and bark. It then evaporates back into the air without ever reaching the soil. Hydrologists call this interception loss.

The rest of the rain reaches the ground in a delayed, uneven way. It drips from leaves and needles as throughfall. It also runs down branches and trunks as stemflow.

That delay matters during an intense storm. A canopy spreads the delivery of water over a longer period. Soil gets more time to absorb what lands on it. Less water collects on the surface at once.

When fire kills or consumes the canopy, that buffer disappears. Rain falls straight onto exposed ground at the full intensity of the storm. The same storm now delivers more water to the surface, and it delivers that water sooner.

Raindrops also hit bare soil with force. On unprotected ground, the impact of each drop can knock soil particles loose and throw them into the air. This process is called splash erosion. Water moving downhill can then pick up those loosened particles and carry them along as sediment.

Fire does not affect every canopy the same way. A low-intensity surface fire can leave much of the overstory alive. A crown fire, which spreads through the tops of trees, can strip a stand of its foliage entirely. In shrublands such as California's chaparral, fire often burns plants down to their stems and leaves little cover above the soil.

Trees scorched by a less intense fire may hold on to browned needles for a time. Those needles can fall in the months after a fire and partly cover the ground. Trees killed outright do not grow new foliage. Where a stand was killed, interception returns only as new trees and shrubs grow back.

Dead trees also stop pulling water out of the soil through their roots. Without that uptake, soil on a burned slope can stay wetter between storms.

Wildfire can burn away the forest floor's layer of litter and duff, which normally soaks up rain and holds soil in place. Once that layer is gone, water moves across the ground faster and carries more soil with it.

Litter is the top part of the forest floor. It is made of recently fallen needles, leaves, twigs and bark that have not yet broken down much. Duff lies beneath it. Duff is a darker mat of partly decomposed organic matter that sits on top of the mineral soil.

Together, the two layers work like a sponge. They take in rainfall, hold it, then release it slowly into the soil below. That storage gives water time to soak into the ground instead of collecting on the surface.

The forest floor also slows water that does start to move downhill. Fallen needles, sticks and uneven clumps of organic debris create a rough surface. Runoff has to wind around and through that material, and its speed drops. Slower water carries less sediment and has less force when it reaches a stream.

A third role is protection. Litter and duff cover the mineral soil and absorb the impact of falling raindrops. Bare soil has no such shield. Raindrops striking it can knock loose individual soil particles. The next flow of water then picks up those particles and carries them away.

Fire can remove all three functions at once. Flames consume dry litter quickly. Duff can keep smoldering after the main fire front has passed. Dry duff can burn all the way down to the mineral soil. What remains is a bare, exposed surface with little capacity to store water.

On that surface, post-fire runoff gathers quickly and gains speed on slopes. Shallow sheets of water can concentrate into small channels called rills. Over repeated storms, rills can deepen into gullies. Each step moves more soil off the hillside and into drainages below.

The forest floor does not burn evenly across a fire. Patches where duff survives keep some of their ability to absorb and slow water. So do spots where scorched trees drop a fresh layer of needles after the fire. Areas burned down to bare mineral soil lose that buffer.

Fine ash left on a burn scar can clog the small pores in the soil surface and harden into a crust. That seal slows the rate at which rainwater soaks in. More water then runs across the ground during a storm.

Ash is the powdery residue left after vegetation, fallen needles and dead wood burn. Much of it is made of very fine particles. They are small enough to wash into the gaps between soil grains. Those gaps are the openings water normally uses to move downward into the ground.

Once those openings fill, the soil surface behaves less like a sieve and more like a lid. Water reaching the ground has fewer paths to follow into the soil. It begins to pool on the surface and then moves downhill.

Rain helps build the seal. Raindrops striking bare ground knock loose fine material and drive it into surface pores. On a freshly burned slope, ash is often the loosest and finest material available. As wet ash dries, it can stiffen into a thin crust that remains in place when the next storm arrives.

This process differs from the water-repellent layer that fire can create a short way below the surface. An ash crust works at the very top of the soil. It blocks water physically by plugging gaps, not through a change in soil chemistry. Both effects can occur on the same burned hillside. Together, they leave the ground less able to take in water.

Ash cover is uneven across a fire area. Where flames burned hot and long, fuel is often consumed more fully, leaving fine gray or white ash. Where burning was patchier, blackened char and partly burned material can remain. That variation is one reason infiltration can differ between neighboring patches of the same burn scar.

Ash is also easy to move. Wind can strip it from exposed ridges before any rain falls. Early storms can wash it off slopes and into the channels below.

Soil burn severity maps show where a wildfire changed the ground most. Land managers rely on them to judge post-fire runoff risk. Burned soil is rated low, moderate or high severity. High severity ground usually carries the greatest risk of runoff and erosion.

The rating describes the soil, not the trees. A crown fire can strip a slope's canopy while the ground beneath burns only lightly. The reverse can also happen. Severity measures how much the fire altered the surface layers that control how water moves into the ground.

Mapping usually begins with satellite imagery. Analysts compare images taken before and after the fire to see how much the land surface changed. The result is a first-draft map, often called a Burned Area Reflectance Classification. Field crews then visit sample sites to check and adjust it.

On the ground, crews look for physical clues. They note how much litter and duff remain and the color and depth of the ash. They also check whether fine roots near the surface survived and whether the soil still holds its crumbly structure. Ground where the organic layer is gone and the soil looks loose and powdery tends to fall into the high category.

Crews also test for water repellency. A common field check is to place drops of water on the soil and time how long they take to soak in. Drops that bead up and sit on the surface point to a repellent layer.

The finished map shapes decisions about what happens next. Planners use it to choose which slopes need treatment first. Hazard modelers use it as an input when they estimate the chance of debris flows and floods.

A severity map is a guide, not a guarantee. Water repellency varies with soil type and with fire conditions, such as how hot the fire burned and how long flames lingered in one spot. Two patches with the same rating can respond differently to the same storm. Severity is also patchy, with lightly burned and unburned ground scattered among heavily burned areas.

A burned watershed can produce a flash flood from a brief, heavy downpour that the same slopes would have absorbed before the fire. On a burn scar, the rate at which rain falls matters more than the total that falls over a full day.

Hydrologists call the process infiltration-excess runoff. Every soil can absorb water only so fast. When rain arrives faster than that rate, the surplus flows across the surface instead of sinking in. Fire lowers the rate. A burst of rain that once filtered into the ground now runs downhill almost at once.

That makes short storms the main concern. A thunderstorm that drops heavy rain for a few minutes can send water racing off a scar, even if its total rainfall looks modest on paper. A gentle, steady rain lasting hours can pose less danger than a sharp cloudburst.

Timing adds to the risk. Runoff from a steep burned slope can reach a canyon mouth or a road crossing soon after the heaviest rain. That leaves little time to react. The storm also does not have to pass over the people it affects. Rain falling on a scar high in the mountains can flood a neighborhood below that stays dry.

The rainfall needed to trigger trouble is not a single figure. It depends on slope steepness, soil type, how severely the ground burned and the size of the drainage. A threshold that fits one scar may not fit another a few valleys away.

The National Weather Service handles this variation with flash flood warnings that name specific burn scars. Local forecast offices watch radar for intense storm cells moving over recently burned ground. Naming the scar lets residents downstream judge whether their area lies in its path. In the U.S., flash flood warnings can also reach mobile phones through Wireless Emergency Alerts.

Seasonal weather patterns shape when this danger peaks. In the Southwest, summer monsoon thunderstorms bring the short, intense bursts that burned slopes handle worst. In Southern California, winter storms can deliver heavy rain onto scars left by the previous fire season.

A post-fire debris flow forms when water racing off a burn scar picks up ash, loose soil, rocks and burned wood until it becomes a thick, fast-moving slurry. It carries far more force and weight than an ordinary flood of water.

The difference between a debris flow and a muddy flood lies in how much the water carries. Floodwater mostly holds sediment in suspension. A debris flow carries so much solid material that it behaves more like wet concrete than a stream. That density lets it push boulders, snap trees and shove buildings off their foundations.

On a burned slope, the process often begins in small channels and gullies. Runoff concentrates there and scours loose material from the channel bed and banks. Each new load of sediment adds weight and erosive power. That added power lets the flow tear up still more material. By the time it reaches a canyon mouth, runoff that started as a thin sheet of water can have grown into a surge of mud and rock.

Communities at the foot of steep mountain fronts face particular exposure. Debris flows tend to spill out where canyons open onto flatter ground. Over time, these deposits build fan-shaped landforms called alluvial fans, and foothill towns are often built on them. The flow slows and spreads there, dropping its load across streets, yards and homes.

After major fires, the U.S. Geological Survey produces hazard assessments for post-fire debris flows. These estimate how likely debris flows are in individual drainage basins and how large they could be. Emergency managers use that information to decide where to focus warnings and evacuations.

The Montecito, California, debris flow shows what the hazard looks like on the ground. The Thomas Fire burned the steep slopes of the Santa Ynez Mountains above the town in December 2017. On January 9, 2018, heavy rain fell on that burn scar. Mud, boulders and burned trees surged down the creeks toward the neighborhoods below. The debris flow killed 23 people.

Montecito's setting fits the broader pattern. The community sits on a narrow strip of land between steep mountains and the Pacific Ocean. Its creeks run from those recently burned slopes straight through residential areas. That layout gave the debris flow a direct path into town.

Water that runs off a burn scar does not stay on the burn scar. It carries sediment, ash and debris into streams and rivers. That flow can flood towns, roads and water systems well beyond the edge of the fire.

The reach comes from the way watersheds are built. Every slope drains into a channel, and every channel feeds a larger one. When a burned hillside sheds water faster than it once did, that extra flow joins the stream network and keeps moving. A community that never saw flames can sit at the bottom of that network.

The storm can also be somewhere else entirely. Heavy rain can fall on a burned mountainside while the town below stays dry. Floodwater may then arrive in a creek or arroyo under clear skies. Residents may have little sign that anything is happening upstream.

The water also carries a heavy load. Sediment and burned debris travel with the flow toward lower ground. Some of that material settles in channels and raises the streambed. A shallower channel holds less water, so the next surge spills over the banks more readily.

Roads and bridges often sit directly in the path. Culverts sized for clear water can plug with logs and mud. Once a crossing is blocked, water backs up behind it. It can then overtop the road or wash it out. Damaged routes can cut off access for residents and emergency crews.

Drinking water is another downstream exposure. Ash and fine sediment can wash into reservoirs and river intakes that supply towns. Murky, debris-laden water is harder to treat. Sediment that settles at the bottom of a reservoir also takes up space meant for storing water. Utilities may have to change how they draw and clean water, or stop drawing from an affected source for a time.

New Mexico has seen burn scar flooding in recent years. Flooding followed the 2022 Hermits Peak and Calf Canyon fire in the northern part of the state. Flooding also followed the 2024 South Fork Fire near Ruidoso, in south-central New Mexico.

Flood and debris-flow danger on a burn scar is highest in the first years after a fire. It declines gradually as plants return and the soil rebuilds its ability to absorb water. There is no fixed date when a burned slope becomes safe again.

Recovery starts with whatever survived underground. Many grasses and shrubs resprout from roots, bulbs or root crowns that the fire did not kill. In California chaparral, shrubs such as chamise and scrub oak can send up new shoots from their bases soon after a burn. Seeds stored in the soil also germinate. Some species in fire-adapted ecosystems have seeds that respond to heat or smoke.

New growth helps in several ways at once. Stems and leaves break the force of falling rain. Roots knit loose soil together and open channels that let water move downward. Dropped leaves begin to rebuild a protective layer on the ground.

Below the surface, soil structure recovers more slowly. Soil organisms, fungi and roots help bind particles into small clumps with pore space between them. That structure lets water soak in rather than race across the surface. As it returns, more rain infiltrates and less becomes runoff.

The pace varies widely from one burn scar to another. Burn severity, soil type, slope steepness and the amount of rain that falls during recovery all shape the timeline. A wet growing season can speed regrowth, while drought can stall it. A slope burned at high severity usually takes longer to recover than one that burned lightly.

Some changes can work against recovery for a time. The roots of trees killed by the fire decay underground. As they rot, they lose their ability to hold slopes together. That process can leave hillsides more prone to shallow landslides even as surface vegetation comes back.

A second fire can reset the process. When a recovering area reburns, the young vegetation and rebuilt soil can be lost before they have stabilized the ground.

Communities below a burn scar can still face the hazard after the first winter or monsoon season following the fire. The risk tapers over time rather than switching off.

Treating burned slopes and warning the people below them are the main ways to cut post-fire flood risk. USDA Forest Service Burned Area Emergency Response teams assess burned land and recommend mulch, seeding and erosion barriers. Debris basins and alerts help protect communities downstream.

The program, known as BAER, is built for speed. Its work often begins while a fire is still burning. A damaging storm can arrive before burned ground has any cover at all.

A BAER team typically draws on specialists such as hydrologists, soil scientists, engineers and biologists. They survey the burn and identify threats to life, property and critical natural and cultural resources. Then they propose emergency treatments. The Department of the Interior runs its own BAER program for land managed by agencies such as the Bureau of Land Management and the National Park Service.

Mulch gives bare soil immediate cover. Straw or shredded wood spread across a slope shields the ground from raindrop impact. It also slows water moving downhill. On steep or remote terrain, crews often drop mulch from helicopters.

Seeding aims to rebuild ground cover with grasses or other plants. It works more slowly than mulch. Seed needs moisture and time to sprout before it holds any soil. Land managers choose seed mixes with care. Nonnative species can crowd out recovering native plants.

Erosion barriers trap sediment on the hillside before it reaches a channel. Crews may lay burned logs across a slope or set out straw wattles. They may also upgrade roads and culverts to carry more water and debris.

Private land falls outside federal BAER authority. The Natural Resources Conservation Service can help landowners there through its Emergency Watershed Protection program.

Other defenses sit downstream. Debris basins catch mud and boulders before they reach neighborhoods. Crews must clear them after storms to restore their capacity. Evacuation warnings and National Weather Service flash flood warnings give residents time to leave before a storm peaks.

None of these measures removes the hazard. Mulch can wash away in a heavy storm. Seed may fail to take hold, and a basin can overfill. The treatments lower the risk on a burn scar, but they do not eliminate it.

How long before flood insurance takes effect: 10 rules and exceptions

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The flood insurance waiting period is usually 30 days for NFIP policies, but mortgage closings, map changes and home sales can shorten or skip it entirely

The moment most homeowners think about flood insurance is often the moment it can no longer help them. A hurricane track shifts toward the coast, or a river gauge starts climbing, and the search for coverage begins. Under the standard rules, a new policy bought at that point would not take effect for weeks.

That delay is the flood insurance waiting period. For policies written through the National Flood Insurance Program, which FEMA manages, coverage generally begins 30 days after the application and the full premium are received. A flood that starts before that date is not covered, even if the policy has already been paid for.

A homeowner who assumes flood protection begins the day the payment clears can end up paying for repairs without insurance. The question of how long before flood insurance takes effect has a firm answer in most cases. That answer is measured in weeks, not days.

The 30-day rule is not absolute, though. Several situations shorten the wait or remove it. Coverage purchased in connection with making, increasing, extending or renewing a loan takes effect at closing. A flood map revision can move a property into a high-risk zone. If the owner buys a policy within a limited window set by FEMA, the wait is much shorter. A buyer who takes over a seller's existing NFIP policy keeps that coverage without starting the clock again.

Other rules work in the opposite direction. A policy that lapses can bring a fresh waiting period when it is replaced. Adding coverage partway through a policy term generally comes with its own wait unless a loan transaction is involved.

The 10 slides that follow cover a selection of these rules and exceptions, one per slide. They explain what each one requires and when it applies. They also look beyond the federal program to private flood insurance, where insurers set their own terms. The final slide covers lender-placed coverage, which a lender can buy when a borrower's required policy lapses. Each slide stands on its own. Readers can go straight to the situation that matches theirs.

A new policy from the National Flood Insurance Program generally takes effect 30 days after the application and the full premium are received. Until that date arrives, the policy is not in force. That holds even when the paperwork is complete and the buyer has a confirmation in hand.

The National Flood Insurance Program, known as the NFIP, is run by the Federal Emergency Management Agency, or FEMA. Congress created it through the National Flood Insurance Act of 1968. Insurance agents sell its policies. So do private insurers that write and service NFIP coverage under FEMA's Write Your Own arrangement. The 30-day standard is the same no matter which company issues the policy.

The clock depends on two things: the application and the full premium. An application submitted without payment does not start the countdown. If the premium arrives later, the effective date moves later with it. For anyone buying coverage, the date the money is received matters more than the date the form was signed.

The rule applies to both parts of an NFIP policy. Building coverage pays for damage to the structure itself, including its foundation, electrical and plumbing systems and permanent fixtures. Contents coverage pays for belongings such as furniture, clothing and electronics. Renters can buy contents-only policies. Those follow the same 30-day standard when bought outside an exception.

The waiting period carries extra weight because standard homeowners insurance policies generally exclude flood damage. A household that assumes its existing policy covers rising water may learn otherwise only when it looks into separate flood coverage. The 30-day count starts from that purchase, not from any earlier point.

The calendar also plays a role. The Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 to Nov. 30. A policy whose application and premium arrive on the first day of the season does not take effect until July 1.

The 30-day period is the default rather than a fixed rule for every policy. Some transactions and map changes set a different start date, and each one comes with its own conditions.

The flood insurance waiting period exists to stop people from buying coverage only when a flood is clearly on its way. Without it, a homeowner could skip premiums for years. That same owner could then buy a policy the week a storm is forecast and collect on damage that was close to certain.

Insurance works by pooling risk. Many policyholders pay premiums, and that money covers the losses of the smaller group who suffer damage in a given year. The arrangement holds only if people buy coverage before they know whether they will need it.

The insurance term for the opposite pattern is adverse selection. It occurs when the people most likely to file a claim are also the most eager to buy, and they buy when their risk is highest. If enough buyers waited for a forecast, premiums collected would fall short of claims paid. The pool would then shrink toward the households facing imminent losses.

Flooding is especially exposed to this problem because much of it can be seen coming. A house fire or a burst pipe usually strikes without notice. A hurricane, by contrast, is tracked across the ocean for days before it reaches land. The National Hurricane Center issues a hurricane watch 48 hours before tropical-storm-force winds are expected in an area. The National Weather Service forecasts river crests ahead of time. Snowmelt flooding along northern rivers tends to follow a seasonal pattern each spring.

That lead time is the gap the waiting period is designed to close. The delay on a new National Flood Insurance Program policy runs well past the warning a hurricane watch provides. A buyer cannot treat a forecast as the signal to purchase coverage.

The rule also shapes how flood coverage fits into household planning. Standard homeowners policies in the U.S. generally exclude flood damage. Flood protection is therefore a separate purchase. The waiting period turns that purchase into a decision made ahead of hurricane season or the spring thaw, not a response to a specific weather event.

A flood policy bought in connection with making, increasing, extending or renewing a loan takes effect at the loan closing. The standard waiting period on National Flood Insurance Program coverage does not apply in that situation.

The exception reflects how flood coverage and mortgages are tied together in U.S. law. The Flood Disaster Protection Act of 1973 requires flood insurance on buildings in high-risk flood zones, known as Special Flood Hazard Areas. The requirement applies when the property secures a loan from a federally regulated or federally insured lender. Loans backed by federal agencies, such as the Federal Housing Administration and the Department of Veterans Affairs, carry the same requirement.

A lender in that position cannot make the loan on an uninsured building. Any waiting period would leave the collateral exposed from the first day of the loan. Starting the policy at closing lets the coverage and the debt begin together.

The exception covers four kinds of loan events. Making a loan is the most common, as when a buyer finances a home purchase. Increasing a loan applies when a borrower takes on a larger balance secured by the same property. Extending a loan lengthens its term. Renewing a loan restarts an agreement that has reached its end date.

Timing still matters. Under FEMA's rules, the application and premium need to be submitted at or before the closing for the policy to start that day. A borrower who arranges coverage after the closing has passed falls back under the standard wait.

For home buyers, flood insurance in a high-risk zone becomes a closing-table item alongside homeowners insurance. The lender checks whether the property sits in a Special Flood Hazard Area, typically on FEMA's Standard Flood Hazard Determination Form. If it does, the borrower must show proof of a flood policy before the loan funds.

Federal rules also set the minimum amount of coverage. It must equal at least the smallest of three figures. Those are the outstanding loan principal, the maximum coverage the NFIP offers for that type of building and the building's insurable value. For a single-family home, the NFIP building coverage limit is $250,000.

A property that a flood map revision moves into a high-risk zone can get NFIP coverage that starts one day after purchase. The shortened wait applies only when the owner buys the policy within a limited window that FEMA sets after the revision takes effect.

The maps in question are Flood Insurance Rate Maps. FEMA produces them to show flood risk community by community. The highest-risk areas on these maps are called Special Flood Hazard Areas. They mark land with at least a 1% chance of flooding in any given year. Their zone labels begin with A or V.

FEMA revises these maps as it gathers new data on rainfall, river flows, coastal conditions and local development. A revision can redraw the boundary of a high-risk zone. A home that sat outside that boundary for years can end up inside it once the new map becomes effective.

The new zone can change what an owner is required to buy. Federal law requires flood insurance on buildings in Special Flood Hazard Areas that serve as collateral for mortgages from federally regulated or federally insured lenders. An owner who never needed a flood policy may suddenly be required to carry one.

The one-day rule gives newly mapped owners a path to coverage without the standard 30-day gap. Without it, they would go a month without protection at the point the government formally classified their property as high risk.

The exception depends on timing. The date that matters is the effective date of the map revision, not the date an owner hears about it. A policy bought after FEMA's window closes falls back under the usual 30-day rule unless another exception applies.

Map changes generally pass through a public process before they take effect. That process includes preliminary maps and a period for appeals. Owners who follow it at the local level can learn that a revision is coming well before the new map is final.

The shortened wait is tied specifically to a move into a high-risk zone. An owner whose property was already mapped as high risk, and who simply decides to buy coverage, faces the standard 30-day period.

A National Flood Insurance Program policy can be assigned to the buyer of a home. The buyer takes over the seller's existing coverage and does not have to sit through a new waiting period.

Assignment works differently from buying a fresh policy. A new application sets its own effective date in the future. An assigned policy is already in force on the day the buyer takes it over. The buyer steps into coverage that was already running under the seller's name.

Continuity around a sale has practical weight. A home can change hands during the Atlantic hurricane season, which runs from June 1 to November 30. A standard homeowners policy does not cover flood damage. A buyer who relies only on a new policy bought outside an exception could own the property for weeks with no flood coverage.

Assignment can matter most to buyers who pay cash. The exception that lets a new policy start at closing depends on a loan transaction. A cash purchase involves no loan. Assignment gives a cash buyer a way to have NFIP coverage in force from the first day of ownership.

Buyers who finance a purchase have the loan-closing route as well. For a property in a high-risk flood zone, a federally regulated or insured lender requires flood insurance as a condition of the mortgage. For those buyers, assignment is one of two ways to have flood coverage in place when the sale closes. A financed buyer can compare the seller's existing coverage with a new policy before choosing.

The policy's declarations page lists the coverage limits and the dates of the policy term. Those details show a buyer how much coverage they would inherit and when the policy comes up for renewal. For a single-family home, NFIP building coverage tops out at $250,000, and contents coverage tops out at $100,000.

The assignment rule belongs to the NFIP, the federal flood program. A policy from a private flood insurer is governed by its own contract, including any terms on transfer to a new owner.

A flood policy renewed with its premium received before the expiration date carries on without a break in coverage. If the policy lapses instead, replacement coverage can face a fresh waiting period before it protects the property.

The difference comes down to timing. A renewal is not a new purchase. It extends an existing contract. The protection already in place rolls into the next term.

Policies under the National Flood Insurance Program are generally written for one-year terms. The renewal question comes up every year, whether or not a flood has threatened the area.

A lapse happens when the renewal premium does not arrive before the policy expires. Once that occurs, the property has no flood coverage. Any flood damage during the gap falls on the owner.

The larger cost can come afterward. Restarting coverage after a lapse may be treated as buying a new policy rather than renewing an old one. In that case, the owner can be left exposed again while a new waiting period runs. A homeowner who misses a deadline in a quiet month could still be uninsured weeks later, when storm season arrives.

The payment method matters as well. Many homeowners with mortgages pay flood premiums through an escrow account managed by their loan servicer. In that arrangement, the servicer sends the payment. The policy can still lapse if that payment is late or missed. Confirming that the renewal was actually paid, rather than assuming it was, reduces that risk.

Owners who pay directly need to watch for the renewal notice from their insurer. They also need to make sure the premium will be received, not merely sent, before the expiration date. The rule turns on when the insurer gets the money.

Grace-period rules for late renewal payments exist, but their terms are set in program rules that can change. Policyholders can confirm the current terms with their insurer or agent rather than counting on extra time.

A mid-term increase in National Flood Insurance Program coverage generally comes with its own waiting period. The higher limit does not take effect right away unless the increase is tied to a loan transaction, such as a mortgage closing or a refinance.

The existing coverage stays in place while the extra amount waits. Take a homeowner with a $150,000 building policy who asks to raise it. That $150,000 stays in force the whole time, and only the added portion is on hold. If a flood hits before the increase takes effect, the claim is paid under the old limit, not the new one.

The split matters most to owners who find partway through a policy year that they are underinsured. Rebuilding costs can rise after a renovation or an addition. A policy written years earlier may no longer match what it would cost to replace the house. Raising the limit closes that gap, but only after the waiting period has passed.

The loan exception applies to an increase the same way it applies to a new policy. A lender may require more flood coverage as part of a new, larger, extended or renewed loan. In that case, the higher limit can take effect at the closing. A refinance with a larger loan balance is a common example. The borrower does not have to wait out a separate period for the added coverage.

NFIP coverage also has a ceiling that no mid-term change can exceed. For a home, residential building coverage tops out at $250,000 and contents coverage at $100,000. Building and contents carry separate limits on the same policy. A homeowner who needs more than those amounts can buy excess flood insurance from a private insurer. That coverage sits on top of the federal policy.

An increase also adds to the cost of the policy. The higher limit carries an additional premium, which covers the added amount for the rest of the current policy term.

A flood insurance policy does not pay for damage from a flood that was already in progress when the coverage began. The standard National Flood Insurance Program policy excludes that loss, even if water reaches the insured building only after the policy takes effect.

This exclusion is written into the Standard Flood Insurance Policy, the contract used for NFIP coverage. It applies to a flood in progress at the time and date the policy term begins. It also applies to coverage a policyholder adds at their own request during the term.

In a claim, the key question is when the flood started, not when the house got wet. The policy defines a flood as a general and temporary condition of partial or complete inundation of normally dry land. That condition must cover two or more acres of normally dry land, or two or more properties, at least one of which belongs to the policyholder. The water can come from the overflow of inland or tidal waters, from the unusual and rapid accumulation or runoff of surface waters from any source, or from mudflow.

Under that definition, a flood can be underway in a neighborhood before water reaches a particular home. A river that has already left its banks upstream, or streets already under water nearby, can mark the start of the event. A policy that begins while that condition exists does not cover the damage that follows from it.

The exclusion closes a gap that the waiting period alone would leave open. Without it, a policy that started on schedule in the middle of a slow-moving flood could still pay for that flood. River flooding can build over days or weeks as water moves downstream. A policy bought early in that kind of event could otherwise come into force before the water arrived.

The rule also covers policies that skip the standard wait. A buyer whose coverage starts at a loan closing is not covered for a flood that began before the closing. The same is true for a homeowner using the one-day effective date after a flood map revision.

For a property owner, the practical issue is timing. Coverage bought after heavy rain has started, or after a river crest is already moving through a region, does not cover that event if the flood was already in progress. The purchase has to happen before any flooding begins in order to protect against it.

Private flood insurers set their own waiting periods, and some are shorter than the 30 days that applies to most National Flood Insurance Program policies. The wait depends on the insurer and the policy. The effective date printed on the declarations page is the date that counts.

Private flood insurance is coverage sold by insurance companies outside the federal program. The NFIP, run by FEMA, writes most residential flood policies in the U.S. Private carriers compete with it for some of the same homes and businesses.

The federal waiting-period rule does not bind these companies. Each insurer writes its own terms into the policy contract. The wait can differ from one carrier to the next. It can also differ between states for the same carrier. A homeowner comparing quotes can treat the waiting period as one of the terms to check, alongside price and coverage limits.

A shorter wait does not mean coverage is available at any moment. Property insurers commonly use binding restrictions, which pause the sale of new policies in an area when a named storm threatens it. A buyer who holds off until a hurricane forecast can find that no private policy is on offer at all.

Private policies also matter for mortgage borrowers. The Biggert-Waters Flood Insurance Reform Act required federally regulated lenders to accept private flood insurance that meets certain standards. Federal banking regulators later issued a joint rule setting out how lenders apply that requirement. A qualifying private policy can satisfy a lender's flood requirement in place of an NFIP policy.

The companies behind these policies are not all regulated the same way. Some private insurers are admitted carriers licensed by a state insurance regulator. Others sell through the surplus lines market. Surplus lines policies are generally not backed by state guaranty funds if the insurer fails.

The coverage itself can differ from the federal product. Some private policies offer building limits above the NFIP's residential cap. Some also cover additional living expenses during repairs, which NFIP policies do not.

When a borrower's required flood coverage lapses, the lender can buy a flood policy on the borrower's behalf and charge the borrower for it. This is known as lender-placed or force-placed flood insurance. Its job is to keep a mortgaged property insured while the loan is still outstanding.

The obligation comes from federal law. The Flood Disaster Protection Act of 1973 and the National Flood Insurance Reform Act of 1994 require flood insurance on loans from federally regulated lenders. The rule applies when the property sits in a Special Flood Hazard Area in a community that takes part in the National Flood Insurance Program. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac apply the same requirement to loans they buy.

The coverage has to stay in force for the life of the loan, not just on closing day. A lender that finds coverage has lapsed, or has dropped below the required amount, must respond.

The process starts with a notice. Under the 1994 law, the lender must tell the borrower that coverage is missing or insufficient. If the borrower does not buy adequate coverage within 45 days of that notice, the lender must purchase it. The premium is then charged to the borrower.

A lender-placed policy may come from the NFIP's Mortgage Portfolio Protection Program or from a private insurer. Its terms are built around the lender's need to protect the loan. The limits, the deductible and the price can differ from what the borrower would have picked.

The borrower can replace lender-placed coverage by buying a policy and sending proof to the lender or loan servicer. The Biggert-Waters Flood Insurance Reform Act of 2012 requires the lender to end the force-placed coverage within 30 days of receiving that proof. The lender must also refund premiums and fees for any period when the two policies overlapped.

The effective date on the replacement policy determines when the borrower's own coverage begins. Confirming that date before the lender cancels its policy keeps the property from going uninsured in between.

Warren Buffett Just Gave Away $5.5 Billion of Berkshire Stock. He Says His Confidence in Berkshire Is "As Strong as Ever."

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The rest of his shares are set to leave his hands by 2034. Does this matter for the stock?

Warren Buffett gave away another big part of his wealth on Monday, Nov. 16. The chairman emeritus of Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA -1.02%)(BRKB -1.03%) converted 6,150 Class A shares into 9,225,000 Class B shares and donated them to four family foundations. At Monday's closing price, the gift was worth around $5.5 billion.

This gift is over three times the 2.7 million Class B shares he gave the same four foundations last Thanksgiving. Buffett also paired it with a short note to shareholders.

"You heard plenty from me in September about my confidence in Berkshire. That confidence is as strong as ever," Buffett wrote.

Berkshire's Class B shares finished at $592.85 on Monday, just under Friday's record close of $592.94. The stock is up around 18% in 2026 as I write.

Warren Buffett. Image source: Getty Images.

This year's note is much shorter than last year's, and most of it is about kindness, not business.

On Berkshire itself, Buffett just pointed back to September. That month, Berkshire announced that he had become chairman emeritus and that his son Howard Buffett had taken over as chairman.

In a letter put out with that news, Buffett wrote that he was more confident than ever in the future. In other words, Monday's gift isn't a vote against the stock.

After Monday's gift, Buffett holds 182,140 Class A shares and 1,162 Class B shares. His stake's worth around $162 billion at Monday's prices, or about 13% of Berkshire's market value.

It's all spoken for. In July, Buffett wrote that he hopes his children can "carry out the disposal of my shares" by Dec. 31, 2034. Either way, he said, the shares will reach the four foundations by then.

Showing how much the giving has picked up, Buffett's two gifts this year come to around 21.2 million Class B shares -- 12 million in July and about 9.2 million on Monday. The four family foundations got around 5.6 million Class B shares from him in two gifts in 2025. This year's total is almost four times as big.

Buffett explained the faster pace last year, saying that his children are all past normal retirement age and that he wants them to finish the work themselves. Of course, he added that the acceleration "in no way reflects any change in my views about Berkshire's prospects."

The foundations aren't built to sit on the stock. Buffett's 2006 pledge letter to Howard's foundation, for example, said there was no need to build large reserves, because new gifts would come every year. The Susan Thompson Buffett Foundation, the biggest recipient, paid around $1.5 billion in grants in 2025 alone.

It's fair to expect most of Buffett's remaining stake to be sold over time. Spread evenly through 2034, around $162 billion comes to about $20 billion of stock a year. That might seem like a lot. But Berkshire bought back around $14.4 billion of its own stock through September, including about $9.6 billion in the third quarter. Monday's 9.2 million shares also equal only about two days of normal trading in the Class B stock, based on its average volume over the last three months.

True, the buybacks might slow. Berkshire only repurchases shares when its CEO thinks the price is under the company's intrinsic value (an estimate of what the whole business is worth). And the stock is now near a record. But selling spread across gifts planned and announced years ahead arguably isn't likely to move the stock much by itself.

The price matters more. Berkshire's third-quarter operating earnings (which strip out the swings in its stock portfolio that hit net income) dropped about 6% year over year.

The largest drag was insurance underwriting, where profit slid about 43%.

But the stock now trades at about 27 times operating earnings over the past four quarters, up from about 22 times operating earnings at the end of September.

Ultimately, I don't see anything in Monday's gift or letter that should worry a Berkshire shareholder. For a new buyer, though, the record price is the bigger issue. I still think Berkshire is worth a look for the long run, but I'd be more comfortable paying under $593 for it.

Anyone can buy what these platforms sell to the public. Private deals usually take only accredited investors.
