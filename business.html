The Woman Who Said Yes While Shaking Her Head No

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

Early on in my career, I was a research manager working on consumer health products for a pharmaceutical company. Part of my job was to develop ideas for new products by testing them in large quantitative surveys to forecast sales. We showed respondents product descriptions I had painstakingly written and asked how likely they would be to buy it.

We tabulated the results to the decimal point, giving the data an aura of accuracy. But there was one problem. What people said in surveys didn’t reliably predict what they bought in market.

I came across a clue as to why sitting behind a two-way mirror. The focus group moderator was reading a product concept we tested in an earlier survey. She asked: “If this was available, would you buy it?” Without hesitating, a woman across the table clearly said yes, while her head was already shaking no.

The woman herself and the moderator didn’t notice. And we all tallied a yes in the backroom.

But her words just didn’t match her gesture. This doesn’t mean a headshake is the same thing as a lie detector. I wasn’t sure what she might do later. But I knew at that time that her answer was less settled than she made it sound.

From then on, I would note verbal and nonverbal responses, and I kept seeing the same split. A yes would arrive with a wince, eye roll, or furrowed brow. Sometimes they would say deadpan they are excited to buy. I would ask them to write down their reasons. The written words seemed well-reasoned and coherent. Over time, the unspoken reactions helped explain what my self-reported surveys were missing. It’s not like the words were useless. But they weren’t the clear window into behavior that the market research industry was built on.

I also had been assuming that honest people could explain their answers. But I started doubting that, and it changed my career path. The pharmaceutical company offered me a paid executive MBA at Columbia. I asked if I could get a degree in psychology. They said no, so I said no. I left to work at a qualitative research firm conducting focus groups and in-depth interviews. The answers weren’t in my spreadsheets, so I’d rather be in the room with people, so I could see the real story show up before the words.

Years later, I found a different kind of research and an aha moment that helped explain the “say versus do gap” that had undermined my earlier self-reported quantitative forecasts.

Neuroscientist Michael Gazzaniga conducted fascinating studies on patients whose corpus callosum, the fibers connecting the two hemispheres of the brain, had been cut to control severe epilepsy. The surgery sharply limited communication between hemispheres, which allowed him to show patients information to one side of the brain without the other side seeing it.

A patient was shown the word “walk” to their right hemisphere and he got up and began walking and the experimenter asked him why. The left hemisphere, which controls speech, hadn’t seen the instruction, so the honest answer should have been "I don’t know." But instead, he said something completely sincere: "I was thirsty and wanted a drink."

Gazzaniga called this left hemisphere function the interpreter: a process that takes something we just did and creates the most coherent explanation based upon whatever information is available.

The woman behind the glass wasn’t a split-brain patient. Her hemispheres communicated normally. We can’t compare the two research studies literally. But what she did share with split-brain patients was the gap: a fluent verbal response that didn’t match her actions. People with intact brains base their explanations on much more information and they often get it right. But Gazzaniga argues that the interpreter is not just a quirk from surgery but something all our brains do as we attempt to make our experiences feel unified and our actions seem like our own doing. The surgery simply made the interpreter more visible; by cutting off information we would normally use.

You can see this in people with normal brains. Researchers at Lund University in Sweden conducted an experiment that let people in a supermarket taste jam. Shoppers were asked which jam they preferred and then asked to taste it a second time. But the experimenters secretly swapped out their preferred jam with another. Most of the participants didn’t even notice the swap, and when asked to describe why they preferred it, they easily explained why, even though they had just rejected it. Despite their brain being fully intact, their reasons seemed like honest reports when they may have been made up.

Have you ever bought something on a whim? You can probably think of a reason why easily. But here’s a harder question: How do you know that is the real reason? Maybe the answer arrived later based on what you know about the product, the moment you bought it, and the person you believe yourself to be?

Or how about bigger decisions, like the job that felt right, the person you just met that you trusted, or that comment from your loved one that made you snap, only to explain it after as: I was just having a bad day. I’m not saying your explanations are fictitious. But maybe it was the simplest story you could put together on such short notice, while leaving out a deeper feeling of being dismissed by them, or some other worry you felt but hadn’t yet named.

Your reasons are not necessarily lies. But alone they’re incomplete. Sometimes there’s a story, and then there’s the real story. When you close a conversation with a quick confident answer you miss an opportunity to dig deeper for a question that deserves to stay open a little longer.

If you ever find yourself doing something surprising, remember your first explanation is just the start not the verdict. Think about what was happening right before and try to parse out if you were moving away or toward something. We tend to avoid bad feelings rather than try to get what we want. We choose a more expensive bottle to avoid looking cheap, agree with someone to avoid a fight, or put off doing something because we fear what might happen. Those reasons are usually not the first things to come to mind. Make sure to check if your story fits everything, not just the things you noticed.

I never learned what that woman finally did. She said yes, but her body said no. And while both responses came from her, neither told the whole story.

Your explanation may be sincere, but it also might be incomplete.

Is It True That Air Force One Pilots Earn Around $215K While Big 3 Widebody Captains Clear $480K?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

United States Air Force VC-46A aircraft and the Air Force VC-25B "Bridge" (aka Air Force One) aircraft are military aircraft owned and operated by the United States Air Force and are crewed and piloted by USAF commissioned officers. These are paid as US military officers, and so the Air Force has little flexibility to provide extra pay to its pilots. The base pay is the same as any other US officer serving any branch (including the Coast Guard and Space Force) and doesn't change by responsibility. So an Army officer receives the same base pay as a pilot of the same rank and service.

This is problematic, as it can cost around $10-15 million to train a fast jet pilot. It has left the Air Force exposed to intense competition from commercial airlines that can offer double the total compensation with what is sometimes considered a better work-life balance. One way the Air Force (and the Marines and Navy) have sought to counter this is with retention bonuses, although these are limited, and commercial airlines do the same. Here is what to know about how much commercial widebody pilots are paid and how much Air Force One pilots are paid.

The Air Force does not pay a special "Air Force One salary." The pilots who fly these special Jumbos (and other Air Force aircraft the President flies on) are commissioned officers assigned to the 89th Airlift Wing. As with other US Air Force officers, they are paid according to their military rank and years of service. They do not get a special salary based on the prestige of the aircraft they fly.

Lieutenant Colonels with less than two years of service earn $7,295 per month in base salary, while those with six years of aviation service have a base pay of $9,250 monthly. For Colonels, the base pay is $8,751 for under two years of service and $10,284 for six years. But while six years is the highest number the US Air Force's table goes up to, in practice all Air Force One pilots have a much longer service and earn more.

Lieutenant Colonels typically have 15–17 years of commissioned service, while Colonels typically have 21–24 years of commissioned service. A Lieutenant Colonel flying VC-46s in 2026 can be expected to have a base pay of around $130,000 to $145,000, plus a Basic Allowance for Housing (BAH) of around $30,000 to $45,000, an Aviation Retention Bonus of around $35,000 to $50,000, plus other benefits for a total compensation of between $200,000 and $250,000. A Colonel can expect to earn $235,000 to $285,000 in total compensation.

The US Air Force pilots who fly the VC-46s are typically experienced Lieutenant Colonels (O-5) or Colonels (O-6). This makes them a higher rank than the typical ranks that fly fighter jets on combat missions. Most fighter jet pilots who actually carry out combat missions are First Lieutenants (O-2) and Captains (0-3). At the next rank of Major (0-4), administrative and leadership responsibilities increase, although many majors continue to fly operational and combat missions extensively.

Majors tend to carry out fewer combat missions, although they do extensively perform combat missions as required, often as squadron commanders of a strike package. It is even less common for Lieutenant Colonels and Colonels to fly in combat, although they can if a need arises. In 2026, the F-15E Strike Eagle Weapons Systems Officer that was downed over Iran is reported to have been a Colonel. On average, pilots assigned to the presidential airlift tend to be more senior in rank than pilots in operational fighter squadrons.

As such, Air Force One pilots do tend to earn more than their fighter jet (and transport) counterparts, although it is related to rank and years of service, not the aircraft per se. The 89th Airlift Wing also employs many majors and occasionally captains, although it's unclear if they fly the VC-46s. The wing has a large range of aircraft.

The 89th Airlift Wing (89 AW) is based at Joint Base Andrews, Maryland, and is responsible for operating the USAF's primary executive and special air mission (SAM) fleet. According to its website, it "provides global Special Air Mission airlift, logistics, aerial port, and communications for the President, Vice President, cabinet members and senior leaders to ensure nuclear command, control and communications, continuity of government and continuity of operations."

Its website also listed its aircraft as including two VC-25A (Air Force One) aircraft, four C-32A aircraft (often called Air Force Two), four C-40B Clippers, and eleven C-37A/Bs based on the Gulfstream business jet. This list appears somewhat out of date, as the wing now operates the VC-25B "Bridge" (ex-Qatari Boeing 747-8) Presidential aircraft. Simple Flying has previously reported that the Air Force has quietly purchased an expanded fleet of shadowy Boeing 757s; these are also operated by the 89th Airlift Wing.

89th Airlift Wing fleet (per USAF, others)

The most notable squadron is the Presidential Airlift Group / Presidential Airlift Squadron, which is primarily responsible for the VC-25s. Other notable squadrons include the 1st Airlift Squadron (operating the C-32A and the C-40B) and the 99th Airlift Squadron (operating the C-37 fleet). The wing has a "ready force" of over 1,800 personnel.

While the Air Force is heavily constrained in how much it can pay its pilots, commercial operators are not. Senior widebody captains routinely earn $400,000 to over $520,000 annually. Top widebody captains' hourly rates at all three carriers are around $465 per hour. To some extent, the pay structure in the commercial sector is similar to that in the military.

Commercial pilots are paid based on seniority and years of service with the airline, not so much the type of aircraft they fly. They are also based on their rank, although in the commercial sector, this is limited to captains and first officers. The primary reason why widebody aircraft captains are paid more than narrowbody captains is that pilots bid to fly the widebody aircraft based on their seniority and years of service.

This is somewhat similar to how it is with the prestigious Air Force One fleet. Those pilots are typically paid more than their fighter jet, transport, and other pilot counterparts, but that is due mostly to their seniority. They can only fly the VC-25s as they are very senior pilots. At the rank of Colonel, most pilots in the United States Air Force do not routinely fly military aircraft on operational missions, although there are numerous exceptions.

The Big Three US-based airlines are Delta Air Lines, American Airlines, and United Airlines. Alaska Airlines now operates a limited fleet of Boeing 787 Dreamliners, and Hawaiian Airlines has a fleet of Airbus A330s. Southwest is one of the world's largest airlines, but it exclusively operates the Boeing 737 narrowbody. Of the big three, American has the smallest and simplest widebody fleet made up of 67 Boeing 777s and 70 Boeing 787s.

Delta Air Lines has the next largest widebody fleet, with 57 legacy Boeing 767s being phased down. It also has a fleet of 81 Airbus A330s and 41 Airbus A350s. Delta has placed an order for 30 Boeing 787-10s with options for 30 more, and it has 16 outstanding orders for the A330neo and 39 orders for the A350. American's current outstanding widebody order book is for 19 Boeing 787s.

United Airlines has the largest widebody fleet, and the second largest in the world after Emirates. It operates an all-Boeing fleet of 88 Boeing 787 Dreamliners, 96 Boeing 777s, and 53 aging 767s. United has recently canceled the delayed order for Airbus A350s, meaning its widebody fleet is to stay a Boeing fleet.

It should be noted that there are other considerations when comparing income between the military and commercial sectors, although these can be difficult to simply quantify. For example, pilots in the US Air Force are guaranteed a government-backed lifetime pension of at least 20 years. With American, Delta, and United, traditional pensions are largely gone for new hires, although they contribute around 18% of pay into 401(k)-type plans.

Under the Air Force's retirement system, a pilot can retire after 20 years of service—often in their late 30s or early 40s—and begin drawing an immediate lifetime pension while pursuing a second career. There is no direct equivalent in the commercial airline industry, where pilots generally continue flying until the FAA's mandatory retirement age of 65 while building retirement savings through employer-funded defined-contribution plans.

Green Thumb Industries ended its run of four consecutive profitable quarters.

One big edge that Green Thumb Industries (GTBIF +8.48%) had until recently was that it was one of the few marijuana retailers to turn a profit. On Nov. 4, however, the Chicago-based company reported a third-quarter loss per share of $0.02, the first time since 2025's Q2 that it has lost money.

That's not the end of the world, though. The key is that Green Thumb continues to grow sales. It reported Q3 revenue of $324.4 million, up 11.3%, year over year, showing growing demand for its consumer brand portfolio.

Here are three reasons to buy Green Thumb Industries.

Green Thumb maintains a cash-generative operating profile and balance-sheet discipline. Operational cash flow remains solid at $58 million in Q3, following $76 million in Q1 and $29 million in Q2, enabling management to aggressively return capital to shareholders.

Over the first nine months of 2026, Green Thumb repurchased 13.4 million shares for $77.7 million, reducing outstanding share count from 206.6 million down to 197.5 million, and recently authorized a new $50 million share buyback program.

The primary reason for the Q3 net loss is the company's continued expansion. It opened six RISE dispensaries in the quarter: Port Charlotte and Port St. Lucie in Florida; Sparks in Nevada; and Hanover, West Mifflin, and Allentown in Pennsylvania.

The company is positioned to capture future growth as several major key states transition toward adult-use legalization or expanded access. Green Thumb holds a vertically integrated license in Virginia, where recreational retail launches in July 2027, and a conditional license under Texas' Compassionate Use Program, and continues to expand retail density in states such as New Jersey, Ohio, and Pennsylvania. Together, these expansion markets provide clear multiyear revenue runways.

Green Thumb is No. 2 in quarterly revenue among the top U.S. cannabis retailers, behind Curaleaf, which recently reported $353 million in Q3. Compared to Curaleaf and Trulieve, Green Thumb is well priced, with a price-to-sales ratio of 1.3. It also has a lower debt-to-earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA) level than Curaleaf or Trulieve because it carries less debt.

In Q3, the company reported cash of $316 million and total debt of $277.3 million. Green Thumb is already benefiting from the rescheduling of cannabis to a Schedule III substance. It spent $28.1 million on income taxes in the quarter, nearly half the $49.1 million it spent in the same period a year ago.

Its operating cash flow was $58 million in the quarter, down from $74.1 million in the same quarter in 2025, but that's still superior to the $53 million Trulieve reported in Q2 and the $22 million that Curaleaf reported in the third quarter.

Here's the Average Woman's Social Security Benefit by Age

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Women's average Social Security benefit runs about $410 less monthly than men's, a gap rooted in earnings history and workforce participation.

Social Security benefits for women average less than those for men across every age group. This report lays out the specific dollar amounts as of December 2025, the most recent data available, to show the current size of that gap.

The average benefit among women is $1,872.43 per month, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA). That figure is $409.85 lower than the $2,282.28 average monthly benefit for men.

The table below shows the average monthly payments for retirees aged 62 to 70. These figures reflect the amounts as of December 2025 and illustrate the consistent difference in checks between men and women.

The SSA bases your benefit on an average of your wages across your highest-earning 35 years. It adjusts those earlier wages for inflation and runs the results through the primary insurance amount (PIA) formula. The persistent gap in benefit amounts between men and women reflects historical differences in lifetime earnings and fewer years spent in the paid workforce.

These payments are designed to replace only about 40% of your pre-retirement income, which is well short of what most retirees need to cover their costs.

Full retirement age (FRA) ranges from 66 to 67, depending on your birth year, and it's 67 for everyone born in 1960 or later. Workers who claim at 62 accept a reduction in their monthly check, while those who wait until 70 earn delayed retirement credits worth 8% per year.

While women receive lower average monthly checks, those benefits typically have to stretch over a longer period. A woman who is 65 today is expected to live about 21 more years, compared to about 18 more years for a man, according to the SSA. This means the average woman needs her retirement income to last roughly three years longer than the average man.

A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027 to help these checks keep pace with inflation. For a 70-year-old woman receiving the average $2,024.08 benefit, that adjustment adds about $73 per month to that figure. The dollar gap is widest at the oldest ages in the table, and spousal and survivor benefits are part of the picture for many women.

The above information focuses on benefits for retired workers, but many women claim spousal or survivor benefits based on their partner's work records instead. This could give them more money than they'd qualify for on their own.

Spousal benefits are the benefits you're eligible for on your partner's work record while they're still alive. They're worth up to one-half of the amount your partner qualifies for at their FRA. You will only be able to receive this amount once your spouse is claiming their retirement benefits.

Survivor benefits are the benefits you're eligible for on your partner's work record after they pass away. These are worth up to 100% of the amount your spouse was eligible for or receiving at the time of their death. You will only get this amount if it's larger than your own retirement benefit.

Contact the Social Security Administration to learn more about whether you qualify for these benefits and how much they could be worth to you.

On Saturday's numbers, minor parties would enter negotiations for a governing deal with leverage to extract deeper policy concessions from a weakened National or Labour.

The near-deadlock was partly produced because voters, apparently weary of New Zealand's economic woes and apathetic about both Luxon and Hipkins, chose to propel a new entrant group to Parliament for the first time in years. Opportunity, led by 38-year-old business consultant and political newcomer Qiulae Wong, is unaligned with either bloc and Wong has said she could work with either side to form a government.

Other winners were harder to identify and on Sunday, "who won the election 2026" was the top trending Google search in New Zealand. Yet his opponent Hipkins, who was turfed from office in 2023 largely over discontent about the same economic issues, didn't appear to benefit on Saturday. The left-wing bloc appeared to fall short of a majority but was still hopeful it could triumph — though that seemed a more remote prospect.

Electoral officials were due on Sunday to begin counting nearly 400,000 so-called special votes, including from New Zealanders who live abroad, a process expected to take three weeks.

Those ballots have historically granted an additional seat or two in Parliament to left-wing parties, which could snatch the current razor-thin majority from the right, although such an outcome would likely leave the left struggling to form a majority too. ‘On par with what the DoD pursued during the Cold War’: Pentagon’s war stockpile plan hits a minerals wall

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Pentagon wants to stockpile enough critical minerals, fuel, and munitions on American soil to keep fighting a major war even if cyberattacks and sabotage knock out factories and power grids at home. One defense expert says the scale of the planned effort is “on par with what the DoD pursued during the Cold War.”

The minerals may be the hardest part. The U.S. produces less than 1% of the most common type of rare-earth magnet it uses, and China, which dominates the processing of many critical minerals, has kept American companies short even in peacetime.

The initiative, known as FORTRESS America, short for Federal Operations for Resilience, Troop Resources, Energy, Supply, and Survivability, was formally established on Sept. 30. It is designed to keep U.S. forces operating if a conflict disrupts domestic power grids and manufacturing supply chains, and it is the same program behind the Pentagon’s push for major bases to plan for 90 days without the grid.

Stockpiling materials isn’t unusual for the U.S. military, Bryan Clark, a senior fellow at the Hudson Institute, told Fortune. What distinguishes FORTRESS is its planned scale and its shift toward domestic reserves rather than overseas stockpiles.

During the Cold War, the Pentagon stored as much or more war matériel overseas, near anticipated battlefields in Eastern Europe or Northeast Asia, Clark said. FORTRESS instead focuses on protecting supplies inside the United States, reflecting growing concern that American factories could themselves become targets.

China could disrupt U.S. industrial production through cyber operations, drones, electromagnetic warfare, or attacks carried out by people inside the country, Clark warned. U.S. agencies have already warned that Chinese state-sponsored hackers had positioned themselves inside American critical infrastructure.

That concern has a recent precedent in Europe. On Dec. 29, 2025, cyberattacks hit about 30 renewable energy sites, a manufacturing company, and a heat and power plant serving nearly 500,000 customers in Poland. Polish officials linked the attacks to Russia’s Federal Security Service and said the attackers tried to destroy industrial systems, but the country avoided a major outage.

Building stockpiles on the scale Clark described runs into a stark industrial reality: America’s defense industry remains deeply reliant on foreign supply chains for its raw materials.

In 2025, the U.S. imported about $60 billion worth of critical minerals other than rare earths, including nickel, lithium, cobalt, graphite, and tungsten, according to the Peterson Institute for International Economics.

The gap is most acute in rare-earth magnets. U.S. demand for the most common type reached roughly 48,000 metric tons in 2025, while domestic sources supplied just 300 metric tons, according to Arthur D. Little data reported by Reuters. U.S. companies are on track to have capacity for about 5,000 metric tons by the end of this year, roughly a tenth of last year’s demand.

The shortage has already reached factory floors. Ford, No. 22 on the 2026 Fortune 500, idled its Chicago Explorer plant for a week in May 2025 when rare-earth supplies ran short.

China has repeatedly tightened rare-earth export controls, and American companies are still struggling to secure enough supply, Reuters reported on Nov. 6, citing U.S. officials. That day, U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer pressed Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng to fully carry out Beijing’s commitments on rare earths and other critical minerals.

A major war could further disrupt overseas production and shipping routes, making it harder for the Pentagon to replenish its stockpiles.

To reduce foreign dependence, Clark said, the Pentagon is pursuing a dual approach: building raw-material reserves while funding domestic mining and processing capacity.

Tungsten, a stockpiling priority Clark identified, illustrates why building reserves takes more than government spending.

The U.S. hasn’t commercially mined tungsten since 2015, when cheaper imports undercut domestic production, according to Scientific American. Its net import reliance for the metal exceeded 50% in 2025, according to the U.S. Geological Survey.

In September, tungsten manufacturer The Elmet Group won a Defense Logistics Agency contract worth up to $2 billion to supply tungsten ores, concentrates, and sodium tungstate to the National Defense Stockpile. Only $150 million of that is guaranteed. The Pentagon also committed $450 million to Elmet, including about $150 million earmarked to restart the Springer tungsten complex in Nevada.

Yet Elmet said it won’t begin deliveries to the stockpile until additional supply becomes available. To avoid diverting existing tungsten from American manufacturers, the company plans to wait until new production comes online from investments in Nevada, Australia, and Spain.

The deal illustrates a problem FORTRESS could face across the board: signing supply contracts doesn’t immediately put minerals into government vaults.

The cost of FORTRESS also remains uncertain. A senior defense official told reporters on Oct. 20 that officials are still assessing costs, Air & Space Forces Magazine reported. The magazine estimated the broader initiative could cost “scores of billions of dollars, if not hundreds of billions.”

To help cover the bill, the Pentagon is exploring public-private partnerships, Dale Marks, assistant secretary of defense for energy, installations, and environment, told Breaking Defense.

Even with capital flowing, analysts warn that supply chain independence can’t be rushed.

“The Pentagon is unlikely to meet the goals in the near term,” Clark said, pointing to the structural difficulty of rapidly stockpiling both finished weapons and raw minerals.

Chris Berry, a minerals industry analyst, told Reuters it will “take many more years” to build the infrastructure needed to compete with China’s dominant supply chain.

The first test comes soon. Starting Jan. 1, 2027, federal rules require defense contractors to stop buying rare earths, magnets, and tungsten from China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea. Washington has repeatedly granted waivers because domestic supply falls short, Reuters reported in July.

For the Pentagon, building the physical reserves may prove far harder than announcing them.

If I Could Give My Younger Self 1 Piece of Investing Advice, This Is What It Would Be

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

There's no substitute for experience, but it still helps to know some important rules of thumb.

If I could give my younger self one piece of investing advice, I'd find most of the material I'd need by reviewing my first-ever investment. My first stock was Lucent Technologies, which in 1996 was spun off from AT&T, and then bought by me at age 10 with all $500 of the birthday money I had saved up from prior years. Shortly after my purchase in late 1999, it began a long decline that saw its price fall 99.2% by September 2002.

The sting of that collapse kept me away from investing for about 15 years. So here's the investing advice that I would give to my younger self if I had to do things over again -- it's one central theme, and it has three parts.

This advice seems obvious now, but new investors still need to learn it.

Funneling all your capital into one stock means one failure can wipe out most of your portfolio. Per research conducted at Arizona State University, about 4.3% of the nearly 26,000 U.S. stocks included in the analysis accounted for nearly all of the market's wealth creation above Treasury bills from 1926 to 2016. That's an argument for owning a large and diversified basket of stocks via an index fund like the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO +0.30%).

The lesson extends to the types of assets you invest in, too. The traditional 60/40 stocks/bonds portfolio split is timeless because holding bonds can make up for underperformance that stocks experience under certain conditions, and vice versa. Today, adding some cryptocurrency into your portfolio is another way to diversify even beyond that.

If you invest in a lot of different companies, some of those investments will not play out.

According to a 2014 J.P. Morgan study, 40% of Russell 3000 index stocks from 1980 to 2014 ended up experiencing a catastrophic loss, which in this case is defined as a 70% or larger drop from the peak with little recovery afterward. In the case of my first investment, in the telecom industry, the catastrophic loss rate was 51%.

The bigger point here is psychological in nature. Expecting every one of your stock picks to work out is not reasonable, but investing requires us to be rationally hopeful each time. The truth is that failure in investing is inevitable, and vastly more common than success. Quitting after the first big failure, as I did, cost me years of compounding, and not having the proper context to process the defeat in a constructive way was especially deleterious.

So when an investment does poorly, diagnose whether the cause was bad luck, incomplete or poor analysis, or something else, and then fix that and move on to the next opportunity. And then if a different investment fails, repeat the process, again and again, until your results improve. Reading about past stock market crashes can also show you how much pain is normal.

I originally picked Lucent for my first investment because it was among the first companies I knew of in general -- at age 10, I wasn't a sophisticated investor, to say the least! Its original identity before the spinoff from AT&T was the legendary Bell Labs, one of the greatest research and development (R&D) organizations of all time, which even as a child I knew enough about to admire.

Still, I did not really have a firm understanding of why Lucent's stock was going to be worth more (or, in this case, less) in the future. I didn't really have an investment thesis. And while it's certainly a tall order to develop that understanding, especially as a new investor, it's the core of the art of investing in stocks, so my advice to my younger self would be to start grappling with these issues to the extent that it would be possible to do so at that point.

Before buying a stock, I'd tell my younger self to write two sentences: one on what the company sells, to whom, and why they buy it, and one on why its profits should be bigger in five years.

Even at age 10, I think I could've worked out those questions. And while that probably still wouldn't have actually saved me from investing in Lucent, I'm sure that I'd approach the questions with a bit more sophistication in the next investment. And improving your process over the long run is the heart of the battle.

Hilton Is Growing Faster Than Marriott, Hyatt And IHG

Hilton opened 182 hotels and 27,000 rooms in the Q3 2026, which works out to 23,000 net additional rooms and net unit growth of 6% from a year prior, according to its earnings release. Net income was $536MM, up from $421MM, and adjusted EBITDA was $1.058bn. Management and franchise fee revenue rose 9.5% which is the company’s core business model.

Comparing that to the other three big loyalty hotel companies, Marriott added roughly 20,000 net rooms and grew 4.6%. IHG reported net system growth of 4.9% after opening 12,900 rooms across 92 hotels. Hyatt posted net rooms growth of 3.8% over the trailing 12 months. The companies define growth a little differently, but Hilton leads on every version I can find, and it did so while adding more net rooms in a quarter than Marriott, the larger of the two companies.

More rooms does not necessarily mean higher earnings. Hyatt’s comparable system-wide RevPAR rose 7.2%, Marriott’s rose 6.5% worldwide and 7.4% in the US and Canada, while Hilton’s rose 5.8%, and IHG’s rose just 5.3%. Hilton ranks third of four on that list which starts to eat into its net rooms added lead. Hyatt’s caveat is its all-inclusive business, where Net Package RevPAR fell 2.5%, which matters because every big hotel brand wants your all-inclusive money right now. This, for me, probably deserves its own post if the drops continue into the fourth quarter compared with YoY.

Net profit confirms the rate growth relative to both peers and net rooms is more tied to RevPAR than absolute growth. Marriott earned $794MM in the quarter and $1.52bn in adjusted EBITDA, well ahead of Hilton. Hyatt’s net income was $58MM, or $122MM adjusted, which is considerably less but HYatt is far smaller (and underperforms.) I wrote last week that Royal Caribbean out-earned Hilton’s whole 2025 in a quarter, and the hotel sector is a reminder that growth and earnings are different races.

Marriott’s development pipeline hit a record of more than 634,000 rooms across over 4,200 properties, with 44% of those rooms under construction. Hilton’s pipeline is 543,100 rooms across 3,869 hotels in 135 countries, with nearly half under construction and more than half outside the United States. IHG’s pipeline is 353,000 rooms, up 3.2% and equal to 33% of its current system. Hyatt’s is about 158,000 rooms, up 12.1%.

Marriott has the biggest pipeline in absolute terms, Hyatt’s is growing fastest of the three that disclosed growth, and Hilton grew 5%. Conversions matter here too. Marriott said conversions (existing independent hotels or those being reflagged) made up over a third of signings and over 40% of openings in the first nine months, and IHG says development splits roughly evenly between new builds and conversions. Hilton opened its first Select by Hilton hotels this quarter, one of several newer brands it is leaning on to fill that pipeline.

As someone whose agency sells hotel rooms, both of these numbers are important but for different reasons. My clients and I prefer to see the rate creep slow or stop (rate regression might be a dream) with more stays. But more properties to choose from is good news too and increases competition.

For travelers, the bigger question is what all this growth does to loyalty programs especially when comparing Hilton vs Marriott. Marriott Bonvoy and Hilton Honors each span well over a million rooms, and their luxury brands include Waldorf Astoria, Conrad, St. Regis, Ritz-Carlton and JW Marriott. But with so many of the new rooms being conversions and limited-service hotels where elite status buys less, that can reduce the shine of the programs. Lounge access and complimentary breakfast (a daily food and beverage credit at most U.S. Hilton hotels) are what business travelers chase but lounges are almost gone completely, and those perks are thinner at a select-service property. Hilton offers elites a fifth night free on standard reward stays and waives resort fees on free nights, and Hilton points pile up quickly through its brand credit cards with American Express. Long term, more hotels should mean more places to redeem, not just more places to earn. But the question also becomes how important those advantages are, and with both Bonvoy’s accelerated status earning, and Hilton’s Diamond via credit card option, the true value of those benefits continue to diminish.

Hilton returned $879MM to shareholders in the quarter, $2.9bn so far this year, and projects about $3.5 billion for 2026. It also moved its stock listing to Nasdaq on November 5, 2026 under the same HLT ticker. IHG has completed $707MM of a $950MM buyback and expects to return over $1.2bn in 2026. Hyatt projects $375MM to $425MM in capital returns. Hotels are on a tear over the last couple of years and Hilton’s growth in room count demonstrates confidence in the market for the future.

Hilton is winning the room race and not the rate race, and Marriott is winning the profit race. I expect Hilton to hold the unit growth lead through 2027, but Marriott’s larger pipeline will make that lead uncomfortable. Hyatt will continue winning on rate while sitting out the growth sprint barring another acquisition which it has been drawn to as of late, which is exactly how a luxury-leaning brand should behave. Still, it’s impressive to see Hilton with such strong numbers for growing the brand.

Here's Why I'm Still Investing in the S&P 500, Even if a Bear Market Is Coming

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Buying before a market decline feels awful. But waiting indefinitely carries its own risks.

The thought is inescapable: If I invest today, what if the market drops tomorrow? It's a question that can give everyone pause. After all, no one wants to see their newly invested money lose its value.

Yet the alternative creates an even more dangerous trap: Once you start waiting, what could possibly convince you it is ever safe to buy? That's why, when dealing with money you won't need for many years, it's better to build a plan based on uncertainty than to keep waiting for the perfect entry point.

War. Economic turmoil. Apocalyptic forecasts. The reality is that these things come and go. Sometimes the market falls as a result. Sometimes it doesn't. In reality, an informed person can always find what may seem like a reasonable excuse to postpone investing.

Worse, this anxiety often shows up no matter which way the market moves: Rising prices can feel expensive; falling prices feel like a warning sign. Nevertheless, studies have made it clear: Those who put money to work in the stock market have consistently outperformed those who have tried to time the market or remained in cash.

One way to clear the psychological hurdle of investing in a falling stock market is to recognize that lower prices can offer benefits. First, they mean you can buy more shares.

For example, imagine an investor who puts $200 to work in the stock market each month, buying an index exchange-traded fund (ETF) valued at $100 a share. That results in two shares per month. However, if the index ETF falls to $80 a share, the same $200 investment buys 2.5 shares per month.

Granted, the earlier-purchased shares have lost value, and buying more doesn't erase that loss. However, for those buying with the intention of holding for decades, the lower price can help them accumulate many more shares.

In the long run, that will result in more assets, which will in turn generate added returns. In other words, it can help power the compounding mechanism that drives buy-and-hold investing.

This is the essence of dollar-cost averaging. By regularly setting aside money for investment, a person can remove the emotional guesswork of investing and, if the market declines, lower the average price paid.

Another reason some people fear investing into a downturn is that they are putting the wrong money to work in the wrong way. That is, investment money should be reserved for long-term goals that won't be interrupted by short-term challenges. Emergencies, bills, and near-term purchases should come from a separate pool of money.

That way, investment money can fluctuate without affecting a person's ability to make ends meet. Moreover, by having a separate cash reserve, a person won't be tempted to sell investments during a downturn to raise cash.

Lastly, it's important to establish the right investing routine. If $200 per month is too much, lower the amount; don't give up on the routine. If finding the right stocks seems overwhelming, start with an index fund that tracks the S&P 500 -- many are incredibly cheap, and buying shares has never been easier. Also, an S&P 500 index fund is a great way for investors to stay diversified, because the index itself spreads money across many large American stocks.

Is a bear market coming? Perhaps. Does that make me nervous about investing? No. That's not to say that a bear market drop wouldn't hurt. It's just that long-term investing requires the fortitude to ride out dips, corrections, and even bear markets.

The best investors are often the ones who can see downturns for what they are: opportunities for long-term investors to accumulate more shares at a lower price.

3 Millionaire-Maker Quantum Computing Stocks

The quantum computing market is full of exciting investments, but not all of them will pan out.

Quantum computing is an emerging industry that could have several huge winners in it. There are countless early-stage quantum computing stocks that I think could provide massive returns, as this technology is set to provide massive economic value once viability is reached. While that may seem like a far-fetched technology, the reality is many in the industry believe 2029 could be the year when viable quantum computing shows up.

That's right around the corner, so what stocks have huge potential? Let's take a look.

IonQ (IONQ +23.42%) is one of the first stocks investors think of when they hear about quantum computing. It was the first quantum computing stock to go public, and it has performed exceptionally well as a company, even if the stock is currently well off its all-time highs.

IonQ currently has one of the most accurate quantum computing technologies, with a 99.99% two-qubit gate fidelity score. IonQ has achieved this thanks to its trapped-ion approach to quantum computing, which yields excellent accuracy but at the cost of processing speed.

Next year, IonQ has plans to release its 256-qubit system, which could drive huge growth for the company as clients adopt early-stage quantum computing technology. That could be the event that is needed to turn IonQ's stock around and could kick-start huge returns.

Quantinuum (QNT +25.99%) is one of the newer quantum computing stocks on the public market. However, it's quickly emerging as a top option. Quantinuum just published results of 99.9995% logical fidelity, which isn't the same measure as IonQ's, but is still a very impressive result.

Quantinuum also uses trapped-ion technology like IonQ, which gives it an advantage in the accuracy measure of quantum computing. It believes it can achieve fault-tolerant quantum computing by 2029, and if it could do that, it would achieve impressive shareholder returns along the way.

Rigetti Computing (RGTI +19.29%) is taking a different approach than these two. It's focusing on superconducting computing, which hasn't yielded the greatest accuracy results. However, superconducting computing can operate at far greater speeds than trapped-ion computing, which will be a major advantage if Rigetti can get its accuracy where it needs to be.

Rigetti's two-qubit fidelity for its largest 108-qubit system is 99.1% (comparable to IonQ's figure) or 99.9% for a single qubit (comparable to Quantinuum's figure). As Rigetti's qubit size decreases, its accuracy goes up, but those systems are so small that doing anything useful on them isn't really viable.

Rigetti's goal is to produce a 1,000-qubit system with 99.9% two-qubit gate fidelity over the next three years, but that will be significantly smaller and less accurate than anything IonQ or Quantinuum is producing. However, because of Rigetti's speed advantage, it may be a longer-term quantum computing play that could make a comeback over the next few years.

We'll see how it pans out, but it's still a viable investment option.

One thing investors need to keep in mind with this trio is that there is no guarantee any of them will reach profitability. These are very high-risk stocks that could provide a high reward, but there's no guarantee either. There are countless impressive competitors in this space, many of whom have massive cash flows to fund quantum computing operations. These three are all upstarts and must continue to get funding from outside sources until they have a commercially viable product and business in place.

If they can make it to that stage, then they will be huge winners and a successful investment. But the reality is there will be a lot of failure in this industry too. Investors need to choose the best of the best, and these three represent that.

Tesla Stock Hasn't Closed at a Record in Nearly 11 Months. Here's How Its Past Record Droughts Ended.

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The EV maker has been here six times before. What did shareholders get once each wait ended?

Tesla (TSLA +4.23%) last closed at a record on Dec. 16, 2025, at $489.88 a share. That was 327 days ago, or nearly 11 months. With Tesla shares trading near $346 as I write this, the stock would have to gain about 41% just to tie it.

Long gaps between records are nothing new for the EV maker, though. Since its IPO in June 2010, Tesla stock has gone 300 days or more without a record close six other times, based on daily split-adjusted closing prices.

Every one of those droughts eventually ended. But what happened next was very different. Two of them gave way to rallies of over 600%. The other four were followed by a new drought within months, and twice within days.

And the difference between the two groups probably boils down to whether Tesla's profits were turning higher.

The first two droughts came early in the electric-vehicle maker's life as a public company. The stock spent 474 days without a record from Nov. 24, 2010, to March 12, 2012, and then exactly 300 days from April 3, 2012, to Jan. 28, 2013. The next two lasted longer, at 942 days from Sept. 4, 2014, to April 3, 2017, and 821 days from Sept. 18, 2017, to Dec. 18, 2019. Most recently, shares went 1,133 days from Nov. 4, 2021, to Dec. 11, 2024, and then 364 days from Dec. 17, 2024, to last December's record.

That makes the current 327-day wait the second shortest of the seven. The middle of the six past droughts is around 650 days, or almost twice as long as this one has lasted.

Of course, the drops along the way were steep. In the 2021-to-2024 drought, the stock's lowest close was around 74% below its prior record.

And in every one of the six, shares closed at least 31% under the old high at some point. This time, the low so far was $298.32 on July 29, around 39% below the December record.

Two of them ended in huge runs. Once the 300-day gap closed on Jan. 28, 2013, Tesla stock kept setting records until Sept. 4, 2014, rising around 652% in that time.

And once the drought ended on Dec. 18, 2019, shares climbed about 1,464% to the November 2021 record that started the longest wait of all.

The other four runs were short. The March 2012 record led to just 22 days of new highs before the next drought began. The April 2017 record brought around five and a half months of records and a 29% climb.

The December 2024 run of records lasted six days. Last December's record ended one drought and began the current one the same day.

In other words, a new record on its own hasn't said much about what happens next. For shareholders, I'd say this matters more than the length of any drought.

What the two big runs share is a turn in profits.

In May 2013, Tesla said it had become profitable in the first quarter "for the first time in our ten year history," earning $11 million. The stock gained about 24% the day after the letter came out.

The 2019 run followed a similar swing. The company made $143 million in the third quarter of 2019. That was a sharp turn from losses of $702 million and $408 million earlier that year. Even more, its net income hit $721 million in 2020 -- after losing money in each of the four years before that.

But the four short runs had nothing like that behind them. The company lost $396 million in 2012 and almost $2 billion in 2017.

And its operating income (profit from the business before interest and taxes) was falling in both 2024 and 2025, down 20% and then another 38%.

That trend hasn't turned yet. In the first nine months of 2026, Tesla's operating income totaled about $2.1 billion, down about 30% from the same period in 2025.

What does this history suggest for anyone owning the stock now?

I don't think 11 months without a new high is a reason to sell on its own. After all, waits this long have been normal for the growth stock, and every one of them has ended.

But the next record might matter less than what's behind it. One that comes with operating income climbing again may look a lot more like 2013 or 2019. Without that, a new high might fade as fast as the last two did.

That's great news for shareholders, but can Novo maintain this momentum?

At first glance, Novo's third-quarter results didn’t seem particularly impressive, as some of the company’s longtime growth drivers underperformed. For instance, sales of Ozempic, the company's famous diabetes medicine, were 29.7 billion Danish kroner (about $4.5 billion), down 3% compared to the year-ago period. The company's weight-loss medicine, Wegovy, recorded sales of 20.4 billion DKK (about $3.1 billion), roughly flat compared with the prior-year quarter.

However, several other medicines helped pick up the slack, and arguably none was more important than Novo's oral Wegovy. The company launched this weight loss pill -- the first of its kind to earn approval -- in January. The Wegovy pill has helped attract many patients who were hesitant to start treatment with injectables. Novo reported that, as of June, oral Wegovy had surpassed three million prescriptions, and more than 80% were for patients who had never taken GLP-1s before. In the third quarter, oral Wegovy generated 5.4 billion DKK (about $810 million) in revenue.

Novo's total net sales were 79.2 billion DKK (about $11.9 billion), up 6% year over year. The company's net income dropped by 16% year over year to 16.7 billion DKK (about $2.5 billion), largely due to non-cash impairment charges. Still, Novo’s results beat analyst estimates, and the pharmaceutical company also raised its guidance for the fiscal year 2026.

It now expects revenue to be flat (at the midpoint) year over year, while the previous guidance implied a 3% year-over-year drop at the midpoint. It's no wonder Novo's shares jumped following this beat-and-raise quarter. But what's next for the drugmaker?

There are several catalysts ahead that could help Novo improve its financial performance. Let's consider three of them. First, the company could make meaningful progress with oral Wegovy. The medicine has now been approved in several other countries, including Germany and the U.K.

These markets might not be as lucrative as the U.S., but they should move the needle for the company. Novo will also likely attract brand-new GLP-1 patients in these regions. The company is also looking to earn label expansions for oral Wegovy. Novo has submitted an application to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for oral Wegovy as a potential treatment for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis.

The original injectable version of Wegovy is approved for this indication, but the pill offers a more convenient option. It could be successful in this niche and continue driving Novo's sales higher. Second, Novo will launch new products to strengthen its position in the diabetes and weight-loss markets.

It is inching closer to approval for CagriSema, a medicine that beat Wegovy in a head-to-head Phase 3 clinical trial for weight loss. CagriSema also posted strong clinical trial results in diabetes. This medicine could help Novo steal some market share from its biggest competitor, Eli Lilly (LLY -0.79%).

Third, Novo has several pipeline candidates that could improve its approved lineup over time. The company's Phase 3 pipeline features zenagamtide, a promising diabetes and weight-loss medicine that is being developed in subcutaneous and oral formulations.

The weight loss market is growing rapidly, and this product, along with others, may help Novo ride that tailwind for a long time, even as competition intensifies. Novo may still experience some clinical setbacks, but after the beating it took over the past two years, the company is slowly staging a comeback. In my view, the stock is attractive following its third-quarter results.

Over Pinchot Pass — John Muir Trail Day 10

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The night before, at Rae Lake, I ate some kind of buffalo stew, which was supposed to provide about 900 calories.  It is funny how flavor (usually) takes a backseat to caloric value on the trail.  I am just so conscious that I am exerting myself — a lot — and need to maximize my fuel intake.

After dinner I had my headlamp on in the tent, probably dictating my notes, and was not aware that Evelyn (or Serena?) was taking pictures of my tent in the darkness under a starry sky.

Night camping at Rae Lakes (photo courtesy of Evelyn or Serena)

My sleep started deep and sound.  Then I did wake up, coughing a bit, and I thought, “Oh, no!”  But it was still much less than before, and I was very tired, so I slept again.  I vaguely remember dreaming about how some people were organizing something-or-other and I was confused how it was going to work.  Maybe it meant I was wondering how to manage this journey?

Around 4:40 in the morning, when I got up to pee, I checked the Garmin and saw the time.  I thought, “Well, I think I’m not going to go back to sleep.”  So I didn’t; instead I fetched my food from the bear box, and ate my breakfast.  But I felt sluggish, like I was swimming through molasses.

How sluggish?  Well, I rose at 4:40 but I did not get on the trail until 7:00 a.m.  Despite everything, it was only thirty minutes later than the previous day, but it was far from efficient.  I said goodbye to Evelyn and Serena; they were heading southbound, and I was heading northbound.  I left the crowded campsite and started on my way.

One consequence of the Rae Lake crowds is that it was hard to find a private place to dig a cathole.  I decided to wait for a better place.  About a half hour down the trail, I pulled off and found a spot that was quite lovely and dug my hole and did my business.  In the middle of it, a deer appeared in the meadow next to me and gazed at me curiously.

Cat hole in the foreground, amazing view in the background!

Less than a half mile further, I discovered a beautiful camping site with a bear box and no crowds at all.  I thought, “Oh my gosh, if I’d just known and been willing to take the risk to go a little further yesterday, it would have been a much nicer situation.”  It was too late for me, but I resolved to tell any southbound hikers I encountered that they would be more comfortable stopping at the last camp site before the main Rae Lake location.

More beautiful and fewer crowds — the place to be!

As I looked at the long day ahead of me, I realized it would be 6-7 miles, all downhill, followed by 8-9 miles all uphill, followed by a mile or two of downhill to my next camp site at Lake Marjorie.  In elevation I started at 10,500 feet, would drop to 8,500 feet, and then would cross Pinchot Pass at 12,200 feet.  I planned for a long day.

I did about four miles on that nice downhill to start the day when I encountered a small stream I had to cross.  In general, these stream crossings had not been too challenging, frankly.  I brought my water shoes explicitly because I thought I would wade through river crossings, perhaps as much a thigh-deep, and there hadn’t been any like that – at least so far.

This one was no different, a two-stone hop across with a couple of different options as to route.  That, it turned out, was the problem.  As I prepared to take my second step I decided to go via a different sequence of stones, and I turned slightly to change direction.  It was a slight turn, nothing notable about it at all.  Except… Suddenly I felt a sharp pain in my left knee.

I let out a yowl and finished crossing.  But I was worried.  Clearly, I had injured something. The only question was, what was it and how badly was it damaged?

I was still able to move it, so that was a good first sign.  Working it gently, it barked at me in certain angles; but just going straight ahead, it seemed not to bother me too much.

I decided to take an Ibuprofen at the bottom of the hill to prevent swelling from whatever it was that had happened.  Five minutes later I changed my mind, thinking, “Why am I waiting  to take the medication?  I just sustained the injury.  I should take the ibuprofen immediately, right NOW.”  And I did.

I continued down gingerly, favoring my knee a bit, worried that this might be something serious, and not knowing whether it would be or not.

My main distraction was lizards.  Yes!  I had observed no reptiles at all above 10,000 feet, but coming lower they started to appear.  I think they were most likely Western Fence Lizards, but they could also have been Sagebrush Lizards.  Because of my focus on my knee, I did not photograph them, but I resolved to do so the next time they appeared.

Finally, I reached the bottom of the downhill.  There was a suspension bridge to cross there, and it was one of the bouncier ones I had ever been on.  With a GoPro I would gladly have filmed myself crossing it, but with my hand-held phone it was too easy to imagine it flying out of my hand into the rushing waters below.

From solid ground I took a couple of pictures of the bridge anyway.

Then I crossed to the other side and began the big uphill.  I was facing around 3,600 feet of elevation gain, while worrying about my stupid knee.  I also worried that the trail might feel kind of endless, and it did feel kind of endless.

Along the way I started leapfrogging another hiker named Amy.  Our hiking styles almost made it inevitable.  I would go fast but then stop and rest; she kept a steady pace.  So, I would go by her moving faster, and she would move by me when I stopped.  Then I would resume and pass her again, and the cycle would continue.  At one point, when I was ahead, I saw a grouse.  I wondered if it would still be there when she came by.

The good news in all of this was that either the Ibuprofen was working, or my knee was not badly injured, or both.

Amy and I had both set Lake Marjorie, on the other side of Pinchot Pass, as our destination for the night.  This was the first camping space about a mile and a half below the summit.

We kept ascending.  The one advantage was that the ascent took place over many miles, so it was rarely going up very steeply.  But over the course of the afternoon, it was feeling quite relentless.  Only the series of waterfalls along the way kept me engaged and appreciative.

…another waterfall falling in perpetuity!

Approaching Pinchot Pass I ran into a guy coming downhill toward me.  We chatted and I learned that, like me, he was 69 years old.  He seemed very demoralized.  He said he felt like he was shuffling, like he could barely even do it.  He said he was planning to get off at Kearsarge Pass and probably would not return.  I said, “Well, what you should do is get off at Kearsarge and go to Independence and then spend a day there doing nothing.  After a day off trail, your mind will be clearer, and then you can decide if that’s the end for you, or if you do actually want to come back.”  That was my recommendation; I don’t know if he took it.

Nearing the top, there were many rocks patterned with white striations.  I wanted to turn to Sam and ask the geologist to explain what had caused them.  But of course, he was back in Boulder by then.

You can see the white stripes in the foreground rocks, but also in the background cliff face. Quartz maybe? Sam could have told me…

The end of the trail up to the pass was a series of switchbacks.  You couldn’t really see where they were leading until, suddenly, ta-da!, you had reached the saddle of the pass.

It was a really, really hard day.  I started at seven a.m. and I got to the pass at exactly five p.m.  If you do the math, that’s ten hours.  And I still had the mile and a half to go down to the lake.

The views over the lakes as the sun was setting were beautiful.  Amy finally caught up to me again, at a stream where I stopped to top off my water supply before making camp.  That late in the afternoon, we were both worried that the camping spots might already be filled up.  But when I saw a truly beautiful location, on nice flat sandy ground, I said, “That’s the one for me!”  Amy kept going to the next spot further down the trail, but I called it a day.

Arriving at Rae Lake the previous day, I was so tired it was difficult to function.  On this occasion, at Lake Marjorie, I set up calmly and reasonably efficiently, and it just felt much more natural than it had up to that point.  I pitched my tent, soaked my food, and set up my sleeping system, of course.  But I also went down to the lake shore where I washed my socks and washed my feet.  While I had my feet in the lake’s icy cold water, I thought, “Oh, I need to ice my knee too.”  So, I went a little deeper into the lake and got my knee in the icy cold water as well.

Unfortunately, my dinner was disgusting.  Some of these freeze-dried meals work great for a cold-soaker, but some really need boiling water to be edible.  I just wish I had kept track of which was which for future hikes!  Dinner was the low point of the day (other than my knee tweak), but I ate it for the calories.

Early Evening by Lake Marjorie. Notice my tent to the right of the boulder and — at that point — nothing to the left of the boulder.

Sitting in my tent pitched next to a large boulder it was getting colder-and-colder at our high elevation.  It was definitely a night for my sleeping bag liner!  In deep twilight I heard a large animal moving around on the other side of the big boulder.  Initially I was quite spooked, but when I checked I saw that a late-arriving hiker was setting up camp there.

For a moment I was annoyed that my delicious privacy had been disturbed.  Then I overcame that feeling and went over to welcome the newcomer and see if they needed anything.  It was a solo hiker named Sally, born in China but now an Australian citizen, also NOBO on the John Muir Trail.  She was friendly and we chatted for a few moments before I returned to my tent to call it a night.

It had been a 17-mile day, my longest yet on the trail.  Sleep came easily.

Modern life is changing the DNA of bacteria in our guts

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Gut bacteria can acquire genes from neighboring microbes, adding to the genetic differences linked to how people live.

Diet, antibiotics, and indoor plumbing help shape which bacteria settle into a person’s gut. That much has been clear for years.

What researchers couldn’t say is whether the bacteria themselves have been evolving in response.

A study of nearly 6,000 gut bacterial genomes finds that they have.

Across the species examined, strains from industrialized communities carried more genes than those from non-industrialized communities.

Most of the extra genes arrived recently. The bacteria didn’t inherit them from a parent cell. They took them from other microbes living alongside them.

So modern life hasn’t only changed which bacteria live in the gut. The same species now carries a different set of genes depending on how its host lives.

Researchers at Kiel University’s Institute of Clinical Molecular Biology and MIT’s Department of Biological Engineering ran the work, with the evolutionary modeling done in Japan.

Everything here was measured in bacterial genomes, so none of it shows that a bigger bacterial genome makes a person healthier or sicker.

All of the genomes come from the Global Microbiome Conservancy, a non-profit effort to bank living strains from communities that microbiome research has mostly passed over.

For this study, 20 volunteers in Cameroon, Ghana, Malaysia, Nigeria and Rwanda gave fresh stool samples. None had taken antibiotics recently.

Scientists processed each sample on site within three hours. The samples were then frozen in liquid nitrogen at minus 321°F (minus 196°C).

In the lab, the material went onto plates inside an oxygen-free chamber. Colonies grew at 99°F (37°C) for one to two weeks. Single colonies were then picked and sequenced.

The cultures yielded 1,841 new bacterial genomes. Added to 4,140 strains already banked, the collection reached 5,981 genomes. They came from 56 donors in nine countries.

Researchers classified donors as industrialized or non-industrialized using a development score for where they lived, compared with their country’s median.

From that collection, the team picked ten species sampled well on both sides of the divide, with at least eight strains from each side. Most are Bacteroides and close relatives, the workhorses of the lower gut.

E. coli was left out on purpose. Its genetic diversity has already been mapped in detail.

In all ten species, strains from industrialized guts carried more protein-coding genes. The difference was statistically significant in eight of the ten species.

Core genes, the ones every strain of a species has, showed no difference. The extra material was all optional genes, the kind one strain carries and the next one doesn’t.

The optional sets also varied more from strain to strain in industrialized hosts, again in all ten species. The team then widened the comparison to 34 species and saw the same pattern.

A bigger genome could mean two different things. Either these strains picked up genes lately, or strains that already had big genomes happened to do well in modern guts.

Separating the two fell to a group in Okinawa, Japan. They compared the family tree of a single gene with the family tree of the species carrying it.

A mismatch between the two marks the spot where a gene was gained, lost or handed over.

Gene gains clustered near the tips of the evolutionary trees, indicating that they occurred relatively recently.

They also piled up on the branches belonging to industrialized hosts.

Between 80% and 90% of those gains were transfers from other microbes. Only a small share came down from a parent cell.

Many arrived packaged inside plasmids and viruses that infect bacteria.

Both deliver several genes at once. Swapping of this kind goes on well beyond the gut, in soil, seawater and thawing permafrost.

Gergely Szöllősi heads the Model-Based Evolutionary Genomics Unit at the Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology.

His group built these tools for a much older problem, and Lénárd Szánthó, a senior bioinformatician in the unit, adapted them to a far shorter stretch of time.

“These methods were built to look back hundreds of millions and billions of years, so it was not obvious that they would tell us anything about something this recent,” said Szöllősi.

“Lénárd made them work for this problem, and the picture that comes out is that bacteria in industrialized hosts have been acquiring genes from their neighbors recently, and this is what makes their genomes larger.”

About 5% of the gene families in a typical species collection tracked with the host’s way of life.

The team checked that against bacterial DNA read straight from the stool of 1,015 people, and 83% of the signals pointed the same way.

A few gene families turned up in more than one species, which is harder to put down to chance.

Two of them help build the outer coat of the cell, the part that other microbes and the immune system meet.

Another repeat hit was an enzyme that breaks down penicillin-type drugs. Genes like that ride the same mobile DNA now spreading antibiotic resistance through cities and farms.

The team also counted genes where protein-changing mutations had built up faster than mutations that did not change the protein. That pattern is the usual sign of a change being favored.

About 1.4% of the genes examined qualified. One of them helps a cell hold its outer envelope together under stress, and it carried the signature in three separate species.

In the five species with the most genomes, the researchers tracked single-letter DNA differences in core genes.

The variants tied to industrialized hosts kept landing on the same few jobs: building the cell wall, handling stress and making vitamin B12.

A companion study from the same group found more vitamin B12 production in the gut communities of people more exposed to industrialized environments.

Another cluster of changes hit the genes that build a molecule on the bacterial surface. The human immune system reads that molecule and reacts to it.

Researchers have not tested whether any of this changes how a person’s immune system behaves. The authors wrote that it is a possibility worth following up.

“I hope our results will inspire further studies to use this knowledge in supporting us to live a healthier life in our changing environment,” Szánthó said.

The analysis can’t say which part of industrialized living is responsible. Diet, medicine, sanitation and exposure to outdoor microbes all travel together, and this data can’t pull them apart.

Bigger genomes may not be an adaptation at all.

Extra genes build up on their own when the pressure to discard useless DNA eases, and the authors couldn’t rule that out.

Ten species is a thin slice of a gut that holds hundreds, and most of the ten belong to one bacterial group.

Species that have never been grown in a lab are missing here, which matters because industrialized guts hold fewer bacterial species to begin with.

The study itself doesn’t test what those extra genes actually do inside a person. Getting that answer will take experiments rather than more genomes.

The authors call for controlled experiments with bacteria, laboratory studies of evolution, and more detailed measurements of people’s living conditions.

The full study was published in the journal Cell.

JetBlue launched Mint on June 15, 2014, on the route between New York John F. Kennedy International Airport (JFK) and Los Angeles International Airport (LAX). The transcontinental market was the right place to start because American, Delta, and United charged premium fares for recliner seats on the same city pair. JetBlue undercut them with flat beds and a markedly better onboard product, and the competitive response reshaped premium transcon flying across all four carriers.

The current product on the A321neo is a considerable step beyond what launched in 2014. The aircraft carries 16 Mint seats, comprising 14 Mint Suites and two Mint Studios, arranged 1-1, so every passenger has direct aisle access. JetBlue states that every Mint seat on its A321neo with Mint is a private suite with a door, and that all of them convert to a fully lie-flat bed with Tuft & Needle adaptive foam cushions and a T&N-designed pillow and comforter.

Here's What the Average Social Security Benefit Actually Covers

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The average Social Security check covers only a fraction of what seniors actually spend each year.

Most retirees lean on Social Security for much of their income, yet the average check was built to replace only part of a paycheck. As of September 2026, the average retired-worker benefit is $2,088.79 per month, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA) Monthly Statistical Snapshot. That adds up to $25,065.48 per year.

A household headed by someone 65 or older spends $61,432 per year, based on the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey from 2024, the most recent data available. While housing and healthcare are major concerns, transportation and food costs also represent significant portions of a typical senior's budget.

The average annual benefit of $25,065.48 covers only 41% of that annual senior-household spending. This is by design, as the government built the program to replace roughly 40% of pre-retirement income. It's not intended to fund a complete lifestyle on its own.

The SSA calculates your benefit based on your highest 35 years of earnings, where earlier wages are adjusted for inflation, through the primary insurance amount (PIA) formula. This leaves an annual shortfall of $36,366.52 that you'll need to cover through other sources.

For retirees without a pension or deep savings, part-time work may be the only realistic way to fill that hole. Inflation adjustments provide some relief, but they're meant to maintain current buying power rather than increase it.

A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027, adding about $75 a month to that average check. This adjustment is designed to help your benefits keep pace with rising prices. The average check is a foundation, not a full budget, and the question is how to cover the rest.

It's possible to beat the average Social Security check by delaying your Social Security application. Every month you wait to apply increases your monthly benefits until you qualify for your largest checks at 70.

If you expect to have an average or above-average life expectancy and you can afford to cover your living costs another way in the meantime, delaying your Social Security application is a viable way to grow your checks. It could also lead to a much larger lifetime benefit.

Things like remaining in the workforce for longer or increasing your income today could also lead to larger Social Security checks in the future because the SSA bases your benefit on your average monthly income over your 35 highest-earning years. If you're earning more now than in years past, working a bit longer could boost your future benefits.

But even in the best-case scenario, your checks likely won't be enough to cover all your living expenses in retirement. Make sure you have a plan for how you'll cover what Social Security doesn't before you sign up.

Replacing even a modest amount of dairy with plant-based alternatives could significantly reduce essential nutrient intake, according to a new study from the University of Surrey. Researchers found that substituting just 20% to 35% of dairy products increased the estimated prevalence of inadequate iodine intake among adolescent girls from 54% to 65%. Among women ages 19 to 49, the figure rose from 32% to 42%.

The study also identified reductions in calcium, riboflavin, and vitamin B12 intake. However, researchers found that fortifying plant-based alternatives and other foods could help prevent these nutritional shortfalls.

In the first UK study of its kind, researchers from the University of Surrey and Queen’s University Belfast analyzed dietary data from more than 3,500 people, ages 18 months to 100 years. Using the National Diet and Nutrition Survey, they modeled how replacing milk, yogurt, and cheese with commercially available plant-based alternatives would affect intake of four essential micronutrients.

Dr Katie Nicol, a research fellow at the University of Surrey and co-author of the study, explained:

“With the growing need to address environmental sustainability, many dietary recommendations are focused on plant-based diets and the reduced intake of animal products.”

“With the rise in plant-based alternative foods, we need to know whether they are like-for-like replacements for the micronutrients that are found in dairy products. This is particularly important for vulnerable groups, including young children and adolescent females, who are more likely to be deficient in certain micronutrients, which could affect their overall health.”

Iodine was the nutrient most affected by dairy replacement. When researchers modeled completely replacing dairy with plant-based alternatives under current fortification levels, the estimated prevalence of inadequate iodine intake reached 80% among adolescent girls and 72% among adolescent boys. These figures reflect modeled dietary inadequacy, not confirmed clinical deficiencies.

Iodine is essential for producing thyroid hormones, which regulate metabolism and support growth and brain development. It is particularly important before and during pregnancy, when iodine requirements increase.

Dr Sarah Bath, associate professor at the University of Surrey and lead author, said:

“The UK is particularly vulnerable to iodine insufficiency due to a lack of an iodine fortification policy such as universal salt iodization, which many other countries have.”

“People may also be unaware of the importance of iodine, which is needed to make thyroid hormones to regulate our metabolism, support healthy growth, and brain development. Iodine is particularly important during pregnancy, when the requirement increases, but it is also essential prior to pregnancy to ensure that stores are optimized. People are also unaware that dairy products are a major source of iodine, particularly in the UK, and that plant-based alternatives, unless fortified, have a very low iodine content.”

The researchers found that mandatory fortification of plant-based dairy alternatives could prevent declines in micronutrient intake. However, even with fortified alternatives, an estimated 59% of adolescent girls would still have inadequate iodine intake. Adding iodine to bread reduced that figure to 37%, suggesting that combining both fortification strategies could be more effective.

Dr Nicol emphasized the importance of aligning nutrition policies with environmental goals:

“Nutrition policy must evolve in tandem with environmental targets; however, it is important that shifts towards more sustainable diets do not compromise on public health. Fortification of foods is one way that this could be achieved; however, government policy is needed to make this happen.”

Dr Anne Nugent, from Queen’s University Belfast, described how the team identified dairy-containing foods in the UK National Diet and Nutrition Survey and modeled replacing them with equal amounts of plant-based alternatives. The researchers accounted for differences in the nutritional content of available products and tested whether fortification could prevent deficiencies without causing excessive nutrient intake.

“During the analysis, we first identified all foods and recipes containing dairy produce (milk, cheese, and yogurt) in the national food survey for the UK, the National Diet and Nutrition Survey. Next, we conducted a series of replacement scenarios whereby we substituted the dairy present with an equal weight of plant-based dairy alternatives. As the nutrient composition of dairy alternatives available on our supermarket shelves can vary, we also applied a condition to make sure that every dairy alternative we had identified in our previous market survey had an equal chance of selection. The last step was to examine the impact on dietary intakes of key nutrients following either the reduction or removal of dairy from the diet, replacing it with the dairy alternatives as above, and asking whether a series of fortification options could mitigate against any shortfalls in intake while avoid situations of excess.”

At the Edinburgh TV Festival 2026, SNL UK director Liz Clare and cast member Emma Sidi discussed two elements of the original that prompted very different reactions — the famous crane shot and the end-of-show credits.

While Clare rejected the crane because of the practical demands of the British production, Sidi admitted that the cast initially found the closing tradition unbearably "naff." SNL UK could not reproduce the American original and expect every production choice or cultural convention to translate, but neither could it reinvent everything without losing elements that make the format successful. So before any episode aired, the crew behind SNL UK had the mammoth task of working out what it could change and what it needed to keep, and while one problem solved itself, another remains unfixable.

One of the defining features of Saturday Night Live is its production setup, which allows the show to move between the monologue and sketches while maintaining the pace and energy of a live performance. SNL UK has adopted much of the American show's approach, but director Clare decided against using its famous crane shot. Speaking at the Edinburgh TV Festival's The SNL Masterclass, director Clare explained, “The camera setup is probably pretty standard. In fact, it's based on the American show, bar the fact that we don't use the Americans have got this big old bit of kit, big crane that's become a feature of the show,” Clare said. “We decided not to employ that camera because it takes up an awful lot of space.”

Britain makes more detective television than any country has any business making, and somehow almost all of it is good. Here is the one you belong in, what it says about you, and the case for it being the best of the four.

You want the tea, the gossip and the vicar behaving oddly. Midsomer is a county where the scenery is beautiful, the fete is on Saturday and the murder rate is roughly that of a small war, and absolutely nobody involved thinks this is strange.

You feel other people's grief as if it were handed to you personally. Broadchurch is less a whodunnit than a study of what a small town does to itself when it finds out that the answer was always going to be somebody everybody already knew.

You go through things rather than around them. John Luther is the best detective in London and a liability to everybody who works with him, and the show is completely honest that those two facts are the same fact.

You say the thing and you do not dress it up. Vera Stanhope turns up in a hat and a mac, is rude to almost everybody, and is the only person in the room prepared to ask the question that the family has been waiting years for somebody to ask.

The decision makes sense for a show that has to accommodate multiple sets, rapid changes and performances staged in the round. A large crane might be a recognizable part of the American production, but that does not mean it is essential to the format. Instead, the British production has adapted its equipment to suit the way the show operates. “But we do have a jib, and we've got some pads,” Clare explained, describing some of the alternatives available to her team. Her approach is less about reproducing the American show's visual identity exactly and more about understanding what its camera work is trying to achieve. As she put it, “It's really just about making sure that what is interesting about the show is that it's played in the round in a way that then helps all of those resets.”

Jamie Dornan’s turn as host in Season 1, Episode 2, offers a good example of how the money saved on the crane allows the series to follow the technical needs of each joke. Dornan played a criminal taking Annabel Marlow hostage, a role that required the production to recreate the appearance of a video recorded on a camcorder. Rather than relying on a conventional studio camera to capture the scene, Clare's team found a more specific solution. “We knew that we needed some kind of camera that he could look like he was recording a sort of hostage video,” she explained. “So we actually got a live really lovely sort of digicam that was a live picture, live feed that we were able to take out of the camera and put in and used to make it look as authentic as possible.”

The closing sequence of Saturday Night Live is one of its most familiar traditions. As the credits roll, the cast gathers around the host, exchanging hugs and saying goodbye to the audience. For Sidi, however, the practice initially seemed awkward and unnecessary. Her reaction highlights a different challenge for SNL UK. Technical decisions can be made according to the needs of the production, but cultural traditions can be harder to assess up front. Sidi admitted that she had never been particularly fond of the American show's endings. “That's something with the American show... the ending, the goodbyes bit. I would just see it and go, ‘This is just so naff. I can't bear to see this,’” she said at the Edinburgh TV Festival. From the viewer's perspective, the sequence can look like an overly sentimental ritual, particularly when performers who have spent the episode confidently delivering jokes suddenly show their relief that it is over in the farewell.

However, making SNL UK changed Sidi's perspective. The cast were experiencing the pressure of putting together a live show, working through rehearsals, changes, and the demands of performing. Sidi explained that she understood the tradition differently once she had experienced that process, “And when we're doing it now, I get it because we have done the heist during the week, you know?... So we're hugging and crying... fuck it, we're luvvies now,” she joked. “You are just like, thank god we did it. It's so real that hugging at the end.”

SNL UK could have dropped the closing sequence to avoid something the cast found embarrassing, just as Clare dropped the crane. Removing them would not change the quality of the series or make a sketch easier to stage. Yet it would have removed a moment that the people making the show have come to value. Sidi even acknowledged that viewers might still find the sequence uncomfortable: “So sorry you have to watch that, but we're gonna keep doing it!” Now halfway through its second season, Saturday Night Live UK is still developing its own identity away from the American original, but some things stay essential to the show regardless of where it is being made.

Arsenal currently always feel one setback away from a big discussion about which forward they need to sign in January. They are obviously monitoring a few options, and are expected to do some business, but Arteta clearly doesn’t want his players thinking like that.

Arteta lamented how his team had two further one-on-ones in the game, only to waste them. He said it was like they “are trying to score the perfect goal”.

That’s another familiar refrain with this club, if from a different era to the 1-0s. Arteta’s desired solution is to be “more pragmatic”, that they “need to be more chaotic”.

That’s undeniably true, but the manager being the one to say this poses some questions of his own.

Another phrase that Arteta used was “volume”. That, when you face a defence as well structured as Hull City, you need to be “generating volume”. In other words, numbers, a critical mass of chances where you eventually score – which is pretty much what they did. Insistence.

It’s been discussed on these pages before that Arteta is a huge “probabilities guy”, where he sees almost every game as an equation. If Arsenal consistently generate an xG of 2 or above and limit the opposition to 0.5, they are likely to win enough games to win the league.

A natural extension of this is an exactingly calibrated team, where Hull City players – like so many others – were left praising “the shape”.

But is that mentality something that encourages “pragmatism” or, as Arteta elaborated, “chaos”? Other descriptions for that are sparkle, ingenuity… magic.

That’s hard to find sufficient space for in a system that demands a lot of energy devoted to pressing and structure.

Odegaard, the match-winner, is almost the ultimate example. The playmaker is capable of some of the most ingenious and exquisite touches in the league, but it does not feel like he is as characterised by that as much any more.

When you ask people at Arsenal about him, in fact, they praise how he understands Arteta’s pressing better than anyone else. It should be stressed that their current level isn’t exactly much of a problem. They’ve won nine out of 10 in the league. They’re brilliantly efficient in everything outside the scoring.

Even this match has to be put in the context of a tough match against a very respectable Hull City side. They’re so well structured you can see them being this season’s Sunderland.

Arsenal were known to be impressed afterwards, with Arteta especially respectful. His team still get through, and you can see them being convincingly ahead by Christmas.

But the league isn’t the level anymore. It’s about beating Paris Saint-Germain in the Champions League, about being European champions as well as English champions.

That is something they really have to insist on.

How Much Are Delta SkyMiles Worth Compared To American AAdvantage Miles In 2026?

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

MC MEDIASTUDIO & Karolis Kavolelis | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

It can be difficult for airlines to truly distinguish themselves because all carriers fundamentally sell the same product: flights. Therefore, airlines need to find ways to differentiate the experience of flying from point A to point B, and that's where loyalty programs come in. By rewarding high-value customers with perks and benefits, airlines can essentially keep customers tied to their network and larger frequent flyer ecosystem.

This is an incredibly lucrative side of the business. Within the US, American Airlines' AAdvantage program was previously the US's largest airline loyalty program, but it has been overtaken by Delta Air Lines' SkyMiles. The two programs, while being similar in many ways, also have a variety of differences.

These may make one or the other more appealing to a specific customer. What's more, the individual value of a mile issued by these two programs is not necessarily the same. It's therefore important to know what the value proposition for each program is and which one is best suited for you.

The true value of airline miles is not a set figure, because this can change based on individual redemptions. However, it is possible to estimate mile valuations at a specific point in time based on systemwide redemption prices. NerdWallet estimates the value of one AAdvantage mile at 1.7 cents, while estimating that one SkyMile is worth 1.2 cents.

Meanwhile, The Points Guy estimates that one AAdvantage mile is worth 1.45 cents, while pegging the value of one SkyMile at 1.2 cents. Across the board, AAdvantage miles are considered to be more valuable than SkyMiles, and this is a unique advantage for American. NerdWallet pegs a United Airlines MileagePlus mile at 1.2 cents, while The Points Guy values MileagePlus miles the same.

There has been criticism of Delta and United for the devaluation of their programs' miles, and AAdvantage offers a material advantage in terms of redemption values. This is important when trying to decide which airline to be loyal to. At all three airlines, you can earn miles by flying or through credit card spending, which has become the primary means of mileage earnings in the US.

The airlines offer a wide range of credit cards, each coming with progressively higher annual fees and increasingly lucrative benefits. In addition, all three carriers are part of airline alliances, which grant certain benefits when traveling with select airlines worldwide, and they also have individual partnerships with airlines outside of the alliances.

Delta Air Lines uses four main Medallion tiers, as well as the general membership status and the invite-only Delta 360 tier at the very top. The four Medallion tiers, named Silver, Gold, Platinum, and Diamond, offer increasing rewards with each higher tier. In order, they respectively require 5,000, 10,000, 15,000, and 28,000 Medallion Qualifying Dollars.

These can be earned through flying or through credit card spending, with Delta not requiring members to actually fly for Medallion status. Similarly, American Airlines has its general membership tier, its invite-only ConciergeKey status, and four main elite status tiers: Gold, Platinum, Platinum Pro, and Executive Platinum. It uses Loyalty Points as the metric for status, requiring 40,000, 75,000, 125,000, and 200,000 Loyalty Points for each tier.

Crucially, however, it's much easier to earn Loyalty Points with American than MQDs with Delta, as the airline essentially awards one Loyalty Point for every eligible base mile earned. Delta awards one MQD for every dollar spent on the base ticket price and associated airline fees on Delta-operated flights, as well as bookings with Delta Vacations.

In addition, flights operated by close Delta partners earn MQDs based on a calculation of fare and distance, while SkyMiles co-branded credit cards use their own calculation: the SkyMiles Platinum and Business Platinum cards earn $1 MQD per $20 spent, while the Reserve and Business Reserve seats earn $1 MQD for every $10 spent.

SkyMiles and AAdvantage both offer four personal credit cards, with American Express issuing the SkyMiles co-branded credit cards while Citi issues the AAdvantage co-branded cards. The top-end AAdvantage Executive World Legend Mastercard notably provides unlimited Admirals Club access, whereas the top-end Delta SkyMiles Reserve Amex only grants 15 Sky Club visits annually unless the cardholder reaches $75,000 in annual spend.

This is a significant difference. As previously mentioned, the AAdvantage cards provide a quicker path to earning elite status. Both sets of cards offer a variety of benefits, and the choice of which one to choose is largely down to personal preference. However, the benefits of going with SkyMiles are that miles never expire, whereas AAdvantage miles expire after 24 months of account activity.

In addition, AAdvantage only offers one business card, whereas SkyMiles offers three, making the latter a better choice for this demographic. The AAdvantage Platinum Select World Elite Mastercard has a lower annual fee than the comparable SkyMiles Gold Amex ($99 versus $150).

Meanwhile, the AAdvantage Executive World Legend Mastercard has a higher annual fee than the SkyMiles Reserve Amex ($695 versus $650). The two airlines offer sign-on bonuses, usually in the form of bonus miles (although they can include more). Both sets of cards also grant status boosts in the form of extra Loyalty Points/MQDs in some cases, although it's still easier to earn AAdvantage Loyalty Points.

American Airlines offers its members multiple additional perks for reaching a certain amount of Loyalty Points. This is solely tied to the number of Loyalty Points reached in a calendar year, with the lowest milestone being 15,000 Loyalty Points. There are also multiple checkpoints, each offering a small variety of benefits that are different from the other milestones.

As a whole, the benefits list is quite diverse, allowing AAdvantage members across the board to receive multiple perks throughout the year. Delta Air Lines also offers SkyMiles members additional perks under the 'Choice Rewards' system. However, these are only available to Platinum and Diamond members.

Still, the rewards list is extensive for both tiers, with Platinum Medallion members able to choose options such as regional upgrade certificates, an MQD accelerator, bonus miles, a charitable donation, a status gift, statement credit, Starbucks Rewards credit, Delta Vacations credit, a Sustainable Aviation Fuel credit, or a Wheels Up credit. Diamond members get even more choices.

Delta is a SkyTeam member, and the SkyMiles program provides reciprocal SkyTeam status for the appropriate tier: SkyTeam Elite for Silver Medallion, and SkyTeam Elite Plus for the higher tiers. American Airlines, on the other hand, is a oneworld member, and oneworld offers three status tiers for loyalty program members.

The Emerald tier slots above anything that SkyTeam or Star Alliance offers in terms of benefits. This is a major incentive for AAdvantage members to chase the program's top two tiers, which grant oneworld Emerald status. SkyMiles does not publish an awards chart, as all award flights are priced dynamically, and American AAdvantage also does this for the most part.

However, AAdvantage does still use an awards chart for partner-operated flights, publishing estimated starting rates by region. What's more, American allows for upgrades from economy straight to business class (excluding basic economy fares), whereas Delta generally only permits upgrades to the next cabin class.

Multiple Delta Air Lines credit cards will help you earn elite status.

For some, there are benefits that make SkyMiles a lucrative program, such as miles that never expire, or the wider range of business credit cards. On the surface, however, there are several benefits to going for AAdvantage over SkyMiles, such as the greater upgrade flexibility, oneworld Emerald status, Admirals Club access, the Loyalty Points system, and the generally superior value per mile.

Still, two important considerations remain, which might triumph over everything else. The first is that passengers generally consider Delta to offer a better passenger experience. Although the Sky Clubs are harder to access, they're generally considered to be nicer, while nearly all Delta narrowbodies offer seatback entertainment.

SkyMiles members aren't necessarily choosing the program over competitors because of the merits of the program, but rather because they want to travel with Delta, and this may be more important to some than certain benefits offered by AAdvantage. However, arguably the most important consideration is location.

Regardless of which program's perks are more valuable, this matters little if someone lives in Atlanta, a major Delta hub, or Dallas/Fort Worth, a major American hub. The network and schedules that the dominant airline in a fortress hub offers usually have a much greater effect on which loyalty program to join. Some airports are more competitive, including smaller spokes as well as hubs like New York or Los Angeles, but even then, network and schedule play a major role.

Here's the Average Social Security Benefit by Age 62 to 80

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Claiming at 62 versus 70 creates a gap that reshapes your entire retirement income picture.

Social Security provides a vital floor for your retirement, but it's meant to supplement your income rather than replace it entirely. The amount you receive depends heavily on when you choose to claim, making it one of the most consequential financial decisions you'll face.

The Social Security Administration (SSA) calculates your benefit based on an average of your wages across your 35 highest-earning years, adjusted for inflation and run through a specific formula. Your final check size is then determined by your full retirement age (FRA) and the age you begin collecting.

Here is the average monthly retired-worker benefit by age, as of December 2025, the most recent data available.

The data reveals a clear increase between ages 62 and 70, with the average benefit growing by 59.7%. This represents a monthly gap of $850.28, or $10,203.36 per year.

Full retirement age (FRA) ranges from 66 to 67, depending on your birth year, and it's 67 for everyone born in 1960 or later. If you claim at 62, you face a permanent reduction of up to 30%, while waiting until 70 earns you an 8% annual increase through delayed retirement credits.

A second pattern appears after age 70, as averages begin to decline slightly. This doesn't mean the SSA cuts benefits for individuals. Instead, older groups likely include more people who claimed early at permanent reductions.

Women also make up a larger share of the oldest groups because they tend to live longer. Because they often have lower lifetime earnings due to the gender pay gap and time spent caregiving, their presence in the data lowers the overall average among the oldest retirees.

Even at the age-70 peak of $2,274.68 per month, or $27,296.16 per year, benefits rarely cover every bill. That peak falls $34,135.84 short of the $61,432 average senior household spending reported by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS).

A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027, adding about $82 a month on top of that age-70 benefit. While these adjustments are designed to keep pace with inflation, they often fall short.

The most useful number for your planning is the average that matches your own intended claiming age.

Even those who claim Social Security benefits at age 70, when they qualify for their maximum checks, rarely live comfortably on their benefits alone. You'll probably need other sources of retirement income to cover what Social Security doesn't.

Personal savings are your best bet if they're available to you. You're free to spend that money on anything you like, but remember it may have to last you 20 years or more, so choose a withdrawal strategy designed to make it last. If you don't have a lot of personal savings, you may need a job or other government benefits to supplement your Social Security checks.

Make sure you come up with a plan for how you'll cover your remaining living expenses before you apply for Social Security. If you're married, be sure to factor in your spouse's Social Security checks and personal savings as well, and make sure you're both on the same page about when each person plans to claim.
