Xinhua Unveils Bionic Humanoid Robot News Anchors with UBTECH

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Xinhua News Agency unveiled two bionic humanoid robot news anchors developed with UBTECH on November 5, 2026, at the Artificial Intelligence and Chinese Modernization forum in Beijing, describing them as the world’s first of their kind.

The male anchor is Xinyu and the female anchor is Xinyan. Both use life-size human proportions and were customized for news scenarios. Xinhua says it holds complete independent intellectual-property rights for the jointly developed robots. During the forum, one robot read the opening news on stage, providing an immediate demonstration of the presentation role.

Chinese film director Zhang Yimou served as chief image designer and led the embodied visual design. His stated aim was to balance the solemn, rigorous requirements of news presentation with natural bionic realism. The design work drew on camera aesthetics and the shaping of personal demeanor, tying the robots’ appearance to the visual conventions of broadcast journalism rather than treating human resemblance as a standalone objective.

The anchors connect through a technical interface to Xinhua Yudian, the agency’s AI current-affairs information agent and authoritative corpus. Xinhua says this arrangement makes the content traceable, secure and controllable. UBTECH’s robotics contribution spans bionic skin, embodied-intelligence hardware, an operating system, an emotional large model and manufacturing of the complete robots.

Zhou Jian, UBTECH’s founder, chairman and CEO, said the resulting brain–expression–control technical chain retrieves reviewed, authoritative current-affairs information in real time and avoids dissemination risks from generative-AI hallucinations. The design makes content governance part of the robot’s operating pipeline: Xinhua’s corpus supplies the reviewed material that the machine retrieves and presents.

Qian Tong, director of Xinhua’s New Media Center, described the robots as a new type of editorial job within the newsroom, with journalists and editors remaining the central editorial actors. Their assigned tasks include information presentation, multilingual output and live interaction in support of editorial staff. That remit positions the humanoids as presentation and interaction tools within an editorial workflow, not as autonomous authors of the news they voice.

Xinhua reports more than 220 digital and intelligent tools introduced in recent years under an approach of empowering journalists rather than replacing them. Forum participants identified human-led, machine-assisted work as a basic principle for intelligent journalism, with human judgment, field reporting and fact-checking remaining responsibilities that AI should not displace.

Zhou listed research and study bases, city reception venues, overseas cultural exchanges and deep-space exploration reporting as future applications. Those are planned scenarios, distinct from the opening-news reading demonstrated at the launch. The proposed settings extend the concept from the demonstrated presentation task into public reception, cultural exchange and specialized reporting.

The New Media Center also plans the Xinhua News Agency AI Industry Alliance (Beijing Base) to link government, media, technology companies and think tanks in an innovation vehicle spanning industry, academia, research and application. The debut was accompanied by a signing ceremony for the base and the launch of a research project on mobile news-image technology and authenticity standards. Those initiatives place the physical anchor demonstration alongside work on industry coordination and the verification of visual news content.

The forum formed part of the seventh World Media Summit, which is co-hosted by Xinhua and the Beijing municipal government and runs through November 9, 2026. It brings together nearly 500 delegates from nearly 330 mainstream media outlets, think tanks, government departments and international organizations across 112 countries and regions.

The debut ties a highly visible humanoid interface to a less visible editorial system built around reviewed source material, defined newsroom tasks and human control. The central open question is how that system performs in live interaction and multilingual output as Xinhua pursues applications beyond the demonstrated on-stage reading.

METR Estimates Anthropic’s Model Capability Growth Has Nearly Doubled

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

METR published an independent assessment on November 5, 2026, judging that Anthropic is likely improving model capabilities on well-specified tasks at around twice its earlier rate and that AI use likely explains a majority of the current acceleration. The conclusions carry substantial uncertainty, and redactions conceal much of the supporting evidence.

Using AECI, Anthropic’s benchmark capability index, METR estimated that benchmark scores have grown 1.9 times faster since October 2025, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.5–2.8 times. That rate implies roughly two years of progress at the previous pace in a single year. The broader judgment compares October 2026 with the rate observed from March 2024 through October 2025.

The calculation includes internal-only models and dates each snapshot by when most Anthropic employees could use it, creating a common basis for released and unreleased systems. METR’s robustness checks produced acceleration estimates ranging from 1.1 to 2.9 times under alternative modeling choices, demonstrating sensitivity to how benchmark difficulty and other analytical assumptions are represented.

The evidence convinced METR that faster growth in training compute did not cause the acceleration, although the supporting details were redacted. Non-AI factors likely played a large role in late 2025, but the current best guess is that they do not explain the sustained speedup.

Staff evidence pointed in the same direction but was uneven. Researcher interviews varied widely around an approximately twofold acceleration. Informal polls put average self-reported uplift from the most capable internal model at about 12 times in October 2026; METR cautioned that the responses required little effort, might not represent staff broadly, and that individual productivity gains do not directly equal organization-wide capability growth.

The current effect comes mainly from AI assisting researchers. AI cannot yet make highly significant, fully autonomous contributions to the most important training work, although researchers described indications that such contributions could emerge soon, with maturity varying across areas.

No convincing evidence emerged that algorithmic progress, training compute, experiment compute or data would impose significant bottlenecks over a 12-month horizon. The reviewers’ interpretation changed several times as new information arrived, leaving them unconfident in that assessment. METR nevertheless judged that the absence of clear constraints or steep diminishing returns creates a significant risk that further capability gains could compound into even faster acceleration; that is a forward-looking risk judgment, not an observed result.

AECI emphasizes well-specified, readily graded tasks, so faster benchmark gains may not translate into equivalent real-world effects. METR said overfitting, non-AI contributors or an unusually weak historical baseline could make its estimate too high, while missed recent acceleration, faster-than-expected removal of human bottlenecks or benchmark scores that understate real-world impact could make it too low. Model 4’s scores above 50% on all 15 benchmarks used for it added a separate concern that saturation could impede future measurement.

METR staffed the eleven-week assessment at 2.5 full-time equivalents, running from August 12, 2026, through October 29, 2026. It reviewed internal benchmark results, pretraining and reinforcement-learning metrics, training-compute measurements, AI-speedup analyses and internal documents, and drew on roughly 15 interviews with key staff. The team did not receive unmediated, employee-like access or sample non-public models.

Anthropic could approve or reject information requests and unilaterally redact non-public material. Its redactions removed from the public report a majority of the evidence METR considered most informative, including detailed AI attribution, algorithmic-progress estimates, specific bottleneck analysis and forecasts for future models. Anthropic described the redactions as protection for confidential information that could accelerate other AI developers. METR retained discretion over methodology and conclusions and accepted no payment, though it worked on Anthropic devices and used free tokens.

The assessment provides an extensive outside examination of AI-driven research acceleration while stopping short of transparency about the underlying evidence. Public benchmark results and staff accounts support faster progress, but redactions prevent readers from independently assessing much of the basis for METR’s attribution and bottleneck conclusions. Those central findings therefore rest heavily on the evaluator’s synthesis of evidence that readers cannot fully inspect.

CCM’s non-solicitation push tests limits of lender control

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

CrossCountry Mortgage has sent cease-and-desist letters to at least 25 former loan officers, invoking two-year non-solicitation clauses and seeking payments for allegedly diverted loans. Attorneys say non-solicits have long existed but were often used as a deterrent because litigation is costly and proof is hard.   AI Summary

CrossCountry Mortgage’s enforcement of non-solicitation agreements is emerging as a test case for how far lenders will go to protect production in a higher-for-longer rate environment — a topic that has divided the industry.

The top-five mortgage lender has sent cease-and-desist letters to at least 25 former loan officers, invoking a two-year non-solicitation window and demanding thousands of dollars in payments under their employment agreements, HousingWire reported Friday. CCM declined to comment on this story.

Non-solicitation provisions, which restrict departing employees from taking the former employer’s clients, referral partners or staff, have long been part of mortgage recruiting playbooks. But attorneys and executives say many lenders – mainly smaller ones – do not enforce them because litigation is costly and proof can be difficult.

That calculation may be shifting as origination volume remains compressed, margins stay thin and experienced producers become more valuable in a smaller market.

“With this high-rate environment continuing, top LOs will still be highly sought after, so they have to be careful on what type of agreements they enter into with a new company, what they own or not when they enter into those agreements” said Colgate Selden, a founding member of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and a shareholder at Baker Donelson. “The companies hiring them have to be careful what they accept from the old company, if anything.”

Alan Pezeshkian, president of House America Financial, said every company has a version of a non-solicitation, but in practical terms, it’s more of a deterrent to the extent that companies enforce it.

“You’ll probably see that happening more now as volume has gone down, so companies have the time, resources and perhaps the need to make sure that they protect their organization and their assets. We have that clause but have I enforced it? No,” he said. “On one hand, you want to convey a message in the industry of where you stand. On the other hand, if you enforce it, you become a villain.”

Lenders are most likely to enforce non-solicitation and related covenants when they believe there is something concrete to track, such as trademark, intellectual property or trade secret infringements, attorneys say. They also act when loans in process move with a departing LO and consumer data is transferred to a new company, which can trigger Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA) and state privacy concerns.

“I still think that CCM was singled out for a practice that is common and widespread,” said Paul Hindman, an industry consultant whose clients include CCM, adding that the distinction between sending notices and pursuing a legal case is important.

To reduce the risk of these disputes, top or more sophisticated loan officers and ‘plug-and-play’ branches are increasingly negotiating exceptions that let them own the leads they personally generate, which are then carved out from the definition of company property. Other agreements spell out differences between in-house leads provided and funded by the lender and those generated by an LO’s own marketing, open houses or personal sphere, so those contacts can be solicited after a move.

Refinance opportunities often fall into the in-house category, because they typically come from the lender’s past customer database and corporate marketing campaigns. Several CCM letters reviewed by HousingWire specifically reference refi business.

That dividing line — who truly owns the customer relationship — is now at the heart of the recruiting and enforcement battle.

Brendan McKay, owner of McKay Mortgage, argues that originators hold the relationship, but concedes the picture changes when the company bought and supplied the lead.

“Threatening loan officers with lawsuits for continuing to serve clients whose trust they personally earned is not only anti-loan officer, but it’s also anti-consumer. It’s time for our industry to start writing employment agreements that recognize the difference,” McKay said.

One caveat: Hindman said employment agreements have expanded from simple two-page documents to “30 pages of legalese,” which makes it harder for loan officers to fully understand what they are signing. Still, he said, LOs cannot ignore contractual terms once they have accepted large financial packages to join.

“You can’t take a million-dollar sign-on bonus and say that you’re not going to do certain things, and then do those things and object to being called out for it,” Hindman said, adding that originators should be clear with borrowers up front when contractual restrictions could limit their ability to keep working together after a move.

Whether a non-solicitation clause can be enforced can come down to who is initiating contact and how the provision is drafted, with outcomes varying by state. Cases in which a loan officer actively reaches out to former customers are generally viewed more favorably for enforcement, attorneys said.

“But when the borrower is approaching the loan officer, that’s looked at a lot differently because you don’t want to restrict consumers – consumers have the right to choose who is going to do their loans, and you can’t go ahead and have an agreement that binds them,” said James Brody, managing partner at Brody Gapp. “When the borrowers reach out, I would have a hard time thinking that a court would go ahead and enforce that.”

Ron Gapp, founding partner at Brody Gapp, added: “When you start saying borrowers can’t approach somebody, it now is becoming a non-compete, and those are two different regimes. Definitely, a non-compete is going to have a lot more scrutiny than a non-solicit would.”

In the brokerage channel, some firms are rewriting agreements in response to LO pushback. Todd Bitter, national director of sales for NEXA Lending, said the company changed its contracts after new hires flagged language claiming ownership of their databases for 12 months after they left: “It’s not NEXA’s to own; it is yours because the loan officer should own their database,” Bitter said.

He described non-solicitation agreements as especially common among retail mortgage lenders, though some brokerages use them as well. Bitter also questioned whether lenders should rely on aggressive restrictions simply because they can.

“Is it legal? Yes. Is it right? No,” he said, adding that many cease-and-desist letters appear designed as scare tactics, even if they never lead to court. “But who wants to find out?”

The same tensions are emerging around referral partners. Some non-solicitation agreements extend to real estate agents and other referral sources, attempting to bar them from directing business to a departed loan officer. But, according to Brody, those provisions are on shaky ground.

“You can’t abridge the rights of third parties with regards to your contracts, and attempt to go ahead and stop those third parties from being able to have the freedom to use the loan officer or company of their choice,” Brody said. “This is something that would be looked at very sternly.”

A top mortgage broker who requested anonymity said the imbalance of resources between large lenders and individual loan officers turns cease-and-desist campaigns into “corporate bullying.” Major shops can afford to send repeated letters and pursue litigation if they choose, while former employees must weigh the cost, time and stress of hiring counsel or risking default, this source added.

CCM’s letter seeks repayment calculated at 75 basis points per loan for loans LOs originated that allegedly violated their agreements. Attorneys say that figure likely represents a rough estimate of the company’s lost economics — essentially the split CCM would have retained if the production had remained in-house, or a “haircut” on profitability. But they caution that originators need to understand exactly what any such formula is tied to.

“When you have a provision in a contract that acts as liquidated damages, you have to look at whether or not it’s a reasonable anticipation of those losses, with some facts behind it, or whether it starts to look like a penalty,” Brody said. “If it looks like a penalty that’s divorced from those specifics, then courts will routinely strike those down because you’re not allowed to have a liquidated damages provision that acts as a penalty.”

Brody also mentioned that switching the burden of proof “in and of itself would need to be analyzed and shouldn’t really be opined off the cuff.”

Browser Location Access: What It Actually Shares and How to Control It

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When a website asks "Allow location access?" — most users either click Allow reflexively or Block without fully understanding what they're granting. Here's what browser location access actually means, what extensions can do with it, and how to make deliberate choices.

The browser's Geolocation API lets websites request your location. When a site calls navigator.geolocation.getCurrentPosition(), the browser shows a permission prompt. If you grant it, the browser returns a latitude/longitude coordinate to the page.

On desktop: Not GPS. Desktop browsers use a combination of:

The accuracy on desktop varies from neighborhood-level (Wi-Fi triangulation) to city-level (IP-only). It's not your exact address.

On mobile: GPS is used if available, which is accurate to meters. Mobile location requests are much more precise than desktop.

When you grant location access to a site, the site receives:

That's it — no street address, no "home address," no place names. The website has to reverse geocode the coordinates to get a named location, which is an additional step they may or may not do.

Your IP address is always visible to sites, regardless of location permission. Many sites geolocate from IP before even requesting the Geolocation API.

Browser extensions are different from websites for location access. Extensions don't use the Geolocation API the same way — they have access to browser APIs that websites don't.

What extensions typically do with location: Most legitimate location-related extensions (weather, map, local search) request location to provide their core function — a weather extension needs your location to show weather. This is expected and appropriate.

Red flags: An extension that requests location access but whose stated purpose has nothing to do with location is worth scrutinizing.

Weather & Clock Dashboard for Firefox uses your location to show the weather for your area. The permissions requested are specifically for location and the ability to fetch weather data from an API (Open-Meteo).

The extension doesn't send your location to its own servers. It passes your coordinates to Open-Meteo (the weather data provider) to get forecast data. Open-Meteo is a public API with an open-data license; it doesn't have accounts or persistent tracking.

For users who don't want to share location even with the extension: you can enter a manual location by city name instead, and the extension will use that. No location permission required.

Firefox gives you granular control over location access:

Per-site management: Settings → Privacy & Security → Permissions → Location → Settings. This shows every site that has asked for location access and what you answered. You can change individual site permissions here without affecting others.

Default behavior: You can set Firefox to block all location requests by default, prompt case-by-case (the default), or allow all. Settings → Privacy & Security → Permissions → Location → check "Block new requests asking to access your location" to default to block.

Incognito/private windows: Location permissions granted in a private window don't persist. The site has to ask again next time.

Allow location for: mapping/navigation sites (Google Maps, OpenStreetMap), weather services, local search, delivery sites where location saves you typing your address.

Consider blocking for: news sites, social media platforms, general-purpose shopping sites, sites where your precise location has no clear functional use.

Always review when: a site you don't recognize or haven't used before asks for location, or a site asks for location but you can't figure out why they'd need it.

The permission prompt is there for a reason. Using it deliberately — not reflexively clicking Allow or Block — is the main privacy practice worth building.

Firefox's default geolocation provider is different from Chrome's. Firefox defaults to routing geolocation queries through Mozilla's geolocation service (which uses Google Location Services under the hood but via Mozilla), which provides some additional privacy layer compared to Chrome's direct Google use.

For higher privacy: about:config → geo.provider.network.url can be changed to alternative providers, or geolocation can be disabled entirely by setting geo.enabled to false. This disables the API completely — all location requests will appear to fail to sites.

The practical privacy tradeoff: disabling geolocation entirely means weather extensions, map sites, and local search can't use your location, which is often the reason you're visiting them. The per-site permission model is a better balance for most users than a global block.

Starting from Abbottabad: What Your Hometown Decides — and What It Doesn't

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I am from Abbottabad, Pakistan. It is a small city in the hills, and it is home. Everything I have built — Buzz Capitol, Digi Skill Pro Academy, findservicenest.com, more than fifteen YouTube channels, six books — started there. People sometimes ask me whether starting from a small town held me back. The honest answer is: it decided some things, and it did not decide others. Knowing the difference matters more than the town itself.

Your hometown decides your starting point. It decides what is around you when you are young: the schools, the people you meet, the examples of what a working life can look like. In a small city, you see fewer big companies and fewer obvious paths into media or technology. That is real, and I will not pretend it is not. When I started, there was no local roadmap for what I wanted to do. I had to find the road myself, mostly online.

But your hometown does not decide your reach. The internet changed that equation completely, and it changed it for everyone at once. A student in Abbottabad can take the same free class as a student anywhere else. A reader in another country does not know or care where an article was written. A client hiring a service does not ask for your address before asking whether you can do the job. The work travels; the writer stays put.

That is the first thing my hometown taught me: place is a starting point, not a limit. It taught me to be resourceful, because when the thing you need is not nearby, you learn to find it online, to teach yourself, to ask strangers for help and to help strangers in return. Some of the most useful skills I have came from that habit — looking for answers instead of waiting for them to arrive.

The second thing it taught me is patience. In a small city, things move at their own pace. People know each other. Reputation travels by word of mouth, and it travels slowly — but it also lasts. That shaped how I think about building anything. I did not chase a quick splash. I built steadily, in my own name, one article and one video and one class at a time, because that is how trust works where I come from: you show up, you do the work, and over time people believe you.

There is a part of this story I want to be careful about. I am not saying that where you are from does not matter at all. It matters. It shapes your accent, your manners, your sense of what is possible, and the problems you understand first-hand. I understand what it means to start with limited resources, because I lived it. That understanding is part of why I made Digi Skill Pro Academy's classes completely free. In 2025, more than ten thousand students joined, and more than five thousand of them reported that they started earning. I do not claim those numbers as a triumph of mine; I mention them because they are the reason the academy exists — to give people starting from places like mine a way in.

Starting from Abbottabad also taught me something about independence. When you are far from the centres where decisions get made, you learn to make your own decisions. Buzz Capitol is an independent news and media platform because I wanted it to be free to say what it thinks. That independence was not a strategy I read in a book. It was a habit I learned from building things on my own terms, in a place where nobody was going to build them for me.

I still live and work from here, and I do not see that as a compromise. The world comes to my work through a screen, and my work goes out to the world the same way. My channels reach more than ten million followers across platforms. My books reach readers I will never meet. None of that required me to leave. It required me to stay, and to build something worth finding.

If you are starting from a small town, here is what I would say. Do not waste energy resenting your starting point. Use it. The constraints of a small place teach you to be self-reliant, and self-reliance is the most portable skill there is. Start with what you can do today, with the connection you have. Publish your work under your own name. Let the internet do what it is good at: carrying good work further than its author's address.

Your hometown decides where you begin. It does not decide how far your work can travel. That part is yours.

Sami Ullah is the Founder, CEO and Editor-in-Chief of Buzz Capitol, Chairman of Digi Skill Pro Academy, and author of six books. He lives and works in Abbottabad, Pakistan.

Illegal gambling ring busted after using Pokemon cards as payouts

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Police in Texas have busted an alleged illegal gambling operation that was using Pokemon and sports trading cards to pay out winnings instead of cash.

Pokemon cards have become increasingly valuable in recent years, with rare collectibles selling for thousands or even millions of dollars. However, authorities in San Antonio believe one group of businesses was taking advantage of their value for an entirely different reason.

According to police, customers who won money on gambling machines were instead handed valuable trading cards, which they could then exchange for cash at a nearby business.

The unusual arrangement came to light after officers raided seven locations on November 4, detaining 23 people and seizing hundreds of gambling machines.

Bexar County Sheriff Javier Salazar explained that the businesses, which operated under the name “777,” were allegedly attempting to get around Texas gambling laws.

Under state rules, certain gaming establishments are restricted in the value of prizes they can award. Rather than paying customers large sums directly, the businesses allegedly used Pokemon and sports cards as an alternative.

Winners could then take their cards to a neighboring store, which would purchase them for cash.

Speaking to the San Antonio Express-News, Salazar explained how customers could receive a card and sell it to collect their winnings.

“So here’s your sports cards that you won for gambling with us, you can go next door and sell this to them, and they’ll give you a couple of hundred dollars for it,” Salazar said. “We know what they’re doing, they’re trying to skirt the law, it doesn’t make it any less illegal.”
