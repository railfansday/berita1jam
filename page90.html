"There'd be nothing worse than, like, 'Let's get a fresh-faced Bond!'" he added. "That would be the worst idea in the world. It would be ridiculous to reinvent it as some young posh kid." At the time, he was just starting to make his transition from teen heartthrob to chameleonic leading man, and was promoting what has since become known as one of Pattinson's best movies, David Cronenberg's "Cosmopolis." Since then, much has changed, and while it hasn't quite been two decades, it seems the actor wasn't kidding when he said he'd one day like to play 007.

Sierra Club Welcomes CalPERS’ $71 Billion Climate-Solutions Investment Milestone, Urges Fund to End Financing of Fossil Fuel Expansion

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

SACRAMENTO, CA — On Monday, the California Public Employees’ Retirement System (CalPERS) reported that the value of investments it classifies as climate solutions reached $71 billion as of June 30, 2026, up from $59.7 billion a year earlier. The increase brings the nation’s largest public pension fund closer to its goal of $100 billion in climate investments by 2030.

Sierra Club welcomed CalPERS’ continued commitment to climate-solutions investing and called on the fund to adopt stronger standards and transparency around what counts toward its $100 billion goal. Sierra Club also urged CalPERS to prioritize investments that support real-world emissions reductions and address climate risks across its broader portfolio to protect the retirement security of the workers and retirees it serves.

In response, Ben Cushing, Director of Sierra Club’s Sustainable Finance Campaign, released the following statement:

“It’s encouraging to see CalPERS make progress toward its climate-solutions goal, but a growing investment total doesn’t tell us how much new money is helping build clean energy and cut emissions. Reducing climate risk also means looking at where CalPERS invests across the whole portfolio. CalPERS has already taken the important step of using its votes to hold company directors accountable for how they handle climate risk. It can build on that work by adopting a policy to stop financing fossil fuel expansion and backing climate projects that need new investment to get built or expand.”

CalPERS said the $11.3 billion increase came from both new investments and gains on assets it already held. CalPERS also reported that financed emissions across the asset classes covered by its analysis rose by approximately 6 percent over the past year, largely reflecting investments in higher-emitting assets that it expects to decarbonize over time. For its global public equity and U.S. corporate credit holdings, CalPERS reported an implied temperature rise of 2.9°C, up from 2.7°C a year earlier.

Last year, Sierra Club and the California Common Good coalition urged CalPERS to publish a full list of the investments it counts toward its $100 billion goal, adopt science-based standards for what qualifies as a climate solution, and exclude fossil fuel investments from that total. Sierra Club has raised these concerns at CalPERS board meetings, and elaborated on them in a December 2025 CalMatters op-ed questioning the credibility and real-world impact of CalPERS’ reported climate investments.

Sierra Club’s 2025 Principles for Climate Solutions Investments outline standards for credible investing, including prioritizing real-world emissions reductions and establishing safeguards for transition financing. Its January 2026 Climate Solutions Gap report likewise highlighted CalPERS as an example of why ambitious climate-solutions targets must be backed by credible definitions, clear eligibility criteria, and transparent reporting.

The Sierra Club is America’s largest and most influential grassroots environmental organization, with millions of members and supporters. In addition to protecting every person's right to get outdoors and access the healing power of nature, the Sierra Club works to promote clean energy, safeguard the health of our communities, protect wildlife, and preserve our remaining wild places through grassroots activism, public education, lobbying, and legal action. For more information, visit www.sierraclub.org.

American Airlines Has A Unique Airbus A321neo Subfleet That Unavoidably Breaks Daily Consistency

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Robin Guess | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

American Airlines operates two versions of the Airbus A321neo that look broadly similar to passengers but cannot be made identical. 10 aircraft acquired from Alaska Airlines have a different emergency-exit arrangement from the airline’s factory-delivered A321neos, a structural feature inherited from their original design. Even after American refurbished the former Alaska jets with its standard Oasis cabin, the fuselage layout prevented the airline from matching the larger subfleet’s seating capacity and seat locations.

The result is a small but persistent source of inconsistency across American’s narrowbody operation. Both versions are presented under the same 32Q fleet code, yet they have different total seat counts, exit rows, and cabin details. Passengers selecting seats based on a particular map may therefore find that an aircraft substitution changes the location of extra-legroom seating or places them in a different part of the cabin. For the airline, the challenge is not simply maintaining two interiors. It is operating a single fleet designation across aircraft whose underlying layouts cannot be standardized through a conventional cabin retrofit.

The subfleet traces its origins to Virgin America, which ordered the aircraft before Alaska Airlines acquired the carrier in 2016. Alaska inherited the Airbus jets as part of the transaction, despite operating an otherwise Boeing-focused mainline fleet. When Alaska later moved to simplify its operations around Boeing narrowbodies, it retired the 10 A321neos and sold them to American in 2023. Only four of the 10 aircraft entered service with Virgin America; the other six joined Alaska directly after the acquisition. By the time American took ownership, the aircraft were relatively young, averaging about 5.6 years old when Alaska retired them. Purchasing an existing group offered American access to additional A321neo capacity without waiting for new production slots, which were already constrained by strong global demand for the type.

The aircraft began entering American’s operation in spring 2024, initially flying from Miami with much of Alaska’s original cabin still intact. At that stage, they had 190 seats, including 16 in first class and 174 in the main cabin. The layout offered a more spacious experience than American’s standard A321neo, with greater seat pitch in several sections, but fewer first class places for complimentary upgrades.

American’s acquisition was therefore a practical fleet decision, not a plan to create a permanently distinct passenger product. The airline intended to bring the aircraft closer to its established domestic configuration. However, the jets’ original door arrangement would prove to be the limiting factor. The timing also offered an opportunity to add capacity relatively quickly. New aircraft deliveries require production slots, financing, and time to enter service, whereas the former Alaska jets were already built and had accumulated comparatively little operating history. Their acquisition helped American expand its narrowbody resources while it awaited additional Airbus deliveries. The trade-off was accepting a small group of aircraft whose design would complicate efforts to maintain a uniform cabin.

The core of the problem is the development of Airbus Cabin Flex, or ACF. Early A321neo aircraft were built with a door arrangement broadly resembling the previous-generation A321ceo: four main passenger doors on each side of the fuselage, including an additional pair positioned ahead of the wing. The 10 former Alaska aircraft belong to this earlier A321neo configuration. Airbus introduced Cabin Flex to give airlines more freedom over cabin layouts and passenger capacity. The redesign removed the second pair of full-size doors and introduced modified overwing emergency exits, alongside changes to the rear fuselage and door positions. This created additional usable cabin space and allowed airlines to install more seats while meeting evacuation requirements. Airbus said the ACF arrangement could support configurations of up to 240 passengers, depending on the chosen layout.

The revised design became standard on later A321neo production, but the former Alaska aircraft were built before that transition. Their physical structure cannot be converted into the later Cabin Flex arrangement through a normal interior refurbishment. Changing the exit layout would require extensive structural work, certification, and significant investment, making a full conversion commercially impractical for a group of only 10 aircraft. American’s larger fleet is made up of A321-200NX aircraft, while the former Alaska jets are classified as A321-200N models. The suffix reflects a difference in the airframe configuration, not merely the airline’s choice of seats or cabin finishes.

American Airlines has received four new Airbus jets in one week as its A321neo and A321XLR fleets expand.

American began bringing the former Alaska aircraft into its standard interior in 2024, with N950XV among the first to undergo the work in San Antonio. The retrofit replaced the inherited cabin with the airline’s Oasis-style arrangement, aligning the passenger experience more closely with other domestic A321s. However, the door configuration remained unchanged, limiting how many seats could be installed and where certain rows could be positioned.

After the refurbishment, each former Alaska A321neo has 190 seats. The airline’s 196-seat A321neo configuration has the same number of first class seats, but accommodates more in the main cabin. Six additional economy seats distinguish the larger group. The former Alaska aircraft effectively retain the capacity of American’s older A321ceo domestic layout, despite being newer-generation aircraft with more efficient engines. The original Alaska cabin had fewer first class seats and more generous spacing in several sections, but American’s standardization work increased first class from 16 to 20 seats and brought economy seating closer to the airline’s usual density. Passengers no longer receive the same broad spacing advantage associated with the aircraft’s initial configuration, but the aircraft still cannot carry as many passengers as the ACF-equipped A321neos.

American Airlines Airbus A321neo Cabin Comparison

For American, the six-seat shortfall is a permanent capacity constraint on each of the 10 aircraft. The figure is modest relative to the airline’s overall operation, but it illustrates how a seemingly small design difference can affect revenue potential over thousands of flights. There is also a cabin-planning consequence. American has to accommodate the same general product standards within a narrower seating footprint, rather than simply copying the exact arrangement used on its newer aircraft. The result is a familiar-looking cabin with a different internal map. Passengers may notice little difference in the overall appearance, but the location of individual seats remains tied to the original aircraft design.

The practical consequence is most visible when passengers select seats. American groups both A321neo versions under the same 32Q fleet code, so the booking process does not always make the underlying configuration obvious. Yet the exit rows occupy different positions because the doors and emergency exits are arranged differently. According to SeatCompare’s 2026 guide, the 196-seat A321neo has exit rows at 17 and 27. On the 10 former Alaska aircraft, the corresponding rows are 11 and 24.

The differences extend beyond emergency exits. The seat maps have different last rows, with row 37 on the 196-seat aircraft and row 36 on the former Alaska jets. Lavatory placement also changes the available seats near the rear of the cabin. SeatCompare notes that the port-side lavatory block removes different A-C positions in the two configurations, meaning the cabin columns do not line up in the same way from one aircraft to the other. Some seats are also narrower or have restricted recline, but the affected rows vary by version. As a result, a passenger comparing seat maps needs more than the aircraft name to identify the best option. The registration numbers N950XV through N959XV identify the former Alaska group.

Maintaining two cabin layouts within one fleet code creates work for American’s reservation systems, airport teams, and customers. Seat maps must accurately reflect the aircraft assigned to a flight, while operational substitutions can make a previously selected seat unavailable or alter its characteristics. While aircraft swaps are a normal part of airline operations, the fleet differences can cause some challenges.

There are also commercial reasons for American to retain the aircraft rather than retire or substantially rebuild them. They are comparatively young, use the same engine family as the airline’s other A321neos, and provide useful capacity on domestic routes. Replacing them would require acquiring aircraft or reallocating existing capacity, while a structural conversion would be costly and difficult to justify for just 10 frames.

American’s larger A321neo fleet is also expanding. ch-aviation reported in February 2026 that the airline operated 75 A321-200NX aircraft alongside the 10 A321-200N jets, with additional standard-range A321neos and XLRs on order. As new aircraft arrive, the former Alaska group will represent a smaller proportion of the overall A321neo fleet, but the configuration mismatch will remain as long as those 10 aircraft continue flying.

American’s former Alaska A321neos demonstrate how aircraft standardization has limits when airlines inherit equipment designed around a different specification. The Oasis retrofit brought their interiors closer to the rest of the fleet, but no cabin refresh can relocate doors or reproduce the Cabin Flex fuselage. Their six-seat capacity gap and alternative exit-row positions are therefore likely to persist.

As American receives more A321neo aircraft, the 10 jets should become a smaller share of the overall fleet. That may reduce how frequently passengers encounter the alternate seat map, but it will not remove the need for accurate aircraft-specific information. The most useful improvement would be clearer differentiation in booking tools, allowing customers to identify the 190-seat version before paying for a particular seat. For airlines increasingly selling individual cabin positions as products, reliable seat-map accuracy is part of the service itself, not merely a back-office detail.

European trading venue 21X and LayerZero say a private, permissioned deployment of Zero handled 10,000 order matches per second in a month-long technical pilot. The companies disclosed the result on November 4, reporting a 45-fold performance improvement against the EVM layer-two baseline they used. It is an unusually specific benchmark in a market full of throughput promises, although an internal pilot is not the same as sustained production trading under real market stress.

The partners say they ran a fully onchain central limit order book with an emphasis on US equity tokens. Matching orders onchain is technically different from recording a trade after an offchain matching engine has already decided the outcome. The reported throughput would therefore address a genuine challenge for blockchain-based venues. The private instance also matters: network membership and visibility can be controlled differently from a permissionless public chain. Comparing the result with public networks requires care because validators, transaction patterns, hardware assumptions and network conditions may not be equivalent.

Regulated markets need surveillance, market-access controls, records, resilience and procedures for trade errors. Some institutions are unwilling or unable to expose all transactional details on a public ledger. A permissioned environment can allow more predictable operations while still using programmable execution and a shared data layer. The trade-off is that permissioning changes trust and governance: someone must define who can participate, how upgrades occur and what happens when the network is unavailable. A fast benchmark does not settle those governance questions or prove that the system is suitable for every regulated market.

Performance comparisons are only as good as their baseline. LayerZero says the pilot was around 45 times faster than a referenced layer-two EVM setup. That is useful for the experiment's stated conditions, but it should not be generalized to all blockchains or all workloads. Throughput can vary when orders are cancelled, modified or subjected to complex compliance checks. Latency, finality, settlement integrity and recovery from outages matter as well. A trading venue would also need to demonstrate that market data and order priority remain fair at high volumes.

Tokenized securities are often described primarily as a custody or issuance problem. But active exchange trading introduces difficult requirements around price-time priority, matching, market making and operations during volatile conditions. The partnership suggests infrastructure providers are taking those less visible tasks seriously. The promise of running an order book onchain is more auditable shared state, but that benefit depends on execution design and on who can inspect the records. A private test is a starting point rather than proof of an operating securities market.

The next milestones are independent testing, disclosure of methodology, resilient deployment across multiple participants and approval for any relevant regulated activities. Real trading volume and incident history would be more persuasive than repeating peak performance figures. If 21X and LayerZero can demonstrate those outcomes, this could become an important example of high-throughput financial infrastructure. Until then, the strongest conclusion is that the companies reported a promising benchmark under controlled conditions.
