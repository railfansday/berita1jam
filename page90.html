On the Couch with Leslie Bibb

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Following standout performances on series like The White Lotus, actress Leslie Bibb shares her design philosophies amid her home renovation.

Since appearing in season three of HBO’s The White Lotus in 2025, American actress Leslie Bibb has had no problem keeping busy. The North Dakota-born, Virginia-raised actress already had screen credits spanning from the Iron Man films to Apple TV+’s Palm Royale, but Bibb’s performance on Mike White’s hit series as one-third of a slightly toxic friendship trio spotlighted the actress’s knack for blending comedy and drama. Not to mention, her blunt bob served as hair inspiration for the masses. After The White Lotus, Bibb went on to guest star on HBO’s Hacks, landing her an Emmy nomination for Outstanding Guest Actress in a Comedy.

Outside her day job, Bibb also happens to be a design enthusiast—she’s currently renovating the upstate New York home that she shares with longtime partner, fellow actor Sam Rockwell. For the interiors, Bibb has teamed up with ELLE Decor A-List designer Shawn Henderson, who often pops up on Bibb’s Instagram account, attending birthday parties and surveying the concrete shell that will one day be a pool. In the kitchen, Bibb is partnering with LG Signature on appliances that gel with the new renovation.

Next up for Bibb is a starring role alongside Brett Goldstein in Amazon Prime series Escorted. She is also executive producing a show with HBO called The Rush Whisperer, a dark comedy about sorority rush. Together with the home renovation, she has trouble choosing what to highlight. “I’m just grateful that I’m able to juggle work and life and my relationship with Sam,” says the actress. For our On the Couch survey, she shares her greatest design inspirations, entertaining tips, and the artists that have her heart.

Favorite Eras of Design: All of them... well, most of them. I'm not into cold, modern pieces from the 1980s or that shabby chic stuff. But I do like a house that isn't just one thing, that is a mix of eras and has warmth—a house that is inviting and says, “Come in, kick off your shoes, grab a drink and let's hang out.” And I love color.

Favorite Sofa: I don't have a favorite sofa, but I do love an L-shaped sofa for watching TV so Sammy and I can be together but also have our own spaces to sprawl out.

Maverick design style trick: I feel incredibly lucky to have my friend and fabulous interior designer, Shawn Henderson, as my secret weapon. Home renovations can be a nightmare, but having Shawn as my partner in crime has really made the experience so much fun. And let's be honest, the kitchen is the heart of any home. When I think of LG Signature, I think about how they design to honor your lifestyle rather than compete with it.

Most beloved buildings: I love the Chrysler Building. It always takes my breath away. Also, there is a church where my parents are buried in the country and I just love it. It is so small and quaint and the view from its windows and the grounds just make me smile.

Destination Museum: Musée d'Orsay because I get to see my favorite painting by Gustave Caillebotte called The Floor Scrapers. Honestly, wandering through museums or art galleries is one of my happy places so in any city you will find me making a pit stop to get an art fix and steal some inspo. I just saw the Krasner and Pollock "Past Continuous" exhibit at the Met and it blew my socks off.

Go-to gallery or galleries: Any gallery, anywhere. Just go in and see what people are making. It will always inspire you in some way.

Favorite Artist/Artwork: I love art and wandering around a museum or gallery is one of my safe spaces. I am mesmerized by pieces from Noah Davis. I love Helen Frankenthaler and Hilma af Klint. Also, I love Nick Cave and Danny Fox and Ramon Enrich and Peter Doig. The photographs from Sally Mann I love, too. Honestly, there are so many. I am in awe.

Favorite getaway: Staycation...and hopefully at our home upstate once we are done!

Best Host/Hostess Gift: I love to wrap a bottle of olive oil in a great kitchen towel so it is two gifts in one. I like a gift that can be put to use.

Entertaining Tips: Always have a well-stocked bar with loads of options and feed your guests. Also, make sure you have a killer dance mix going because it ain't a party unless people start dancing. And if you have more than 25 people, hire a bartender. Otherwise you won't get to socialize and you will be just hosting all night and you will be exhausted.

Cocktail or Mocktail of Choice: I am obsessed with this nonalcoholic Kolsch beer by Best Day Brewing Company. And winter is upon us, so a great martini. I am in Italy right now and am besotted with Negronis.

Watch to Wear: There are two I really love: one I own that Sam gave me so long ago, my Rolex, and the other is one I want to get, which is the Cartier Baignoire.

The value of combining legal knowledge, technology, workflow design and project management in e-discovery

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ari Kaplan recently spoke with Chantelle Jalland, the associate director of advanced e-discovery and digital services for Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the United States at Ashurst Perkins Coie; and Satinder Soni, the managing director for the United Kingdom at Cimplifi.

Ari Kaplan: Tell us about your background and your role at Ashurst Perkins Coie.

Chantelle Jalland: I qualified as a lawyer in Australia almost 20 years ago, and when I moved to the U.K., I quickly moved into the world of e-discovery. At that stage, what attracted me to e-discovery was predictive coding, which meant the machine would learn from those decisions and reduce the need for humans in that really boring manual review phase. When we fast-forward to the release of gen AI, what was incredibly exciting for me was that you could use LLMs to code the documents from a prompt instead of that initial training round. In my current role, I feel incredibly fortunate to work with lawyers who want to use the latest technology to do things differently, so I don’t have to fight like I used to. My remit now at Ashurst Perkins Coie is much broader than e-discovery. I have a team that also builds custom AI solutions across transactional and advisory practice groups, so I very much appreciate the industry recognition moving toward this legal data intelligence.

Ari Kaplan: Tell us about your background and your role at Cimplifi.

Satinder Soni: I’ve been in the industry for over 20 years. My career started on the right side of the EDRM model, focusing on courtroom technology, transcription services and evidence presentation in court. E-discovery was just surfacing at that point. It really wasn’t a thing. I then moved into an e-discovery software company, which was acquired by several conglomerates. I then moved into a global risk consultancy, Control Risks, working with the investigations team on cyber breach investigations, digital forensics and e-discovery. I then moved to Ankura, which took a different approach to e-discovery, focusing more on analytics and subject-matter expertise in construction and turnaround restructuring, forensic accounting and white-collar crime investigations. Then I moved to Cimplifi, a U.S.-based company focused on e-discovery, investigations and contract analytics. They moved to the U.K. almost two years ago. Our U.S. clients had cross-border matters that needed U.K.-based expertise to be compliant with GDPR. We have grown our team here. We have a project management team with diverse professional skills, ranging from consultancies and accountancy firms to technology companies. It’s a great company to be in.

Ari Kaplan: What’s making e-discovery more complex today, and how is that changing the way you approach each new matter at the outset?

Chantelle Jalland: One of the biggest challenges is the size of new datasets. We had over 45 terabytes of data for a recent matter. I’ve handled some really large cases before, so this wasn’t the biggest, but given the facts of the case and the scope of the material issues in the dispute, I expected the volume to be much smaller. It was a surprise, based on the facts, that it was that large, and it was simply the product of more data being created today. We also have platform switching. This morning, I was on a call with a colleague about a particular client matter, then received an email on the matter to an external party, then I had some internal Teams chat messages, and in one of those messages, they also shared a live document with status updates. That was all within a few minutes, so everything was moving so much faster, and there were four different sources of information on one particular matter or issue. If you think about that and expand it, if every single person is just adding this tiny bit of additional information, it’s going to lead to a data explosion. That’s also the biggest opportunity because the large matters are the ones that are interesting and exciting. They’ve got things that will go wrong or problems, and you want those differences to make it more interesting and something new to solve.

Ari Kaplan: What e-discovery challenges do your clients most often ask you to solve?

Satinder Soni: Everything has a different variable, and each e-discovery matter is a challenge because it is a reactive industry. Our clients come in quite hard and fast, and we have to take a step back to understand the data scoping. I have a separate challenge where they underestimate how much data there is, so you have to ease the shock of what it’s actually going to cost. But if you apply the right technology, phase it out, structure the workflow and talk them through it, the challenge then is pacing it, being realistic and providing timelines. The latest challenge really is applying AI into their workflows and driving the mindset shift they need to use a different type of analytics. They’ve just gotten used to CAL, and there is a bit of fatigue with it being more efficient and more cost-effective. We say it with every new tool we bring in, and the challenge is always the mindset shift.

Ari Kaplan: The Ashurst Perkins Coie Advanced Legal Solutions offering combines legal knowledge, technology, workflow design, project management and flexible resourcing. How does that combination enhance client service, and how do you define success before the work begins?

Chantelle Jalland: There’s tremendous value in having multiple service offerings under one roof. We also have our risk advisory or consulting arm, which enables us to undertake deep data analytics tasks, and this gives us what we refer to internally as our triple threat. We have Advance, our new law arm, risk advisory and our core services team. Before the work begins, much of the success comes back to those foundational principles, and it builds on what Sati mentioned about managing expectations on timelines and being realistic. Historically, a partner will promise a client that we will deliver something in a certain amount of time for a certain cost, and that promise may not be realistic because the scope fundamentally changes or the expectation isn’t technically possible within the timeframe. They didn’t understand the basic principle that if you don’t understand the component, you can’t advise in that area, and that’s where we bring the right people into the conversation earlier. We have that open conversation and set small milestones or goals, so we’re meeting our client’s expectations along the way. And like everyone, I take great pride in an accurate cost estimate. But really, that data can expand at a different rate compared to a previous matter that you might have done an estimate on, or there’s a subset of data that’s got a processing error. There are so many things that can vary from what you might expect. As it shifts, it’s about having that conversation and letting everyone know it’s different from the original ask, and what I’ve found is clients appreciate that openness and willingness to have the conversation. You are advising, and there’s a lot more respect for the advice and the experience that can be offered in this area.

Ari Kaplan: Where are clients rethinking their approach most often, understanding their data earlier, managing review, coordinating providers, or generally adjusting their litigation strategy?

Satinder Soni: All of the above. E-discovery is a complex process. I’ve seen it where you’ve got your litigation strategy set, then you do the data scoping piece, and then you look at the early data and assess it. And when the review is happening, that’s when they realize we’re missing a custodian or this data source. Then the litigation strategy has to be readjusted. Then we go back to the data assessment. It can expand into other litigation support service lines, like asset tracing, or you need a forensic accountant. Chantelle put it really well. Communication is key. Understanding your stakeholders is part of that. Setting realistic timelines is part of that. It flits between the litigation strategy and understanding your data and that feedback loop.

Ari Kaplan: What needs to happen earlier in a matter for a legal team, the e-discovery team and a service provider to work most effectively together?

Chantelle Jalland: We tend to manage matters in-house with our legal team, but we also work with service providers on some matters. Sometimes a client has a preexisting relationship with a preferred provider, or we choose to engage a service provider due to their expertise. We recently had a matter involving historic offline databases that were in the format of a decommissioned product, so we needed to convert them for a current document review platform. It was more efficient to use an existing solution, rather than script something custom for that matter. What needs to happen to work effectively together is to get on a call early with all the right people. You can cut through so much with a phone call, making sure everyone is working toward the same plan from the outset.

Ari Kaplan: When your team is engaged by a client under pressure, what distinguishes a trusted adviser from a provider that simply executes instructions?

Satinder Soni: You should never be an order taker. Just because the client says it, doesn’t mean it’s always the right thing. They come to us because we are experts in the field. They trust us to guide and support them through the process. Often, it goes wrong when the client calls, and everyone’s excited, and they dive right in, applying the same methodology from the previous project. Take a step back. Standard information to ask for at the beginning includes e-data, what has happened, the discovery objective, the goals they’re trying to achieve, and the timeline they’re working toward. Review data types, nonstandard files, foreign languages and whether the data is structured or unstructured. Really get into the details that will influence the e-discovery project and help it achieve its goals. We advise gathering as much information as possible in advance, so we can choose the right technology, manage timelines and predict accurate costs. In those instances, accurate cost prediction is also key. Our job as a trusted adviser is to say, “We can save costs if we do it this way.”

Ari Kaplan: Where is AI making the most meaningful difference in your work today, and where is its promise still a bit ahead of its practical value?

Chantelle Jalland: AI has meaningfully changed my work. From a personal productivity perspective, AI call summaries make follow-up emails and meeting organization much easier. When I check a draft email, there are several small examples I use every day. On a larger scale, for high-impact matters, we have seen really effective use in due diligence, whether it’s legislative reviews for businesses or from an advisory perspective. The area where I see the most promise is large-scale document reviews. We are using AI for document review in a way that replaces CAL. So you filter down, you get a set of, say, 20,000 documents, and run it through. It is more effective than traditional manual review. It’s more effective than CAL. However, I see huge leaps ahead when it comes to document review. Most excitingly, I think, coming back to what Sati said earlier about working in a reactive industry, we are doing everything later. It’s actually about bringing those AI use cases further forward. Within a corporation, it’s about tracking themes, using the data more effectively, so you can identify the last 10 litigation matters or regulatory investigations that are actually costing you money, analyze that data, and then try to prevent future matters. That’s where it’s really exciting.

Ari Kaplan: When would you choose not to use AI in a litigation support matter?

Satinder Soni: We should all be using it in some way, shape or form. Traditionally, it was suggested that not all matters needed AI or analytics, either because it was cost-prohibitive, the dataset wasn’t compatible, or there wasn’t enough data to perform analysis. Recently, we’ve seen a shift toward using AI on small matters, such as DSARs. I have seen clients use AI on DSARs, and it seems effective. It frees up time for project managers to do more thoughtful work because DSARs don’t really go anywhere. They’re weaponized most of the time. So if they’re using it on investigation and litigation matters, their time is better spent. This is where AI can be used thoughtfully. There are still alternatives. CAL is being phased out, but it’s still there and still serves as a bridge to advising against linear review. But if people are not yet comfortable using AI, which hopefully they are, it’s in your everyday work now, so I just think, piece by piece, they’re trying it. I can’t think of any instance of not using AI.

Ari Kaplan: As we leverage technology to perform more work, which human skills become increasingly valuable, and how do you develop them within your team?

Chantelle Jalland: Communication is hugely important, so it’s something I’m focusing on from a recruitment perspective. We have to think about which skills can be taught and which are harder to teach. There’s analytical thinking. We need to make sure that the advisory stage isn’t so automated or process-driven that it’s replaced by AI. It’s about understanding that judgment is still needed and how to apply it. AI will be useful in many contexts, but how you use it will vary by use case. The risk is quite high if you don’t check the outputs, for example. It’s also about making sure people understand that risk. When it comes to training, it’s not a one-and-done. It’s continuous, and the same is true for communication. In a global law firm, different teams adopt different styles, and what works for one team doesn’t work for another. It’s about continuous learning without people feeling fatigued by constant learning.

Ari Kaplan: What are the hallmarks of great client service in an AI-enabled environment?

Satinder Soni: Great client service is all about communication, even when it’s bad news. Hopefully, it’s never bad news, but when it is, pick up the phone and have a conversation. We’ve gone crazy with short-form messaging, and what can be said in 10 emails can be said in a five-minute conversation. Then, with AI, be thoughtful about how we’re using it. Don’t fall into the trap of using AI for everything, and keep learning. There is a job you need to do: Put clients first, break down tasks, add structure to your work, and don’t just let AI take control.

Ari Kaplan: How do you see e-discovery and legal tech evolving?

Chantelle Jalland: I am very positive and optimistic about the future of e-discovery. There are many opportunities because, essentially, all the button-clicking will be gone soon, which is a great opportunity for people who work in e-discovery. You can apply a wide range of tools. It’s not just one technology. There are MCP connectors that you can use to adapt to certain platforms. So you are going to have so much more flexibility in the options. It does create more risk, potentially, if you’re not following a standard protocol, but what is exciting is that you can differentiate yourself in the market by specializing in solutions for certain use cases. Going back to first principles: understanding the problem, how you’re going to solve it, and considering all your options. It’s not going to be as linear. In the next five years, I would even say in the next year, we will be having a very different conversation about workflows in particular.

Gina, Jan and Harry return to the car. Only Jan gets in. In his quivering, fearful state, Eddie trips and drops the detonator.

That’s Jan up in smoke, along with Harry’s plans for a quiet life. It may spell the end of Jan and all her problems, but it’s only the beginning of Eddie’s. He now has Mad Dog O’Connor to answer to, and Harry.

Then the credits roll, and what a gloriously cruel place to leave it.

The only other loose end is Frankie. We’re left to assume he met a long, painful death at the business end of Maeve’s car battery, but we never see a body. I truly hope he does a Harry and wriggles out of one last tight hole—Maeve’s hole—with one good knee and a long memory.

Season 3 can’t come soon enough. When Harry works out whose finger was on the detonator, Eddie’ll wish a knifey little twerp is all he could be.

When the client is a good fit on paper, but still not a good fit

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I was so excited to work with a new client. 

Perfect for my expertise and the type of company I prefer to work with. Meeting the client for an exploratory call felt like a dream. A well-respected brand to add to my portfolio.

But once the contract was signed, it was a struggle from Day 1. We parted ways six months later. 

Most advice about struggling client relationships assumes there is an obvious problem, like that the client is overly demanding or hard to reach. But in this case, it was something else. The vibes were off. When that happens, it’s less obvious what to fix. 

Recognizing the difference between a difficult client and a poor fit can help you decide sooner whether to adjust the working relationship or simply walk away.

Why a client can look right on paper and still not work

The things I vet for during the sales process can be different from the things that make a working relationship successful. 

Before signing, I look at the potential client’s industry and the scope of work. The client’s budget has to be aligned with my rate, and I have to feel confident that I can deliver what the client is asking for. That’s the relationship “on paper.”

But what’s often unknown is how the client interacts with a consultant or contractor (like me). I’m also unaware of any internal politics at the company that might impact the working relationship. Those details usually become apparent once I start delivering actual work. 

Is it a difficult client or a poor fit?

A difficult client has problems like not paying on time or asking for work outside of the scope. These can be fixed (or at least managed) with a solid contract and clear boundaries. Most of the biggest client red flags solopreneurs face are in this category.

A poor fit is different. The client may pay on time and stay within scope, but other parts of the working relationship feel strained. Maybe stakeholder feedback gets under your skin because of how it’s presented. Or you find yourself dreading the work, even though, on paper, it’s what you had agreed to do.

One thing to ask yourself: Would a different consultant or contractor have the same trouble with this client? If the answer is yes, you’re likely dealing with a difficult client. But if you can picture someone else doing great work for them, the problem is probably fit.

A second question: If this client always pays on time and never asked for anything outside of scope, would you still want the work? A “no” usually means fit is the issue.

What to try before you walk away

A poor fit sometimes might improve if you’re able to directly address the thing that’s causing you angst. It’s worth trying before you decide.

If the problem is, for example, “too many cooks” providing you with feedback and contradictory opinions, you might be able to solve this. Something like:

“It’s easier for me to make sure I deliver what you need if you consolidate your feedback. Can you work on your end to send me a list of thoughts/changes? That way, I don’t start down one path only to find that you want me to go in a different direction.”

In some cases, your client might be willing to change — they may simply not have experience working with a contractor or consultant. 

You might also give yourself a checkpoint, like an additional month of work after the conversation. If nothing has changed by then, you have your answer.

Admitting the relationship isn’t working

When you work solo, a poor fit can drag you down. In a company, a colleague might take over the account, or a manager could reassign the work. As a solopreneur, there’s no one to hand it to. And since your income depends on the work, ending the relationship means replacing that revenue.

But staying has a cost. Stress. Frustration. All the things you were supposed to escape by choosing a solopreneur life instead of a corporate job.

If the conversation with your client doesn’t change anything, more effort probably won’t fix it. At that point, calling it a poor fit and moving on is a business decision. 

It helps to remember that a poor fit says nothing about your skills or the client’s character. It simply didn’t work out.

In another case involving a cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) simulation, testers noted that one pair of glasses informed the wearer that emergency services had been called and were scheduled to arrive at the scene, despite no one having made the call.

Some glasses did not properly process safety warnings on children’s toys, others made mistakes when reading medication leaflets, and some misinterpreted warnings for extreme weather events.

The AP did not specify the models it tested, saying only that they tested six models ranging from under €100 ($110) to over €500 ($550), including two that "were extensively tested in simulated real-life situations where human safety played a key role."

A spokesperson also declined to match any specific behavior to a model by price point (minor dating advice linked to a €354 pair of glasses, for example), adding that the research was intended to highlight the risks of AI glasses in a broader sense.

"Glasses that film you unnoticed are already concerning. Glasses that also give you incorrect advice when someone's life is at stake go a step too far," said Monique Verdier, vice chair at the AP.

"AI offers many opportunities, but we must be able to trust that technology. With the camera glasses examined, that trust cannot currently be justified."

The warning issued this week follows the regulator's previous work on smart glasses. The AP reminded the public in September that everyone has the right to privacy and encouraged organizations to ban them where necessary.

The same warning mentioned the potential risks regarding data processing under the GDPR, as well as those related to the training of AI.

The AP doubled down on these earlier criticisms, saying the latest tests showed that the AI functions rely on processing data using servers mostly located outside of the European Union. Relatedly, it found that users were not given sufficient information about how the AI was processing the data they captured.

On a cybersecurity note, the tests threw up a range of issues, including the ability for people nearby the glasses-wearer to connect to the device without permission, view recordings, and in some cases operate the camera.

Further, the regulator echoed the pre-existing concerns about smart specs - those that helped give them their colloquial name “pervert glasses.”

Its tests found that with "virtually all of the camera glasses examined," the recording lights that are supposed to clearly show when a user is recording were dim and easily coverable.

The AP said that models could also be wangled into recording even when that light indicator was covered, which goes against what manufacturers so often claim.

"As a result, bystanders can barely see whether they are being filmed," it said.

The technology has a number of beneficial use cases, such as assisting the visually impaired, those with lower literacy levels, or for any number of other scenarios where people need help understanding the world around them

However, the watchdog's official stance on the technology, as it is today, deems smart glasses "unsafe… due to the risks to users and bystanders."

Verdier said politicians in the Netherlands and across Europe should take note of the results when developing AI policy.

"It is important that we, as a society, learn to deal with these types of high-risk AI products," she said.

"Simply banning everything provides some leeway in the beginning, but we cannot sustain that indefinitely. It is important that we collectively work on awareness, resilience, and ethical conscience."

When DEF CON announced a no pervert glasses policy ahead of its event in August, the move was broadly welcomed by attendees.

"Love to see a ‘no pervert glasses’ policy at DEF CON," said Eva Galperin, EFF director of cybersecurity, at the time.

Several other organizations have implemented similar restrictions, including Monopoly Events, which manages UK Comic Cons, national treasure pub chain Wetherspoons, and even Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE).

Most members of the general public will have at least come to terms with the fact that the smartphone generation, and their willingness to snap shots anywhere and everywhere, means there is no enshrined human right to privacy in public spaces.

Public video recording once carried an air of expectation or consideration for bystanders’ personal privacy, although smartphone users have slowly eradicated that over time.

Like it or not, it's generally understood that a smartphone is usually recording something - or someone - at any given time, especially in urban areas.

On paper, the fact that smart glasses can record in public spaces isn’t wildly different from what’s already come to be expected from smartphones.

However, given how similar they appear to analog glasses and the ability to cover the recording light, it makes the act of public recording via glasses far more surreptitious, and thus concerning.

With smartphones, it's easy to see from the user's body language - holding the phone out in front of them - that they are recording. There are no overt signals displayed by wearers of smart glasses during image or video capture, which fuels a sense of unease about the technology in general.

A quick search online for "smart glasses privacy violation" yields a slew of complaints about the technology, often regarding behavior toward women and children.

There are also worries about how easily the devices can get past security checks, potentially providing opportunities to those who wish to cause greater harm than a mere blight on personal privacy.

A joint investigation by The Observer and BBC Radio 4 recently revealed how AI-enabled glasses went undetected through the airport-style security scanners used in the UK parliament.

The two news outlets found that a £60 ($78) pair of Chinese-made glasses bypassed the scanners and could be used to read upside-down documents on MPs' desks, recording data such as phone numbers and addresses to be accessed at a later point. ®
