This week, Bob Dylan is knock, knock, knockin’ on heaven’s door — well, arguably the closest earthly thing to it — with the folk-rock icon meeting Pope Leo XIV at the Vatican on Wednesday (Nov. 4).

The Catholic headquarters revealed that the two figures had linked up by sharing a photo on Instagram of them shaking hands. With the pope smiling warmly in his usual traditional white robes, Dylan wears a hooded black zip-up underneath a jacket and looks at the Bishop of Rome intently.

“Pope Leo XIV met with American singer-songwriter Bob Dylan in a private audience on Wednesday, November 4,” the caption simply reads.

Pope Leo XIV ascended to the religious institution’s top job in May last year following the death of Pope Francis, becoming the first American-born cardinal to do so. Charlotte Roy leads All Saints to 1st district cross country title

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

All Saints recorded yet another first for its girls cross country since Dale Meskimen took over a couple of years ago. Two years ago, the Saints won their first girls cross country county title. On Wednesday, they won their first district title.

With a dominant effort led by individual winner Charlotte Roy, All Saints ran away to win with the girls team title in the Class 1A, District 5 meet at Taylor Park in Largo.

Roy won the girls race in 18:17.80 as she and Lakeland Christian junior Emma Wilkinson (18:56.90) were the only two runners to run sub-19 minutes. All Saints finished with 30 points with Indian Rocks Christian far behind in second place with 59 points.

"It was not bad at all," Meskimen said. "I'm pretty pleased."

Roy ran the race as Meskimen wanted as she took control in the final half mile. The Saints placed four runners in the top 10 as Kyle Cook finished sixth (20:07.20), Lindsey Cook finished eighth (20:29.10) and Savannah Simonds took ninth (20:40.10).

All Saints' No. 5 runner Julia Marra Andrade finished 13th in 21:31.10 and even No. 6 runner Maggie Koch (24th in 22:58.40) finished ahead of Indian Rocks Christian's No. 5 runner.

"Charlotte ran basically the way we wanted to," Meskimen said. "Kylie was a little off today. Out of all the groups, she's probably the one that didn't run the way I was expecting, but she still didn't, obviously, run horrible. Lindsey ran a season best today. It's the third race in a row where she's ran faster, so I'm hoping that that's a sign that her leg is coming back at the right time. Then 4 and 5, Savannah and Julia, they only missed their PR by one second."

Wilkinson led the LCS girls to a third-place finish as Kenley Allen was 11th in 21:04.10, and Kenzie Reber was 16yh in 21:42.90. LCS coach Milton Lyons said he put his runners through a tough week so he wasn't worried about their times this week.

"She (Wilkinson) ran slightly faster than she did last year on tired legs," he said. "The goal for her is to peek at states and try to get as close to that 18-minute mark as, as possible. So that's the biggest goal."

Victory Christian's Ryleigh McInnes finished 17th in 21:49 as the Storm finished seventh.

Lakeland Christian graduated eight seniors from last year's district champion teams but that didn't stop the Vikings from repeating as team champion.

Led by junior Kage Goldsmith's fourth-place finish in 17:50.40, Lakeland Christian finished with 61 points to run away for the easy team win. Indian Rocks Christian was far back with 100 points. It was the seventh district title in school history for the boys cross country team.

LCS had four runners finish in the top 20. Seventh-grader Reed Corbett ran 17:59.50 to finish seventh, followed by freshman Ayden Norris in 13th place (18:42.30) and freshman George Asche in 18th place (18:54.90).

The Vikings' No. 5 runner, freshman Luke Glenn, was 21st (19:06.80).

"It was great for them, considering we lost eight seniors last year, so I'll take that any day," Lyons said. "The times were not there, but that I knew that going into it because of the weather, and we had a tough week of practice on purpose because, we're just trying to peak at the right time."

Santa Fe Catholic junior Henry Lauk was the top boys finisher from Polk as he finished third in 17:39.50, about 11 seconds in front of Goldsmith. Victory Christian's Elijah Gonzales finished with in 18:31.90.

10 Classic Rock Albums You Must Listen to Before You Die

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Classic rock is maybe definable as the kind of rock you're likely to hear on classic rock radio stations (unsurprisingly), but a better definition could be narrowing it down to rock released during a certain period. That period was when rock was at its most popular and culturally dominant, and arguably, during that time, rock music was also at its best, so it’s classic rock, and then it also happens to be rock that is classic (in terms of quality and historical importance).

There’s an attempt to single out some particularly great classic rock albums below, all of them worth listening to before you die. Or, less morbidly, listen to them all while you're still breathing; before the bucket’s kicked. That’s still pretty morbid. Sorry. But these albums are amazing, and enough so that you’ve probably heard most of them before, but if you haven’t, they're essential to give a spin at least once before… you know…

It feels uncool to call them Creedence Clearwater Revival instead of just “Creedence,” but either way, here's Cosmo’s Factory, which is probably the band’s best album. Creedence (yeah, let’s be cool for a bit; for fun) is entirely classic rock in sound, really being demonstrative of this particular era of rock, and so sure, Cosmo’s Factory is blunt, certainly straightforward, and pretty easy to listen to quite passively.

Sometimes it’s just what you need, though? Something loud, catchy, and very of its time, but all of those things in good ways. Cosmo’s Factory is strong enough that you might not love Creedence, or the particular kind of rock they made, but you're still likely going to, at the very least, tolerate this particular album. And if you are fond of classic rock and/or Creedence, then it’s safe to say you're going to go well beyond tolerating this, since you'll probably love it.

If there’s one flaw with Damn the Torpedoes, it’s that it starts in a way strong enough that you could argue the momentum isn't entirely made, yet that’s a nitpick, since the album after the first three tracks is still strong. It’s more just that the run from “Refugee” to “Here Comes My Girl” to “Even the Losers” is especially great, with three high-quality songs on their own all feeling perfectly sequenced, and thereby made even better.

But Damn the Torpedoes is not a long album, so once you're three tracks down, there are only six to go, with “Don't Do Me Like That” and “Louisiana Rain” among those remaining songs. Tom Petty and the Heartbreakers had a knack for pumping out punchy rock music early on, so the two albums before Damn the Torpedoes and the one following it are also great, yet it’s Damn the Torpedoes that’s the most consistent and memorable of the bunch.

Make no mistake: The Rolling Stones had a great run throughout a good chunk of the 1960s, but there’s an argument to be made that it was their 1970s run that demonstrated the band at their strongest. Sticky Fingers is especially strong, and the one being singled out here, but it wouldn’t be wise to overlook the 1960s, and then ‘80s-wise, the band was still going fairly strong there, even if their music in that decade wasn’t quite as “classic rock” in sound.

Sticky Fingers is great, and most of it holds up incredibly well over half a century later.

Anyway, Sticky Fingers was the album that helped demonstrate The Rolling Stones could well be the new kings of rock, making a great start for the whole decade, right around a time when another legendary British band had broken up, leaving the future of rock temporarily uncertain, to some extent. More on that other band later, by the way. But yes, Sticky Fingers is great, and most of it holds up incredibly well over half a century on from its initial release.

Easily one of the best double albums of all time, Electric Ladyland is probably The Jimi Hendrix Experience’s greatest album, which is a big take, even without too many albums by the band. It comes down to all those albums being phenomenal and influential, and it’s just the case that Electric Ladyland, with its size and also all the wild things it’s going for – and successfully trying to cram into – a single album.

Electric Ladyland is still such a wonderful and strange album to dive into, even today, with it coming up on nearly six decades since it first came out (safe to assume it would've been even more mind-blowing to hear back in 1968). There’s an undeniably interesting atmosphere to the album overall, and it’s surprisingly consistent, all the while saving two of the best songs until the very end: a cover of Bob Dylan’s “All Along the Watchtower” and then “Voodoo Child (Slight Return).”

This spot could’ve easily gone to Who’s Next, which has the singles and perhaps more historical/cultural importance, so consider that an honorable mention for that possibly easier pick (the one that’s slightly more straightforward, not to mention potentially simpler to praise). But Quadrophenia is even better, and maybe ever so slightly underrated, being like Electric Ladyland in the way it sustains itself for twice as long as a normal album, but then it’s also more of a concept album.

Broadly, Quadrophenia is about struggling to fit in while growing up and dealing with intense isolation, and lots of what it tackles lyrically still packs a punch. The story might not be as comprehensible as was the case with The Who’s Tommy, but it doesn’t really matter when there’s so much to feel throughout Quadrophenia, since it’s very emotional, cathartic at times, and then also a bit intense at times thanks to how full-on the introspection gets at other points.

Bruce Springsteen had a remarkable run throughout the 1970s and 1980s, since it’s that period of time that saw the release of the majority of his all-time great albums. There are albums after that era that are still pretty great, but then again, it’s also hard for anything to be close to Born to Run in terms of quality. And even compared to the other Springsteen albums of those two decades, Born to Run is special.

It does a lot to condense everything great about Springsteen into just eight largely phenomenal tracks, and while they'd all be worth mentioning, it’s especially worth mentioning the title track (an all-timer), “Thunder Road,” and “Jungleland,” the former kicking off Born to Run expertly, and the latter being one essentially perfect closing track. Everything here works, and it’s 40-ish perfect minutes of classic rock, in the end.

It says something about how great Led Zeppelin were, as a band, that you could probably put an album of theirs without “Stairway to Heaven” in a ranking like this and it would still feel right. The first half-dozen albums are all very hard to fault, but among greatness, it’s probably Led Zeppelin IV that well and truly is the greatest, and sure, “Stairway to Heaven” helps, but the other songs here are also fantastic.

Technically, this album’s untitled, but it’s easier to call it Led Zeppelin IV after the band put out three self-titled albums in the couple of years before Led Zeppelin IV. There’s also a great variety of tracks here, with some not being particularly rock-heavy, but then other parts do indeed get very energetic and loud. The slower songs make for a nice contrast, and Led Zeppelin IV transitions from song to song incredibly well, with the pacing and overall flow being impeccable.

There are arguments to be made for several other albums by The Beatles that aren’t Abbey Road to be worthy of being called the best, but it’s Abbey Road that feels like a culmination of the band’s entire run. No shade to those other albums, though, since the band was remarkably consistent and boundary-pushing, especially through the last half of the 1960s (see also Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band, the self-titled or "White" Album, Revolver, Rubber Soul, and Magical Mystery Tour, if you want to count that one).

Abbey Road has some all-time great tracks on it, and the whole thing flows incredibly well, too, especially all those short songs in the second half that make up a medley. It’s got almost everything great about The Beatles packed into one album, and is also significant for being the final one the band recorded, even if Let It Be, largely recorded earlier, was the last album of the band’s to be released (and the only one that didn’t come out in the 1960s, since it dropped in 1970).

Pink Floyd is known for making albums that you can really get lost in, and The Dark Side of the Moon is especially dense and immersive in a mostly good way. “Mostly” because this album is pretty heavy-going at times, so maybe it’s not for every mood, yet it’s for a lot of them, and it is overall about life quite broadly, helping it be a little easier to get into than some of the band’s other great (and also ambitious) rock albums.

So, it’s great within Pink Floyd’s discography, and then when judged on its own, The Dark Side of the Moon is still phenomenal and everything. It’s just such a quintessential album – rock or otherwise – that it’s hard to know how to even praise it or convince someone who’s yet to listen to it that they really need to get on it. Also, it’s probably the kind of thing almost everyone’s heard at this point, so maybe it’s an odd recommendation for present purposes. Still, uh, maybe re-listen to it before you die? Yeah, there we go. That works.

If you want to find great David Bowie songs, you can go as far back as the 1960s, since “Space Oddity” came out then and is an obvious classic, but if you want essential albums of his, it’s best to start in the 1970s. He kept going beyond that decade, of course, with his final album (Blackstar) even being a masterpiece, but the most masterful of his masterpieces is probably The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars.

And since no one has really ever done it better than Bowie, why not also call it the best classic rock album of all time? The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars is just too good, with everything being perfect to the extent that it’s hard to know what else to say. Why bother saying anything when you can just listen? Thankfully, The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars never really gets old, either.

I don't see a difference between trad IRA and Roth in terms of threat to disability benefits. Either the trust is making qualified distributions and paying 3rd parties or it isn't. Is It Over for DAPT After Left Atrial Appendage Closure?

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

— Antithrombotic monotherapy worked at least as well at 6 months

Antithrombotic monotherapy was similar to dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) for 6-month outcomes after left atrial appendage (LAA) closure, the SIMPLAAFY trial showed.

The incidence rate of combined all-cause death, stroke, systemic embolism, or major bleeding at 6 months was 5.1% with reduced-dose non-vitamin K oral anticoagulants (NOACs), 3.3% with aspirin alone, and 6.9% with DAPT, reported Saibal Kar, MD, of Los Robles Hospital and Medical Center in Thousand Oaks, California, at the Transcatheter Cardiovascular Therapeutics annual conference hosted by the Cardiovascular Research Foundation.

Statistically, the strategy of reduced-dose NOACs was at least as good as DAPT (risk ratio [RR] 0.75, 95% CI 0.48-1.18), as was aspirin alone (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.29-0.82).

"These findings support the simplification of antithrombotic management after uncomplicated LAA [closure] and may be individualized according to competing bleeding and device thrombosis risks and patient preferences," Kar said.

The study was simultaneously published in JAMA.

Among the secondary analyses in the trial, there was no excess stroke at 6 months with the aspirin or NOAC monotherapy regimens. However, bleeds alone gave both an edge over DAPT, while low-dose NOAC therapy stood out for a relatively low incidence of device-related thrombus (DRT) at 45 days (0.9% vs 3.4% with DAPT and 2.7% with aspirin).

As it stands, standard practice varies widely for antithrombotic therapy after LAA closure on account of a "lack of legitimate evidence" for guidance, said session discussant Mohamad Alkhouli, MD, of the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota.

"Aspirin seems to emerge a winner," Alkhouli suggested, based on SIMPLAAFY, while he noted that "the jury is still out" for NOACs.

Complicating the picture is the ANDES trial, which found that after LAA closure, short-term NOACs were no better than DAPT at preventing DRT at 60 days, and were associated with even more DRTs at 1 year (5.2% vs 2.3%, P=0.154).

Kar said these data gave him pause on making a blanket recommendation for NOACs after LAA closure.

Meanwhile, 12-month results from SIMPLAAFY are still pending.

"The fact is that we really didn't know what to do with these patients," said Kar. "What we will do beyond 1 year, I don't know."

SIMPLAAFY included patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation who had the Watchman FLX Pro LAA occluder successfully implanted. The investigators randomized 1,857 patients 1:1:1 to aspirin 81 to 100 mg alone, initial DAPT for 6 months (followed by aspirin thereafter), or initial reduced-dose NOACs -- 2.5-mg apixaban (Eliquis) twice daily or 10-mg rivaroxaban (Xarelto) daily -- for 3 months (followed by aspirin thereafter).

Across the three study groups, mean age was 76 years, 43% were women, mean CHA2DS2-VASc score was 4.2, and mean HAS-BLED bleeding risk score was 2.6.

Acute events through 7 days were rare in general. Rates of clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding were 1.0% with aspirin alone, 1.6% with DAPT, and 1.5% with low-dose NOACs; major bleeding rates were 0.2%, 0.5%, and 0.2%, respectively.

The proportion of participants adhering to their assigned medications (for 80% or more days) was 87.9% with aspirin alone, 76.8% with DAPT, and 85.3% with reduced-dose NOACs. "Both monotherapy treatments were better tolerated than DAPT," Kar noted.

A subgroup analysis indicated that people ages 75 and older had disproportionately better outcomes on aspirin (RR 0.34, 95% CI 0.18-0.64) or reduced-dose NOACs (RR 0.53, 95% CI 0.31-0.93).

LAA imaging at 45 days suggested no differences in the Watchman device seal across groups.

Kar stressed that only uncomplicated LAA occluder implants were included in the trial, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Some high-risk populations were also excluded (e.g., those with severe kidney disease, recent stroke, myocardial infarction, or major bleeding).

Of note, there was no prespecified comparison of the two monotherapy arms.

Hong Kong artists create a poetic moment in Venice

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Kingsley Ng’s installation Laundry Nocturne (2026) Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

For Fermata: Hong Kong in Venice, the Hong Kong-born artists Kingsley Ng and Angel Hui are training visitors’ attention on the quiet moments and subtle details connecting both cities. The exhibition, marking the 25th anniversary of Hong Kong’s first participation in the Venice Biennale, is curated by the Hong Kong Museum of Art (HKMoA) and continues until 22 November. It is housed in a historic Venetian residential building in the floating city’s Campo della Tana neighbourhood, a setting that underlines the meditative, lived-in quality to the works.

“What we hope visitors take away from the exhibition is a moment of reflection,” says Dr Maria Mok, the director of the HKMoA. “Through Kingsley and Angel’s attention to everyday details, we hope visitors feel invited to slow down and become more attentive to their surroundings, even within the hustle and intensity of urban life.”

One of Kingsley Ng’s installations, for instance, Laundry Nocturne (2026), consists of a projection and soundscape that evoke the play of light across laundry hung between buildings. It was inspired by the artist’s experience visiting Venice, where billowing laundry on clotheslines is still common, though for Ng it also evoked memories of Hong Kong.

Kingsley Ng in Venice Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

“The laundry becomes a point of entry into a wider soundscape of nighttime labour: recordings from a wastewater treatment plant, a newspaper-printing facility and a postal sorting centre evoke the work that continues while much of the city sleeps,” Ng says. “These modest, often overlooked processes that keep a city alive do not only connect Venice and Hong Kong. They are like a low hum in almost every city, to be picked up by an ear that doesn’t only listen to loud grandeur.”

For her installations, Angel Hui shines a spotlight on humble features of Hong Kong’s everyday life, including goldfish in plastic bags and a neighbour’s ornate but faded window frame. In her magical rendering, glowing plastic bags embroidered with goldfish motifs are suspended overhead, while a handwrought iron window made by Hui and traditional metalsmiths rotates slowly in the gallery.

“In Hong Kong, a goldfish bag is an everyday object; in Venice, it seemed almost precious—delicate and exposed,” Hui says. “The experience reminded me that a work does not need to be grand to hold meaning. Sometimes, its poetry lies in inviting us to look more attentively, and to meet the familiar with tenderness.”

Angel Hui’s installation I Would Like to Open a Window for You (2026) Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

Both artists, through their site-specific installations, encourage visitors to mark a pause while heightening their attention to details of light, sound, colour and texture. This invitation to contemplation is echoed in the exhibition’s title, Fermata, which is a type of musical symbol instructing a performer to hold a note or a pause for an extra moment, and the term for the places where Venetian vaporettos stop to let off and pick up passengers. For Hui, this title and its dual meanings resonate across the exhibition’s disparate locales.

“The difference in pace is pronounced: Hong Kong often presses forward, while Venice seems to make room for pause,” she says. “This is why Fermata—a moment of stopping, of stillness—felt so resonant. In both cities, the ordinary endures, and in that endurance, everyday objects acquire a quiet radiance.”

The exhibition’s title and installations also complement the theme of this year’s Venice Biennale, In Minor Keys, set by the late curator Koyo Kouoh.

Angel Hui in Venice Photo © Hong Kong Museum of Art

“I come to see the works as experiences to return to and live alongside,” Ng says. “For me, that is one meaning of In Minor Keys: making time for the ordinary and immediate—the air, light and weather around us—and becoming more attentive to our presence within them.”

The artists’ ability to turn familiar objects and moments into tableaux where time pauses and senses are heightened is key to the exhibition’s power. It allows their installations to resonate across localities, from the Venetian Lagoon to Hong Kong’s cityscape.

“The artworks merge into the Venetian neighbourhood, from laundry hanging across nearby alleys and above our courtyard exhibition space to the view of the neighbourhood outside Kingsley’s Laundry Nocturne gallery, to wrought-iron windows that echo Angel’s work,” Dr Mok says. “All these details are blurring the boundary between the exhibition and its surroundings. They also show how small moments of daily life can quietly synchronise across different cities and audiences.”

Billy Joel at Home: 10 Photos of the Piano Man Through the Decades

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

For Billy Joel, New York is more than just a place. It’s an inextricable part of what makes the singer who he is. “My speech patterns are New York. The music that I like, it was all New York–based. That’s what I think of when I think of home,” he said in his 2025 HBO documentary Billy Joel: And So It Goes. “It’s my roots.”

The Bronx native grew up in Hicksville, a middle-class suburb of Nassau County on Long Island. His family struggled to make ends meet. “There was poor, and then there was Billy Joel poor. He was lucky if he had enough money to buy a pack of cigarettes,” his childhood friend and fellow musician Jon Small said in And So It Goes. According to Joel’s sister Judy, no matter how hard times were, their mother Rosalind insisted he take piano lessons. His talent “had to be nurtured,” Judy said in the HBO doc. “She knew that he was going to be something someday.”

Before he indeed became something, Joel worked on an oyster boat. “I used to look up at this mansion on the hill and wonder what it would be like to live in a house like that,” he said in the documentary, referencing the waterfront property on Centre Island he nicknamed MiddleSea. “I used to think, ‘They’re rich bastards. They never had to work a day in their life.’ Well, I own that house now. It’s not finished yet. And neither am I.”

Read on for a glimpse into the Grammy winner’s life, from his early days in New York to his brief stint in Los Angeles and return home.

Joel’s music career took the Long Island native to Los Angeles in the early 1970s, and he signed with Columbia Records in 1973. After three years in LA, he and his first wife, Elizabeth Weber, returned to his home state, where he completed his fourth studio album, Turnstiles. “I wanted to get back to New York and make more of a rock and roll album,” Joel explained in And So It Goes. “I was on a bus on my way back to Highland Falls, New York. Elizabeth found us a house up on the Hudson River, and it was fall. It was just gorgeous. The colors were ablaze, the trees were, like, gold and red, and I remember thinking, ‘This is the New York State that I loved when I was a kid. This is magic. We don’t have this in California.’ And I wrote this love song to New York on the bus.” It would become “New York State of Mind,” one of Joel’s most popular tunes.

The singer-songwriter poses with some of his records in January 1978.

Joel’s fifth studio album, 1977’s The Stranger, was a runaway success—but the singer, pictured alongside his accolades in New York in 1978, told Playboy a few years later that his newfound fortune didn’t faze him. “I read these stories about Peter Frampton’s multimillion-dollar mansion, 15 Rolls-Royces and all that,” he said. “Who cares? It’s all bullshit. What was his last record like? Was it any good? That’s the question. People get hung up on the money thing. It’s nice to have four motorcycles. It’s fun. And I walk into the garage and go, ‘Holy shit! I’ve got four motorcycles!’ But I don’t go beyond a certain point.”

Joel, his then-wife Elizabeth, and their dog in 1978.

Instead of splurging on a million-dollar mansion, Joel told Playboy that when he and his wife decided to buy a home, “We got a $300,000 house with a mortgage like everybody else.” Their glass house, featuring floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking the Long Island Sound, was located in the town of Oyster Bay on Long Island’s North Shore. Joel appears poised to hurl a rock at the home on the cover of his 1980 album, Glass Houses. “The old thing about people who live in glass houses shouldn’t throw stones—I don’t believe it,” he said. “I think, Why not? Take chances. I really wanted to throw a rock at my own house.”

Joel and Elizabeth at their Long Island residence.

In the 1970s, Joel asked Weber to be his manager. “The company I’m with now is New York–based,” Joel said of his representation. “It’s called Home Run—nothing to do with baseball. It’s a home-run agency.” Weber got right to work. She recalled in his HBO documentary that the “story [Joel] likes to tell” of her early days on the job is that he woke up the next morning to phones everywhere and “cabinets installed.” However, she said, “That’s not really true, but it’s close to true.” The couple (seen here at Joel’s grand piano in their living room) split time between their Manhattan apartment, where they worked, and Oyster Bay. Weber left her role managing Joel when the two split, and her brother Frank picked up where she left off. Home Run dissolved amid financial woes in the ’80s, and Joel eventually pressed charges against Frank over mishandled funds; the parties reached a settlement, the terms of which have not been disclosed publicly.

The musician mixing tapes in the music studio of his New York home.

The Oyster Bay house, a midcentury-modern residence, was not far from where Joel grew up in Hicksville. Photographed here in his at-home music studio in 1978, the singer’s career was on the upswing—but his relationship with Weber was growing tense. “Having a big boom in record success, I think my marriage was starting to unravel around then; 1979, it wasn’t the happiest time,” Joel once said. “Record success doesn’t translate into happiness.” Joel assumed things were paying off financially, but he admitted he “wasn’t aware” how much money he was actually bringing in. “I know we were living in a nice house, my wife was going out and buying all kinds of nice stuff,” he said. “I never really went out and did that.”

Joel multitasks in his LA home studio in 1984.

Though Joel had settled down on the East Coast, he told the Los Angeles Times that his California years cross his mind “all the time.” The singer, pictured here in LA in 1984, wrote his hit song “Piano Man” based on his experience as a lounge musician (using the name Bill Martin) at a now-defunct bar near the corner of Wilshire Boulevard and Western Avenue during the early 1970s. “I wonder what happened to the people I knew—the people in the bar in ‘Piano Man,’” he said. “It was the Executive Room in the Wilshire district. I got tips and made union scale, and I was able to afford an apartment in Studio City. The weather was great; the people were nice.”

Though it appears Joel lived like a rock star in Los Angeles—as seen here in 1984, perched on a sheepskin-style throw over a leather sofa with a pack of Marlboros and a landline telephone by his side—he told the Los Angeles Times in 2023 that he felt more like “an exiled writer living in Paris” during his early stint in the City of Angels. “I went from being an insignificant nobody to the Piano Man while I was there,” he said. “I still look back to my time in LA with a certain amount of romance.”

Joel is pictured here at the dinner table in 2001. When The New Yorker paid him a visit at his Centre Island residence in 2014, he described the interior’s aesthetic as “Catholic martyrs and saints, grotesqueries.” In the library, Joel—who decorated the place himself—had a model of the Queen Mary on his mantel, and in the living room facing the fireplace was a triptych of the Last Supper along with silver jugs and bowls on a nearby table.

Joel’s various properties have passed through some famous hands. He and his second wife, supermodel Christie Brinkley, owned a 12-acre oceanfront property in the East Hampton hamlet of Amagansett. The couple raised daughter Alexa Ray Joel in the Long Island dwelling. It was later sold to Jerry and Jessica Seinfeld for $32 million. According to The New Yorker, Joel also sold a duplex on Central Park West to singer-songwriter Sting for $5 million and a house on Martha’s Vineyard to businessman Mickey Drexler. At one point, Joel purchased a home in the Hamptons from Jaws star Roy Scheider.

“As a little kid, I always liked boats and the romance of living on an island,” Joel told Parade in 2018. The singer—pictured here on an East Hampton dock in 2001—has always felt at home on the water. What I Didn’t Tell You About My Flip-Flop

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

I left off writing at the edge of the 100-Mile Wilderness. I planned two more posts, one about the Hundred-Mile, the last about Katahdin, but I couldn’t write them.

After Katahdin I was home, as I’d planned to be. I’d be back in Wisconsin a week, get another cortisone shot for my arthritic knee, spend time with my wife, see friends, then catch Amtrak back to Pawling and push south to Springer.

That was the plan, the one I told you about. But the truth is, by the time I hit Maine, I’d stopped believing it.

I had my reasons, and they were good ones.

Heading south would have meant finishing in December and a flip within my flop to get through the Smokies before snow turned the trail into a post-holing nightmare.

Then there were my spring classes to prep (I still teach), my wife’s birthday, an election to vote in, and one more Boundary Waters trip to take. Plus, I looked forward to resuming my barista job. I was excited about improving my latte art and counter skills.

Better to come back next year, I told myself. Better weather, more time, and a straight shot to Georgia. I would finish the first week of November. Perfect.

I’d thru-hiked the PCT and the CDT. I was fine doing the AT as a two-part LASH. I had nothing to prove.

Unlike Killington in 2024, getting off the AT this time wasn’t a surrender. It was a conscious choice. I would be back next summer, but I would return as a section hiker. And to my surprise, that mattered. A lot.

Many of us deal with depression after getting off trail. I am a member of this sad clan. On trail I made new friends, shared parts of myself that hadn’t seen the light of day in years, shed pounds, and became crazy-fit while eating like a joyful pig.

Being on trail expanded my life and gave it purpose by shrinking it. The daily routine of miles and chores, the resupply stops of splurge and cleansing, and the slow accretion of accomplishment.

But now, back home and staying, that bigger life has abruptly ended. The better version of myself, the one who crushed miles despite the bad joints, is slowly fading.

I’ve been following Guru on Instagram, the guy in the kilt I hiked the 100-Mile Wilderness and summited Katahdin with. Seeing his photos of autumn sunsets, fall-stripped trees and leaf-strewn trails, I can smell the damp leaves, feel the cold air. I think, “That could have been me.”

Getting through the Whites showed me what my old body could do. My confidence only grew as I clambered through the Mahoosucs, the Bigelows, and hard sections with no name. I flew over the roots and rocks of Maine, nearly ecstatic. When I climbed Katahdin, I was at my peak.

There are no guarantees I’ll come back and finish. Not for lack of desire, but because of my waning ability. My arthritis, my stamina, and who knows what other shoe might drop between now and next July.

I now believe I should have kept going. It’s not just that I can’t trust my body; it’s like I broke a promise to myself.

When I set out to hike the Triple Crown in 2022, the subtext read “thru-hike.” My dad’s passing, and my AT attempt in 2024 that followed, changed the rules. It seemed that just finishing, no matter the method, was all that mattered.

I am a thru-hiker. An identity of which I am ridiculously proud. I’ve had my face wind-blasted raw below whooshing turbines outside Tehachapi, been chased by wildfires through Oregon, and dragged a norovirus-weakened body through the Cascades. On the CDT, I pushed through joint pain requiring oral steroids in the Wind River Range and dunked frost-stiffened shoes into the Gila River so I could jam my frozen feet into them. To get off trail 6,000 miles into my Triple Crown, even by choice, now feels like a betrayal of what I set out to do.

It took me a long time to write this post. I’d planned to keep writing my way to Katahdin, a story I still need to tell. But I had to come clean first. Thank you for following me this far.

Next time, I’ll tell you about the Hundred-Mile.

Cost per task: Using cheap classifiers to route across Gemini models and thinking levels

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Per-token API prices keep falling, yet engineering bills for autonomous coding agents keep climbing. Price per million tokens isn't the metric that actually governs production economics anymore. The dominating metric is now cost per successful task: total spend across every loop iteration, tool call, retry and internal thinking pass divided by the number of tasks that pass verification.

When an agent calls a high-thinking reasoning configuration for every micro-decision in its harness, you're paying deep-reasoning prices for questions a lightweight configuration already answers accurately. To prevent this unnecessary overhead, consider adding a lightweight routing layer that can intercept routine decisions before they ever reach an expensive reasoning model.That is the premise behind wiring TypeSafe's Jev classifier in front of Google Antigravity's model calls. Rather than generating text token by token, Jev—a specialized structured-output classifier designed for high-throughput decision pipelines—evaluates structured classification primitives in a single forward pass: a Choice from a list, a numeric Score or a Noul, which returns a 0 to 1 probability for a binary statement.

For full disclosure, Google has no partnership with TypeSafe, and this article is not an endorsement of TypeSafe or its products. Jev is used here as one example of a lightweight classifier, and the same routing pattern can be built with other classifiers, including Google-native ones. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab, for example, runs the same kind of classifier on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, in your own Google Cloud project.

We ran a 50-question, auto-graded, coding-agent benchmark against live Gemini and Jev APIs to test whether a near-free classifier can route each task to the cheapest model that still gets it right. Here's what we learned:

The naive way to ask Jev to route a coding task is a single broad Choice question: simple or complex. When we tested that first on 16 coding-agent tasks, Jev routed nearly everything to simple, even tasks requiring an LRU cache trace or modular exponentiation. TypeSafe's documentation warns against this exact anti-pattern. If a question weighs multiple factors or requires a meta-judgment, decompose it into an atomic check.

When we replaced the broad Choice question with one atomic Noul question, it fixed the separation immediately. The question was: does answering this require performing more than one sequential calculation, simulation step or logical deduction, rather than a single direct lookup, format check or one-step classification?

On those initial 16 tasks, Jev routed 13 of 16 in agreement with our hand-labeled difficulty on the first run and 14 of 16 on the second pass. Many engineering teams ask whether they even need a classifier API when they can write a zero-cost regex and prompt-length heuristic in Python, matching words like sequence, modulo and race condition or escalating any prompt over 35 words.

On the 16 development questions used to write those exact rules, the local heuristic scored 15 of 16, or 93.8%. To test whether that heuristic survived contact with realistic coding-agent traffic, we froze its code without changing a character and added 34 unseen held-out tasks across seven coding-agent buckets: fact extraction, schema validation, short classification, single-hop code checks, state simulation traces, math or logic chains and subtle language-semantics bugs.

On the 34 held-out questions, the frozen heuristic dropped 23.2 percentage points to 70.6%. Realistic simple tasks such as extracting a port from a Kubernetes YAML manifest, a file path from a unified git diff header or a line number from a Python traceback exceeded 35 words and triggered false escalations. Conversely, concise multi-step code traces such as mutable default arguments across calls, Python dictionary key hashing on {1: "a", True: "b", 1.0: "c"} and C short-circuit evaluation lacked the heuristic's hardcoded puzzle words and were falsely routed to the simple path. Jev classified all 34 unseen tasks with 100.0% agreement, 34 of 34, reaching 48 of 50, or 96.0%, across the full suite.

A common mistake when building a fast-path router is assuming that setting a low maxOutputTokens cap on a reasoning model will make it spend less on easy tasks. Our 50-question data shows why that assumption fails.

When we compared gemini-3.8-flash with maxOutputTokens=300 against gemini-3.8-flash with maxOutputTokens=4096 while leaving thinkingConfig at the API default, both configurations spent almost identical thinking tokens on the 27 tasks Jev labeled SIMPLE:

Capping the model's output at 300 tokens didn't save any money on easy tasks. The cap is only a ceiling, and easy answers never came close to it. On the easy tasks, the capped version actually cost slightly more than the uncapped one. All the cap did was break 7 of the 25 hard tasks. On six of them, the model ran out of room while still reasoning and never gave an answer. On the seventh, it squeezed its reasoning to fit, finished on time and got the answer wrong.

So a Jev router that sends easy tasks to the capped model and hard ones to normal Flash got all 50 right. It still cost slightly more than skipping the router and using normal Flash for everything, and it was slower. If your cheap option doesn't actually make the model think less on easy tasks, no router can save you money. Not even a perfect one that costs nothing.

The obvious way to spend less on thinking is to ask the model to think less. So we ran a second round of tests with two lighter options: Flash-Lite, which doesn't think at all by default, and Gemini 3.8 Flash with its thinking level set to low. We added those results to the same results file, matched question by question.

The strongest setup without a router was Gemini 3.8 Flash on low thinking. It solved all 50 tasks. That's the number a router had to beat.

The Jev router beat it. It sends every task to Flash-Lite first and escalates only what Jev flags as complex. It also solved all 50 tasks, and it can help reduce both cost and response time compared with running low thinking on everything. On prompts this short the cost difference per task is small, so for an interactive agent the shorter wait is the bigger benefit.

Why it works. On its own, Flash-Lite answered every simple task in half a second but missed 6 of the 25 hard ones. Jev flagged all 6 as complex with high confidence and escalated them. That brought its cost close to what a perfect router would achieve.

The keyword heuristic was cheaper, but only because it got things wrong. It sent 5 hard tasks to Flash-Lite, and 2 of them failed, for 48 of 50.

Escalating to a heavier setting didn't pay here. Routing hard tasks to Flash on default thinking was cheaper than running default thinking everywhere. It still cost more than running low thinking on everything, which already solved all 50. A heavier escalation target only makes sense when the low setting starts failing.

Limits: one run per setup, 50 prompts with hand-assigned difficulty and simple automatic grading.

Two things in our results only show up once you look past it.

Routing and caching pull in opposite directions. Our test prompts were short, standalone tasks, and on those the Jev router helped reduce both cost and response time. Real coding agents often look different. They send the same big block of context on every turn, such as a map of the repository and the tool definitions. Agent Platform Context Caching stores that block so you pay 75% to 90% less to resend it. The catch is that a cache belongs to one model. If your agent switches between Flash-Lite and Flash from turn to turn, it either keeps two caches or loses the discount. The practical split: route short, self-contained tasks like log parsing or schema checks, and keep long, context-heavy turns on one model.

Match the model to the workload. Gemini 3.1 Pro is built for deep, multi-step reasoning, and it brings that same depth to every request, including short lookups that don't need it. For a workload made mostly of quick checks and extractions, a Flash configuration is the better fit, and Pro is best saved for the tasks that need its reasoning depth. Model configurations can also apply different safety settings to security-related prompts. In our run, the Pro configuration declined two short code-security review prompts that Flash answered. If your agent performs security reviews, test those prompts on the configuration you plan to use.

Other ways to build the router. Jev is one way to get a fast classifier, but not the only one. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab runs the same Choice, Score and Noul questions on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, hosted on a Compute Engine GPU VM or on Cloud Run in your own Google Cloud project. An open-source server exposes the same API in front of it, so the routing code in this article only needs a different endpoint. Our results come from Jev, so measure accuracy and latency on your own tasks before switching, and keep in mind that a self-hosted model is billed by the GPU-hour rather than by the token.

Taken together, the results give three practical rules.

Start with Flash on low thinking before you build a router. It solved all 50 tasks and cost considerably less than the default setting. Lowering the output token limit is not a substitute, because it doesn't reduce how much the model thinks on easy tasks. Set the thinking level directly, with thinkingLevel: "LOW".

Use Jev to route short, standalone tasks. Send simple tasks to Flash-Lite and hard ones to Flash on low thinking. Ask Jev one simple yes-or-no question per factor. In our test, this helped reduce cost and response time compared with running Flash on low thinking for everything, and came close to what a perfect router would achieve. Keep long, context-heavy turns on a single model so caching keeps working. For high-volume warehouse workloads, Jeff Nelson's BigQuery and Cloud Run cascade pattern applies the same idea.

Measure what your agent actually spends. Send your agent's traces and thinking-token counts to Cloud Logging and BigQuery, so you can see which tasks drive your spend and catch truncation or retries that inflate it. The benchmark code will be published after open-source review.

In summary, the most cost-effective way to run an agent is to match each task to the model that fits it. Start with a fast model on a light setting. You'll often find it already gets the job done. Then add a small, almost free classifier like Jev to send only the hard tasks to a stronger model, and ask simple yes-or-no questions rather than one broad one. Judge every option by what it costs per task done right, not per token.
