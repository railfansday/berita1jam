Eighth place, one quarter of the way through the season, is too risky a spot.

It is OK to drop points intermittently in such a long, arduous campaign, but the move to sack Nuno was motivated by West Ham’s fear of those points practically spilling out underneath them, like marbles.

It would, of course, have been nice for fans to see a move not motivated by fear; perhaps a change in the summer would have felt harsh, but it would have been proactive, and there is an argument it would have felt encouraging in a different sense.

On the topic of encouragement, West Ham’s free-scoring start to the season was a refreshing sight after recent times at London Stadium, and they remain the highest-scoring team in the division, but their goal difference has already been matched.

And for Nuno, 52, and his players, the issue has not just been conceding too many goals, but also the timing of those errors: they have conceded first in nine out of their 16 games in all competitions. That has signalled a concerning fragility, which has tasked West Ham with fighting back far too often – in a season when many expected them to lead from the front, no less.

Admittedly, there was a degree of naivety to this sentiment. Over the summer, it was all too easy for neutral fans to assume that West Ham would walk the Championship, when supporters of the east London club knew how misguided these claims were. You need look no further than West Ham’s previous outing in the Championship, in 2011/12, when they let a top-two finish slip away before going life-and-death with Blackpool in the play-off final.

Again, that is far too risky a spot this time around, and cannot be justified when you own the most expensive squad in the league (at an estimated £282m, with the £18m gap to Wolves proving substantial at this level).

There is surely greater quality at West Ham than any other club in the Championship, and some observers will feel a coach would not need to get too much right to promote the Hammers. In that regard, Nuno was doing too much wrong.

With Scott Parker immediately emerging as the frontrunner to replace him, perhaps the conversation can move away from risk. But only in the short-term, you would think.

Parker, a former Hammer, secured promotion with Fulham, Bournemouth and Burnley in his first season in charge of each club. That record is remarkable. It is simply a shame the stories didn’t end there. Fulham and Burnley were immediately relegated in their subsequent seasons, while Parker left Bournemouth by the end of August after a shocking start to life in the Premier League.

Of course, as a West Ham fan, you might not care to look that far ahead. The priority is purely promotion to the Premier League.

Moreover, a feelgood factor could stem from the nostalgia of Parker’s return. To quote a great modern sketch by Exploding Heads: “He knows the club. He knows the ground... even though it’s different than when he was there.”

In all seriousness, there would be genuine appreciation towards the former midfielder, who won the fan-voted Hammer of the Year award three years in a row. Fans might hope fortunes have been hiding in plain sight.

“Mr West Ham” Mark Noble played alongside the 46-year-old at Upton Park and is now sporting director (he will oversee preparations for Wednesday’s game versus Cardiff). Would Noble and Parker have the chemistry in the office that they had on the pitch? Well, it’s worth noting that they were relegated together in 2011.

Against what you might assume, they would also be unlikely to settle the debate about the “West Ham Way” (chiefly, whether it even exists). While West Ham have scored an unusually high number of goals under Nuno this season, Parker’s MO is clean sheets; that would mark a jarring about-turn, stylistically.

But maybe an about-turn is what West Ham need. They are heading in the wrong direction, even if slowly, and slip-ups cannot be allowed to become a full-on skid. West Ham have problems higher up than in the managerial seat, but finding someone to fill it is the only immediate concern.

From non-aligned to pro-Western: How Cyprus has loosened ties with Moscow, built partner-ship with the US

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Republic of Cyprus long balanced ties between Russia and the West. Now it is phasing out Russian weapons, expanding defense cooperation with Washington and redefining its place in the Eastern Mediterranean.

In an interview with the Cyprus News Agency on October 4, the new US ambassador to Cyprus, John Breslow, outlined plans to upgrade the Andreas Papandreou Air Base in Paphos to accommodate large US military transport aircraft.

He also announced plans for more frequent visits by US warships to Cypriot ports and an expansion of joint military activities.

But one statement carried particular political weight: his assurance that "the United States will protect Cyprus in the future."

Although the remark does not constitute a formal US security guarantee, it reflects the growing cooperation between the two countries.

For a country that for decades maintained close ties with Moscow, viewed US foreign policy with suspicion and is not a member of NATO, the prospect of an expanded American military presence marks a significant geopolitical shift.

It is perhaps the clearest indication yet of Cyprus's gradual loosening of ties with Russia and its increasingly close strategic alignment with Washington.

The shift is particularly striking given Cyprus's political history.

In the years after it gained independence from the UK in 1960, the Republic of Cyprus sought to balance its relations between East and West.

Under the leadership of Archbishop Makarios III, the first president of independent Cyprus, the country became a founding member in 1961 of the Non-Aligned Movement, a forum of states that did not align with either of the two superpowers of the day, the United States and the Soviet Union.

This approach allowed Nicosia to maintain close ties with the Soviet Union without severing its connections to the West.

Even after joining the European Union in 2004, Cyprus maintained strong political, economic and military ties with Moscow.

Relations with the US, by contrast, remained overshadowed by the Turkish invasion of the island in 1974 and Washington's failure to take decisive action against Ankara.

The US prioritized maintaining its strategic relationship with Turkey, a key NATO ally, over taking a tougher stance against the invasion.

The US position fueled widespread anti-American sentiment in Cyprus, culminating in a violent demonstration outside the US Embassy in Nicosia on August 19, 1974, during which US Ambassador Roger Davies was shot dead.

Mistrust deepened further in 1987, when Washington imposed an arms embargo on the Republic of Cyprus, officially intended to prevent an arms race on the island and avoid undermining efforts to resolve the Cyprus conflict.

The US arms embargo contributed to the reliance of the Cypriot National Guard (the armed forces of the Republic of Cyprus) on Russian-made weapons systems, strengthening Moscow's position as a major military partner.

Cyprus's response to Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014 illustrated the strength of these ties.

The Cypriot government expressed serious reservations about imposing tougher European sanctions on Moscow. Then-Foreign Minister Ioannis Kasoulides warned that such measures could have devastating consequences for the Cypriot economy.

Relations with Moscow deepened further in February 2015, when President Nicos Anastasiades visited the Russian capital and signed a series of agreements with Russian President Vladimir Putin.

These included a naval cooperation memorandum facilitating visits by Russian warships to Cypriot ports. Moscow also agreed to more favorable repayment terms for its €2.5 billion loan to Cyprus in 2011.

Cooperation remained close as recently as October 2021, when then-Foreign Minister and current President Nikos Christodoulides signed a Strategic Cooperation Framework for 2022–2024 in Moscow alongside his Russian counterpart, Sergey Lavrov.

The decisive turning point came just four months later, in February 2022, with Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

According to Konstantinos Adamides, associate professor of international relations at the University of Nicosia, the invasion accelerated a geopolitical shift toward the West that had already begun.

"For the first time, the Republic of Cyprus openly and unequivocally chose to side with the West, despite the economic and political costs of breaking with Moscow," Adamides told DW.

Nicosia aligned itself with EU sanctions against Russia, despite the Cypriot economy's strong links to Moscow, particularly in services, investment and tourism.

The decision marked a significant departure from Cyprus's traditional foreign policy approach of maintaining a balance between Russia and the West.

According to Adamides, Cyprus's position toward Russia paved the way for closer relations with the US, particularly in the field of security.

Nicosia, he explained, succeeded in convincing Washington that it could serve as a reliable partner in the Eastern Mediterranean.

The rapprochement had already gained an institutional foundation before the war, with a bilateral statement of intent on security cooperation signed in 2018.

Further initiatives followed, including the Cyprus Center for Land, Open-seas and Port Security (CYCLOPS), a US-funded training facility inaugurated in Larnaca in April 2022. The center provides training for security personnel from Cyprus and other countries in the region in areas such as port security, counterterrorism and preventing the trafficking of hazardous materials.

An even more significant development for the Cypriot National Guard came in September 2022, when Washington announced the full suspension of the arms embargo imposed in 1987.

The decision allowed for the export of lethal military equipment to Cyprus, subject to the necessary US approvals. The suspension, which was not permanent and remains subject to periodic renewal, has been renewed until September 30, 2027.

Cyprus's strategic security reorientation is also significant because the country is not a member of NATO.

The crash-landing of an Iranian done on the British military base at Akrotiri in March reignited a debate in Cyprus about whether the country should join NATO.

At the time, President Nikos Christodoulides said that Cyprus was preparing for potential membership at military, operational and administrative level and would apply to join NATO "even tomorrow if it were possible."

He stressed, however, that joining the alliance is not possible because "the political conditions are not there, taking into account the well-known position of Turkey."

Cyprus's changing strategic orientation is also reflected in the modernization of its armed forces.

After decades of relying heavily on Russian military equipment, the country is seeking to gradually reduce its dependence on Russian-made T-80 tanks and Tor-M1 and Buk air defense systems.

The process accelerated following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, as sanctions against Moscow made it increasingly difficult to secure spare parts and ammunition for Russian weapons systems.

Another important step came in January 2025, when then-US President Joe Biden authorized Cyprus to participate in American defense procurement programs, including Foreign Military Sales (FMS) and Excess Defense Articles (EDA).

The decision gave Nicosia access to additional mechanisms for acquiring US military equipment, further strengthening defense cooperation between Cyprus and the United States.

Accusing University Lecturer Who Called Police of "Blatant Racial Profiling" Leads to $37,500 Defamation Verdict

You can see the verdict form, from Thursday, here. The jury had asked whether they could suggest additional remedies besides the money:

But the court referred the jury to Instruction No. 10, which focused solely n monetary damages.

The backstory, from the opinion this April Judge Rebecca Pennell (E.D. Wash.) in Riera v. Central Wash. Univ., which let the case go forward:

Mr. Riera was employed in a fixed term, non-tenure track faculty at Central Washington University (CWU)…. On the morning of April 1, [2024,] Mr. Riera called the CWU police to report an older, "apparently homeless," woman wandering around Samuelson Hall. He said he wanted to make a report "before things get … out of control." An officer reported to Samuelson Hall and confirmed the identity of the woman as a CWU professor. No further action was taken by campus police or Mr. Riera.

The CWU professor shared her experience with two colleagues. The colleagues immediately filed bias complaints with CWU, alleging the target of Mr. Riera's call—a Black woman—had been the victim of racial profiling.

This led to a great deal of institutional response, including a discussion at a faculty senate meeting. Defendant Erdman, "a lecturer at CWU and member of the faculty senate, emailed unofficial minutes [of the meeting] to non-tenured faculty," and her notes included this:

Without naming names or describing the incident, [CWU President] Wohlpart expressed his outrage at a recent unconscionable incident on campus.

From my own knowledge, I think I can tell you the basics: It was an incident of blatant racial profiling. Campus police were called because a person of color was sitting quietly in the lobby of a CWU building for a brief interval. The person turned out to be a highly distinguished faculty member—but was compelled to produce and show ID before being left in peace.

(I must remark that this is outrageous on multiple levels. All CWU buildings are public; they belong to the State of Washington and its people. Anyone may sit in the lobby of any of our buildings. There was no reason at all to call the police—except, I guess, that this was a person of color.)

Wohlpart called the incident "unacceptable" and said that Central is working to see to it that such a thing doesn't happen again on our campus….

Riera sued Erdman, among others, and the court allowed his defamation claim to go forward in part:

[M]ost of [Erdman's] statements cannot be characterized as false. The only exception is Defendant Erdman's statement that what happened on April 1 "was an incident of blatant racial profiling." A jury could conclude this statement falsely asserts that the person who called police on April 1 engaged in intentional racial profiling. And, given widely-shared public records revealed Mr. Riera as the caller, a jury could also conclude Defendant Erdman's statement was about Mr. Riera.

Defendants argue that regardless of truth or falsity, Defendant Erdman's statement cannot be considered defamatory because it falls under the common interest privilege. "The common interest privilege applies with the declarant and the recipient have a common interest in the subject matter of the communication." The privilege applies to "persons involved in the same organization, partnerships, associations, or enterprises who are communicating on matters of common interest." Examples include officers of a nonprofit association or partners to a partnership. The privilege arises "when parties need to speak freely and openly about subjects of common organizational or pecuniary interest." …

If the privilege exists, it can be lost in two circumstances (1) if the speaker is not acting in the ordinary course of their work or (2) the speaker's statement was made with actual malice; i.e., reckless disregard for the truth. Whether the privilege has been lost under either scenario is a question of fact.

Here, the parties appear to agree that members of CWU's faculty senate are members of the same organization who share a common interest. The dispute lies in whether an exception applies.

The Court concludes there are issues of fact regarding whether Defendant Erdman's statements fell outside the scope of the privilege. Going to the first exception, Defendant Erdman had no official responsibility for taking notes at faculty meetings and she was not required to share her notes with other members of the senate faculty. A jury could therefore conclude that her statements were outside the ordinary course of her work as a member of a senate.

With respect to the second exception, Defendant Erdman was not a witness to the April 1 incident and no investigation had yet taken place. A jury could therefore conclude Defendant Erdman was reckless in asserting that the call to CWU police was an act of blatant racial profiling…. [T]he allegations against Defendant Erdman raise questions of fact that must be resolved by a jury.

Note that, by the time the case came to summary judgment, plaintiff had abandoned any defamation claims against the University, and the court granted summary judgment as to defendant's defamation claims against President Wohlpart. The one remaining defendant at trial was Erdman.

The Hungarian military scrambled a fighter jet to help the aircraft land safely in Budapest.

Further to the east, coverage by the BBC and Guardian highlights a separate threat to a flight from Israel that landed in Sofia (SOF) on Tuesday morning, citing local reports in the Bulgarian media.
