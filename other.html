Avec cette 5ème défaite consécutive (la 7ème au total), les jeunes Verts sont dorénavant avant derniers et relégables, seul Borgo (1 point) faisant moins bien qu’eux. Well, Someone 3D-Printed A Whole Engine And It Actually Ran (Sort Of)

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

You've probably sat around a campfire at some point in your life and figured out firsthand that plastic and heat don't mix. Whether you threw an empty soda bottle or marshmallow bag in and watched it shrivel up, or propped your sandals up on the stones around the fire and witnessed your soles melting, you get it.

Plastic is a pretty good insulator, but it has a pretty low melting point. ABS has a melting point of around 440 degrees Fahrenheit, while most available PLAs melt at 340-ish degrees. If you've spent any time looking at your engine's oil or coolant temperature gauges, you'll know that a running engine is pretty stable in the low 200-degree range, but the combustion chamber can get up to 2,500 degrees during the peak of the power stroke. Plastic doesn't stand a chance, which is why most engines are crafted from cast iron or aluminum.

We've already seen what happens when you try to use a 3D-printed cylinder head; the results aren't pretty. With the kind of heat an engine sees, plastic just isn't the preferred material to work with. There are certainly some engines that have made extensive use of plastic, including the Torlon-based engines from Polimotor Research in the 1970s and 80s, but there isn't a high-enough-heat plastic to manage combustion chamber heat spikes, so a blend of metal and plastic is necessary.

YouTuber and mad scientist Alexander's Laboratory recently built and ran a pair of 3D-printed plastic engines, a single-cylinder and a parallel twin. We'll talk through both engines and their unique challenges in a second. First, we should take a step back and think about the implications of potentially building real working 3D-printed engines as prices come down and the technology both improves and becomes commonplace.

Have you ever built (or rebuilt) an engine for one of your project cars? There are just so many pieces and parts you have to buy, and there's so much measuring and clearance-checking to be done before the parts can all get bolted together. It's time-consuming and expensive, but the longest time you'll spend working on it is the shipping time to get all of the parts. Imagine if, someday, every engine in every car shared interchangeable components that could be stocked at any local auto parts store, and the rest could be 3D-printed at home. Wouldn't that be cool?

There are certainly still components that would need to be made out of metal, of course. The obvious first step would be to use metal for the combustion chamber, cylinder head, pistons, and crankshaft. You would also need to figure out a bearing system that works for the crankshaft and camshafts. Then there are things like the valves, valve springs, hardware, water pump components, and the accessory drive pullies.

Alexander is able to make most of his engines out of plastic, including the water pump and drive gears, but I'd go with metal for those on a full-scale engine, purely for longevity's sake. In the end, if you could make it work, you would have an engine significantly lighter than a comparable aluminum cylinder head and engine block unit. Recent research indicates swapping from a metal component to a 3D-printed plastic replacement cuts weight by 30% and costs by 15%. With enough research, you could add structural lattice inside some areas of components to make it even lighter, or allow better coolant flow to critical heat spots. Now this is exciting!

If you wanted to build a 3D-printed plastic engine, what steps would you need to take so it would run for more than a few seconds? As mentioned above, some metal is needed to keep it alive. Alexander strategically used machined steel for both the cylinder walls and the combustion chamber surface. This is smart, as it allows a sealing surface for the metal valves and the ability to thread in a common spark plug. The crankshaft is also metal, as is the surface of the pistons, but pretty much everything else comes straight off the printer.

In the single-cylinder-engine video above, Alexander had some issues with sparks and grounding. It's harder to get everything to ground out properly when the engine is non-conductive, of course. Once it gets running, though, it sure sounds like a pretty normal single-cylinder four-stroke engine, the kind you might find in a lawnmower or go-kart. After getting everything dialed in, Alexander piles on the throttle until the engine decides to eat itself. We don't get an indication of how long it ran, but eventually the carbon-reinforced plastic connecting rod gives up the ghost and shoots through the side of the plastic block.

Trying again with a more balanced twin-cylinder engine, Alexander might actually be on to something. Using some beefed-up components, a little more metal, and better heat management, this little 250-cc twin seems, in the video anyway, to run and work as a standard small motorcycle or scooter engine would. With a better-built engine and more care taken in its running, the twin just keeps on chugging. Maybe it's time to start developing open-source plastic engines that can run with just a few off-the-shelf components. It could be cool.

If you see a teal USB port on your device, you may be interested to know that the color is generally designated for USB 3.1, Gen 2 or above. After its introduction in 2013, USB 3.1 was standardized by the USB-IF, the USB Implementers Forum, with a focus on high transfer speeds and increased power output to devices connected to it. The color isn't a rule, of course, and there are a lot of 3.1 Gen 2 USB ports that have different colors.

While the higher speeds, which can reach upward of 10 Gbps, are a fantastic feature, they're also usually backward compatible with USB 3.0 and prior. You won't necessarily get the same speed benefits, but as USB is one of the most standard ports in computers, you will be able to use most previous generations of USB-A devices with it without issue.

The USB 3.2 ports can reach a top-end speed of 20 Gbps, and though teal-colored versions do exist, they're a bit more uncommon. If you're unsure of the exact possible speed, you can use the Device Manager on Windows or System Report on macOS.

Considering how well USB 3.1 works with high-speed external SSDs, you will be able to significantly cut down any transfer time that may have been slower in the past. This would also help with something like music or content creation, as sample libraries that normally take a long time to move onto SSDs or large video files can be done in minutes.

As teal USB ports tend to favor 3.1 Gen 2, the power output is also a huge benefit. How much did an iPhone cost in 2007 vs. 2026? The gap is smaller than you think

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Here's how the first iPhone's price stacks up against Apple's latest once inflation is factored in.

Understandably, it can feel like iPhone prices are out of control these days. The iPhone Duo starts at $1,999 and costs as much as $3,199 if you want to max out its storage. An iPhone Air is $1,100, despite having one rear camera and sporting inferior battery life next to the iPhone 17, never mind the iPhone 18 Pro. You can buy a decent laptop for the same price or less.

If you've been alive long enough, you might be pining for the days when iPhones were hundreds of dollars cheaper. For a brief time, mostly during the 3G era, price was part of their appeal. While they were hardly dirt cheap, it was possible to get a revolutionary do-it-all device without bearing the whole cost upfront or absorbing an absurd amount of debt.

In reality, the difference between old and new iPhones isn't as big as you might think. The leading factor is, of course, inflation, but you're also getting far more capable hardware, and some early carrier deals might be messing with your perception.

Let's start with the inflation issue. The original iPhone started at $499 for a 4GB model. There's no exact 2026 equivalent (the vanilla iPhone 18 has yet to be released), but we can get close enough with 2025's iPhone 17, which is currently $899. Superficially, that's a $400 gap. But if you adjust for inflation of the US dollar, the 2007 iPhone jumps to over $800.

Remember also that the 2007 model required a two-year AT&T contract, so your monthly payments subsidized the phone. Today, you're buying an unlocked device unless you specifically seek out a carrier deal. Those AT&T plans were also expensive. You were paying at least $60 per month in unadjusted dollars for unlimited 2G data. Today, you might pay a similar amount or less for unlimited 5G.

Many subsequent models like the iPhone 3G and 3GS sold for only $199, but again, the trade-off was a two-year carrier contract. Adjusted for inflation, the unlocked iPhone 4 ($599 in 2010) costs around $920 today, slightly more than the $899 iPhone 17. Carriers expected to profit from iPhone owners over the long term, so they were willing to absorb some of the cost to get them in the door.

Newer iPhones include not only better parts but also more of them. The 2007 iPhone didn't have a front-facing camera for selfies or video calls, nor did it have an NFC radio for Apple Pay or features like AirDrop, Find My or GPS. Modern iPhones can fetch location data from satellites and even send emergency messages when no cellular or Wi-Fi is available.

iPhones and other smartphones have become so capable that for some owners, they may be the only portable devices they need. A smartphone can replace cameras, flashlights, radios, navigation systems, gaming handhelds and portable TVs. You can even use one for serious productivity tasks like writing and spreadsheets. Back in 2007, Apple lacked a framework for third-party apps — that had to wait until 2008.

There is an argument to be made that Apple should care more about affordability. In 2026, $899 is still too much for a lot of people, to the extent that putting out a $1,999 model might feel like Apple is playing a cruel joke. Still, Apple has been surprisingly consistent, even holding base iPhones at $799 until memory prices forced its hand.

Are Tree Saddles Really Better Than Treestands for Hunting Whitetails?

Early in my bowhunting career, I set up a ladder stand on top of a ridge that had oaks and plenty of deer sign. On opening morning, I watched a deer parade go down a trail that was 20 yards out of range. I sat in that stand several more times and never had a deer come within bow range. If I had a mobile stand, I could have easily adjusted my hunting strategy to move closer to the active trail.

Mobile hunting for whitetails is all about making aggressive moves and constantly hunting fresh spots. Mobile hunters carry everything they need for a hunt so they can set up on the fresh sign and easily adapt to the conditions. That’s why they need lightweight and easy to use hunting platforms.

That old faithful ladder stand just won’t cut it when you need to slide another 100 yards closer to a bedding area. A mobile setup is also great for public land where you can’t or don’t want to leave your stand. It’s also less expensive than buying multiple permanent stands.

There are three main choices for the mobile hunter: a climber, a hang-on stand, and a tree saddle. I’ve hunted from all three of these platforms in my search for the perfect mobile hunting set up. To help with this comparison, I also reached out to Aaron Warbritton, from The Hunting Public YouTube channel. He’s been at the tip of the spear of the mobile hunting trend and offers a wealth of experience. Together we’ll see how each platform stacks up against each other in five categories: weight, setup, huntability, comfort, and price.

A heavy pack can feel fine for a few minutes, but when yards turn to miles, you feel every pound. For hunting deep in public land, a lightweight set up is a must. Weights will differ depending on the model of stand and climbing method. These weights will give you an idea of what you can expect a complete kit to weigh. Here’s how all three systems compared:

Model: Tethrd Mantis Saddle and Predator platform.

Kit Includes: Saddle, tether, platform, lineman’s belt, and three climbing sticks.

Total Weight: 14.1 pounds. *The three climbing sticks separately weigh 7.5 pounds.

Kit Includes: Stand, safety harness, lineman’s belt, and three climbing sticks.

Total Weight: 23.9 pounds. *The three climbing sticks separately weigh 7.5 pounds.

If weight is your primary concern then a saddle is the clear winner. You can even make the saddle lighter by using a lighter climbing system.

Weight isn’t only a factor when carrying a stand, it also matters for setting it up. This is especially true when hanging a saddle platform or hang-on stand. When you climb to the height you want to hunt, you have to hold the stand or platform in one hand while tightening the stand’s straps with the other. A light platform for a saddle, like the Predator platform (3.3 pounds), is much easier to hang then the heavier base of a hang-on stand (12.2 pounds).

“I would say that now 90 percent of my hunts are with a saddle and a platform; just because it’s lighter, the footprint is smaller, and it’s faster for me to set up,” Warbritton says.

With a saddle and a hang-on, you’ll need your choice of hang-on stand and a climbing method. I use the Lone Wolf Alpha stand and climbing sticks. The stand and sticks were designed to pack neatly together making it a very convenient and easy to use system. You can climb the tree and hang your stand in one trip with practice and some paracord. I’m pretty slow and careful when climbing and it usually takes me 15 to 20 minutes to climb and get set. The hang-on is fast, but the saddle is slightly faster and easier for me to set up.

A climber is attached to the tree before you climb and doesn’t require climbing sticks. There’s also no platform to hang once you reach hunting height. You can scale a tree fairly quickly with one, and it’s a less technical climb than a saddle or platform. However, that speed comes at the cost of versatility. Climbers need a straight, limbless tree, which isn’t always available. I much prefer the versatility of other platforms that allow me to climb almost any tree. If you live in an area that has a lot of trees suitable for a climber, then these negatives might not be such a big deal.

The size of a climber is another negative. “I haven’t hunted out of climbers a ton, but I’ve hunted out of them enough to know I don’t want to use them. Their footprint is so big that when you’re packing them in, the stand gets tangled on stuff,” Warbritton says.

Another reason to move away from climbers is the noise they make when climbing. It’s discouraging to put a lot of effort slipping into your spot, but then make a scraping noise as you ascend into position. This is especially true when hunting close to bedded deer.

By comparison saddles and hang-ons are much stealthier to carry and set up. If you are proficient with either of them, you can be up a tree within earshot of a bedded buck without issue.

Proficiency doesn’t come overnight with any of these platforms, but tree saddles especially take time to learn. The saddle has a steeper learning curve because it’s so different than a traditional treestand. The first time you lean back into your saddle it might feel like leaning back on a rocking chair at the edge of a cliff. However, that uneasy feeling quickly fades and I personally feel much safer in my saddle than I ever felt in a hang-on or climbing treestand. That security comes from the fact that if you fall off your platform it is extremely easy to get back on because the tether is always pulled tight—making the drop almost unnoticeable.

Beyond the saddle’s learning curve, the hang-on and saddle are neck and neck when it comes to packing, setting up, and hunting out of them.

“They’re really close. They definitely have their differences. Whichever one that you decide to use, you need to practice with it. Whether you’re using climbing sticks or strap on steps, you need to practice packing, hanging, and then shooting out of them because they’re different,” Warbritton says.

When the rut kicks in and you basically live in a tree all weekend, a comfortable stand is a must. While all three mobile options are pretty comfortable, the standard treestand seat is hard to beat. Warbritton agrees with that preference.

“The only thing I like about a treestand is if I’m going to sit all day, I’d rather have a treestand,” he says.

Tree saddles are comfortable, but a lot of the comfort in a saddle depends on your tether height because it controls the weight distribution. You’ll need to find a sweet spot that works for you and that takes some practice. I haven’t found that sweet spot for comfort just yet, but I do think that I’ll eventually get the saddle to be as comfortable as my treestands.

Like weight, the price will depend on the manufacturer and model. Just like our weight evaluation, we’ll compare the products I have personally owned and used.

A Tethrd mantis tree saddle, platform, lineman’s belt, tether, and a set of Lone Wolf climbing sticks will run you around $650. Without climbing sticks the system is about $500. If you want to add some accessories like Ropeman ascenders, pouches, and a recliner, you’ll be looking at spending around $900. It’s not a cheap investment, and you’ll have to decide if the above-stated benefits outweigh the cost.

Hang-ons and climbers are less expensive. A premium climber like the Summit Explorer SD and Hunter Safety System safety harness will run you around $450. A Lone Wolf Alpha hang-on, climbing sticks, Hunter Safety System safety harness, and lineman’s belt will set you back around $500. Warbritton and I both prefer tree saddles because they are lightweight, easy to pack, fast to set up, and make mobile hunting easier. Is it the best system for you? You’ll have to evaluate the features against your hunting needs to make that decision. The good thing is, all three systems will give you the ability to be a mobile hunter.

“I just think that either way you’re going to be hunting mobile and you’re going to have a lightweight setup that’s going to allow you to see more country, learn more stuff, and potentially kill more deer,” Warbritton says.

West Ham fans’ furious reaction raises an uncomfortable question

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

As the Hammers subsided to their first away defeat in the Championship, long suffering supporters, who’d undertaken the 480 mile round trip to be served up a pretty spineless team performance, decided to let their team have it with both barrels.

After last week’s displeasure culminating an Hammers fans singing ‘sacked in the morning’ to their own manager, this weekend brought the players into the crosshairs as the next logical target for their ire.

..With Nuno Espirito Santo out of the job and with Scott Parker just 72 hours in, the 7,500+ travelling fans turned on their stars.

Under no illusions after the result – and the away fans’ response – yesterday: Scott Parker

..As well as booing their own goal keeper pretty much throughout, if the reports on the radio were correct.

Traditionalists will likely be aghast at the thought of fans undermining their own team in this manner, whilst those who participated no doubt feel it was the only way to show their contempt for the perceived lack of commitment and effort which those eleven representatives  of the Claret and Blue heritage displayed.

Not to mention the hundreds of pounds it costs each supporter, every game, merely to arrive at the turnstiles in support of our club. But is it the ‘right’ thing to do?

For me – it’s a no. I started attending games with my son when he was ten years old and however bad it got (it was pretty dire in that season of 2003-4) we never left early, never booed our own players (Luis Boa Morte excepted, I must confess to once letting my frustration with his indolence bubble over) and always, always applauded those who’d put in a shift, regardless of outcome. And I’d not even heard ‘you’re not fit to wear the shirt‘ sung by West Ham fans in my couple of decades’ worth of attending games.

I just don’t see it helping team morale or positively influencing the final outcome. Which is, after all, the point of ‘supporters’.

Giving ‘The Sun’ another gleeful headline to point out our sufferings sticks in my throat too.

But don’t get me wrong, each has absolutely the right to their own response. Just don’t see it helping is all.

From the old Bobby Moore Upper to the Billy Bonds' stand these days - I've been watching since '03 and a supporter since about 1970.. Favourite player - Dean Ashton: Curse you Shaun Wright-Phillips. I still watch YouTube repeats of the Cup Final of 2006 hoping in vain that Shaka Hislop grows another six inches and stops Steven Gerrard's injury time equaliser. Can tell I'm getting old knowing I watched both Mark Noble's debut and his last game at West Ham. Pulling on a Claret and Blue replica shirt still makes me feel the same butterflies as when I was seven years old. Magic.

The most recent setback against Ferencvaros on Thursday has left another bitter feeling, so now Ruben Amorim must galvanise his team again. It will not be an easy one by any stretch though, given Genoa have won four of their last five under Daniele De Rossi, and three in a row.

Speaking of needing a bounce-back, Adrien Rabiot was disappointing in the loss against the Hungarian side. Nonetheless, the Frenchman has bagged five goals and three assists in his first eight league appearances this season. Sergej Milinkovic-Savic was the last to get as many in the first nine Serie A games back in 2022-23, with 10 contributions (three goals and seven assists).

Rabiot has been involved in a goal in each of his last five appearances, and the last Milan midfielder to do better in Serie A was Kaka in 2004 (seven). His opponent today will be Morten Frendrup, someone that Milan have been repeatedly linked with over the years. The Dane remains Genoa’s metronome in midfield, and the shield in front of the back three.

Summer signing Milutin Osmajić has been Genoa’s main threat in quite a low-scoring side, with four goals to his name in Serie A. He is keeping Lorenzo Colombo out of the side, though the Italian has provided a couple of assists for the Montenegrin when they have been fielded together in a front two.

After a disastrous performance against Ferencvaros, Koni De Winter will be hoping for a much better outing this afternoon. The Belgian has been in the headlines for rejecting a Belgium call-up meaning he will stay over the break, and he will aim to channel that same display against Marcus Thuram in the derby.

In addition to Rabiot, the other player to establish himself as a major threat for Milan is Diego Moreira. The Belgian has been directly involved in seven goals (four goals and three assists) in his first six Serie A appearances for the Rossoneri, and that came after a bit of a slow start too. He was decisive in the derby, with two assists and a beautiful free-kick goal.

He is expected to have quite a free-ranging role on the right, but what will be very important is getting players around Goncalo Ramos to try and unlock the Portuguese. So, Moreira will likely come up against the left-sided centre-back Johan Vasquez, someone who has become a mainstay in the Genoa side having entered his fifth season there. He does have three bookings in his first nine games, so that could be something to target.

Speaking of Goncalo Ramos, the concerns about his scoring form (or lack thereof) are beginning to grow into full-blown panic. He still has only two Serie A goals to his name so far, one of which was a penalty, and his overall involvement in the Rossoneri’s attacking play seems to be fluctuating between very little and non-existent.

Amorim and the team must get him firing, or it will continue to look like €70m+ spent very badly. Today will not be an easy duel for the former PSG man either as he faces Leo Ostigard, someone who will be well up for the physical battle. As we have seen, Ramos can get frustrated when he faces players who man-mark him, so he will have to be smart and sharp with his movement, ideally getting some more service too.

With an honourable mention going to Christian Pulisic vs. Alessandro Marcandalli, we had to end by mentioning Stephan El Shaarawy. He scored 21 goals in 83 league appearances for Milan, becoming a fan favourite during his time. The winger has also become the main creative force for the Grifone, having provided an assist in each of his last two league appearances.

Given his propensity to cut inside in order to create danger, both Mario Gila but especially Yunus Musah will have to watch the 34-year-old very closely indeed. Milan have been exposed in transition several times this season, and there is no doubt El Shaarawy would love to make a statement against his old club.

Jayden Bogle should have been shown a red card during Leeds United’s win over Tottenham, says former Premier League referee Keith Hackett.

As Spurs’ three-game winning streak was snapped on Saturday afternoon, the biggest talking point came shortly before half-time when Mathys Tel was eventually forced off after being on the receiving end of a strong challenge from Jayden Bogle.

Replays showed the the Leeds defender had landed his studs on the Frenchman’s left Achilles, but - to the surprise of many - avoided any punishment from referee John Brooks.

In fact, VAR reviewed the challenge for around four minutes and ultimately deemed it did not meet the threshold for serious foul play.

Standard Sport understands Spurs are set to submit a formal complaint about the decision to not send off Bogle, writing to refereeing body, Pro Ref, for an explanation.

Meanwhile, upon reviewing the incident himself, Hackett believes the nature of the challenge should have resulted in a red card.

“This challenge should frankly always be red carded,” he said in a post on X, formerly Twitter.

On the incident, though, Spurs head coach Roberto De Zerbi said he still had full confidence in VAR and insisted his side could not blame the officials for the result at Elland Road.

“No, I have big confidence in the referee,” De Zerbi said when asked if he would have wanted referee John Brooks to go to the monitor to look at Bogle’s challenge.
