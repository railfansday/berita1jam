Luckily for the Dallas Cowboys, CeeDee Lamb's wild catch-turned-fumble late on Sunday afternoon didn't cost them the game at Lucas Oil Stadium.

The Cowboys beat the Indianapolis Colts 27-23 after a game-winning touchdown from Javonte Williams in the final two minutes. But it was a sequence with Lamb earlier in the fourth quarter that could have cost them the contest.

Lamb made an incredible catch in the fourth quarter, and managed to complete the grab while just barely staying in right up against the sideline. But instead of completing the play, Colts rookie cornerback Kenneth Harris didn't actually touch Lamb.

So Lamb jumped up and ended up making it all the way down 48 yards deep into Colts territory. But as Lamb was tackled, he actually lost the ball going down. That gave the ball right back to Indianapolis after a review.

It looked like that was going to cost Dallas the game, too. The Giants made it all the way down into scoring position, thanks to three major penalties — including a 40-yard pass interference call on DaRon Bland.

But inside the red zone, Quinnen Williams forced a huge fumble to give Dallas the ball right back and stop what would have been a go-ahead touchdown.

Eventually, it was Williams' touchdown that pushed Dallas ahead for good and gave it the four-point win.

Dak Prescott went 21-of-34 for 335 yards with two touchdowns in the win, which pushed the Cowboys to 4-5 on the season. They are in second in the NFC East race, behind only the New York Giants. Lamb had 195 yards and a touchdown on 10 catches. He found the end zone in the second quarter on a 44-yard catch.

The Cowboys will take on the San Francisco 49ers next week.

Veterans Day, Service, Music, and Gratitude

“Thank you for your service.” At a checkout counter, at an airport, or in a hallway, there’s rarely time to say much more. We offer our thanks and continue with the day.

At a Veterans Day ceremony, music could give us a little longer to consider what we’re thanking someone for.

When a bugler plays “Taps,” people grow quiet. Some bow their heads; others look toward the flag or watch the musician. Someone remembering a friend’s funeral may find those notes difficult to hear. A child attending a ceremony for the first time may look to a parent to understand why everyone has stopped talking. We hear the same music, but it brings different memories to each of us.

The national anthem gathers us in another way. Some people sing confidently; others search for a comfortable octave or stand silently. For a few minutes, we give our attention to something we share. What we make of that moment varies, especially in a country where our disagreements run deep. Still, it can be moving to hear people make the effort to sing together. And we may respectfully wonder about those who don’t stand.

Ceremonies have their place. We come together to honor, to remember, and to share those who have served and what we have in common as Americans. We also want to know more about the people we have gathered to honor.

People leave military service with very different feelings about their time in uniform. Some speak readily about it. Others have little interest in revisiting those years, particularly with someone they barely know. Pride may sit alongside grief or disappointment. Friendships can endure for a lifetime, as can questions about what happened and why.

A song may open a conversation. Maybe it was the one everyone complained about in the barracks, then sang along anyway. Someone else might remember listening through headphones during a long wait, or hearing a recording sent from home. A ridiculous song might bring back a friend more vividly than a formal tribute. A love song might bring back the person waiting at home.

Playing a medley of the armed forces songs is interesting, inspiring, and even surprising. When we listened to the medley, we were surprised to know the words or at least remember the tune to every song. They stir deeply, each bringing back a memory, an image. They do remind us to honor the service of individuals and the corps of fellow citizens who helped ensure and sustain our democracy.

We know how a few bars of music can take us somewhere: to the kitchen of a childhood home, a college roommate, a drive we had almost forgotten. For a veteran, a song from those years may be welcome, painful, or both.

“What did you listen to then?” gives someone room to decide where to begin and how much to share. The answer may have little to do with combat or sacrifice. It may be about being nineteen, missing home, or discovering a musician whose recordings became a lifelong companion.

We would like to hear more of those stories in the ways we observe Veterans Day. A school could invite a veteran to choose a piece of music and talk about its place in their life. A community concert could include songs local veterans suggest, along with the memories they choose to share. At home, a family might listen to a recording together and discover something they had never thought to ask.

We may hear an account that unsettles our assumptions about service. Someone may prefer to talk about the music itself. If they do, we can follow their lead.

Veterans Day also gives the rest of us reason to examine our responsibilities. Freedom, equality, the rule of law, and human dignity depend on what people do in ordinary civic life. Honoring service includes caring for veterans and their families, and considering how faithfully we uphold the ideals we invoke at ceremonies.

This Veterans Day reminds us that we are seeking a more perfect union and that we are one people. With gratitude.

This November, I hope we make time for the familiar tributes and for a few personal songs. After the ceremony, someone beside us may have a story about a recording they’ve kept for fifty years, or a friend who always sang off-key.

One of Android's biggest learning curves is the way Google's operating system organizes apps. Unlike the iPhone, which puts all your apps on the home screen by default, Android tucks them into a dedicated window called an app drawer. This lets you customize your home screen without hundreds of app icons cluttering it up.

On modern versions of Android, including Samsung's feature-packed One UI skin, you slide up on the home screen to bring up the app drawer, but that wasn't always the case. For years, many Android versions had an "all apps" button on the home screen. That button is essentially now redundant, replaced by the aforementioned swiping motion, and Samsung has stopped showing it on the home screen by default in recent One UI updates.

The only problem is that some users rely on the Apps button, either because they aren't aware of the new swipe-up gesture or because they're simply too used to having it around and don't want to adapt. Thankfully, Samsung left the "all apps" button as an option for users who want it back. You'll just need to dive into a settings menu to enable it.

To add the "all apps" button back to your Samsung Galaxy's home screen in One UI 9, you'll need to access the contextual options menu. Find a space on your home screen and long-press it or pinch as if zooming out on a photo. Either input will bring up several options. Tap the Settings button (the gear icon) in the bottom right of the screen, then scroll down to find and toggle on the option labeled Show Apps screen button on Home screen.

Return to your phone's home screen, and you should now see the "all apps" button on the right side of the favorite apps dock at the bottom of the page. Note that the button takes up space you could use for an extra app in your favorites dock, so it's only worth turning back on if you really don't want to start swiping up to access the app drawer instead.

Samsung is traditionally pretty good about retaining features that longtime users may have come to rely on, and offering the option to enable the "all apps" button is a good example. In some cases, though, the Korean giant might be too good. Samsung is the last major manufacturer still using Android 3.0 Honeycomb's three-button on-screen navigation by default, even though stock Android introduced gesture-based navigation with Android 9.0 Pie in 2018. Users who prefer the latter can switch between the two in Settings, though.

36 Years Later, The Best Pennywise Line In IT Is Still The Creepiest In Horror TV History

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Although Stephen King has written plenty of terrifying villains in his time, one standout quote from 1990’s IT miniseries has the unique distinction of remaining the single scariest line in the history of the medium. There are some heavy-hitting competitors when it comes crowning the creepiest single line of all time on TV. Littlefinger’s Game of Thrones speech about chaos is chilling, but hardly outright scary so much as it is enthralling.

Meanwhile, the creepiest line in Twin Peaks is unbelievably tragic in context, but it is delivered with a plaintive sadness entirely at odds with the panic-attack-inducing terror of what’s happening onscreen. This disconnect is typical of David Lynch and Mark Frost’s inimitable murde mystery masterpiece Twin Peaks, but it also means that the scariest line in the show is only scary in context.

In contrast, the true scariest line in TV history is one that can still send chills down the spine of viewers familiar with the series despite how comparatively tame the show itself is. 1990’s IT miniseries had to cut a lot out of Stephen King’s 1986 novel of the same name, both to conform to network TV censorship standards and to fit within a reasonable runtime. However, the show found room for Tim Curry to deliver the definitive version of Pennywise’s promise, and it is one that still haunts nightmares worldwide decades later.

In the very first scene of 1990's miniseries version of IT, as poor doomed Georgie attempts to retrieve his little paper boat from a flooded storm drain, Curry’s grinning clown welcomes him with a cryptic promise. He reassures George that “They float, Georgie... They float... and when you're down here with me... You’ll float too!” The line is delivered not with genuine malice, but rather a jarringly friendly glee that would fit a kid’s TV host.

The late, great Curry was a master of delivery, and his version of Pennywise arguably outdoes even the far gorier HBOMax prequel Welcome to Derry’s killer clown as he often seems like an actual real-life clown. A comedic veteran, Curry brings the same panache and charisma seen in everything from The Rocky Horror Picture Show to Muppet Treasure Island to the role of Pennywise and, unlike Bill Skarsgard’s more feral, animalistic Pennywise, he plays the clown as an actual children’s entertainer.

The result of this risky choice is a number of scenes, including the infamous "Prince Albert in a can" gag, that are simply genuinely funny instead of being scary. However, rather than ruining the character’s mystique, the comedy of these scenes only serves to make the moments where Pennywise becomes an inhuman monster all the more terrifying.

It is a delicate tonal tightrope, one that other King adaptations like versions of Carrie often have to tread when depicting characters who are both scary and potentially ludicrous, like Margaret White. However, Curry manages to not only nail the balance between Pennywise’s clowning and his evil, but also to encapsulate both contrasting versions of the character in this single line.

When Pennywise begins to tell Georgie about how they float down in his underground lair, he seems genuinely happy to be sharing a secret with the kid, and Curry’s giddy enthusiasm makes Georgie’s unfazed response feel believable. As his giddiness gives way to gleeful, deranged sadism, it feels horribly believable that Georgie might not notice what is so worryingly off about this clown until it is too late.

Over the years, later Stephen King shows and the increased prevalence of killer clowns in movies and TV have led new viewers to complain that no kid would go anywhere near Curry’s obviously evil monster in a million years. However, rewatching It’s opening scene without knowing where the story is going, what makes its most famous line so scary is how genuinely friendly the Stephen King villain seems to his utterly innocent, sheltered prey.

Construction crews in California accidentally unearthed the bones of a large mammal that lived 40 to 45 million years ago

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Fossils found during construction in Oceanside, California, may belong to a giant hoofed mammal that lived 40 to 45 million years ago.

During routine excavation for a city infrastructure project in Oceanside, California, Joseph El Adli, a principal paleontologist with Helix Environmental Planning, spotted fossil bones about 12 feet (3.7 meters) below the surface. He recognized their importance and secured the area.

The bones belong to a large, hoofed mammal that lived 40 to 45 million years ago, the City of Oceanside announced on September 22, 2026.

The animal may be a titanothere, nicknamed “thunder beast,” or Amynodon, historically called a “swamp rhino.” Laboratory work should settle which.

He was monitoring the dig as the project’s paleontologist. The city places monitors on major infrastructure work as standard practice and to comply with California environmental laws.

The find includes several limb bones, ribs and numerous other bones and fragments. The largest piece appears to be a femur.

El Adli’s team worked for seven days to expose the bones from beneath several tons of hardened mudstone and sandstone.

The fossils were then wrapped in a plaster field jacket and moved to an off-site laboratory.

The exact location has not been disclosed, to protect public safety and the integrity of the site.

The bones came from Member C of the Santiago Formation, a thick sequence of sedimentary rock in northwestern San Diego County.

Paleontologists based the age estimate on the known age of that rock and the types of organisms found in it. The range falls within the Eocene Epoch, in a stretch of time called the Uintan North American Land Mammal Age.

According to the city, the animal roamed ancient floodplains in the area.

The same rock unit in the city has yielded Diegoaelurus vanvalkenburghae, a predator named in a 2022 description of Diegoaelurus. It belongs to the machaeroidines, the oldest group of sabertooth mammals.

Known from a nearly complete lower jaw with most of its teeth, it is the youngest machaeroidine on record. Its authors tentatively tie the group’s extinction to faunal change at the shift from the middle to the late Eocene, part of a wider turnover among mammal predators.

The fossil site that produced it, known as Jeff’s Discovery, also holds a tapir relative called Hesperaletes and a hyaenodontid predator, Limnocyon.

The paper was led by Shawn Zack of the University of Arizona College of Medicine in Phoenix, with Ashley Poust of the San Diego Natural History Museum.

Both candidates belong to Perissodactyla, the order of odd-toed hoofed mammals that includes today’s horses, rhinos and tapirs. Titanotheres are also known as brontotheres.

Oceanside officials describe them as animals roughly the size of a black rhino, with deep chests, a distinct shoulder hump and small horns.

In 2001, paleontologists collected brontothere remains from a sandstone layer at another construction site in Oceanside, near Rancho del Oro Road.

That animal, Parvicornus, stood about 5 to 6 feet (1.5 to 1.8 meters) at the shoulder, according to the San Diego Natural History Museum. It had only a very small horn, more of a bump, near each eye.

Brontotheres began as dog-size animals in the early Eocene and grew larger over roughly 20 million years. Those in South Dakota’s Badlands, about 38 to 34 million years ago, reached around 8 feet (2.4 meters) tall and 16 feet (4.9 meters) long.

The Badlands animals carried blunt, paired horns on their noses, sometimes more than 3.3 feet (1 meter) long. Males had larger horns than females.

These herbivores most likely ate soft leaves in Eocene forests. The group died out at the end of the Eocene, likely as the climate turned cooler and drier, the National Park Service notes.

Amynodon is the namesake of the amynodonts, which the American Museum of Natural History describes as large-bodied, hornless rhinos. Amynodon itself had a squat, hippo-like body, city officials say.

Their nickname comes from an older idea that they spent much of their time in water. Evidence for that is very limited.

Only Metamynodon, the last North American amynodont, may have been semi-aquatic. Its fossils turn up in sandstone laid down by rivers.

Amynodonts lived in Asia and North America from the middle Eocene through the early Oligocene. They have no living relatives and sit far from today’s species on the rhino family tree.

Amynodon had a large hollow in the skull in front of each eye socket. It likely anchored muscles for an enlarged upper lip.

At the laboratory, preparators will remove the plaster jackets. They will then harden and stabilize the fossils with museum-grade glues and resins.

Once the bones are fully freed from the surrounding rock, paleontologists expect to narrow down the animal’s precise identity.

The specimens are expected to join a museum collection, where they will be preserved and available to scientists and the public. The museum has not been named.

Several jacketed blocks have not been opened yet, and a skull may be inside one of them.

Well, Someone 3D-Printed A Whole Engine And It Actually Ran (Sort Of)

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

You've probably sat around a campfire at some point in your life and figured out firsthand that plastic and heat don't mix. Whether you threw an empty soda bottle or marshmallow bag in and watched it shrivel up, or propped your sandals up on the stones around the fire and witnessed your soles melting, you get it.

Plastic is a pretty good insulator, but it has a pretty low melting point. ABS has a melting point of around 440 degrees Fahrenheit, while most available PLAs melt at 340-ish degrees. If you've spent any time looking at your engine's oil or coolant temperature gauges, you'll know that a running engine is pretty stable in the low 200-degree range, but the combustion chamber can get up to 2,500 degrees during the peak of the power stroke. Plastic doesn't stand a chance, which is why most engines are crafted from cast iron or aluminum.

We've already seen what happens when you try to use a 3D-printed cylinder head; the results aren't pretty. With the kind of heat an engine sees, plastic just isn't the preferred material to work with. There are certainly some engines that have made extensive use of plastic, including the Torlon-based engines from Polimotor Research in the 1970s and 80s, but there isn't a high-enough-heat plastic to manage combustion chamber heat spikes, so a blend of metal and plastic is necessary.

YouTuber and mad scientist Alexander's Laboratory recently built and ran a pair of 3D-printed plastic engines, a single-cylinder and a parallel twin. We'll talk through both engines and their unique challenges in a second. First, we should take a step back and think about the implications of potentially building real working 3D-printed engines as prices come down and the technology both improves and becomes commonplace.

Have you ever built (or rebuilt) an engine for one of your project cars? There are just so many pieces and parts you have to buy, and there's so much measuring and clearance-checking to be done before the parts can all get bolted together. It's time-consuming and expensive, but the longest time you'll spend working on it is the shipping time to get all of the parts. Imagine if, someday, every engine in every car shared interchangeable components that could be stocked at any local auto parts store, and the rest could be 3D-printed at home. Wouldn't that be cool?

There are certainly still components that would need to be made out of metal, of course. The obvious first step would be to use metal for the combustion chamber, cylinder head, pistons, and crankshaft. You would also need to figure out a bearing system that works for the crankshaft and camshafts. Then there are things like the valves, valve springs, hardware, water pump components, and the accessory drive pullies.

Alexander is able to make most of his engines out of plastic, including the water pump and drive gears, but I'd go with metal for those on a full-scale engine, purely for longevity's sake. In the end, if you could make it work, you would have an engine significantly lighter than a comparable aluminum cylinder head and engine block unit. Recent research indicates swapping from a metal component to a 3D-printed plastic replacement cuts weight by 30% and costs by 15%. With enough research, you could add structural lattice inside some areas of components to make it even lighter, or allow better coolant flow to critical heat spots. Now this is exciting!

If you wanted to build a 3D-printed plastic engine, what steps would you need to take so it would run for more than a few seconds? As mentioned above, some metal is needed to keep it alive. Alexander strategically used machined steel for both the cylinder walls and the combustion chamber surface. This is smart, as it allows a sealing surface for the metal valves and the ability to thread in a common spark plug. The crankshaft is also metal, as is the surface of the pistons, but pretty much everything else comes straight off the printer.
