The big news is the fact that the Blue Jays finally landed a starting pitcher. They were reportedly involved in trying to sign Freddy Peralta, Tarik Skubal and Trevor Rogers. Taira has been pitching in Nippon Professional Baseball since 2019.

The 27-year-old has spent most of his time as a reliever, but has two campaigns as a starter under his belt. He served as a starter last season, winning the Pacific League ERA title.

Taira has a chance to be the No. 5 starter with his new deal, and he has the potential to exceed expectations. He has an eight-pitch mix and has a fastball that averages 95.8 mph.

He throws from a low arm slot and relies on his four-seamer, cutter and sweeper against righties. Against lefties, his sweeper takes a backseat to his changeup.

Why you should never reply with STOP when you receive spam texts

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

An honest opt-out method for marketing messages sends a different signal to shady scammers.

You've likely noticed the "STOP to cancel" note when you receive texts from short codes. And for legitimate communications that you asked to receive from restaurants, surveys, loyalty programs and similar, STOP is a fine way to cut them off. But it's not your only option for ending unwanted texts, and for many kinds, it can do more harm than good.

Nobody wants garbage texts about phony job offers, illicit encounters or other scams, but it's important to think carefully about how you respond to them. Rather than deleting them and dealing with more in the future, you have other options — and they're ones that won't prompt more incoming junk.

Remember to treat any messages from unknown numbers with caution, since they're used for "wrong number" scams and all kinds of other schemes. Don't tap an unexpected link, even if it claims you have an unpaid toll or need to confirm details to fix a delivery problem.

However, not all the texts you get from unknown numbers are like this. Spam texts that advertise lonely singles, too-good-to-be-true job offers, package delivery scams or other types of nonsense aren't operating within normal marketing laws. You might thus reply STOP out of habit, even if they don't include a note about that.

By doing so, you're confirming to the sender that your number is active, which will likely result in you getting even more junk texts. It costs spammers almost nothing to send out many thousands of texts to random numbers, but few of those are confirmed as live, so you're making yourself a target. Replying can also demonstrate that you aren't able to recognize a text scam, possibly tagging you for future efforts if they think they can hook you.

Only reply STOP when you want to stop getting alerts you specifically signed up for. For anything else, never respond; instead, you should use your phone's tools to deal with unwanted messages safely.

On iPhone, you should see a Stop or Report Spam button under messages from unknown senders that include a STOP note. Tap this, and you can choose Delete and Stop or Delete and Report Spam. Both erase the conversation from your phone; the former sends a STOP reply after doing this, while the latter reports the message to your carrier and Apple. For messages without a mention of STOP, the only option is Report Spam.

A similar option appears at the bottom of conversations with unknown senders; tap Delete followed by Delete and Report Spam. In case the number keeps contacting you, tap the number at the top of the conversation and choose Block Contact to cut off all communication.

On Android, you can report an entire conversation as spam in Google Messages by holding it in the list, then choosing Block > Report spam. Alternatively, open a conversation, tap the three-dot More button, then choose Details > Block & report spam. You'll also have the option to block the number.

For either platform, you can also long-press the message and forward it to 7726 (SPAM on a keypad). This reports the text to your carrier for analysis, helping it block spammers across the network. You might get a response asking for the sender's number, so wait to block it until you've confirmed this.

Even when you know what to do upon receiving junk texts, it's better if you don't get them in the first place. Android and iOS both have ways to filter low-quality messages so you aren't getting alerted for nothing.

If you have an iPhone, visit Settings > Apps > Messages, where you have a few choices. Turn on Filter Spam to send obvious garbage to a separate Spam folder. Enable Screen Unknown Senders, then messages from numbers not in your contacts will go to their own folder. And under Text Message Filter, enable the built-in Text Message Filter to put other texts into groups like Transactions and Promotions.

The Allow Notifications menu controls which of these groups send you alerts. Some third-party iPhone apps offer their own message filtering options, which will appear here. Once it's all set up, access the filtered groups from the three-line Filter button at the top-right corner of Messages.

On Android, open Google Messages, tap your profile picture at the top-right corner and choose Messages settings. Choose Protection & Safety, then you can choose from Standard protection or Enhanced protection for junk messages. In your profile picture menu, choose Spam & blocked to review filtered messages.

Many mobile carriers offer their own protections from spam messages (like T-Mobile ScamShield), which are worth a look if you're still receiving trash. Depending on your carrier, you might activate this by changing a toggle in your account, dialing a code to activate or installing a separate app. The base features should be free; it's almost certainly not worth paying for any premium subscriptions on offer. Third-party tools like Truecaller are another option.

Otherwise, be careful of where you give out your number. While spammers often get your number from leaks, other times your contact details spread around when you sign up on a shady website or a company sells them. When making a new account, always check the settings to see if you can limit your information from being shared for marketing purposes. That can help stop spam calls as well as texts.

According to the article, backflow happens when water moves backward through a plumbing system. Changes in water pressure can cause dirty water or harmful substances to enter clean water pipes.

A backflow preventer helps stop this problem. These devices are often found in businesses, apartment buildings, sprinkler systems, and some homes.

The article explains that water suppliers require backflow testing to help protect public drinking water. Testing schedules vary based on local rules, device type, and property use.

How Should Property Owners Prepare for Backflow Testing?

Property owners may receive a notice from their water supplier with a testing deadline. The notice may also explain how to schedule testing and submit the results.

Before the appointment, owners should make sure the tester can reach the device. Clearing the area around the backflow preventer can help avoid delays.

The article notes that water service may need to be turned off briefly during testing. Owners can ask about possible shutoffs before the appointment.

A trained tester uses special tools to measure water pressure and check whether the device's valves work properly.

These checks help determine whether the backflow preventer can stop water from flowing backward. Testing usually does not require major plumbing work.

Afterward, the tester records the results. If the device passes, the report confirms that it met the testing requirements at that time.

What Happens If a Backflow Preventer Fails?

The article explains that worn parts, damaged components, or dirt inside the device can cause a failed test.

A trained professional can identify the problem and determine whether repairs or replacement are needed. After the work is completed, another test is usually required.

Property owners should keep their reports and submit results to the water supplier when required.

The U.S. is getting ready to hike duties on imports from China, the European Union and 14 other economies in response to their industrial subsidies, according to The Wall Street Journal.

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer could announce the results of his office's investigation as soon as Tuesday, according to Bloomberg. The timing could change, according to The Wall Street Journal. Tariff rates and an effective date are unclear.

The probe falls under Section 301 of the 1974 Trade Act, a provision that lets a president act alone in levying tariffs in response to unfair trade conduct, according to Bloomberg. The Office of the U.S. Trade Representative opened the investigation in March, following the high court's invalidation of President Donald Trump's earlier worldwide tariffs.

Greer has said that state backing, subsidies among it, has left the targeted economies with more industrial production capacity than they need. He has said that this lets companies in those economies undercut competitors abroad on price, which hurts American manufacturers. The U.S. Trade Representative's office declined to comment, according to The Wall Street Journal and Bloomberg. The White House did not respond to requests for comment from The Wall Street Journal or Bloomberg.

The new tariffs are expected to apply to the bulk of goods coming from the targeted nations, among them China, Japan, South Korea and the E.U.'s 27 member states, according to The Wall Street Journal. Steel, aluminum and automobiles are expected to be left out because they are already subject to duties under another law tied to national security.

Administration officials have said they plan to set rates close to the ones Trump put in place during 2025, ahead of the Supreme Court's invalidation of his initial round of tariffs in February 2026, according to The Wall Street Journal. Countries with trade agreements, including Japan, the E.U. and South Korea, are expected to face rates of 15% or lower. Vietnam and India could be hit with steeper rates that reflect the agreements they struck with Trump.

China is expected to be charged a steeper tariff than many of its peers because Trump officials have described its subsidies as especially far-reaching, according to The Wall Street Journal. The administration could add wording that would let it increase tariffs if a country's overcapacity in industry grows worse or if the country fails to change its policies during later talks.

The new duties are expected to stack on top of tariffs announced in July over forced labor, according to The Wall Street Journal. Those tariffs, which run from 10% to 12.5% on about 60 economies, are being contested in a lawsuit before the Court of International Trade, according to Bloomberg.

Hike to Over 100 Arches, Hidden Waterfalls, and Towering Cliffs in Kentucky’s Largest Canyon

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The trail to Natural Bridge will work up a sweat, but the effort is rewarded. (Photo: Michelle Polizzi)

While thoroughbred horse racing and barrel-aged bourbon have made Kentucky famous, the state is also home to biodiverse forests and geologically rich mountains. Western Appalachia is a hiker’s paradise, with hundreds of miles of trails, accessing some of the Bluegrass State’s most striking landscapes—and the Red River Gorge is one of its crown jewels.

This ancient canyon spans tens of thousands of acres in eastern/central Kentucky. The powerful Red River carved the Gorge millions of years ago, creating an outdoor playground of cliffs, creeks, waterfalls, and sculpted rock. It has more than 60 official hiking trails and more sandstone arches than anywhere else in the eastern U.S.

Here’s what to see, where to hike, and how to play if you’ve just got one weekend in Kentucky’s Red River Gorge.

Every first-time visitor to the Red River Gorge must visit Gray’s Arch—no exceptions. This massive natural sandstone structure will give you a crash course in the area’s unique topography, including a diverse hardwood forest, massive rock outcroppings, and steep staircases built directly into the hillsides.

While most visitors stick to the 2.5-mile out-and-back trail to Gray’s Arch before turning around, it’s worth extending your hike to 5.8 miles on the Gray’s Arch Long Loop Trail. This route takes you past the crowds that gather beneath Gray’s Arch. It winds deeper into the canyon, through the cool, damp forest, over creeks, and beneath looming rock overhangs that still hold Indigenous petroglyphs.

Whittleton Campground has a free day-use lot with a trailhead accessing multiple hikes. Two trails lead to the Whittleton Arch, and another goes to Hensen’s Arch. Try the standard USFS Whittleton Trail, a 2-mile loop that starts and ends at the campground. Or take the longer Whittleton Branch Trail #216. This 4.8-mile out-and-back accesses the arch and follows a creek for most of its length.

From the campground, you can also hike the Hensen’s Arch Trail. In less than a mile, you’ll reach a second sandstone arch and a deep sinkhole where you can descend into the Earth via a set of metal stairs.

If you’re still feeling adventurous after you hike, head to SUP Kentucky for a Crystal Kayak and SUP Glow Tour, where you’ll paddle underground into a submerged former limestone mine. Thanks to limestone’s natural filtration properties, the water is astoundingly clear. Watch schools of rainbow trout swim beneath your LED-illuminated translucent kayaks, as colors refract upon the ceiling.

The highlight of Kentucky’s Natural Bridge State Resort Park is the massive natural bridge (for which it’s named). This impressive geologic feature stands 65 feet high and 78 feet in length.

There are several ways to access the arch. Hikers can take the 5.5-mile Natural Bridge and Battleship Rock Trail, which winds past five different trail shelters. Hikers walk beneath some of the country’s tallest tulip poplar trees—some standing more than 150 feet. Natural Bridge is the trail’s climax. You can view the rock formation from numerous vantage points, and even walk across the arch to the lookout point.

For a longer, more challenging approach, try the 7.5-mile Sand Gap Trail. It can be done as an out-and-back, or connected with Natural Bridge Trail to make a loop.

For more ridge, arch, and canyon trails, head 30 minutes north of the Natural Bridge State Resort Park to the Auxier Ridge Trailhead. From this hub, hikers can access the 6.0-mile Double Arch Loop, which follows Auxier Ridge and passes Courthouse Rock, Haystack Rock, Double Arch, and Arch of Triumph.

The Star Gap and Skyview Arch Trail also starts at the Auxier Ridge trailhead. This roughly 6-mile out-and-back hike is narrow and woodsy. It includes some overgrown sections and short scrambles with about 1,300 feet of elevation gain. But the effort is rewarded with access to Skyview Arch at the end.

Dispersed camping is allowed around Auxier Ridge. Or check out the Sandstone Drifter, a reservable cliffside treehouse accessed by a suspension bridge. But be warned: climbing its 177-stair floating staircase is a short, steep hike in itself.

When rock-climbing traffic slows in winter and early spring, the Muir Valley Nature Preserve is a perfect place to see waterfalls at their full splendor. That’s when precipitation is highest, meaning larger, fuller falls and a chance to see giant icicles and frozen cascades of water. A $15 parking fee is required, and visitors must sign a waiver before entering.

Connect Horseshoe Falls, Joe Ponder Falls, Stadium Wall, and Nellie’s Falls to create a moderate, 4.4-mile hiking loop that spans three waterfalls and winds beneath sandstone overhangs. To extend the hike, add Liz’s Arch and Washboard Wall Trail for an additional 1.5 miles, exposing hikers to more arches, cliffs, and several rock-climbing areas.
