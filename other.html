Hike to Over 100 Arches, Hidden Waterfalls, and Towering Cliffs in Kentucky’s Largest Canyon

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The trail to Natural Bridge will work up a sweat, but the effort is rewarded. (Photo: Michelle Polizzi)

While thoroughbred horse racing and barrel-aged bourbon have made Kentucky famous, the state is also home to biodiverse forests and geologically rich mountains. Western Appalachia is a hiker’s paradise, with hundreds of miles of trails, accessing some of the Bluegrass State’s most striking landscapes—and the Red River Gorge is one of its crown jewels.

This ancient canyon spans tens of thousands of acres in eastern/central Kentucky. The powerful Red River carved the Gorge millions of years ago, creating an outdoor playground of cliffs, creeks, waterfalls, and sculpted rock. It has more than 60 official hiking trails and more sandstone arches than anywhere else in the eastern U.S.

Here’s what to see, where to hike, and how to play if you’ve just got one weekend in Kentucky’s Red River Gorge.

Every first-time visitor to the Red River Gorge must visit Gray’s Arch—no exceptions. This massive natural sandstone structure will give you a crash course in the area’s unique topography, including a diverse hardwood forest, massive rock outcroppings, and steep staircases built directly into the hillsides.

While most visitors stick to the 2.5-mile out-and-back trail to Gray’s Arch before turning around, it’s worth extending your hike to 5.8 miles on the Gray’s Arch Long Loop Trail. This route takes you past the crowds that gather beneath Gray’s Arch. It winds deeper into the canyon, through the cool, damp forest, over creeks, and beneath looming rock overhangs that still hold Indigenous petroglyphs.

Whittleton Campground has a free day-use lot with a trailhead accessing multiple hikes. Two trails lead to the Whittleton Arch, and another goes to Hensen’s Arch. Try the standard USFS Whittleton Trail, a 2-mile loop that starts and ends at the campground. Or take the longer Whittleton Branch Trail #216. This 4.8-mile out-and-back accesses the arch and follows a creek for most of its length.

From the campground, you can also hike the Hensen’s Arch Trail. In less than a mile, you’ll reach a second sandstone arch and a deep sinkhole where you can descend into the Earth via a set of metal stairs.

If you’re still feeling adventurous after you hike, head to SUP Kentucky for a Crystal Kayak and SUP Glow Tour, where you’ll paddle underground into a submerged former limestone mine. Thanks to limestone’s natural filtration properties, the water is astoundingly clear. Watch schools of rainbow trout swim beneath your LED-illuminated translucent kayaks, as colors refract upon the ceiling.

The highlight of Kentucky’s Natural Bridge State Resort Park is the massive natural bridge (for which it’s named). This impressive geologic feature stands 65 feet high and 78 feet in length.

There are several ways to access the arch. Hikers can take the 5.5-mile Natural Bridge and Battleship Rock Trail, which winds past five different trail shelters. Hikers walk beneath some of the country’s tallest tulip poplar trees—some standing more than 150 feet. Natural Bridge is the trail’s climax. You can view the rock formation from numerous vantage points, and even walk across the arch to the lookout point.

For a longer, more challenging approach, try the 7.5-mile Sand Gap Trail. It can be done as an out-and-back, or connected with Natural Bridge Trail to make a loop.

For more ridge, arch, and canyon trails, head 30 minutes north of the Natural Bridge State Resort Park to the Auxier Ridge Trailhead. From this hub, hikers can access the 6.0-mile Double Arch Loop, which follows Auxier Ridge and passes Courthouse Rock, Haystack Rock, Double Arch, and Arch of Triumph.

The Star Gap and Skyview Arch Trail also starts at the Auxier Ridge trailhead. This roughly 6-mile out-and-back hike is narrow and woodsy. It includes some overgrown sections and short scrambles with about 1,300 feet of elevation gain. But the effort is rewarded with access to Skyview Arch at the end.

Dispersed camping is allowed around Auxier Ridge. Or check out the Sandstone Drifter, a reservable cliffside treehouse accessed by a suspension bridge. But be warned: climbing its 177-stair floating staircase is a short, steep hike in itself.

When rock-climbing traffic slows in winter and early spring, the Muir Valley Nature Preserve is a perfect place to see waterfalls at their full splendor. That’s when precipitation is highest, meaning larger, fuller falls and a chance to see giant icicles and frozen cascades of water. A $15 parking fee is required, and visitors must sign a waiver before entering.

Connect Horseshoe Falls, Joe Ponder Falls, Stadium Wall, and Nellie’s Falls to create a moderate, 4.4-mile hiking loop that spans three waterfalls and winds beneath sandstone overhangs. To extend the hike, add Liz’s Arch and Washboard Wall Trail for an additional 1.5 miles, exposing hikers to more arches, cliffs, and several rock-climbing areas.

Flightless kākāpō parrots from New Zealand are genetically healthy despite 10,000 years of inbreeding

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Scientists sequenced the DNA of New Zealand's endangered kākāpō and found that 10,000 years of inbreeding actually helped natural selection clear out harmful genetic mutations.

On New Zealand’s Stewart Island, flightless parrots called kākāpō spent about 10,000 years cut off from the rest of their species, breeding with relatives. In theory, that should have left the birds with more and more harmful mutations.

A 2021 study of their DNA found the opposite: the island kākāpō carried fewer harmful mutations than mainland kākāpō, which have since died out.

The researchers wrote that the finding is evidence a small population can survive hundreds of generations of isolation. The species is still critically endangered, though, and many of its eggs fail to hatch.

Nicolas Dussex, a researcher at the Centre for Palaeogenetics in Stockholm, led the study. Few birds anywhere are as inbred or as endangered as the kākāpō, Dussex said.

Kākāpō likely numbered in the hundreds of thousands before people reached New Zealand. Overhunting and predators introduced by people reduced them to 51 birds by 1995.

Fifty came from Stewart Island. The other was a mainland male named Richard Henry.

When the study came out, 201 kākāpō were alive, all on island sanctuaries.

For 49 kākāpō, Dussex’s team analyzed each bird’s complete set of DNA, called its genome. Thirty-five were Stewart Island birds.

The rest were mainland birds: Richard Henry and 13 museum specimens. Those specimens were collected between 1847 and 1924.

An inbred bird’s DNA has a recognizable pattern. A chick gets one copy of its genome from each parent. When the parents are related, long stretches of the two copies match.

In the Stewart Island birds, matching stretches made up 53% of the genome on average. In the mainland birds, matching stretches made up 15%.

So the island birds were far more inbred. The team then counted the most damaging kind of mutation, the kind predicted to stop a gene from working.

Mainland birds carried 34.1 of those mutations on average. Island birds carried 17.6 of them, about half as many.

Many of these mutations seem to do harm mainly when a bird inherits the same one from both parents. That happens more often when the parents are related.

Chicks that inherit the mutation from both parents are less likely to survive or breed, the team suggested. Over many generations, the mutation becomes rarer. Geneticists call that process purging.

The team tested the idea with computer simulations spanning 25,000 years. A simulated population that stayed small, like Stewart Island’s, ended up with fewer harmful mutations than a larger one like the mainland’s.

But in a simulated population one-tenth as large as the island’s, the harm from mutations grew instead. So purging seems to need enough birds for natural selection to work.

After the kākāpō’s recent decline, the team wrote, the birds may now face the same growing harm as that smallest population.

Conservation managers have favored matings with Richard Henry and his offspring, to help the population keep its ability to adapt.

“We show that the single male survivor from the mainland, Richard Henry, has more harmful mutations than Stewart Island birds,” said Love Dalén of the Swedish Museum of Natural History, who supervised the study.

“Therefore, there could be a risk that these harmful mutations spread in future generations.”

Richard Henry was also genetically distinct, Dalén added, and may have carried useful genetic diversity. The team recommended weighing that benefit against the risk and, if necessary, limiting breeding between the two family lines.

Dussex’s team counted mutations in DNA and didn’t measure what they do to a living bird. Their effects on survival and breeding weren’t known.

The team called for research into the genetic causes of the kākāpō’s low fertility and hatching success.

In some other shrinking species, harmful mutations increased as numbers fell, the authors wrote. In others, the mutations decreased.

Next, the team planned to examine other highly inbred birds and mammals, to learn whether the kākāpō is a rare exception.

The full study was published in the journal Cell Genomics.

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Archaeologists in Texas uncovered a roughly 6,500-year-old hunting kit with darts, an atlatl and a rare preserved pronghorn hide.

Deep inside San Esteban Rockshelter, south of Marfa, Texas, archaeologists recovered parts of a hunter’s weapon kit that someone left behind thousands of years ago.

The Center for Big Bend Studies at Sul Ross State University presented the find in its spring 2025 newsletter. The center puts the kit’s age at about 6,500 years and calls it “potentially the oldest example of a full weapon system in North America.”

The kit as a whole has not yet been described in a peer-reviewed study. Only one of its pieces, a spear-thrower known as an atlatl, has been the subject of a journal paper.

The kit includes six stone-tipped dart foreshafts – the front section of a dart – and four self-tipped hardwood dart foreshafts

There are also four dart nock-ends, the rear tips that fit against the atlatl. A broken hunting boomerang and a partial atlatl complete the weapons.

The excavators also found a folded pronghorn hide. It had been tanned, and its hair was still intact.

Spaced holes ran around the hide’s perimeter so it could be tied to a frame, as Plains groups often did.

“We just sat there and stared at it in wonder,” said Bryon Schroeder, then director of the center. Someone had folded the hide and set it on top of a rock, he explained.

“And nobody touched it for 6,000 years,” Schroeder said.

An atlatl is essentially a wooden stick with a handle on one end and a hook or socket on the other. It acts as an extension of the human arm, increasing leverage against the dart, the National Park Service explains.

The San Esteban atlatl is described in a 2023 atlatl paper by Schroeder and Devin Pettigrew, an assistant professor at the center.

A radiocarbon date indicates that the atlatl is more than 6,000 years old. That made it the oldest directly dated atlatl in North America at the time, the authors reported in the Journal of Big Bend Studies.

In a paper published in 2026, Pettigrew and other archaeologists at the center measured how fast modern throwers launch darts.

That research shows that heavier darts, paired with strong and skilled throwers, become lethal to very large animals such as mammoths, the center said.

“In most archeological sites in Texas, only things made of stone survive,” notes Texas Beyond History, a project of the University of Texas at Austin. The dry Lower Pecos rock shelters are an exception.

Those shelters have yielded hardwood dart foreshafts and rabbit sticks, as well as bow fragments and arrow foreshafts from the bow-and-arrow era. Complete atlatls are very rare even there.

Unique depositional conditions favored the preservation of the Schöningen wooden spears in Germany. A 2025 dating study placed them at about 200,000 years old.

A mammoth-ivory boomerang from Poland is one of the oldest known in Europe, an Obłazowa Cave analysis concluded. A statistical model built on dated bones from the same layer places it between 42,290 and 39,280 years ago.

“We can use the wood to reconstruct the environment and learn more about the amount of time they spent working on tools,” Schroeder said.

Schroeder’s reading of the scene is that a person came to the back of the cave and sorted through the gear piece by piece. Some parts were judged good and others not, and then the person went on their way.

“But that one small act is going to have profound implications in understanding the environment,” he said.

The shelter sits in Presidio County along Alamito Creek, a tributary of the Rio Grande. A spring-fed rock pool, or tinaja, lies in front of it.

The center has worked there since 2019 with the Odyssey Archaeological Research Fund at the University of Kansas. The partners were looking for traces of the region’s earliest people.

The Odyssey fund was established in 2003 with an endowment from Joseph and Ruth Cramer. Its director is Rolfe Mandel, a distinguished professor of anthropology at the university.

In 2020, the excavators uncovered a hearth-like feature with burned and unburned bird eggshells dated to about 15,200 years ago.

“Though this site was pot-hunted in the past, the many large boulders that spalled from the ceiling preserved pockets of shelter deposits,” the Kansas team’s site summary states. Pot-hunting means digging for artifacts to collect.

The atlatl foreshafts and darts came from intact deposits in an area the excavators call the Sandal Shaft. The Kansas team dates them to about 6,200 years ago, a little younger than the center’s figure.

The pronghorn hide lay about 2 inches (5 centimeters) above them, capped with a metate, a grinding stone.

The hide yielded a raw radiocarbon age of 5,621 years, plus or minus 24, not yet converted to calendar years. That makes it about 600 years younger than some of the foreshafts and darts, the Kansas researchers report.

The information was obtained from the Center for Big Bend Studies.

Fourth Circuit Upholds Virginia's Law Terminating Agreements to Help With Immigration Enforcement - but Strikes Down anti-Mask Law

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

The ruling on Section 287 agreements overturns an earlier district court decision.

In September, I wrote about the the federal district court decision striking down Virginia's new law terminating Section 287(g) agreements, under which various state and local law enforcement agencies agree to help with federal immigration enforcement. Today, in United States v. Virginia, the US Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit reversed that decision. But, at the same time, in a 2-1 ruling, they struck down Virginia's law barring law enforcement officers (including federal ones) from wearing masks except in relatively narrowly specified circumstances. Here is an excerpt from the court's opinion (written by Judge Heytens) on the 287(g) issue:

We vacate the preliminary injunction that bars enforcing the 287(g) Law with respect to existing agreements. The district court concluded the Federal Government was likely to succeed on the merits because rendering such agreements unenforceable violate both the intergovernmental immunity doctrine and the Contracts Clause. We disagree. The intergovernmental immunity doctrine does not apply because the 287(g) Law neither directly regulates the Federal Government nor prevents private parties from contracting with it…. The 287(g) Law also does not violate the Contracts Clause for two separate reasons: (1) because the existing agreements expressly permit unilateral termination by the non-federal party; and (2) even if they did not, the Commonwealth would have constitutionally valid justifications for requiring state and local officials to terminate or renegotiate any such agreements.

Later in the opinion Judge Heytens expands on why Section 287(g) termination does not violate the Contracts Clause:

We conclude the Federal Government cannot satisfy that test for two different, yet complementary, reasons. First, all the agreements covered by the district court's injunction expressly permit the Commonwealth's agents to unilaterally terminate without cause, which means the 287(g) Law does not "substantially impair" (Sveen, 584 U.S. at 819) a contractual relationship at Step 1. Second, even if the agreements did not so provide, neither the Commonwealth's agencies nor those of its localities may contract away "an essential attribute of [the Commonwealth's] sovereignty." United States Tr. Co. of N.Y. v. New Jersey, 431 U.S. 1, 23 (1977). Here, that attribute is the Commonwealth's power to decide whether and on what terms to assist with the ongoing enforcement of federal law.

I largely agree. In my post on the district court decision, I offered some additional reasons why terminating these agreements doesn't violate the Contracts Clause.

The anti-mask law presents more difficult issues because - as the majority emphasizes - it directly regulates federal law enforcement officials. On the other hand, the law imposes the same constraints on state and local officers, as well, and therefore doesn't discriminate against them. On balance, I agree with Judge Robert Bruce King's dissent:

At bottom, a single ICE officer should never be able to override a duly enacted statute of the Commonwealth of Virginia in the absence of some federal law, regulation, or other valid supporting authority. Consider what purportedly conflicts with the Mask/Identity Statute. A federal statute? None. A treaty of the United States? None. A federal regulation or executive order? None. A written policy located anywhere within ICE or the Department of Homeland Security? None has been identified. What we have today is a single sentence in a Declaration asserting that individual ICE officers possess the discretion and ability to conceal their identities when they alone deem it appropriate. No supervision is at all necessary or required and the agency itself has no involvement in the ICE officer's daily decisions on whether to wear a mask. I cannot and will not accept the proposition that individualized discretion, untethered to any identifiable source of federal law, enjoys constitutional supremacy over a duly enacted statute of the Commonwealth.

As the majority recognizes, federal law enforcement officers are not generally exempt from state law while on the job. For example, they must obey state traffic laws when driving their vehicles. A valid federal law could override a conflicting state law. But here, there is no such overriding federal law, or even a regulation (though I'm not convinced the latter alone would be enough). Just the preferences of ICE agents. Those preferences are not enough to secure an exemption from state law.  Federal law (when constitutionally valid) is supreme over state law. The preferences of individual federal officials or agencies are not.

Stalwart is now a drop-in replacement for Microsoft Exchange

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

For more than twenty years, “the mail server” in most companies meant Microsoft Exchange, and “the mail client” meant Outlook. Today we are releasing Stalwart 1.2.0, and with it Stalwart becomes a drop-in replacement for Microsoft Exchange.

Stalwart now implements the protocols that Exchange clients speak: MAPI over HTTP, Exchange Web Services (EWS), Exchange ActiveSync and Autodiscover. Outlook, Apple Mail and the mail apps on phones and tablets connect to Stalwart as they would to an Exchange server, with mail, calendars, contacts, tasks and notes in one account. Users type their email address and password, Autodiscover does the rest, and they keep working in the client they already know.

Exchange support is part of the open source Community edition, under the AGPL.

Before going into what Stalwart now does, it is worth looking at where self-hosted Exchange stands today, because it explains why so many organisations are looking for a way out.

Microsoft has not announced the end of Exchange Server. On paper, on-premises Exchange still exists. In practice, every road Microsoft is building leads to Exchange Online:

None of these steps forces anyone to move on its own. Together, they leave an Exchange administrator with a shrinking set of choices: keep running a server that will not grow, or move the organisation’s mail into Microsoft’s cloud.

For many organisations the second option is not acceptable. Email is the record of a company’s contracts, negotiations, legal advice, HR matters and customer data. Placing it with a US provider means placing it within reach of US law, including the CLOUD Act, which allows US authorities to compel American providers to hand over data they control wherever it is stored. Public administrations, healthcare providers, law firms, defence contractors and many European companies cannot accept that, or no longer want to. Data sovereignty means that you decide where your data lives, under which jurisdiction, and who can read it. A mail service in someone else’s cloud cannot offer that, and an on-premises server whose vendor is steering its customers toward that cloud offers less of it every year.

The obstacle to leaving Exchange has rarely been the server. It has been the client.

Many people have spent their whole working life in Outlook. They have years of muscle memory in it: their folder layout, their rules, their calendar views, their shared mailboxes, the way they book meeting rooms. Many organisations also have workflows built around it, such as delegated calendars managed by an assistant. Replacing Exchange with a standard IMAP and CalDAV server has always meant telling every one of those users to switch to a different mail client, or to reconfigure Outlook into a reduced IMAP mode where calendars and contacts no longer sync. For a migration project, that is often where the plan dies.

With Stalwart 1.2.0 the users do not need to change anything. The administrator moves the mailboxes and points the DNS records at Stalwart, and users connect with the same Outlook they used before.

The Exchange protocols are not a separate silo inside Stalwart. They are another door into the same accounts. A message filed in Outlook is in the same mailbox an IMAP or JMAP client sees, a meeting accepted on a phone appears in the calendar that CalDAV clients read, and a contact added in Apple Contacts shows up in the CardDAV address book. A user can keep Outlook on the office desktop, the native mail app on a phone and a JMAP webmail in the browser, all on one account.

Rules created in Outlook’s Rules and Alerts are stored on the server as mail rules, which run as Sieve when the message is delivered. They apply when Outlook is closed and whatever client the user opens next. Delegates and folder permissions set in Outlook become regular Stalwart shares, so they apply to every protocol. Shared mailboxes open automatically in Outlook from group membership, as they do with Exchange.

Not every part of an Exchange deployment has a counterpart. Stalwart does not offer public folders, archive mailboxes, mobile device management, RPC over HTTP (Outlook 2010 and earlier) or NTLM and Kerberos sign-in: clients authenticate with a password, an app password or OAuth. The Exchange documentation covers configuration, DNS and Autodiscover, each family of clients, and the differences from Exchange that administrators should know about before a migration.

One Outlook is missing from the list above: the new Outlook for Windows, the one that Windows now installs by default and that classic Outlook keeps offering through a toggle in its title bar.

The new Outlook does not work with Stalwart, and it does not work with any self-hosted server, Exchange included. It is built to work through Microsoft’s cloud. Microsoft does not support on-premises Exchange accounts in it at all.

The new Outlook does accept IMAP accounts, and that might look like a way around the problem. It is not. When an IMAP account is added to the new Outlook, the connection to the mail server is not made from the user’s computer. As heise and c’t reported in November 2023, the new Outlook sends the IMAP server name, the user name and the password to Microsoft, and Microsoft’s cloud connects to the mail server on the user’s behalf. Every message then passes through Microsoft’s infrastructure on its way to the screen. Microsoft does not only see the mail; it holds the password to the mailbox.

For an organisation that runs its own mail server to keep its data under its own control, this defeats the purpose of the whole exercise. We advise against using the new Outlook with any self-hosted email deployment, whether it runs Stalwart or anything else. Users who see the “Try the new Outlook” toggle in classic Outlook should leave it off.

The same applies on phones. Outlook for iOS and Android reach on-premises mailboxes through a Microsoft cloud service as well, which requires a hybrid Exchange Online setup. On mobile devices, the native mail, calendar and contacts apps, which connect directly over ActiveSync, are the better choice.

The obvious question is why Stalwart is adding Exchange protocols now. EWS is being removed from Exchange Online, Microsoft’s own future for Outlook is a cloud client, and classic Outlook is reportedly supported until 2029. Why build support for protocols that will be obsolete in a few years?

Because the companies that run Exchange today cannot wait a few years, and because those protocols are the bridge they need to cross.

An organisation with a few hundred Outlook users cannot replace its mail server and its mail client in the same weekend. It can replace the server, though, if nobody notices. With Stalwart speaking the Exchange protocols, the server moves first: mailboxes are migrated, DNS records change, and on Monday every user opens the same Outlook and the same phone apps they used on Friday. The organisation’s mail is back under its own control from day one, and no one’s work is interrupted to get there.

The client can then change at the pace that suits the users. Over time, as classic Outlook reaches the end of its life and the new Outlook remains tied to Microsoft’s cloud, we expect users to move gradually to third-party email clients built on open standards, and in particular on JMAP. Outlook will stop being an option for anyone who cares about where their email lives, and by then the people who used it will have had the time to choose something else without a deadline over their heads.

Exchange support is not where Stalwart is going. It is how organisations get from where they are to where Stalwart is.

Exchange compatibility is not a small piece of work. It adds about 200,000 lines of code to Stalwart, and we know that some of you would rather not carry that weight if you never plan to see an Outlook client. The complaint, which was filed late last week and lists President and CEO Leah Binder alongside her organization, follows a similar suit brought by Tenet hospitals in the state that convinced a judge that nonparticipating hospitals were being “unfairly” penalized in Leapfrog’s methodology.

The group was ordered to withhold safety grades for those hospitals and in grade releases since has voluntarily chosen not to award ratings to hundreds of others that did not submit recent quality data to its surveys. Leapfrog appealed that decision in September, and has said it is reviewing a potential methodology change for upcoming gradings to incorporate non-submitting hospitals.

The Florida Office of Attorney General James Uthmeier’s lawsuit cites that case. It argues that Leapfrog’s poor scores for “at least seven Florida hospitals” misled patients, and outlined specific cases in which patients had declined care over the perceived poor performances of those hospitals.

“The Leapfrog Group told Florida patients its hospital grades were peer-reviewed and backed by experts. That was a lie,” Uthmeier said in a Friday release announcing the litigation. “When hospitals declined to fill out its survey, Leapfrog handed them the worst scores in the country without a shred of data, and patients refused surgery because of it. Leapfrog and its CEO should expect to be held accountable.”

The office requested that the court permanently bar Leapfrog from “misrepresenting to Florida consumers … the data, sources, methods or expert approvals underlying any hospital grade or score they publish,” or from representing the scores as peer reviewed. It also seeks to limit Leapfrog’s solicitation of charitable contributions, make corrective disclosures, and pay out other relief to providers, patients or other affected individuals and groups.

Leapfrog, in a statement to Fierce Healthcare, said the office’s case “poses the specter of outright government censorship,” and was “piggybacking” on the Tenet hospitals’ case “with old allegations Leapfrog strongly refuted.”

The methodology change at the heart of the cases came ahead of Leapfrog’s fall 2024 safety grade publication. The Tenet hospitals, in their case, highlighted internal communications and other testimony that questioned the validity of the changes. Both lawsuits suggest the changes were made to maximize hospital participation in Leapfrog’s survey, which would bring downstream increases to revenues it collects from allowing hospitals to advertise their grades.

Uthmeier’s office has also filed a friend of the court brief to the appellate court now considering Leapfrog’s appeal, where it similarly argues that Leapfrog’s conduct is a violation of Florida’s consumer protection laws and not protected by the First Amendment.
