Latin Grammys 2026: Rosalía Wins Album of the Year

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Janet Jackson also presented Daddy Yankee with the person of the year honor.

Spanish singer Rosalía took home the top prize of album of the year at the 2026 Latin Grammys on Thursday night for her 2025 album Lux.

She opened the 27th annual Latin Grammy Awards, live from Sin City, with an emotive medley of her Lux tracks “La Rumba Del Perdón,” “La Perla” and “Dios Es Un Stalker,” joined by angelic dancers ornamented with halos atop a stage meant to look like heavenly clouds.

It was a reminder of the expansiveness of her album: an offbeat, full-hearted embrace of her classical training through huge orchestral movements, inspired by the lives of female saints. Her “La Perla” also won song of the year, and during her speech, she thanked Mexico and invited her collaborators on the song, the American urban sierreño trio Yahritza Y Su Esencia, to join her on stage.

Daddy Yankee, the 2026 Latin Grammy Awards’ person of the year and first Latin urbano artist to receive the honor, ripped through his biggest hits an hour into Thursday’s award show, treating the stage like a stadium floor.

It was one for the history books: There were outfit changes, set changes and his native Puerto Rico always at the forefront as he ran through “Ella Me Levantó,” “Con Calma,” “BOTZ,” “Despacito,” “Lo Que Pasó, Pasó,” “Sonríele,” “Rompe,” “Pokito” and, of course, “Gasolina” — joined by Bizarrap, Bad Gyal, Feid, Jay Wheeler, French Montana, LL Cool J and Diljit Dosanjh.

When it came time for Daddy Yankee to receive his honor, it was delivered to him by Janet Jackson “with admiration and respect,” as she said in her speech. When he accepted the award, he concluded by saying “Puerto Rico, recuerda. … Puerto Rico no es pequeño. Pequeño el mapa.” In English: “Puerto Rico, remember. … Puerto Rico is not small. The map is small.”

The award show took place live from Las Vegas’ MGM Grand Garden Arena. It aired live on Univision, UniMás, Galavisión and ViX and hosted by actor, producer and musician Roselyn Sánchez.

For the first award of the night, Pablo Alboran and Backstreet Boys’ Howie D presented Juanes with best pop/rock album for Juanesteban.

Performances were at a high level: Karol G was joined by a full orchestra and 12 Mariachis for her música mexicana ballad “Ese Hombre es Malo.” Mon Laferte broke hearts with her soulful “Femme Fatale.” Silvana Estrada’s “Dime” bridged culture and heritage with her signature Venezuelan cuatro, a four-stringed instrument similar in structure to a ukulele.

Kany García stunned with her acoustic, aflamencado single “La Niña Que Fui.” The Uruguayan-born Jorge Drexler’s rendition of “Las Palabras” felt like theater. And Argentine rapper Milo J performed “Solifican12” and “Gil,” joined by the Latin American folk icon Gustavo Santaolalla.

Carín León brought the house down with “Desde Que Te Tengo,” as did Juanes and Vivir Quintana with “Humano.” Elena Rose was joined by sword-wielding dancers for “Alma.”

Brazilian superstar Anitta did a medley of her “Equilibrium” hits — “Desgraça,” “Choka Choka” and “Meia Noite” among them — an eclectic mix of Brazilian funk, samba, bossa nova, semba, reggae, electronic pop performed in Portuguese, Spanish and English.

Soon after, she was back on stage accepting the trophy for Portuguese language contemporary pop album. She used her speech to shine a light on the Black culture of her native Brazil.

There was a touching tribute to the late ranchera icon, Mexican singer-songwriter José Alfredo Jiménez, produced by Edgar Barrera: The Warning covered “Te Solté la Rienda,” Reik did “La Media Vuelta,” Sofía Reyes and Yami Safdie dueted “Si Nos Dejan” and Camila Fernández brought it home with a big belting “El Rey.” Not soon afterward, she headed back to the stage to perform a full-throated “Serpiente De Cascabel.”

It was the first of two tributes: The second was for Willie Colón, the social activist and Grammy-nominated architect of urban salsa music, who died in February of this year. His son, Miguel Colón, as well as Nathy Peluso and Luis Figueroa and others performed.

Awards were handed out quickly, too. Karol G took home the trophy for urban music album for Tropicoqueta. Xavi won contemporary Mexican album for Dosis, his first Latin Grammy. Fonseca was presented the trophy for contemporary tropical album shortly thereafter for his album, Antes Que El Tiempo Se Vaya.

Best new artist went to Colombian singer Aria Vega, who gave her acceptance speech through tears. This year, the Latin Recording Academy made changes to the category so that only artists with fewer than three albums or 25 singles released were eligible to win.

Most of the evening’s awards were handed out during a pre-televised Premiere Ceremony. That included Bobby Pulido winning the Latin Grammy for best Tejano album for his release Bobby Pulido & Friends: Una Tuya y Una Mía (Vol. 2/En Vivo). Just days earlier, Pulido made history in the U.S. midterm elections by securing a seat in the House of Representatives for the Democrats by flipping a Texas district seat.

“I want to tell you that I’m retiring from music, and I’m leaving with so much gratitude,” he said in his acceptance speech.

Managing Offshore Wind in the Face of Uncertainty

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

This report lays out key adaptive management principles and recommendations for regulators.

Despite current headwinds from the Trump administration, offshore wind energy is essential for transitioning away from harmful fossil fuels to a clean energy economy. Offshore wind could provide more than three times the electricity required to power the United States, giving the country an opportunity to fight climate change, reduce local and regional air and water pollution, and grow a new industry that supports thousands of high-quality careers.

While the need for clean energy is urgent, we can and must ensure that all offshore wind in the United States, including floating offshore wind systems, are developed responsibly and in a manner that avoids, minimizes, mitigates, and monitors for adverse impacts to biodiversity and coastal and marine ecosystems. “Adaptive management” is crucial for meeting the dual goals of facilitating a rapid transition to renewable energy while minimizing impacts to the ocean in the face of uncertainty—and continuing to do so over time.

This report explores how adaptive management can be implemented in offshore wind development, provides examples of its use in other energy sectors today, and offers clear recommendations for agencies and policymakers.

XBOX CEO Casually Reveals Wolfenstein 3 In Production

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Daniel is a lifelong gamer with a love for various genres and gameplay styles who enjoys exploring games and playing from various angles.

The XBOX brand, through its various acquisitions, has managed to obtain ownership of some of the oldest, arguably most important video game IPs. One of those IPs is Wolfenstein, the series that gave us the first true FPS with the release of Wolfenstein 3D all the way back in 1992. Since 2014, the Wolfenstein IP has been in the hands of MachineGames, with Bethesda serving as publisher. Microsoft owns ZeniMax, Zennimax owns Bethesda, and so XBOX usually gets first dibs when Wolfenstein comes knocking.

You might not think it, but a serious shooter like Wolfenstein 3D was a huge influence, according to Randy Pitchford.

It’s rather fitting, then, that someone high up the chain of command in XBOX would have the privilege of divulging new Wolfenstein news. Though, even then, I certainly wouldn’t expect the someone in question to be its literal CEO, nor would I expect said news to drop on a random Thursday, but let nobody say they can’t make things interesting.

On her personal X, formerly Twitter account, XBOX CEO Asha Sharma rather casually announced that MachineGames has officially begun production on the next mainline Wolfenstein game, the first since Wolfenstein II: The New Colossus was released in 2017. Obviously, since production has just started, details are basically non-existent, but both Sharma and MachineGames’ own X account did give us a couple of tidbits to chew on.

Sharma’s tweet keeps the announcement fairly quick and concise, reading, “Wolfenstein 3 is officially in production at @MachineGames. One of gaming's most important franchises, and a team that has done remarkable work bringing it forward. Looking forward to sharing more.”

One of gaming's most important franchises, and a team that has done remarkable work bringing it forward.

Assuming the game will have some manner of subtitle like its predecessors, Sharma didn’t divulge what it was. What she did share, though, was a screenshot of the game’s apparent script. This screenshot is apparently set during the game’s first chapter in a locale called “Nazitraz,” presumably a prison of some sort, in which series protagonist B.J. Blazkowicz confronts a man named “Otto,” who blubbers in fear in his presence. Series mainstay Fergus Reid also appears to be present, but the screenshot cuts off before he says or does anything.

The Elder Scrolls 6 has finally had its title officially confirmed, weeks after it was not so subtly leaked to the wider public.

Shortly after Sharma’s tweet, MachineGames shared the same script screenshot on its own X account alongside the caption, “His reputation precedes him. Wolfenstein 3 is in production.” MachineGames’ post also added a photo of two actors in the midst of motion-capture work: Debra Wilson, who previously played Grace Walker in New Colossus, and B.J.’s actor, Brian Bloom.

Wolfenstein, as a series, has been pretty quiet for a few years now. As I mentioned, New Colossus was the last mainline game to release, which was nearly a decade ago in 2017. The series has had spin-offs since then, including Wolfenstein: Youngblood and Wolfenstein: Cyberpilot, both of which were released in 2019. The former was a co-op game starring B.J.’s teenage daughters as they searched for their father in France, while the latter was a VR game in which an unnamed hacker works to hack various Nazi robots. Unfortunately, neither of these games were particularly well-received.

Still, there’s no reason to be pessimistic, especially considering that it was MachineGames that brought us the unexpected slam dunk that was Indiana Jones and the Great Circle in 2024. I’d say that’s pretty definitive proof that the studio still knows how to put an excellent game together, and I’m optimistic about what’s on the horizon.

Incidentally, MachineGames is also involved in a creative capacity for the live-action Wolfenstein series that’s currently in development for Prime Video. Who knows, we might just have a one-two punch of new Wolfenstein media to look forward to in the not-too-distant future.

Scott Bakula Officially Returns To Star Trek For Adorable Reunion With Porthos

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Star Trek's list of iconic characters feels like it could go on forever, but there's probably no more adorable or unexpected duo than Captain Jonathan Archer and his beloved beagle, Porthos. Scott Bakula's role in Star Trek: Enterprise as Archer ushered in a new era for the franchise aboard the Enterprise NX-01, with the doggo Porthos being a somewhat out-of-place addition to the crew that completely changed the dynamic of the show and the captain's character.

Archer's relationship with Porthos brought a touching and wholesome element to the franchise, as the loyal beagle was a constant source of companionship for the captain through thick and thin, with their bond providing some of Enterprise's most endearing moments. Now, more than two decades after the series concluded, Scott Bakula's Captain Archer is officially returning to Star Trek this week for an overdue reunion with his adorable four-legged sidekick.

Wizards of the Coast has found an adorable way to translate Archer and Porthos' relationship into the new Magic: The Gathering crossover releasing this week. The new "Captain Jonathan Archer" card brings Scott Bakula's Enterprise captain back to the franchise, but easily the best part of the comeback is that his faithful canine companion automatically joins him.

Archer is a rare green 3/3 Legendary Creature costing three mana to play, including one green, but when he enters the battlefield, he creates Porthos, a legendary green 1/1 Dog creature token. For those unfamiliar with Magic, tokens are essentially game pieces created through other cards' abilities that serve as creatures, artifacts, or other permanents on the battlefield, functioning much like regular cards despite not being part of a player's main deck.

Porthos even has his own collectible token card, so players can actually reunite the adorable duo on the battlefield without having to draw and cast two separate characters. It's an incredibly fitting tribute to their relationship in Enterprise, where Porthos was Archer's constant companion (despite having no official Starfleet duties). Even better, the pair actually complement each other through Archer's second ability, Federation.

By spending one green mana and tapping Archer, players can give a creature they control +X/+X until the end of their turn, with X being the number of different creature types among their non-Borg creatures.

Archer counts as both a Human and Officer, while Porthos adds Dog to the mix. Together, they provide three distinct creature types, so Archer can actually turn his adorable 1/1 companion into a formidable 4/4 creature. It's a clever translation of the partnership and a great nod to longtime Enterprise fans.

Archer is included as part of the main Star Trek expansion (TRK), available through boosters, while Porthos is included as a separate collectible token card. The crossover also includes four themed Commander decks: Federation Fleet, Landing Party, Klingon Fury, and We Are the Borg, as well as unique scene cards featuring mosaic-style themed sets from across the franchise, such as Unimatrix Zero.

Magic: The Gathering x Star Trek officially releases Friday, November 13 to celebrate the franchise's 60th anniversary. However, it's worth noting that Wizards of the Coast has warned that certain products, including Commander decks and select bundles, are experiencing shipping delays and may arrive after the official launch.

Laser scanner that maps the chemistry inside tissue samples has been miniaturized into a handheld portable device

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A handheld probe maps tissue chemistry without stains, and in lab tests its images closely matched those from a full-size instrument.

Machines that can map the chemistry inside a slice of tissue, showing where proteins and fats sit without using dye, have been too big to leave the lab bench.

A research team has now modified the imaging head of one to fit a probe that is 8 inches (20 centimeters) long. In side-by-side tests, its pictures closely matched those from a full-size lab instrument.

That matters because full-size machines can’t be carried to where patients are. A probe this size could someday work in the room where a tumor is removed.

So far, though, it has only tested the chemistry in thin slices of preserved tissue and plastics.

The work was done in Rohith Reddy’s lab at the University of Houston, and a pathologist at MD Anderson Cancer Center supplied bone marrow samples. Reddy already has a use in mind.

“Ultimately, this technology could make it possible to assess tissue during cancer surgery,” said Reddy, who leads the team. “After removing a suspected tumor, a surgeon could scan the freshly excised tissue.”

This would “help determine whether it is malignant or whether cancer cells remain at the surgical margin. This complementary information would be available while the patient is still in the operating room instead of having to wait for results from laboratory testing,” Reddy explained.

The instrument works with mid-infrared light. Like all infrared light, it’s invisible, and its waves are longer than the ones a TV remote sends. Molecules absorb this light at wavelengths set by the bonds holding their atoms together. That gives proteins, fats and DNA each a pattern of their own.

The system uses two lasers aimed at the same tiny spot. One sends mid-infrared light in bursts, 1,000 times a second. Where molecules in the spot absorb it, the spot warms very slightly. A second laser, a red one, picks up the small changes that the warming causes.

Sweep that spot across a sample, point by point, and the result is a map of where a chosen chemical sits. Because visible red light does the detecting, the map shows tissue chemistry detail finer than a single cell.

No stain is needed. Pathology labs normally dye tissue before anyone looks at it, and dyes are often impractical outside the lab because of toxicity, regulation and cost, the team wrote.

Shrinking the machine meant solving one stubborn problem. The red light and the mid-infrared light have to land on exactly the same point. However, lens materials that work well for one tend to work badly for the other.

Materials that pass red light often absorb mid-infrared, while ones built for mid-infrared can bend different colors by different amounts and blur the signal.

Reddy’s team sent the infrared light through a special glass fiber made for those wavelengths, which did away with bulky open-air optics.

They also swapped lenses for mirrors, since a mirror reflects both kinds of light. A curved mirror at the tip focuses both beams onto one spot.

“Careful optical design and alignment helped to minimize the image distortions that mirrors can cause. We also designed the handheld scanner to connect to the light source through a fiber-optic cable, allowing it to be maneuvered easily around a sample,” Reddy said.

The lasers and electronics sit in a base unit of less than 1.5 square feet (0.14 square meters). Commercial instruments of this kind take up more than 9 square feet (0.84 square meters) of optical table, more than a card table’s top.

The team first scanned a test chart patterned onto a crystal slide. The handheld system picked out details about 0.00008 inches (2 micrometers) across. Its images of the chart closely matched a commercial benchtop instrument’s.

Next came human tissue: thin slices of cervical cancer from two patients and ovarian cancer from two others.

Images from the handheld probe and the benchtop machine correlated at 0.90, compared at a coarse level of detail, on a scale where 1 would be a perfect match.

In ovarian cancer tissue, the tissue chemistry patterns it recorded agreed with a standard lab infrared measurement at 0.935 on a similar scale.

In plain terms, the small probe recorded close to what the big machines did. Its scans also lined up with dyed slices of the same tissue, the kind that pathologists read every day. Connective tissue that the dye highlighted showed up strongly in the undyed chemical image.

A bone marrow biopsy from a patient gave a harder test. Doctors check marrow for a buildup of collagen, a kind of scarring linked to the marrow making fewer blood cells. They usually spot it with a stain that turns collagen blue.

Without any stain, the chemical scan picked out the dense framework of bone in the sample. It also showed the collagen wrapped around a blood vessel, matching the blue in a stained slice next to it.

The team tried a second scanning style on mouse kidney tissue, with the mirror at the tip both turning and moving. That scan showed a glomerulus, one of the kidney’s tiny filters, along with chemical detail that a plain image from the red laser alone didn’t have.

Every sample was a thin, preserved slice on a slide. The team hasn’t imaged living tissue, and none of the samples was fresh from surgery, which is the setting Reddy described.

“Although the current platform is still a research prototype, it establishes a technical foundation for field-deployable, label-free chemical imaging,” Reddy said.

The probe also used only part of the range its laser can reach. That laser is built from four chips that point in slightly different directions, so crossing from one chip to the next meant realigning the light into the fiber by hand.

In these tests, a moving mirror inside the probe or a stage that shifted the sample did the scanning.

The team hasn’t yet tested whether these scans can help a doctor tell cancer from healthy tissue. The researchers plan to widen the range of infrared light the probe uses and to speed up scanning, which would make holding it freehand more practical.

Before any clinical use, its repeatability, safety and diagnostic performance still have to be tested under realistic clinical conditions.

The full study was published in the journal Optica.

Early humans may have kept souvenirs half a million years ago

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Early humans may have collected keepsakes 500,000 years ago, a habit researchers suggest could have helped shape memory and a sense of self.

Somewhere in your house there’s a stone from a beach. A shell from a holiday. A pinecone your kid picked up on a walk three years ago that you can’t bring yourself to throw away.

It does nothing but it means everything. And according to a new study, you’re not the first human to feel that way. Not by about half a million years.

The study was led by Ran Barkai and Yoav Ram at Tel Aviv University.

The team argues that early humans in the Levant were already picking up colorful pebbles, fossil-studded rocks, and old weathered tools and carrying them home for reasons that may have gone beyond practical use.

According to the scientists, the habit of anchoring memories to objects didn’t just reflect the human mind. It may have helped build it.

The most striking evidence comes from the Sakhnin Valley in Lower Galilee.

Out of more than 200 surveyed handaxes at this Acheulean landscape, roughly ten were shaped around fossil imprints or unusual geological formations embedded in flint nodules.

Those nodules were harder to knap. Toolmakers normally avoided them.

But someone chose them anyway, then went out of their way to position the fossil or geological oddity at the center of the tool’s face.

The researchers argue that these choices were deliberate.

At Qesem Cave, small round colorful pebbles showed up starting around 400,000 years ago and kept showing up for the next 200,000 years. Their practical purpose remains unclear.

Then there’s something stranger. At Revadim, Jaljulia, and Qesem Cave, people picked up old flint tools that had been lying in the landscape long enough to develop a visible patina.

They resharpened the cutting edges, but they kept the old surfaces: the scars, the color changes, the story written into the stone.

Under magnification, wear traces show these recycled tools were barely used in their second life.

The fresh edges made them functional; preserving the patina may have held personal meaning.

The researchers call them “memory objects.”

Most discussions of weird prehistoric objects land on the same explanations. Art. Ritual. Status symbols. Shared cultural meaning.

This study does something different. It argues these objects worked at the personal level first.

No audience was required and no shared convention needed. Just one person who remembers what the stone means.

The authors lay out four things that separate a souvenir from a decoration: it’s tied to a specific episode in someone’s life, it helps verify a real experience against an imagined one, keeping it takes deliberate effort, and its meaning is private.

That last part matters. A keepsake points to something that happened to you, and only you need to know its meaning.

So how does holding a rock help you remember? The mechanism the authors point to is source monitoring. That refers to the brain’s way of sorting real experiences from dreams, hearsay, and imagination.

The hippocampus reconstructs episodes from partial cues. The perirhinal cortex handles familiarity, and the parahippocampal cortex fills in contextual detail.

The authors propose that a keepsake’s physical features could engage these memory systems together.

That matters more than it sounds. In small societies built on reciprocity, people who could reliably distinguish real experiences from imagined ones may have had an advantage.

Over enough generations, routine souvenir-keeping may have created a “souvenir niche” that favored brains better wired for autobiographical memory and sense of self.

“The disappearance of large animals, particularly elephants, required early humans to develop new hunting methods and become familiar with hunting grounds inhabited by animals such as fallow deer and wild cattle,” Barkai said.

“Objects taken from these places may have helped them maintain a personal connection with the places that would henceforth provide their livelihood.”

The elephant vanished from the Levant around 400,000 years ago. So did Homo erectus and  the Acheulean tool tradition.

Everything shifted. Smaller, faster prey meant learning new territories from scratch.

Familiar landscapes couldn’t be taken for granted anymore. Portable pieces of those places became worth carrying home.

The authors are clear this isn’t proof. Not every odd stone was a keepsake.

But the pattern is there. Objects with no clear practical role were carried far from their source, curated over long use-lives, and found near hearths and living areas rather than tool-making debris.

Aesthetics and raw material shortage don’t explain it cleanly.

“When early humans brought a colorful stone or an old tool they had found in the landscape back to their cave, that object may have helped them remember a significant event or encounter for the rest of their lives,” Ram said.

“In this way, keepsakes may have become a kind of ‘external memory device,’ supporting the evolution of brain mechanisms responsible for autobiographical memory, which is so characteristic of us as humans and so important in shaping our identity.”

The study is published in the journal Evolutionary Anthropology.
