Whitney Museum selects a staff curator and a stage director to lead 2028 biennial

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Meg Onli and Zack Winokur Photo by Bryan Derballa

The Whitney Museum of American Art has selected Meg Onli, a curator at the museum, and Zack Winokur, a choreographer and director for the stage, to curate the 2028 edition of the Whitney Biennial. It is due to open in March 2028.

“Every Whitney Biennial springs from a distinct curatorial alchemy,” Scott Rothkopf, the Whitney’s director, said in a statement. “Meg is one of the most compelling curatorial voices working today, with an extraordinary ability to champion artists and bring fresh perspectives to the history of American art. Zack is an equally inventive artistic programmer who has become one of the leading voices of his generation through groundbreaking work across opera, theater, dance and the visual arts. Their partnership promises a radical new vision for the 2028 Biennial.”

Onli is one of the Whitney’s star curators, having joined the institution as a curator-at-large in 2023, then as a curator in 2024. She co-curated the 2024 Whitney Biennial, titled Even Better Than the Real Thing, with fellow Whitney curator Chrissie Iles. Most recently she co-curated the museum’s current Roy Lichtenstein retrospective (until 10 May 2027).

“From the Whitney’s early Annuals to its many subsequent iterations, the Biennial has a rich history of reinvention that is already giving us much to think about,” Onli said in a statement. “I couldn’t be more excited to explore what the Biennial can be alongside Zack, who is such a dynamic thinker, and to see where our conversations with artists lead.”

Winokur’s work spans dance, music, visual art, opera and theatre. He is the co-founder and artistic director of the performing arts collective AMOC* (American Modern Opera Company), which last year staged a five-week takeover of the Lincoln Center campus on Manhattan’s Upper West Side. He was previously the artistic director of Little Island, a park and cultural event space designed by Heatherwick Studio and MNLA that rises out of the Hudson River not far from the Whitney. His projects for the stage and screen have included directing productions at Opera Philadelphia and the Santa Fe Opera, and working on the expanded version of The Wizard of Oz currently showing at Sphere in Las Vegas.

“I’m honoured to be curating the Whitney Biennial with Meg,” Winokur said in a statement. “Our shared desire to centre objects and people and the collision between them already echoes our collaboration. It’s that energy that makes the dialogue with her so fun and inspiring. As an artist, what excites me most is being in service to other artists, and I’m delighted to have the opportunity to do so in the context of the Whitney.”

The 2026 Whitney Biennial, which was organised by staff curators Marcela Guerrero and Drew Sawyer, closed in October. It was seen by more than 550,000 visitors, according to the museum’s figures, around 75% of whom identified as either Millennials or Gen Z, making it the youngest audience since the Whitney started tracking visitors’ ages.

The exhibition’s 2026 edition was also remarkably controversy-free, though the exhibition is often a lightning rod for debates, protests and political statements. The 2019 edition was led by Whitney curators Jane Panetta and Rujeko Hockley, and came to be known colloquially as the “tear gas biennial” due to weekly protests and participating artists’ threatened withdrawals over the Whitney’s vice chairman at the time, Warren Kanders, who was the chief executive of Safariland, a defense and security contractor that manufactured tear gas canisters that had been deployed against asylum-seekers at the Mexico-US border, among other materials. Kanders eventually resigned from the museum’s board; he is currently the chairman and chief executive of Cadre Holdings, a Florida-based manufacturer of security and law-enforcement equipment.

The 2017 Whitney Biennial, which was co-curated by Christopher Y. Lew and Mia Locks, was largely overshadowed by the wide-ranging debate over a single painting in the exhibition: Dana Schutz’s Open Casket (2016), in which the artist portrayed the famous image from 1955 of 14-year-old Emmett Till lying in an open casket following his brutal abduction, mutilation and murder. The artist Hannah Black wrote an open letter criticising the work’s inclusion in the biennial and calling for its destruction, sparking protests inside and outside the museum and arguments about censorship, identity and cultural appropriation.

The 2028 Whitney Biennial will be the 83rd edition of the exhibition, which started out as a series of medium-specific exhibitions before becoming the Whitney Annual in 1937, then transitioning to its every-other-year format in 1973. It remains the most closely watched recurring exhibition of contemporary art in the Americas.

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

PSA Airlines’ pilot union leaders have approved an agreement that extends the regional carrier’s pilot contract by two years and locks in three rounds of pay increases.

The Air Line Pilots Association announced the agreement Wednesday. It extends the existing contract through April 1, 2030. The previous extension, ratified in 2022, had set an amendable date of April 1, 2028.

Current pay rates will remain in place until June 1, 2027, when the first increases take effect. Additional raises are scheduled for June 1 in each of the following two years. The deal also changes scheduling and work rules. ALPA’s announcement did not specify the size of the raises or detail the scheduling changes.

“When we asked our pilots what mattered most, the answer was clear: Protect our pay. This agreement does that,” said Capt. Chris Sabia, who chairs the union’s PSA Master Executive Council, in a news release.

ALPA represents approximately 2,260 pilots at PSA, which is a wholly-owned regional subsidiary of American Airlines.

watchOS 27.3 arrived this week in beta alongside iOS 27.3, with a major new feature for the latest Apple Watch models: Siri Recap, which I’m already really loving.

If you bought an Apple Watch Series 12 or Apple Watch Ultra 4 this fall, there are two exclusive new features coming in watchOS 27.3: Siri Recap and Live Rewind.

Both ‘Audio Intelligence’ features were announced at Apple’s September keynote, but just debuted in beta this week.

Siri Recap in particular has stirred some controversy. Here’s what the feature does, per Apple:

Siri Recap ambiently takes notes on your conversations throughout your day and creates high-level Apple Intelligence–generated summaries that you can use later to jog your memory, so you can stay present in the moment. Siri Recaps can be set to automatically delete after a period of up to 7 days unless you choose to explicitly save them.

It’s understandable why users would be concerned. Once enabled, Siri Recap can turn your Apple Watch into a device that’s always listening and often takes notes on what it hears.

This article isn’t about making the privacy case for the feature. You can read Apple’s privacy protections here. Briefly though, key details include: there’s no speaker attribution or logging of sensitive information like financial data, authentication data, or personal identifiers; no audio is stored or recorded; the feature is opt-in; and you can set times or places where it stays disabled.

Speaking strictly of the functionality of Siri Recap itself, however, I’ve been very impressed.

I enabled Siri Recap after installing the iOS and watchOS 27.3 betas on Tuesday. When I checked the first recaps on Wednesday inside the Siri app, I was blown away.

Siri had created a record of the things I’d done Tuesday afternoon and evening with remarkable accuracy and detail, as seen above.

One example that especially shocked me: after shopping for Christmas decorations with my family, Siri documented the size of a table runner we looked at. The thing is, I had no memory of anyone even mentioning its size. My wife must have, but despite me missing it, Siri wrote it down.

Another fun one: Thursday morning during breakfast, my son wanted to listen some music on our HomePod. Later that day, I found that the songs we’d listened to were saved at the bottom of my latest recap.

It’s still very early days with Siri Recap, which is only in beta right now, but what I’ve seen this week has been really exciting. And so far at least, I’ve seen zero information in a recap that I’d consider too sensitive to be included.

If you want some help with this quest, here's where to find Princess Ruto in Zelda Ocarina of Time.

Once you talk to King Zora in the southeastern side of Zora Domain, he will tell you that his daughter is missing and he has searched for her as far as Lake Hylia — the exact place you must go. You just need to figure out how to get there.

Men Are Outspending Women on Botox. Here’s What They’re Asking For

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Guys are going to increasingly wild lengths to achieve their version of the idealized man. Looksmaxxing, as we’ve come to know it, has included surgical procedures to reshape their eyes, taking a hammer to their face to create a more chiseled jawline, and breaking their legs to gain a couple inches. But an OG in cosmetic procedures is seeing a resurgence in men, too.

Chances are you’re familiar with Botox: localized injections to smoothen wrinkles in common areas like the forehead, the eyes, or around the mouth. Long viewed as a treatment limited to the ladies, it’s increasingly becoming another form of male upkeep. Now we’re learning that men are outspending women on the popular procedure. According to an October 2026 report by Guidepoint Qsight, a medical aesthetics data and intelligence provider, men spent an average of $529 per neurotoxin visit in Q3 of 2026, compared with $455 for women. (The figures include not just Botox but also competitors like Daxxify and Xeomin.)

The potential explanation is simple: Botox is an easy way to look a little younger, more well-rested, and even less angry or severe. (As a built-in bonus, it’s virtually painless—especially compared to the barbaric looksmaxxing trends circulating as of late.) According to a 2025 survey of 2,900 aesthetic plastic surgeons, men accounted for over 15% of all Botox cases that year. It was also their top non-surgical treatment by a landslide.

The growing cohort of men opting into “Brotox” believe it’s money well spent. Nancy Samolitis, MD, a board-certified dermatologist in Los Angeles and co-founder of facile, has been treating men for years but has recently seen an uptick in male clientele, aged anywhere from 35 to 70. These days, “they’re more interested in skincare and more open to procedures,” she says. Many also book their first treatment nudged by their spouse or significant other who are existing patients. Others come in simply wanting to look better but not knowing what treatment to get. In many cases, Botox fits the bill.

While the uninitiated might think the injections make guys look surprised or less masculine, good work can be undetectable. Still you, just fresher.

Kyle Waldrop, a 37-year-old product designer in Los Angeles, first started getting Botox three years ago. “I’ve always been good about keeping up with a skincare routine and taking care of myself, so Botox felt like a natural extension of what I was already doing,” he says. His goal isn’t to change his face but to slow down wrinkles before they become obvious. He gets Botox for his forehead lines and crow’s feet, and recently started treating his neck lines, too.

But it’s not always about looking younger. Men are also using Botox in less obvious places. David Kim, MD, board-certified dermatologist and the founder of Soho Dermatology in New York City, treats the chin for more projection, as well as the underarms to combat excess sweating. Bell Yoo, NP, a board-certified nurse practitioner and the founder of Mimo Aesthetics in West Hollywood, adds that she injects the masseter (and sometimes temporalis) muscles for guys who struggle with jaw clenching or teeth grinding.

Aesthetically, men usually aren’t looking for the same thing from Botox as women. For starters, many want to soften their lines without erasing them. Preserving some natural movement is key. “My female patients often love that smooth, ‘glass skin’ appearance, whereas some of my male patients think it looks too waxy or oily,” Yoo says. Men also tend to opt out of a Botox brow lift, which many women request. “Men would much rather maintain a slightly heavier, lower-set brow than end up with an overly arched or ‘Spock’ brow,” Yoo says. Per Samolitis, this requires different injection points and doses tailored for guys. Kim, meanwhile, is careful not to overdo the crow’s feet. “It looks more natural when men have lines around the eyes, and it makes their face look better overall,” he says. These techniques quell the fears that guys have about looking less masculine or overdone. For most, the ultimate goal is to look relaxed and rejuvenated—not tight and taut. “When it’s done well and with the right amount, you look like yourself, just a bit more rested,” Waldrop says. “I still have all my natural movement and expressions, which is important to me.”

All that subtlety comes at a price. Men may end up paying more than women to treat the same areas, but the higher price tag is actually expected: Men often have larger, stronger facial muscles, which require more units to achieve their desired results, Yoo says.

While more men are embracing Botox, some remain tight-lipped about it. Additionally, Bond says many people don’t know that they can participate in the process. In October, she and Kristin Stitcher, former deputy chief of the Disability Rights Section, launched Public Powered Policy, a project dedicated to informing the public about the rulemaking process and empowering them to participate in it.

They were joined initially by a former paralegal who has since started attending law school and now serves as an adviser.

“Our goal is to reach a large swath of people, not just lawyers,” says Bond, who serves as the project’s executive director. “We are trying to reach the teachers in Missouri and the potato farmers in Idaho.”

A podcast, Regularly Speaking, marked the group’s launch. It focused on the Federal Trade Commission and what responsibility online platforms should have when scammers use their advertising tools to impersonate banks or government agencies. The show is available on Apple, Spotify, and YouTube.

“Our goal is not to tell people what to think or comment, but to give them the information on how to comment and let them decide how they want to do that or if they want to do that,” Stitcher says.
