Feedback Needed: Project by former DOJ lawyers shows people how to participate in federal rulemaking process

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When Rebecca Bond left her position as chief of the Justice Department’s Disability Rights Section in May 2025, she knew what she wanted to do next.

“I wanted to help people understand the power that they have to impact policy by telling their stories when a rule is open for comment,” says Bond, who spent decades working with federal regulations. That taught her how important public input is in the rulemaking process and taught her that there’s a general lack of understanding as to how rulemaking affects people in simple ways.

Additionally, Bond says many people don’t know that they can participate in the process. In October, she and Kristin Stitcher, former deputy chief of the Disability Rights Section, launched Public Powered Policy, a project dedicated to informing the public about the rulemaking process and empowering them to participate in it.

They were joined initially by a former paralegal who has since started attending law school and now serves as an adviser.

“Our goal is to reach a large swath of people, not just lawyers,” says Bond, who serves as the project’s executive director. “We are trying to reach the teachers in Missouri and the potato farmers in Idaho.”

A podcast, Regularly Speaking, marked the group’s launch. It focused on the Federal Trade Commission and what responsibility online platforms should have when scammers use their advertising tools to impersonate banks or government agencies. The show is available on Apple, Spotify, and YouTube.

Stitcher says that while rulemaking can sound dull, it’s astonishing how much it impacts people’s day-to-day living.

Rules “can affect the quality of your water and air, what’s in your kids’ school lunches, and what transportation is available to you,” says Stitcher, the group’s director of policy and programs.

The group also communicates timely information about upcoming rules via Instagram reels, using the handle @publicpoweredpolicy. And Bond and Stitcher plan to educate the public through writing opinion pieces and training grassroots organizations.

Public Powered Policy is sponsored by the Social Good Fund, a California nonprofit. Bond also received a Leadership in Government Fellowship administered by Assets Under Movement, and the project welcomes individual donations.

The Public Powered Policy website explains the basics of how the administrative rules are written, the process for draft versions, and how to participate in public comment and make an impact.

Someone giving specific feedback about “lived experience” can influence what a rule ultimately says, according to Stitcher and Bond.

“Our goal is not to tell people what to think or comment, but to give them the information on how to comment and let them decide how they want to do that or if they want to do that,” Stitcher says.

Stitcher advises people commenting on rules to simply tell their stories with as much personal detail as possible.

“You are the expert in your own life,” Stitcher says. “They won’t know unless you tell them.”

Inside the Governor’s Mansions That Will Soon Be Home to 19 Newly Elected Leaders

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

In January, the United States’ 19 newly elected governors won’t just have inauguration day on their calendars, but moving day, too. All but five states have governor’s mansions for their leaders to live and work from, with the exceptions being Arizona, Idaho, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and Vermont. These executive residences tend to be grand in style and scale, often possessing a long, rich history—even some haunted ones.

Sometimes, governors forego their official residences in favor of privately owned homes. For example, California governor Gavin Newsom recently listed the $7.5 million Fair Oaks home he lived in instead of the official executive residence, a 19th-century Victorian in Sacramento. Montana’s governor’s mansion is currently empty and in disrepair, while a governor hasn’t inhabited the official residence in Nebraska in over a decade.

Most states, however, have longstanding traditions of their leaders living in their official governor’s mansions. Virginia has the oldest continuously inhabited executive residence—every governor has lived there since 1813. If our new league of governors opts for tradition, here’s where they will be resting their heads for the next few years.

Republican Tommy Tuberville won Alabama’s gubernatorial race and will be able to move into a 119-year-old mansion in Montgomery following his inauguration. Built in 1907 by architect T. Weatherly Carter, the 17-room neoclassical residence features a row of four Corinthian columns at its front entrance, which opens to a foyer with a grand bifurcated staircase.

California’s new governor-elect is democrat Xavier Becerra, who will soon reside at a three-story Victorian in Sacramento. Built in 1877, the 20,000-square-foot house was thoroughly renovated in 2015, decades after Ronald and Nancy Reagan’s brief stint living there in 1967 before deeming it uninhabitable. The mansion was then used as a museum until its 2015 update, when it became home to the state’s governors once again. It has 30 rooms, Italian marble fireplaces, original wood floors, and a kidney-shaped swimming pool.

In Denver, democrat Phil Weiser will receive the keys to the red brick governor’s mansion known as Colorado’s Home. Built in 1908, it has been the state’s official governor’s residence since 1960. The Colonial Revival–style home has 27 rooms and shares its one-acre grounds with gardens, a historic gazebo, a greenhouse, and a carriage house. Thanks to John Hickenlooper, governor from 2011 to 2019, the home also has a rotating selection of local beers on tap.

Republican Byron Donalds just made history as Florida’s first Black governor. The state’s Greek Revival–style governor’s mansion in Tallahassee was completed in 1956 on the site of the original governor’s mansion built in 1907, which had fallen into disrepair. A screened swimming pool, a greenhouse, and a rose garden are among its amenities.

Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms has the distinction of being the first Black woman governor-elect in the United States. Her soon-to-be residence in Atlanta is a 30-room Greek Revival mansion that was built in 1967 by architect A. Thomas Bradbury. Across three levels, it measures in at 30,000 square feet.

Iowa’s governor’s mansion, known as Terrace Hill, will soon be home to democrat Rob Sand. Dating all the way back to 1869, Terrace Hill is an 18,000-square-foot Second Empire–style residence in Des Moines where Iowa’s governors have lived since 1976. Its first two floors are open to the public as a museum and events space.

Democrat Hannah Pingree will move into the Blaine House in Augusta, Maine. Originally built in 1833 in a combination of Federal and Greek Revival styles, the mansion has been the state’s official governor’s residence since 1919, when renovations gave it a more Colonial style. Connecticut’s SmartMLS has announced that it will introduce a new Ownership Verification tool from Property Shield designed to help real estate professionals combat seller-impersonation and vacant-land scams.

According to a release, the new technology, expected to become available to SmartMLS next week, provides an additional way for real estate professionals to verify the identity of someone claiming to own a property before accepting a listing.

Seller-impersonation fraud typically begins when a scammer poses as a property owner and contacts a real estate professional seeking to list and sell property the scammer does not own. These schemes can be particularly difficult to identify when the property is vacant land or when the purported seller communicates remotely.

“Seller-impersonation scams can be difficult to identify.” said Kathy Elson, CEO of SmartMLS. “We want to give our users a tool to help verify a prospective seller’s identity and identify a potential problem as early in the listing process as possible.”

An additional step when something doesn’t look right

Property Shield’s Ownership Verification tool is designed to help determine whether the individual claiming to be the property owner is who they say they are.

The verification process uses identity verification and facial-recognition technology in conjunction with property ownership information to provide an additional layer of protection when questions arise about the identity of a prospective seller.

Rather than relying solely on documents, emails, telephone conversations, or information supplied by the prospective seller, real estate professionals will have a tool available to help evaluate whether the person attempting to list the property is legitimate.

“Real estate professionals are often on the front line when seller-impersonation fraud is attempted,” Elson said. “The challenge is that a sophisticated scammer may have enough information about the property and its owner to appear legitimate. Ownership Verification gives our users another way to ask an important question before proceeding: Is this really the property owner?”

Helping stop fraud before a listing enters the MLS

Ownership Verification complements the Listing Fraud Protection technology SmartMLS has already introduced through its partnership with Property Shield.

The two technologies address fraud at different points.

Listing Fraud Protection helps identify when legitimate SmartMLS listing information is copied and used in potentially fraudulent advertisements across the web.

Ownership Verification is designed to help identify potential seller impersonation before a fraudulent listing enters the MLS.

Together, the tools provide SmartMLS users with additional ways to identify and respond to real estate fraud.

“Seller impersonation and vacant-land fraud are difficult problems because the person committing the fraud may never meet the agent face-to-face,” said Alexander Fahsel, CEO of Property Shield. “Ownership Verification gives real estate professionals another layer of information to help determine whether the person they are dealing with is actually connected to the property they are attempting to sell.”

Technology supporting professional judgment

Ownership Verification is intended to provide an additional fraud-prevention tool, not replace the judgment and due diligence of the real estate professional.

A successful verification should not eliminate other appropriate steps when circumstances surrounding a prospective listing raise concerns. Similarly, an issue identified during verification can provide an important signal that additional investigation may be warranted before proceeding with the listing.

“Our goal isn’t to replace the due diligence brokers and agents already perform,” Elson said. “It’s to give them a better tool when they need it. If something about a prospective seller or listing doesn’t add up, we want our users to have another way to investigate before moving forward.”

Earthquake Faults Can Suddenly Become Almost As Fluid as Water, Study Finds

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Scientists have uncovered evidence of an unexpected process that may allow earthquake faults to slip enormous distances.

Rocks along Japan’s Boso Peninsula have preserved evidence of an extraordinary process that may occur during powerful earthquakes. Researchers discovered ancient fault structures suggesting that clay trapped between moving rock surfaces became nearly as fluid as water, potentially allowing the fault to slip enormous distances with little resistance.

The findings, from a research team led by Professor Tetsuro Hirono of Osaka Metropolitan University, could help explain how some earthquakes produce the massive seafloor movements capable of triggering devastating tsunamis.

The researchers examined exposed rocks that formed in an ancient seafloor environment approximately 15 million years ago. Within an old earthquake fault, they identified unusual wavy and curved layers, along with swirling structures resembling vortices formed by moving fluids.

The team described these formations as “turbulent-flow-like.” Using fluid dynamics to analyze their shapes, the scientists found evidence that a process called thermal pressurization had caused clay within the fault to become fluidized.

Thermal pressurization occurs when rapid movement along a fault generates intense frictional heat. As water trapped in microscopic spaces within the rock heats up, pressure increases, reducing the frictional resistance that normally keeps the fault surfaces locked together. This weakening can allow much greater movement during an earthquake.

“Our analysis suggests that the viscosity approached that of water,” Professor Hirono explained. “This would have been a dramatic reduction. The fluidized state would have acted as a form of dynamic lubrication, causing the fault to weaken dramatically. The resulting large-scale slip would have produced a large displacement on the fault near the seafloor, potentially causing a tsunami.”

The mechanism could help explain the extreme seafloor displacement associated with some major earthquakes. In 2011, a magnitude 9.1 earthquake struck eastern Japan, with shaking and fault rupture lasting nearly six minutes. The earthquake shifted the ocean floor enough to generate a devastating tsunami. Although the ancient rocks do not establish whether the same weakening process occurred in 2011, they provide physical evidence of how faults may undergo dramatic changes during seismic events.

The researchers hope to apply their analytical methods to modern faults, using changes in the viscosity of fault materials to better estimate how far those faults might slip during an earthquake. More accurate assessments of fault movement could improve estimates of potential seafloor displacement and associated tsunami hazards.

“Understanding the change in viscosity in fault-slips may allow us to more accurately recognize the magnitude of fault slips, which would support tsunami mitigation efforts,” Professor Hirono concluded.

When an offense consistently finds itself facing long third downs, it usually means something has already gone wrong on first and second down. Good offensive design should create opportunities from one play to the next, manipulate defensive reactions, and give the quarterback answers against different coverages. Too often, Seattle's offense looks like a collection of unrelated plays rather than a cohesive system.
