How Jonathan Gannon built a Top-5 Packers defense without Micah Parsons

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

If you're reading this, you obviously know that on Aug. 28, 2025, the Green Bay Packers traded defensive tackle Kenny Clark, and first-round picks in 2026 and 2027, to the Dallas Cowboys for the services of transcendent edge-rusher Micah Parsons. And when the 2025 season began, it appeared to foster a major improvement in Jeff Hafley's defense. In the first half of the 2025 season, Green Bay's defense ranked 10th in DVOA and Parsons was on a tear. Through Week 15 of the 2025 season, he totaled 14 sacks and 79 pressures, looking every bit the force he had been with Dallas.

Then, in Week 15 against the Denver Broncos, Parsons suffered a torn ACL, and things quickly fell apart for the Pack on Parsons' side of the ball — and overall. The previously 9-3-1 Pack lost every game from then on, and their season ended abruptly with a 31-27 Wild Card playoff loss to the Chicago Bears.

There wasn't enough juice without their defensive epicenter to get things done. The Packers fell to 25th in Defensive DVOA in the second half of the season, and a primary reason was pretty clear.

EPA/play allowed: -0.048 (favorable for the defense)

Pass defense: 367 attempts, 6.29 yards per attempt allowed

Run defense: 273 attempts, 4.13 yards per carry allowed

Without Parsons on the field (469 snaps):

EPA/play allowed: +0.09 (unfavorable for the defense)

Pass defense: 230 attempts, 6.82 yards per attempt allowed

Run defense: 231 attempts, 4.18 yards per carry allowed

"We just need to get healthy," Parsons told me on September 16, three days after the Packers lost their season opener to the Minnesota Vikings, 39-22. "I think once we get healthy, and I'm back on that grass or that turf, we're going to take it to another level."

Thing is, new defensive coordinator Jonathan Gannon couldn't wait around for Parsons to get healthy. The hope was that he'd be ready by Green Bay's Week 6 game against the Cowboys, but that didn't happen. Gannon had to do the best he could with what he had. And the extent to which he's done that is one of the NFL's most impressive stories in the 2026 season.

The Packers currently rank fourth in Defensive DVOA, and Gannon's concepts have been profoundly unsettling to their opponents.

So, how is Gannon doing so well in a new environment, with players he didn't know last season? Everything is working at all three levels.

As you would expect, Gannon is blitzing at a pretty high rate (31.9% this season, 12th-highest in the NFL) to maximize pressure, but Green Bay's pressure rate of 38.7% is only 18th-highest, per SumerSports. Lukas Van Ness leads the team with five sacks and 28 total pressures. Rookie Dani Dennis-Sutton is second with four sacks, and fellow edge-rusher Brenton Cox Jr. is second with 22 pressures. This isn't a Murderer's Row in that regard, but the ways in which Gannon has used his guys up front give those guys an edge.

It's also one reason the Packers have been so effective when they don't blitz.

So, how do you generate effective pressure when your pressure rate isn't that high? In Gannon's case, he's been marvelous with his simulated pressure designs. Showing a blitz look and then having supposed blitzers drop into coverage can create mental pressure for a quarterback when you do it right, and the Packers have done it right more often than not.

Now, imagine Parsons as part of all this skullduggery.

Gannon thought that he'd have linebackers Edgerrin Cooper (season-ending ruptured Achilles tendon) and Zaire Franklin (hairline shin fracture) full-go, but things happen. Now, Isaiah McDuffie and Ty'Ron Hopper are the primary 'backers, and Hopper has been a revelation. He made NFC Secret Superstars for Week 8 after a 27-19 win over the Carolina Panthers in which he totaled one sack, four pressures, six solo tackles, three pass breakups, and an interception. Hopper became the first Packers player since Hall of Famer Charles Woodson in 2009 to record three pass breakups, a sack, and an interception in the same game.

Anytime you can put yourself in the same conversation as Charles Woodson, that's obviously a good thing.

When Gannon runs all of those sim pressures, he needs linebackers who can both threaten pressure and drop into credible coverage. Hopper certainly has it on the ball, and McDuffie has been an effective starter of late.

"Ty'Ron Hopper. I can't say enough great things about him," Matt LaFleur said after the Panthers game. "The night he had. I think, what? Sack, PBU, interception? What a great performance. I think that's something that we did see coming more so in the preseason. I can't say enough about the job that obviously [linebackers coach] Sam Siefkes does with our inside linebackers to get them ready, and I just see a lot of growth from that group. They're playing really well."

They are, and they jibe with the secondary very well.

Safety Evan Williams made Secret Superstars following Green Bay's 23-13 Week 6 win over the Cowboys — the one Micah Parsons wasn't ready for, unfortunately — and that was a game in which Williams, the 2024 fourth-rounder out of Oregon, really put his name out there nationally as one of the NFL's better players at his position(s). This season, Williams has a sack, nine pressures, 49 solo tackles, 13 stops, and two tackles for loss. In coverage, he's allowed 11 catches on 20 targets for 79 yards, one touchdown, two interceptions, four pass breakups, and an opponent passer rating of 41.5.

That's all great, but where Williams really shows up in Gannon's defense is how well he plays to all three levels of the defense. Whether he's starting at the line of scrimmage and backing up to the deep half in coverage, or running the opposite by coming down for a pressure or a run fit, or maintaining the LOS integrity from the box, there isn't much Williams can't do right now.

And when you have one of those players, it allows you to be as multiple as you'd like without the breakdowns you see on defenses where the personnel doesn't quite measure up.

"He's intelligent… I love how Matt says it, 'if you wanna play like a G, you have to prepare like a G', and he studies and when you study the way he does, he knows what's going on," Gannon said of Williams in late October. "Nothing is catching him by surprise… He's playing very well right now."

And now, with all that said… here comes No. 1.

We all know how potentially explosive LaFleur's offense can be, but it's past time to recognize that as the 5-3 Packers progress through the rest of the season, it's the defense that has an equivalent (at least) amount to say regarding how far the team will go.

That's very good news, because Jonathan Gannon has proven to be quite the "G" himself.

Court leans toward narrow ruling on inmate’s use of habeas to challenge accelerated transfer to a halfway house or home confinement

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

The Supreme Court heard oral argument on Nov. 2 in Maxwell v. Dinis about whether a federal inmate may use a habeas statute, 28 U.S.C. § 2241, to contest the Federal Bureau of Prisons’ refusal to accelerate his transfer to a halfway house or home confinement.

Maxwell, a former attorney, was convicted at trial for his role in conspiring with a mob-related organization, La Costa Nostra, to take over a Texas-based mortgage-loan company, FirstPlus Financial Group, Inc., and drain it of its assets. As Maxwell neared the end of his sentence, he sought to contest the BOP’s calculation of good-time credits that would accelerate his transfer from prison to a halfway house or home confinement. The question for the court was whether Section 2241, which does not clearly spell out what claims may be brought but whose scope has largely been shaped by precedent, allows him to do so.

Maxwell’s attorney, Masha Hansford, pressed for a broad rule that habeas is available to an inmate like Maxwell who is asserting a right to a “distinct and lower level of custody.” But that position almost immediately encountered some pushback. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson questioned “whether we really need to use this case to define every circumstance in which habeas is appropriate.” Hansford agreed that the court need not adopt a test that allows inmates to challenge all changes to the level of custody and was content to have the court resolve Maxwell’s case by applying a narrower test endorsed by the government that would permit an inmate to use habeas to challenge the denial of an accelerated transfer to parole or, as here, a halfway house or home confinement.

Justice Samuel Alito pressed Hansford on whether an inmate may use Section 2241 simply to dispute how BOP calculated good-time credits or whether the inmate must also show that any error in calculating would also entitle the inmate to accelerated transfer to a halfway house or home confinement. After Hansford replied that Maxwell met all the requirements for the transfer and only the calculation of good-time credits was at issue, Justice Neal Gorsuch followed up by pressing whether the court needs to say that an inmate could bring a habeas challenge about the calculation of good-time credits alone. Hansford replied that the court did not need to say a dispute over good-time credits alone would suffice for a federal inmate to bring a claim in Section 2241.

Turning to Hansford’s level-of-custody test, Justice Sonia Sotomayor noted that, in a prior case, Justice Antonin Scalia had referred to habeas as covering a “‘quantum change’ in the level of custody,” but Hansford was asking the court “to create new language, ‘materially less restrictive custody.’ Why don’t we stick to what we have?” Hansford agreed that the court need not broaden its test and that Maxwell’s requested transfer to a halfway house or home confinement amounted to such a quantum change.

Assistant to the Solicitor General Kevin J. Barber, arguing for the government, contended that “there’s no need to adopt” Maxwell’s “amorphous level-of-custody rule.” He explained that the court “has always made clear that habeas is available when your claim is seeking discharge from actual physical confinement in prison to a different kind of custody,” such as a halfway house or home confinement. Here, the government relied heavily on Preiser v. Rodriguez, which held that habeas supplies the sole avenue for a state inmate to challenge the denial of credit toward release on parole.

When Gorsuch pressed Barber on the source of the government’s rule and why an inmate’s transfer to solitary confinement or maximum security would not be covered, Barber pointed principally to history and tradition in the court’s habeas cases. Barber explained, “we don’t have any tradition of claims being recognized as habeas claims challenging just transfers within prison systems.”

Despite agreeing that Maxwell could bring his claim in Section 2241, the government argued that no court could actually grant him relief. Specifically, the government pointed to 18 U.S.C. § 3621(b) and its terms barring courts from reviewing “a designation of a place of imprisonment under this subsection.” According to the government, service of a sentence in a halfway house or home confinement is “a designation of a place of imprisonment” governed by that statute.

But the government did not appear to have the votes to prevail on this argument, at least at the Supreme Court. Justice Brett Kavanaugh pressed Barber on whether that issue could be left for the lower court to decide on remand. Barber conceded that the court “absolutely could.” Sotomayor questioned Barber about whether the government is right that Section 3621(b) applies to Maxwell’s claim. But after Barber began responding, Justice Amy Coney Barrett reiterated Kavanaugh’s point that “we don’t have to get into any of this, right?” Barber again agreed.

Jackson pressed Barber on a separate point, that the BOP is expected to transfer Maxwell to a halfway house or home confinement in January 2027, which would soon make his claim moot. Jackson disputed why the court would revisit its decision to hear the case. Barber apologized for not pressing the Section 3621(b) issue or mootness in opposing certiorari, but said these factors spoke in favor of the court dismissing the case as improvidently granted.

The amicus, Brian W. Barnes, was appointed to argue in favor of the ruling by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit that habeas is unavailable for Maxwell’s claim. When pressed by Justice Elena Kagan, Barnes agreed that habeas is available to “encompass a move from incarceration to parole,” as Preiser held. Kagan pointed out that “parole looks an awful lot like what’s going on here.”

The justices’ questions suggest that they are unlikely to limit Preiser and reject an analogy between Maxwell’s requested relief and the parole in that case. The justices also showed little appetite for reaching the government’s Section 3621(b) argument. How they would handle Maxwell’s expected release to a halfway house in January 2027 was less clear, but that circumstance could result in a speedier ruling.

Still, we drafted enough Borg and artifact creature cards to assemble a competent deck; we just ran into three straight Borg mirror matches after an early run of wins against red-green Landfall and green-white doctor decks, and that ended the first draft on a sour note.

We have ten draft archetypes in total, one for each traditional MTG guild or dual-color combo, with some looking far more exciting than others.

We felt infinitely more optimistic about this deck, given that it didn't feel like we were competing with anyone else in the draft class for the cards that worked best in this archetype, but after the first few picks, all dilemma cards appeared to dry up, or came in mana colors we simply didn't want to stretch to.

The downfall of our first deck came because we felt forced to go with three colors rather than two, and the same ultimately happened here, and we had to force a few red dilemma cards into the deck, then hope that we had done enough to still try and grab a few wins against the inevitable Borg wave coming my way.

That said, because of the nature of draft and having only a 40-card deck, by the time we had our big threats in hand, we often had little protection, so we had to hope our opponent had no answers to our creatures and spells.

Frustrated with how the Premier Drafts were playing out, we switched to sealed events, which, instead of drafting as a group, give you six Play Boosters to use to assemble your deck. We returned to the Borg archetype, having come up against it in more than half our previous matches across two drafts, and it became clear why it's proving so popular.

You can draft cards to build a really effective mana curve, so you can play from turn one and increase your power on the battlefield every turn. As much as we love the idea of strong archetypes standing out above the crowd, when one in particular is being so heavily played, it can suck the fun out of the draft modes in general as it all becomes too repetitive.

While not officially an archetype, the second round of sealed play gave us the cards we needed to build an aggressive red-white deck that used cheap but effective instant and sorcery spells and creatures that could snowball quickly. We still had some of the power 8+ cards, but not enough to say the deck was built around them.

The key, however, was finding a Flare of Fortitude in the packs, which let us protect all cards on the battlefield and our life total, and we didn't even have to pay the mana cost as long as we could sacrifice a white creature. This card was key to every victory, as it often caught opponents out when we were fully tapped and passing the turn.

Ultimately, the red-white build surprised us with how effective it can be, and while it's not as aggressive as some of the red-black Klingon decks we faced, it still managed to reach six wins under our control.

We returned to Premier Draft for the final run of the session, and it was a lot more fruitful than the previous two. Going into this draft knowing we wanted to avoid Borgs at all costs led to much more success in drafting an archetypewe were happy with.

Having played against a few Klingon decks before drafting one, it was a little easier to scope out the must-haves, and we also knew being ultra-aggressive was the best way forward. We wanted our opponents on the ropes or potentially beaten by turn four or five, and that's how most of the wins played out. The losses, as you might expect, came from games that went much longer; we simply ran out of steam, and our opponents eventually found answers to our bigger threats and controlled the game from then on.

Klingons were ultimately the archetype we had the most fun with, and that goes a long way toward making the draft experience feel much better overall.

We're basing this off the decks that we built and the decks that we played against. It's not a fully committed ranking, but just our snapshot impressions after 40 games played across these five drafts (and a few more unrecorded since).
