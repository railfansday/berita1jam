Prime Video’s 3-Season Gritty Crime Thriller Is The Perfect NCIS Replacement

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

After more than two decades and over 500 episodes, NCIS has practically perfected the weekly crime investigation formula. From murders involving Navy personnel to the chemistry between its Major Case Response Team, there's a reason the CBS procedural has stayed popular for so long as one of the best crime dramas. Still, even longtime fans might want something darker than the usual case-of-the-week format. That's where Prime Video's Psycho-Pass comes in.

With three seasons and six feature films from Production I.G, Psycho-Pass offers a different take on the crime thriller. Written by Gen Urobuchi, the mind behind Fate/Zero and Puella Magi Madoka Magica, the series moves its investigations to a disturbing version of 22nd-century Japan. The government doesn't just punish criminals after a crime. Its nationwide Sibyl System monitors citizens' mental states, stress levels, and personalities to calculate their chances of becoming criminals through a score called the Crime Coefficient.

That's what sets it apart from NCIS. The Public Safety Bureau's Criminal Investigation Division still tracks dangerous suspects, but its Dominator handguns can decide whether someone gets arrested or executed based on that score. Sometimes, that judgment comes before a crime has even been committed. It's familiar detective work with a much darker idea of justice.

What makes Psycho-Pass such a good fit for NCIS viewers is that it gets the appeal of a proper investigative team. The story follows 20-year-old rookie Akane Tsunemori, who joins Division One of the Public Safety Bureau’s Criminal Investigation Department after topping her civil service exams. Much like Timothy McGee or Eleanor Bishop joining NCIS as fresh faces, Akane has to find her footing in an established team. The difference is that her new colleagues operate under a much darker system.

Division One has two ranks: Inspectors and Enforcers. Inspectors like Akane and her strict senior partner, Nobuchika Ginoza, oversee investigations, while Enforcers are latent criminals with Crime Coefficients above 100, released from lockup to help track down other offenders. They are essentially the Bureau's hunting dogs, and the Dominator handgun makes their work even more disturbing. It can switch from a Non-Lethal Paralyzer to a Lethal Eliminator depending on a suspect's Crime Coefficient.

Akane sees the system's cruelty on her first night when a terrified hostage is marked for execution after her trauma pushes her score into the lethal range. Akane stops her own lead Enforcer, Shinya Kogami, from pulling the trigger, establishing the show's central conflict between machine-made justice and human judgment.

The procedural appeal is just as strong. Shion Karanomori handles forensic analysis, Tomomi Masaoka brings old-school police experience, and Kogami uses sharp instincts to get inside suspects' heads. Like Abby Sciuto, Kasie Hines, Ducky Mallard, and Gibbs, each member brings something different to an investigation. The big difference? NCIS solves crimes after they happen; Psycho-Pass investigates people the system believes might commit them.

What makes Psycho-Pass worth watching for NCIS viewers is that it doesn't ditch the things that make a good crime procedural work. There's still a tight investigative team, forensic experts picking apart evidence, detectives following leads, and criminals who keep the department on its toes. The difference is that the crime scenes are in futuristic Tokyo, and the technology used to catch killers is just as disturbing as the crimes themselves.

So on to the Emirates, and again the debate was around VAR.

It's a situation few would question. After all, an offside player winning a penalty surely goes against the very logic of the law?

If an offside player is running to the ball and is fouled before they attempt to play the ball or challenge an opponent, then the foul is considered to come before the offside offence.

The law seeks to prevent situations whereby defenders have carte blanche to foul attackers, just because the opponent is offside.

But is a complex area of law and is very much open to interpretation.

In February 2019, the season before VAR came in, an offside Harry Kane was awarded a late penalty in a north London derby after being dragged down by Shkodran Mustafi.

Two years ago, Newcastle were awarded a spot-kick against Bournemouth through a VAR intervention when Adam Smith tugged back an offside Fabian Schar.

Yet last December, after being awarded a penalty against Leeds, Brentford's Dango Ouattara was ruled to be offside and the spot-kick ruled out through VAR.

So which interpretation is correct? Kane and Schar, or Havertz and Ouattara?

Ouattara was somewhat different, as he appeared to be engaging with Gabriel Gudmundsson as the ball was played.

The Havertz example is more like the one involving Schar, who was running forward when his shirt was tugged back.

But there is an additional consideration, and this is where Oliver's thinking will lie.

Officials are asked to consider the likelihood of the attacker becoming involved in the play from a second phase. Let's say the ball could be first played by a team-mate, which then makes them onside. Then they are not purely benefitting from being offside.

In the case of Schar and Kane, they could have benefitted from that second phase.

Havertz was the only attacker who had any chance of being involved in the play, so the VAR determined offside was the fair outcome.

Richard Lloyd, Television Guitarist, Dies at 75

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The musician was diagnosed with bile duct cancer in March 2026.

Richard Lloyd, a founding member and guitarist of influential New York rock band Television, has died. He was 75.

Lloyd died Saturday morning (Nov. 7) at his home in Chattanooga, Tennessee, according to to Rolling Stone.

“His loving wife Sheila and his dog Willie Mays were with him at the end,” his family told the publication.

His cause of death was cholangiocarcinoma, a cancer of the bile ducts. Lloyd was diagnosed with the disease in March 2026, and a GoFundMe campaign was later launched to help cover his medical expenses and other bills.

Born in Pittsburgh in 1951, Lloyd moved with his family to New York City at age 6 and grew up in Greenwich Village. He initially studied drums before turning to guitar. After spending time in Boston and Los Angeles, he returned to New York in 1973.

That year, he joined forces with Tom Verlaine, Richard Hell and drummer Billy Ficca to form Television. The band became a regular at CBGB, the Lower Manhattan club that helped launch the careers of fellow New York acts including the Ramones, the New York Dolls, Talking Heads, and Blondie.

Television released its debut album, Marquee Moon, in 1977. The album earned widespread critical acclaim for its intricate arrangements and the distinctive interplay between Lloyd’s and Verlaine’s guitars. Although it achieved limited commercial success, the album went on to become one of the most influential releases of its era. The group followed it with Adventure in 1978 before breaking up later that year.

Lloyd launched his solo career with the 1979 release of Alchemy, but struggles with substance abuse derailed his career in the years that followed. After getting sober, he returned with his second solo album, Field of Fire, in 1985. He continued releasing music over the following decades, with his final solo studio album, The Countdown, arriving in 2018.

He also worked as a session guitarist, contributing to recordings by artists including Matthew Sweet and John Doe of X, while continuing to tour and teach guitar. In 2017, he published his memoir, Everything Is Combustible: Television, CBGB’s and Five Decades of Rock and Roll.

Television reunited to record its self-titled third album, released in 1992, and continued performing periodically in the years that followed. Lloyd announced his departure from the band in 2007 to focus on his solo career.
