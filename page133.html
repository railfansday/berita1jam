You have a shortlist, a trial window and a meeting where someone will ask which tool to buy. A convincing demo does not show how a tool handles your files. This checklist is for the part after the demo: what the tool’s output means, what happens when it is wrong, and what it costs to find out.

One scoping note first. These questions cover tools that do structural analysis to check whether a file was modified after it was created. Identity verification, issuer verification and checking whether the content is true are different jobs, done by different tools and processes. Structural analysis of the file complements them; it does not replace any of them.

Judge a document fraud detection vendor on three things before the headline features: what its verdict proves and what it does not, how it reports the cases it cannot decide, and whether you can test it on files where you already know the answer. Then check that its data handling and pricing are written down plainly enough to put in a contract. If you are still deciding whether to buy at all, the build-vs-buy question comes first.

Every tool returns a result. The question is what that result is a claim about. A check on the file can tell you whether the file shows evidence of change after it was made. It cannot tell you whether the person is honest or whether the figures on the page are real.

A vendor that answers the second question without hesitating is telling you something useful. A reasonable standard for that answer: content that was false from the start carries no evidence of a later edit, so a file check cannot catch it. And intact means no modification was detected, not that the document is genuine. You want every vendor’s documentation to draw that line as clearly, whatever words it uses.

Some files cannot be judged by a given method. A document made in ordinary office software has no original to compare against. A scan or a filled form can be redone from scratch. Sometimes the file’s history simply cannot be established, and sometimes the analysis does not finish. A tool that forces each of these into ‘pass’ or ‘fail’ is hiding a limit, not removing it.

The last question matters for your own process. An undecided result should lead to a defined step, such as asking the issuer for a fresh copy, not to a rejection. If you want a worked example of reason-based handling, see what an inconclusive result means.

Any automated check will sometimes flag a legitimate file. A vendor that claims otherwise is making a claim you cannot test. What you can test is what happens next.

Treat any confidence label as a description of the evidence, not a guarantee. If a person can be refused on the strength of the result, your own process needs a human review and a way for that person to contest the outcome.

Exclusive: Wherobots AI agents automate complex spatial data engineering

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cloud-native spatial analytics and artificial intelligence startup Wherobots Inc. today introduced an artificial intelligence agent that enables users to build and deploy spatial data pipelines using natural-language instructions, reducing the need for specialized programming expertise.

Called Waldo, the agent can discover data sources, generate queries, perform analysis, visualize results and schedule recurring operations. It’s designed for applications involving location-based information such as satellite imagery, road networks, GPS tracking data and geographic features.

Spatial data presents unusual processing challenges because it combines multiple data types, including geographic coordinates, imagery and geometric shapes. Analyzing this information at scale often requires specialized tools and scarce technical expertise.

A recent survey by Global Marketing Insights Inc. concluded that the global geospatial workforce is 10 to 15 years behind in building the skill levels employers need.

Wherobots, which was founded in 2022 by the creators of the open-source Apache Sedona spatial analytics engine, said Waldo can compress development projects that traditionally take weeks into days.

“Waldo is our new agentic interface,” said Damian Wylie, Wherobots’ head of product. Unlike general-purpose AI coding assistants, he said, Waldo can invoke specialized computational engines that can process enormous geographic datasets.

“We’re not deploying some net-new [large language model] under the hood,” Wylie said. “We’re connecting existing LLMs to our product and also giving it the context it needs to speed up that spatial data workflow.”

The agent orchestrates several components of Wherobots’ platform. Its Havasu spatial data and knowledge catalog discovers and prepares public geographic datasets alongside customers’ private information. RasterFlow, Wherobots’ serverless Earth intelligence and inference engine,  processes satellite and aerial imagery using computer vision models to identify features such as buildings, roads and land-use changes.

WherobotsDB executes the resulting analysis using more than 300 spatial functions, while Wherobots Maps visualizes the results. Completed pipelines can be scheduled to refresh automatically as new data arrives.

The distributed processing platform uses existing cloud storage and enterprise data catalogs, including Amazon Web Services Inc.’s Glue and Databricks Unity Catalog, eliminating the need for customers to migrate their data, Wylie said.

In a demonstration, he showed how Waldo could identify the drainage area surrounding Montana’s West Boulder River using geographic and elevation data. The agent located relevant datasets, constructed a geographic representation of the watershed and displayed the results on a map.

The resulting geometry could support subsequent analysis of snowfall, water runoff or changing environmental conditions, Wylie said. Waldo can also process satellite imagery using specialized machine learning models, including Meta Platforms Inc.’s Segment Anything Model 3.

Potential commercial applications include identifying delivery vehicles that deviate from planned routes, assessing weather-related transportation disruptions and analyzing advertising impressions based on billboard locations and mobility patterns.

Wherobots said customer Action Engine Inc. is using the technology to build recurring fleet analytics pipelines and reports that it can deliver new spatial analyses two to three times faster that was possible with manual processes. Another customer used the platform’s distributed computing architecture to reduce a large-scale data-joining operation from approximately a week to less than an hour, said Benjamin Pruden, Wherobots’ go-to-market executive.

Waldo isn’t intended to eliminate human oversight, executives said. Users can inspect generated queries, review geographic visualizations and create Jupyter notebooks to examine individual processing steps. Wylie acknowledged that the technology can’t guarantee accurate results from every prompt.

“That’s an area we’ll invest,” Wylie said. “We’re not at the stage where we’re going to say every prompt is the best quality you’ll ever get.”

Waldo is available in public preview, with agent tokens currently included at no additional charge in Wherobots Professional Edition. Customers pay primarily for the computational resources they consume, with controls to select processing capacity and monitor costs.

Grand Theft Auto VI: The Ars Technica review

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jason and Lucia’s crime-heavy love story fuels a wonder of digital urban simulation.

Just a minute ago, I was shooting my way out of a cocaine deal gone wrong, darting behind furniture for cover. Now I’m sitting on the floor of my home, working through the instructions to construct an Ikea-style coffee table with my partner. A tiny heart with a plus sign pops up, showing that our relationship is stronger for this quotidian domestic effort.

This is the contrast at the heart of Grand Theft Auto VI, a game that seems to revel in both action movie bombast and the mundanities of everyday life for an up-and-coming young criminal couple.

The reimagined and expanded Vice City is still the grand, crude, satirical criminal playground you’d expect after 25 years of 3D Grand Theft Auto games. And that world is as full, detailed, technically impressive, and polished as you would expect after this latest installment received a reported billion-dollar budget and roughly a decade of development work involving thousands of Rockstar staffers.

But it’s the scenes between the over-the-top and somewhat repetitive action scenes that really make this Grand Theft Auto stand out. Almost every explosive gunfight or high-speed car chase is counterpointed by a sublimely dull, lightly interactive moment of mere routine existence: grilling burgers with pals by the water, playing fetch with a random dog, helping a neighbor tune up a car, or joking around over 18 holes of mini golf.

Taken together, the juxtaposition lends a sense of surreal hyperreality to the goings-on in Vice City and its environs. Each of the game’s two sides gives the other a chance to breathe, forming a series of emotional peaks and valleys that stretch across a truly gargantuan amount of detailed simulation and pure gaming content.

Grand Theft Auto VI never lets you get too settled with a budding life of crime or a budding family life. And it’s all the better for it.

A large part of why this works so well is Jason and Lucia, the dual protagonists whose relationship drives the story. While that relationship starts off a bit awkwardly—thanks chiefly to Lucia’s brief prison stint—it quickly develops into a true criminal and life partnership. Both sides of the relationship are driven by a mutual desire to move up in the criminal world and eventually settle down to a legit family life, if they can just earn enough from the next big score.

Of the two, Jason is definitely the duller partner. An ex-military meathead with a ripped body and a dull, affect-free vocal delivery, a decent chunk of Jason’s lines essentially boil down to “Yeah, sure, that’s fine.” While the game struggles to expose a bit of a tender and thoughtful side hiding underneath Jason’s cool demeanor, the writing can’t quite break through the emotionless masculine facade that Jason wears as a semi-permanent mask.

Lucia is much more interesting as a vivacious and amiable Latina eager to grab any opportunity presented to her. The game takes some time to dig into the difficulties of Lucia’s underprivileged youth and highlight her strained relationship with an unreliable mother, helping to add some important depth to her chip-on-her-shoulder journey of self-improvement. And even the boring Jason seems that much more appealing seen through the lens of Lucia’s good-natured teasing and genuine affection.

While Jason and Lucia can and do operate independently—and the game lets you change between them frequently—it’s their moments of togetherness that really stand out. There’s an easy, rom-com comfort to their time together, whether they’re discussing their future on the couch (“I feel like I haven’t done anything normal in a minute”), taking some time for quick romance (the game cuts away before things get too hot and heavy), or laying down covering fire for each other during a firefight (the lack of a co-op mode for these missions feels truly baffling).

There’s a minor relationship simulation component to the game, with a simple “heart level” that can rise or fall based on some basic decisions. Don’t expect a lot of dating-sim style drama, though; as long as you make the completely obvious choices when they’re presented, it’s hard to let the relationship fall apart. That’s a good thing, too, because this is a relationship that’s easy to root for, even when the participants are literally getting away with murder.

In true Grand Theft Auto fashion, Jason and Lucia’s particular way of chasing The American Dream is through a life of crime. Their travels through the criminal underworld start off kind of slow, as Lucia’s begging gradually compels Jason to reluctantly introduce her to the two-bit operators with which he’s found himself involved.

For the first half of the game, the pair are constantly interacting with a rogues’ gallery of new criminal acquaintances, each more over-the-top than the last, and all of them having specific needs that somehow only Jason and Lucia can fulfill. Many of the missions these small-time crooks have on offer end up feeling like the criminal equivalent of errands—drive here, take that, lose the cops, repeat.

And while a few standout characters develop complex interpersonal relationships with Jason and Lucia over multiple missions, most of these early side characters feel like one-note caricatures that can be distilled to a few tropey traits.

It isn’t until the back half of the game that the main storyline really starts to focus on what it does best: integrating Jason and Lucia into a persistent ring of professional criminals working toward bigger and bigger scores. Here, the missions build on each other, with characters going undercover, casing targets, and learning new skills that eventually come together in some truly tense and bombastic heists. These missions also do the best job of showing off the dual protagonist system, frequently shifting the perspective between Jason and Lucia to highlight their different skills and views on how things are going down.

Outside of this core plot, though, many of the missions and side stories in GTA VI make Jason and Lucia feel like side characters in their own story. I found myself beginning to tune out during some of the many missions in which one or both protagonists serve as glorified security props or overpaid getaway drivers amid other characters’ contrived turf wars. And even dozens of hours into the game, there were still missions that felt like tutorials, seemingly built around the opportunity to introduce some new simulation system or control scheme.

Through it all, the game can’t seem to totally shake the style of sophomoric humor that feels a lot less “edgy” today than it did when Grand Theft Auto III hit back in 2001 (for players who are old enough to remember that release, at least). There are countless extended bits where the entire joke seems to boil down to “Man, kinky sex is weird, isn’t it?” or “Hey look, it’s a male penis (or something that looks similar to one).” And the game seems to take particular pleasure in forcing Jason into uncomfortable situations with plenty of T&A, much to Lucia’s good-natured bemusement.

A word of warning for parents: This is a game that definitely earns a hard “M for Mature” rating and shows how standards for that rating have shifted since GTA: San Andreas earned an AO re-rating for hiding a single sex scene amid its legacy code.

There are moments where GTA VI’s satire cuts a bit deeper. I especially liked a couple of side stories that skewered buzzword-spewing crypto bros and multi-level marketing schemes with some welcome over-the-top excess. Overall, though, the game’s warmed-over one-liner takes on “wokeness” felt about as fresh as jokes about mothers-in-law and TV dinners, without much in the way of thought-provoking statements on society as a whole in 2026.

Grand Theft Auto VI has a gift for finding excuses to lure you into an expertly designed space and then throwing together a reason for why you have to shoot/punch your way out. That might sound snarky, but I actually mean it more or less sincerely; I was frequently surprised by the scenarios the writers dreamed up to turn what seemed like a routine trip to some beautiful locale into a firefight with way more armed goons than seems strictly necessary.

The moment-to-moment shooting will feel very familiar to Grand Theft Auto fans, by which I mean it’s dead simple to hide behind convenient cover and pop up to pick off relatively stupid enemies one by one with a dizzying array of available weaponry. The rare enemies that do manage to flank you seem to have incredibly bad aim, even at close range, and both Jason and Lucia can take a whole lot more punishment than the average quick-to-crumble grunt.

Driving around town is significantly more exciting—you’ll be darting between cars in heavy traffic to chase down antagonists, avoid the police, or simply get where you’re going faster. It’s still a blast to sample as many vehicles as you can, from zippy but easy-to-wreck sports cars to tank-like trucks that can plow through traffic, tiny motorbikes that can squeeze through tight spaces, and off-road vehicles that can explore off the beaten path.

Driving through the city also offers ample opportunities to get distracted from your main goals by dozens of side activities that dot the map. Even as I struggled to work through the story on deadline, I found myself getting sucked in for hours trying to master drone racing, chasing gold medals in countless mud-splattered rally races, or hunting for one of the many meditation spots hidden in out-of-the-way corners.

While most of these side activities offer money or improve character stats, some are simply there to be experienced. Completionists will have a bear of a time tracking down every single hidden collectible, rare car, robbable location, and beautiful landmark the developers have placed throughout the city. Grand Theft Auto VI seems set to be one of those games you can just load up to hang out in long after the main story missions are done.

Even with a fast vehicle, much of that “hang out” time can be spent simply crossing the game’s massive map. Getting from the depths of the wilderness in the north to the tip of the keys in the south is a six-plus-mile trip (measured at an apparently real-world-accurate scale) that can take 10 minutes to weave your way across on a good traffic day. During these cross-city treks, the tenor of the city’s architecture morphs noticeably as you pass from rich gated communities through the glitzy skyscraper-laden downtown and on to downtrodden neighborhoods, backwoods swamps, and laid-back island beach communities.

As nice as Vice City’s outdoor architecture is, GTA VI’s interiors are where the game’s developers really show off. Practically every story mission seems to introduce a new cavernous indoor space that’s fully furnished and lovingly crafted to look naturally lived-in and also utterly unlike anything you’ve seen before. And while you can’t go into every one of the thousands of buildings you pass on the streets of Vice City, you can enter a surprisingly large number, each with its own distinct layout and decorations.

Another element that makes all these spaces feel lived-in is the game’s surprisingly detailed crowd system. Grand Theft Auto VI sometimes packs over a hundred non-player characters into a single space, with no two characters looking or acting alike in size, shape, dress, or animation. Working your way through these crowds can be integral to some of the game’s missions, but even when you’re just casually exploring, it’s fun to take advantage of eavesdropping opportunities, listening in on individual conversations that build into overlapping chatter (realistically muffled through walls and over distance).

Grand Theft Auto VI’s spaces also seem specifically designed to show off the game’s incredible lighting system, which really shines on a PS5 Pro. The effect is most apparent at night, when car headlights streaming through a window or the rotating blue-and-red of a cop car can cast the entire world in an otherworldly glow.

But even in “normal” lighting conditions, I’d occasionally find myself slack-jawed at the details: a laptop screen partially reflected in a character’s glasses, the way rotating pyrotechnic laser effects played across dancers in a crowded club, the fluttering shadows cast by a leafy tree near sunset, or the finger smudges visible on your cell phone in bright sunshine.

I could go on and on about the little “attention to detail” touches that add up to a more engaging world simulation. Characters who fall in the dirt get dust on only the specific parts of their clothes that hit the ground. Older characters look distinctly wrinkly, with veins that show through their thinning skin. Fabric and hair flow naturally in the wind and as characters move. Rain beads on car roofs and drips off overhangs during a storm.

Through it all, one moment really stood out to me. As I prepared for an upcoming firefight, I had Lucia start eating some chips to buff her health. Halfway through the bag, I had her pick up a nearby soda, too. At first, Lucia held the chip bag in one hand and used the other to pick out individual chips. But once that other hand was occupied with a soda, she started shaking the bag above her head to pour the chips directly into her mouth.

When I thought about the effort that went into coding and recording that specific animation for such a highly specific edge case, I thought about the millions of man-hours that went into making Grand Theft Auto VI the ultimate expression of this kind of urban crime/life sim. It might be a long time before we see this level of focused effort on a singular interactive vision again.

GTA 6 Review - An Astounding, Impossible New Bar For Video Games

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The most anticipated video game of all time is a triumph on every level that exceeds years of unbelievable expectations. You're not ready for Leonida.

Throughout much of my nearly 100 hours of playtime, reviewing Grand Theft Auto 6 felt like a paradox. Finally getting a chance to see what Rockstar has been cooking for close to a decade is both the most exciting moment of my whole career and the most high-pressure thing I’ve ever been lucky enough to take on. How do you possibly begin to talk about something that had whole studios built just for its NPCs?

GTA 6 is the biggest video game of all time, but we really only knew the basics. Something that dawned on me less than an hour in when it became clear just how much had been held back. Even with the weight of 13 years of anticipation on my shoulders, I’m also well aware that nothing I say about Jason and Lucia’s felonious tour of Leonida will influence the minds of anyone even half interested in it.

The great irony of this herculean task is that, more often than not, Grand Theft Auto 6 left me completely speechless, pointing at the screen and gibbering like an idiot at what Rockstar has accomplished. By every measure, it’s a masterclass of a game that not only lives up to the impossible hype but somehow goes even further by redefining what it means to be a GTA game.

Following the narrative heights of Red Dead Redemption 2 and its beloved Arthur Morgan is an unenviable task, but Grand Theft Auto 6 doesn’t disappoint. It follows Jason and Lucia, two small-time criminals who reunite and are quickly forced to rise through the ranks of Leonida’s underworld after a failed job puts them up against Vice City’s most dangerous forces.

So far, so GTA, but there’s more to the plot than the rags-to-riches setup would have you believe. Unsurprisingly, I can’t tell you much about the overarching story and what sets it in motion, but Jason and Lucia’s involvement has far more emotional and mature stakes than any other game in the series before it. Rockstar takes the pair to places I never would have predicted (including some surprisingly dark moments), and it all lands because of how expertly written and characterised both protagonists are.

Jason is more stoic and guarded than any of the more out-there characters we followed throughout Los Santos, but he’s got a heart of gold and lots of charm underneath the gruff exterior, which mostly comes out when he’s with Lucia. He’s harder to read and takes time to warm up to, but by the end, Jason stands comfortably alongside the series' most memorable protagonists, Niko, CJ, and even Red Dead’s revered gunslingers.

Lucia, on the other hand, is without a doubt the best protagonist in Grand Theft Auto history. She’s fiercely loyal, emotionally mature, witty, and doesn’t take crap from anyone, almost to a fault, as her passion and eagerness to prove herself often land her in trouble. As the series’ first female lead (no, I don’t count the pixel blobs from the first game), there’s a lot of pressure on Lucia, but she easily reaches the lofty bar set by Arthur Morgan.

“We believed in this idea of putting a relationship at the center of the game and trying to make a GTA that was still as funny, crazy, shocking, and fun as the previous games but with added heart. But yeah, the process of making something like this you always question things. As you saw with the 2022 hack, game development isn’t pretty. It can take a long time and a lot of iteration to make good on an idea and to get things working. But I think simply the idea that the relationship would be something that the player needed to work at an earn (or ignore if they want to!) felt like exciting new territory.” - Rupert Humphries, Senior Vice President of Narrative

Of course, as great as Jason and Lucia are on their own merits, they come as a pair in GTA 6 and are together for nearly all of the story’s biggest moments. I had some doubts about the Bonnie and Clyde-style romance, as it's not exactly familiar territory for Rockstar. But after seeing their chemistry and working to give them an unbreakable bond through hand-holding, dates, clubbing, and, of course, doing crime as a pair in my playthrough, I simply can’t imagine the two not being madly in love. Some of my favourite moments in the whole game were simply taking Jason and Lucia on a date, grinning as they flirted, and getting unhealthily invested in their relationship.

It’s not just a win for the story, either, as Jason and Lucia’s cooperative gameplay is a huge step up from GTA 5, with far more options than just swapping, from letting your partner drive you to waypoints and having them cover you in firefights to asking them to fence your stolen goods or hang back as a getaway driver. In GTA 6, having a partner in crime finally means exactly that.

That romance might be the focal point and main standout of GTA 6’s story, but there’s plenty to love about the excellent supporting cast as well. Whether you’re supposed to love them like the cool-as-ice mentor figure Raul or hate them like some characters I won’t dare mention, there’s not a single weak link. Though the writing can occasionally be slightly less biting than previous games, Jason and Lucia’s tale tops GTA 4 as the series’ best narrative.

Perhaps my favourite character of the whole game is Karim, the hacker in Raul’s group who provides some surprising Watch Dogs 2 vibes.

Sony is right - Grand Theft Auto 6 is probably going to play best on PlayStation.

It would have been easy (or at least easier) for Rockstar to take GTA 5’s lucrative formula and just polish the graphics a bit, but one of the biggest strengths of Grand Theft Auto 6 is how it completely rebuilds the main tenets of the whole series. For starters, core mechanics like movement, shooting, and driving all feel far better than ever thanks to extensive improvements and tweaks.

Take GTA’s infamous mash X to sprint controls, for instance, which have been completely reworked for a more standardised and smoother experience. Jason and Lucia are both far more agile than previous protagonists and have more vertical options at every moment, which makes the simple act of getting around on foot much more enjoyable. The only downside to the rework, and spoilers for the rest of this review - the game as a whole - is that the controls can sometimes be a little overcomplicated due to how many interactions you have at your disposal.

It did take me a good few hours to adjust to the new control scheme, but it’ll be incredibly tough to go back now. There is an option for the classic version if you really struggle, though.

“There was lot a lot of internal team feedback to look at our controls and responsiveness after we launched RDR2, so we doubled down on exploring an ‘experimental’ control scheme as it was called internally at the time. The experiment kind of stalled mid development due to other competing priorities but was revitalized by Rob (Head of Development). After that, we all strongly felt it should be the default control scheme as we’d had consistently good feedback from the team and user research. We still offer classic GTA controls in the setting menu for those prefer them. Indeed, we have many people at R* who still do! There are also a lot more options for players to explore in the menu. Hopefully, there is a scheme or set of options for everyone to enjoy the game!” - David Hynd, Vice President of Technology

Shooting, meanwhile, takes cues from Red Dead Redemption 2 and its weightier weapons, which already felt inspired by Max Payne 3’s stellar gunfights. There’s even a Dead Eye/Bullet Time slow-motion focus mode for both protagonists, rather than being locked as a special ability for one character, and it becomes an essential tool as you go up against the gangs and cops of Leonida. It’s the best combat has ever felt in a Grand Theft Auto game by a country mile.

The same goes for driving, which sits somewhere between GTA 5 and GTA 4 thanks to a greater focus on vehicle weight, physics, and damage while somehow still retaining the buttery smooth controls and flow. Driving is also the most immediately obvious example of Rockstar not just improving how Grand Theft Auto 6 plays, but recontextualising every mechanic’s implementation with a greater focus on worldbuilding and player involvement.

Unlike previous games in the series where you could grab a fast car and bomb it down the city streets without so much as tapping the brakes, Leonida’s denser traffic and narrower streets (including a ton of painfully realistic roadworks) mean you need to be paying attention at all times. It’s only when you start to master the new systems and put the effort in that you can weave through traffic like a pro, which makes it infinitely more rewarding. Couple that with cars requiring far more work to attain thanks to higher security and a ridiculous amount of upgrade and modding options, and vehicles become so much more than just something you steal, park in your garage, and don’t give a second thought to.

On the other hand, gunfights are no longer about just aiming for the head with whatever arsenal you’ve got in your back pocket, as you now need to incapacitate enemies with clean, targeted shots to the knees and arms if you want to keep a professional criminal profile. You also have a more limited choice of weapons, forcing you to plan ahead and keep your trunk stocked before a firefight. Even crime, the main thing Grand Theft Auto is known for, is completely different in GTA 6. Not only are the police more advanced than ever before, with multiple stages of realistic awareness of your actions that incentivise masks, stealth, and being as efficient as possible, but there’s far more effort that needs to be put in for even the simplest of sins.

"A lot of work, yeah! There were a few key areas we needed to change for cops in particular on this game. The perception/recognition system needed a complete overhaul. On V/RDR the cops always knew where the player was located, but with the new model they now rely on proper search/investigation routines, guided by witness reports and evidence (e.g. dead bodies, abandoned vehicles). K9 units can sense the player too, so you need to keep on your toes when they are around! And, of course, we added appearance and outfit recognition to add depth/roleplay mechanics to cop evasion. Having an unknown wanted state created lots of great gameplay moments, where you could nonchalantly leave a crime scene undetected. And adding in Heat added a level of persistence that was lacking in previous games. The wanted UI also took a lot of time to finesse/balance. We added threat indicators to give the player more feedback around how they were being perceived by cops/enemies in game. This opened up some cool stealth gameplay opportunities, but was also crucial seeing as we removed enemies from the radar. Once you are spotted, we now rely on cops calling it in before you fully known. This gives the player a window of opportunity to deal with the situation before things kick off.  But yeah, it’s been a massive effort. Fueled by constant tweaks and iteration over many years." - David Hynd, Vice President of Technology

Gone are the days of completing a few story missions and having millions in the bank without anything to spend it on, as money is incredibly hard to come by in Vice City. You really need to work for it, whether through simple but low-reward gas station stick-ups and jewel store heists, or storming gang hideouts for drugs you can sell on to fences. What’s most impressive about all of the crime opportunities is that they’re completely emergent and up to the player, a massive leap forward from Los Santos story-specific heists and very basic convenience store shakedowns.

As if being able to pull off story mission-esque heists at any moment wasn’t enough, GTA 6 has also made some notable improvements to the main campaign. When they’re not trying to make Jason and Lucia feel like real people with quieter moments, story missions are energetic, bombastic, and, unlike some criticisms of Red Dead Redemption 2, give the player more freedom and control than before. While I’m sure some players will prefer the classic, more thoughtless approach, for my money Grand Theft Auto 6 is a better and more considered game on every single front, pushing the series formula to impossible new heights.

In a game that’s all triumphs, the biggest of all is Grand Theft Auto 6’s meticulously crafted world, which is so unbelievably impressive it single-handedly justifies the long wait. From the murky swamps near Leonida Keys to the neon-lit upper echelons of Capri, Leonida is jaw-dropping at every turn (fittingly for Vice City, especially at night) and a downright feat of engineering to get it all working without any issues on PS5 Pro. Some things, especially the weather system and frankly ridiculous lighting, need to be seen to be believed.

I’ve never stopped to smell the roses so much in a game, but it’s impossible not to soak in all of the work and love that’s gone into every explorable interior, every beach packed with NPCs you can interact with or just hear have detailed conversations, every neighbourhood with potential things to steal, and every flea market with functioning shops. Almost all of the effort feels necessary to deliver on the promise of Leonida, but you can often feel Rockstar flexing its penchant for the little details (such as food being realistically eaten and the unique games that are playable on the PlayStation parody) that make these worlds feel so real.

Grand Theft Auto 6 also easily has the best radio selection the series has seen since Vice City. Justice’s original soundtrack is also incredible and punctuates major story moments perfectly.

Rockstar’s foray into heightened realism in Red Dead Redemption 2 has clearly left its mark on GTA 6, which focuses heavily on immersion and realism. You can shower, work out at the gym to build muscle, eat food to put on weight, get tired from not sleeping enough, need to keep vehicles filled with fuel, and have more hairstyle and clothing options than you know what to do with. I was a little worried it’d go too far and end up a little intense like RDR2, but Rockstar has taken the right amount of inspiration from that game while still making sure GTA isn’t getting too close to a life-sim.

“I personally love going into a gas station, buying a hot dog and a slushie and walking out alternating between eating and drinking and walking down the street doing that, then throwing the empty cup in the trash. I love getting a six pack of beers and walking along drinking them one at a time, throwing them around!”- Rob Nelson, Head of Development

Beyond being so detailed and immersive, Leonida is also just an incredibly varied and handcrafted county that feels unique no matter where you go. It’s a far cry from GTA 5’s handful of city areas mixed with vast wilderness that ends up getting ignored, and it helps that it’s stuffed with side missions, minigames, and emergent events that keep the water-cooler spirit Rockstar’s games are so well known for.

It might feel like I’ve said a lot about GTA 6 (it certainly does to me, anyway), but even with close to 100 hours, there are still whole areas I’ve barely explored, mechanics I’ve only glanced at, easter eggs to find, and activities that are begging for more of my time - it’s an absolute juggernaut. There’s also so much I can’t talk about or simply don’t want to get into to keep the sense of discovery for everyone else.

Without a shadow of a doubt, Grand Theft Auto 6 is the best open-world game I’ve ever played, and one of the best games full stop, with only incredibly minute nitpicks barely worth mentioning that do nothing to drag it down from masterpiece status. Its jaw-droppingly detailed world, completely revamped and recontextualised mechanics, and heartfelt narrative with two stellar leads put it far above the rest of the incredible series and set an impossible new bar for the medium as a whole. I'm sure we’ll never see another game with such unreal hype that actually lives up to and exceeds expectations, but you can rest easy knowing that the long road to Leonida was worth walking.

The GTA 6 Review

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Rockstar’s magnum opus searches for love and catharsis in desperate times — and finds them

Who gets to break the rules in America? A cryptobro living off of financial scams. A girlboss CEO who turns feminine vulnerability into a pyramid scheme. A cop who shoots a civilian in the face and walks away scot-free. It’s an exclusive club of societally acceptable grifters and killers, and Lucia Caminos’ name isn’t on the VIP list.

When we first meet the co-star of Grand Theft Auto 6, she’s scraping the bottom of the cultural barrel. She’s a lowly street rat. The only person who seems to give a damn about her is Jason Duval, another small-time crook who connects with her during a star-crossed carjacking turned meet-cute. It doesn’t take long for the law to interrupt her rom-com dreams, landing her in prison with other low-tier riffraff crammed together like sheep in a pen.

When Lucia is finally set free, it’s not because society has decided that this financially desperate Latina has earned a second chance. It’s because of an overcrowding crisis at the jail.

In a sprawling satirical sandbox filled with loudmouth radio DJs and over-the-top conservative cartoons, it’s the sharpest stab GTA 6 lands on American culture. Justice is big business in the Florida-inspired state of Leonida, and people like Lucia are products to be cycled in and out of a flawed system. All the while, the real criminal masterminds of Leonida trash their penthouses into the wee morning hours.

Lucia’s unjust origin story is the key to understanding and embracing GTA 6’s tantalizing power fantasy, panicked parents and politicians be damned. Rockstar Games’ astonishing open-world adventure, the most impossibly grand and expensive video game that may ever exist, doesn’t just generate its morbid pleasure from cop-killing sprees and armed robberies; that comes from living in a reactive place governed by intricate rules that beg to be bent and busted. If you can learn to outmaneuver police helicopters and escape with duffel bags full of unmarked bills, you’ll be rewarded with a small taste of the law-breaking freedom that’s only offered to a certain class of crook in America.

It’s no wonder that, after decades of moral panic, GTA once again earns its title as public enemy #1 — only this time, Rockstar accomplishes that less through bloodshed and more with an empathetic love story built for an era of stark economic uncertainty. It’s a bold choice, considering the series’ cold-blooded reputation, but it’s also what makes GTA 6 one of Rockstar’s finest games to date.

Following an opening prologue that ends with Lucia behind bars, the story really kicks off as her sentence is winding down. Jason, now living in a dilapidated shack in the Keys, has found a modest living doing odd jobs for Brian, an old-timer who has mastered the art of laying low far outside of Vice City. When Jason receives word that Lucia is getting an early release, it lights a fire under him to get his house in order so that the lovers can have a chance at a normal life. Key word: normal.

The opening chapter, filled with low-level crimes and mundane roof-patching missions, makes for a strong introduction to a story about a couple scraping by. Whereas some GTA games explicitly focus on the myth of the American Dream, following wannabe kingpins who can’t get enough money and power, Jason and Lucia’s motivations are more down to Earth. The duo’s ultimate goal is to earn enough cash to skip town and support one another. While you can spend your riches on guns and designer clothing, your big-picture aim is to fill up a savings account via the in-game BuckMe cash app. That’s the real dream.

It’s a more human GTA story that’s sold by Jason and Lucia. Both characters get equal screen time during the campaign, and both are compelling leads in their own way. Jason is a veteran, a detail that’s never deeply explored, but one that subtly establishes him as a person who has been left behind. Why is he living in a leaking shack and reduced to working shady gigs while patriotic right-wing media dominates Leonida’s radio waves and TV stations? There’s a lingering sense that he’s been used and tossed aside on the literal outskirts of America.

Jason is also a true himbo: a monotone musclehead who reveals his heart of gold early on when quietly obsessing over home renovation tasks once he learns Lucia is getting out. He has the energy of a young Channing Tatum, with charm and sincerity always poking through his tough guy persona. Jason also works as the perfect straight man for the oddball rat race that is Leonida. He’s just as confused as you are when a stranger escapes a car crash and decides to hop back into the burning vehicle to ensure he gets a bigger insurance payout. The shit people will do for money in this town.

But Lucia is the real star of the power couple. She’s unapologetically brash, and has no reservations about getting back to a life of crime after getting out of prison. That can make her seem one-dimensional at first, but it’s grounded in a real anxiety that crops up between her snarkiest one-liners. A key flashback shows her struggling to make ends meet as a ride-share driver, weathering racist abuse from passengers who in turn tank her rating. There’s a sense that she’s hit a dead end in a world that hates her; even an app is rigged against her. She sees little choice but to accept risky jobs in hope that she can put even $500 into her bank account. The shit this town makes us do for money.

Leonida is full of natural predators that circle its swamps like hungry alligators.

The central story, which has the rhythm of a five-season prestige TV series, sees the couple graduating to bigger crimes and even bigger apartments as they move throughout the state. Small gigs in the Keys soon give way to harmless carjackings upstate, and then to tense bank robberies in Vice City’s urban core. While there are some thrilling heists along the way that require meticulous planning across multiple missions, the story can sag when it’s inventing elaborate excuses to tutorialize features, reposition Jason and Lucia around the map, and introduce last-minute villains in time for its rushed final stretch. Instead, its most effective moments come in all the quiet conversations that happen between bank jobs, where Jason and Lucia trade insecurities about the viability of their shared future in a place like Leonida.

That feeling does not exist in a cultural vacuum. In true South Park fashion, Rockstar takes as many potshots as it can at the current state of American culture. It plays out like a damning, if sometimes dated, retrospective of the past decade — you could subtitle it “I Hate the 2020s.” Jason finds himself tangled up in a crypto scam. Lucia stumbles into a multi-level marketing scheme disguised as a noble women’s wellness business. Flip on a radio, and you might catch the all-too familiar sounds of a conservative whining about trans athletes in school sports, only to reveal that he’s a little too eager to "protect" young girls, if you catch my drift. Leonida is full of natural predators that circle its swamps like hungry alligators.

That’s the environment that our antiheroes find themselves sinking in while the bastards swim. Petty theft will earn someone like Lucia an immediate trip to jail, but everyone else appears to be getting away with much worse. Through its satirical mirror, GTA 6 questions why some people need to play by the rules while others don’t. (You can even see that reflected in the game’s own comedic targets: Rockstar goes out of its way to avoid lampooning powerful political figures like Donald Trump, but it’s not shy about dunking on rural rednecks and people living in the slums, struggling with substance use disorders.)

It doesn’t always feel like there’s much an individual can do to topple structural inequities in real life. But in a safe digital playground like this? You can start to understand the inherent appeal of blowing off steam in GTA 6’s punching-bag world.

While there’s a lot to unpack in this downright enormous game, the basic catharsis of GTA remains unchanged here. When you’re not working through story missions filled with third-person shootouts and car chases pulled straight from a Hollywood movie, you’re free to cause as much chaos as you want in Leonida. Jack a car, mug a pedestrian at gunpoint, rob a gas station — live your criminal fantasies as long as you can before the police rain on your parade. Decades after it first stirred moral panic, GTA’s appeal remains its unmatched ability to let players see just how much they can get away with as you test your relationship to authority.

Video games are a perfect avenue for that because they’re built on rules. You can only jump this high. You can’t access this area until you’ve found the right key. Killing this character will result in this consequence. Every time you enter a new world, your immediate task is to learn the law of the land. Your second, and more fun, task becomes figuring out how and when to break those laws. GTA 6’s greatest strength is that it offers an entire nation of laws. They spill out from countless criss-crossing systems that require you to keep your head on a swivel at all times.

Steal a car without scanning it first with a phone app that reveals its security system, and you might find yourself panicking when an unseen tracker starts beeping. Fail to find someone hiding in a back room during an attempted bank heist? They’ll have enough time to get free and trigger an alarm. Robberies are an especially strong addition to GTA 6 in that respect — not just because they let you explore interior spaces, but because they require you to get every detail right. You need to act quickly to compel guards to drop their guns, threaten cashiers to hand over safe codes, or destroy any security camera footage hiding in a server room. There are so many ways you can screw up unexpectedly, but that makes it all the more satisfying when you pull off a clean getaway just before the cops pull up to the scene of the crime.

Even GTA’s signature wanted system requires much more of your attention now, as police are now able to track your car, face, and outfit if someone sees you. Listen to the cops on your scanner, and you’ll hear them sharing the vehicle and outfit you were spotted in, or even your build. You’re not even fully out of the woods when you outrun the fuzz, as you’ll leave a temporary heat level in the area you committed a crime as cops continue to look for you. My first three-star chase was a 30-minute cat-and-mouse game that had me abandoning vehicles and scrambling over fences to get away. Just when I thought I was safe after having thrown on a hat and glasses, the chase began again once a passing cop car got a look at me for a moment too long.

All of this is different for a GTA game that is chasing a certain level of hyperrealism. Here, Rockstar very much builds on the groundwork it laid in 2018’s laborious Red Dead Redemption 2. For instance, you can build muscle by following a strict workout routine that requires you to do a certain number of reps within a set time period, and even build rests into your training regiment. If you want to steal a locked car without smashing the window and setting off an alarm, you’ll need to use a slim jim to carefully pick the lock. While that attention to detail could often feel tiresome in the slow-paced world of Red Dead, everything moves mercifully faster here, which makes sense considering that you’re barreling around town in sports cars instead of plodding down dusty roads on horseback.

Still, it takes some adjusting to. In my first sessions, I found myself almost scared to do anything I wouldn’t do in real life. Something about the photorealistic visuals has changed my usual GTA brain chemistry; I was suddenly stopping at traffic lights and refusing to cut in front of cars. But that would end up being a functional anxiety. Every session offered another opportunity to see what rules I could bend. Broken traffic laws led to quiet stick-ups in isolated pawn shops led to full-scale assaults on Leonida’s most well-guarded banks. Progression like this is baked into a story of escalating crimes, but it’s also built into the very psychology of GTA 6.

It’s easy to get reductive when discussing the broad appeal of GTA. Many would have you believe that its popularity is a symptom of a sick society that glorifies crime and violence. Maybe there’s some truth to that, but it doesn’t get to the heart of the series. Its joy has more in common with a kid screwing around in class as much as they can without landing in detention. When you possess little power in the world, there’s a pleasure in getting to push what buttons you can. (Even calling out sick from work to play GTA 6 on launch day can be seen as workers exercising the tiniest bit of institutional rebellion.) That’s made all the more powerful in this story, with these characters, within this laundry list of intricate laws.

None of this would matter much if the world wasn’t worth living in, but Leonida makes for the perfect setting. An enormous map that has been in the works since 2015, as Rockstar North co-studio head Rob Nelson told Polygon in an interview last week, GTA 6’s take on Florida packs an entire state’s worth of scenic landscapes and local eccentrics into one coherent place. While returning players might be most excited to spend time in the glitzy Vice City, that’s arguably the least interesting location in the game.

In the south, you have the Leonida Keys. Almost designed with the same tutorial zone philosophy underpinning Breath of the Wild’s Great Plateau, it perfectly captures the compact, distant nature of the real Florida Keys. It feels like you’re in a world physically detached from the state, where you don’t have a lot of avenues for escape if you go on a murder spree. It’s the perfect place to learn the rules of the road before things open up the further north you get. That stands in contrast to the very top of the state, a wide open rural landscape that’s built for hunters and hikers (and, unlike IRL Florida, actually features topography of differing elevations). Some of my favorite moments of the game so far have simply come from biking around mountains and stumbling into off-road racetracks and crumbling ruins.

It’s the rare open-world game that feels a mile wide and a mile deep.

There’s a lot to take in, especially considering how much work Rockstar puts into locking down the right aesthetics. The world itself is astoundingly detailed, with everything from a crowded strip club to a stray island off in the water feeling like it was hand designed and placed with purpose. That comes at the expense of peak performance, as GTA 6 runs at 30 frames per second with some dips when police chases hit the five-star mark. But that’s a negligible impact on the overall experience, given how smoothly it runs most of the time. (I played GTA 6 on a PS5 Pro.) And it’s a fair trade for a world that deserves its own line of postcards.

Driving around it is an auditory delight too, as GTA 6 features a soundtrack so large and diverse that you have to wonder if half of the budget went to music licensing. Metallica and Slayer are there for the metalheads, Charli XCX and Chappell Roan for the modern pop aficionados, and a whole lot of Trick Daddy and Juvenile for those who want to pay respects to the greats of the Dirty South rap scene. All of is spread between satirical commercials and radio shows that clue you into Leonida’s political climate as you drive. (Though be prepared to hear a lot of low-hanging dick jokes; GTA 6’s writers lean hard on cocks as a comedic crutch.) Every little artistic touch helps bring Leonida to life beyond its landmarks.

While there’s plenty to see and hear, I’m more impressed with how much of the world doesn’t feel constructed for players to explore. I occasionally drive by the kind of roadside construction areas that you pass by while on the highway. I don’t think much of them in real life; that’s not an area that I need to pull over and see! GTA 6 mirrors that experience, loading the world with so many mundane sights that ground the world in realism. You can go to them, of course. You might even find a collectible trading card or a wad of cash there. But there’s always a sense that life is happening in Leonida, even when you’re not there.

The best example is in how Jason and Lucia stumble into happenings in Leonida. While the map is filled with markers indicating main story quests, robbery locations, and other activities of note, many other things can just happen out of nowhere. Entire side quests can be discovered by noticing a guy drunkenly barreling down the highway on a tractor, or following the voice of a man looking for his lost cow. Those missable moments often hide the best quests in GTA 6 and imbue the world with more personality than you can imagine.

Other world events aren’t there to kick off missions at all. In one session, I heard the sound of police sirens and followed them downside road to find the cops surrounding a garage. A woman emerged and revealed that she was making kitty litter in there, not drugs. That wasn’t tied to a quest, nor did it have any story implications or rewards tied to it. It’s just a thing that I happened to catch. I’ve similarly seen plane crashes in the distance, watched the cops get into a standoff with a furry, and passed under a bridge as a group of troublemakers above threw tires down on the cars below. Sometimes I insert myself into those situations. (I sure gave those rabble-rousers a talking-to.) Other times, I decide it’s just not my business. The fact that I can make that choice adds a remarkable level of depth to Leonida, a place where you’re always reminded that Jason and Lucia aren’t the only two people who are up to some shit. Everyone is off on their own hustle.

That barely begins to cover what you can experience in Leonida. There are shooting ranges, golf courses, basketball hoops, meditation spots, diving expeditions, stunt jumps, drone-flying challenges, scannable landmarks, and more collectibles than you can imagine. Hell, there are multiple street racing leagues that will make you feel like you’re playing Forza Horizon 6 as streets get repurposed into natural circuits. There’s even an extensive vehicle modification system to allow you to fine-tune cars to your liking. It’s the rare open-world game that feels a mile wide and a mile deep.

What’s surprising about all of that is that it somehow avoids feeling totally overwhelming. Maybe it’s just because there’s so much to do that it’s easy to accept from the jump that you will likely never see it all, but I haven’t felt a compulsion to do anything in GTA 6. Everything is just another potential avenue to make money in a world where stable employment isn’t an option for Jason and Lucia. Want to earn a living fencing cars? You could just do that. Feel like busting up meth labs and selling the goods instead? You’ll have plenty of opportunities. The ultimate power of GTA 6 is that you have the freedom to pick your poison and carve out your own life in Leonida. Why shouldn’t Lucia have that privilege that’s afforded to everyone around her?

So, you can break all the rules in GTA 6. You can do more crimes than ever before. You can kill seabirds and throw them at people. You can amass a fortune on mounds of stolen cocaine and unmarked bills. So what?

That’s an important question not just for finding value in a video game that you could probably play for a decade, but in real life too. One of my deepest daily anxieties comes when I think too hard about existing in an employment battle royale. I know that I need a job to get the money to live, but what’s the point? Aren’t I just busting my ass for the privilege of waking up another day and busting my ass again? I spiral over it constantly; the eat-work-sleep cycle becomes claustrophobic the more I focus so tightly on it that I can no longer see its endpoint. (I imagine that Rockstar’s own staff may be grappling with similar feelings right now, as they are currently fighting to unionize.) It’s easy to lose your hope, motivation, or sense of self when you don’t have something to fight for.

GTA 6’s secret weapon for addressing that malaise comes back to Jason and Lucia. A video game starring a couple has a lot of upsides on a pure design level, and Rockstar takes full advantage of that dynamic. You can freely swap between characters outside of some early story stretches, allowing you to dress up and manage two heroes with distinct personalities. Some missions even have you flipping between them during dramatic set pieces, letting you cover yourself when you wind up pinned down during a tense shootout. When you’re about to do a bank robbery, you can invite your partner to the spot and go in together to ensure you can carry more bags and cover your vulnerabilities. When you leave, you can even command one character to head off and fence the spoils instead of lugging it back to a buyer manually. All of these scenarios allow you to think more strategically about how you pull off crimes, and how you spend your time.

But what’s more effective is how the two can interact when they’re not on the job. GTA 6’s real hero feature is an optional relationship system that allows you to create a romantic bond between Jason and Lucia. When I’m playing as one character, for instance, I can pull out my phone and call or text my partner. (You can text and drive, but be prepared to learn why you should never do that in real life.) That can be just to check in and see how they’re doing, or to invite them to come hang out on a whim. They’ll arrive either in their own car or a ride-share a few moments later, and you’ll find yourself on an impromptu date.

The benefits of GTA 6’s activity-filled world and systems-heavy design reveal themselves in those tender moments. One time, I took Lucia out for a round of mini-golf. Another time, I invited her out to the club and spent hundreds of dollars on champagne for us as we danced together. At any point, you can pull up a menu that allows you to strike up a conversation, flirt, or even hold hands. The two can also fall into natural conversations while hanging out. One time while driving, a country song came on the radio. Lucia started talking about how she thought country music was a big joke growing up, prompting a sweet little exchange between the couple as they chatted about the genre. It felt like a real conversation that would come up with your partner during a road trip.

I already know the wild story moments that GTA 6 will be widely remembered for — there are some fantastic ones involving a pulse-pounding airport caper and an accidental porn set misadventure. But I don’t think I’ll remember many of the repetitive drive-and-shoot chases that clog up a long story that recycles the same ideas a few too many times. Instead, it’s the intimate interactions that stick with me now. One of my favorite moments in the entire game didn’t happen during a mission or cutscene at all. It was when I woke up one morning to find Lucia standing on the balcony of our apartment. I walked up behind her, gave her a kiss, and held her in silence as we looked out across the beach below. I let it play out as long as I could before heading out for another day of blood and bullets.

That’s the so what. Through Jason and Lucia’s genuine chemistry, GTA 6 gives you a better reason to work through Leonida’s daily grind. Yes, there’s always another bank to rob or car to steal, but that’s not the reason you wake up in the morning. It’s to prove that love can survive in a cruel culture that tries so hard to beat it out of people. So be it if you have to play dirty to keep pace with the cold-hearted crooks who make their fortune from someone else’s pain. It shouldn’t be this hard to settle down with your lover and enjoy a sunset together in the rotten social climate that Rockstar has been eager to lampoon for decades at this point.

People will hem and haw about Grand Theft Auto’s purported glamorization of lawlessness. They always have; they always will. But it will be different this time. GTA 6’s empathy is bound to be the most controversial thing about it.

There’s a Ton of Conspiracies About Lab-Grown Meat Right Now. Here’s What’s Really True.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Experts break down what’s fact versus fiction.

Lab-grown meat is having an absolute field day on social media, as tons of outlandish claims and conspiracy theories about it are forming: The lab-grown industry is trying to sneak it into our grocery carts by labeling it as natural meat! Here’s why you need to avoid it at all costs! In truth, there’s a lot more to the story, and a ton of those claims are false.

Formally known as cultivated meat, lab-grown meat has been in development for more than a decade, but it’s only recently begun to capture mainstream attention. In 2023, it received regulatory clearance from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA). Now, it’s beginning to make its way into the market (albeit in limited quantities), says Steven Surico, RD, a registered dietitian with the Food and Nutrition Department at Good Samaritan University Hospital.

Meet the experts: Elliot Swartz, PhD, is a cultivated meat researcher and the senior principal scientist for cultivated meat at The Good Food Institute. Steven Surico, RD, is a registered dietitian with the Food and Nutrition Department at Good Samaritan University Hospital. Lauri Wright, RDN, is a registered dietitian and associate professor of nutrition and dietetics at the University of South Florida College of Public Health.

As such, lab-grown meat is becoming a grossly misunderstood concept. (Cue: The recent influx of misleading social media videos and AI-generated content.) These posts can blur the line between fact and fiction, leaving consumers unsure of what’s real, scaring them when there’s nothing to be afraid of. “The concern is that cultivated meat is something highly artificial, when in reality, it’s not,” says Surico.

Here’s everything you need to know about lab-grown meat, including what it is, whether it’s being sold in stores, and if you should truly worry about it, according to experts.

Cultivated meat is grown directly from animal cells, as opposed to natural meat that’s produced by raising and slaughtering an animal, says Elliot Swartz, PhD, a cultivated meat researcher and the senior principal scientist for cultivated meat at The Good Food Institute.

“A small sample of cells is fed basic nutrients, like amino acids, fats, vitamins, salts, and sugars, in clean, controlled environments similar to those used to make yogurt, cheese, and beer, where the cells are grown and developed into meat or seafood,” he explains. As the cells multiply and develop, they form the muscle and other tissues that make up meat. Once mature, Swartz says they’re harvested and prepared as a product that should, at least in theory, offer the same taste, smell, texture, and consistency as ordinary meat.

Lab-grown meat vs. plant-based alternatives: “Beyond Meat products are made primarily from plant ingredients and do not contain animal muscle cells, whereas cultivated meat starts with animal cells and grows those cells into animal tissue,” Surico says.

Cultivated meat may address some of the challenges associated with natural meat production, particularly when it comes to food safety, public health, nutrition, and the environmental impact of raising livestock. According to Swartz, one of the biggest advantages is the greater control over the production process, which could offer significant food safety benefits.

Research shows that raising large populations of animals in close quarters can create opportunities for bacteria, other pathogens, and diseases to spread. This can increase the risk of transmission to humans through food (or animal) contact, but cultivated meat could help reduce these risks, Swartz explains.

Cultivated meat is also better for the environment by reducing the land, water, and greenhouse gas emissions needed for meat production. Research from The Good Food Institute even suggests that if cultivated meat can be produced commercially in large quantities using clean energy, it could have a significantly smaller environmental footprint than conventional meat.

Finally, because the cells are grown in a controlled environment, researchers can influence the nutritional composition of the meat, says Lauri Wright, RDN, a registered dietitian and associate professor of nutrition and dietetics at the University of South Florida College of Public Health. For example, they can adjust the amount and types of fat in the meat to reduce saturated fat and cholesterol while increasing desirable nutrients like protein, she explains. Theoretically, this could lead to healthier meat that tastes the same, studies suggest.

While some people are afraid of lab-grown meat because they’re not really sure what’s in it, each product undergoes extensive testing by the FDA and USDA. So, its nutritional content is thoroughly tested to ensure it meets food safety standards, adds Swartz.

Yes, but availability is extremely limited. Cultivated meat has received approval from the FDA and USDA for sale in the U.S., as well as in Singapore and Australia, per The Good Food Institute. However, regulatory approval doesn’t necessarily mean that products are widely available. Plus, eight states in the U.S. have enacted bans or temporary restrictions on cultivated meat, including Alabama, Florida, Indiana, Mississippi, Montana, South Dakota, Nebraska, and Texas. These restrictions are largely driven by concerns about protecting traditional agriculture and livestock industries.

So, contrary to what social media might have you believing, cultivated meat isn’t something you can pick up at your local supermarket just yet. In the U.S., the only product currently being served is Wildtype’s cultivated salmon, which is available at a handful of select restaurants in Washington D.C., Oregon, Arizona, and California.

While it’s difficult to predict exactly when lab-grown meat will be in grocery stores, Swartz expects a gradual rollout, with smaller, more specialized retailers being the first to carry it. “You could start to see cultivated meat products more widely introduced in higher-end retailers in the early 2030s, but I don’t think you’ll see it in big box grocery stores until the late 2030s,” he says.

In grocery stores, it’ll be clearly labeled as lab-grown or cultivated meat. “Due to the USDA regulations and labeling, it should be very clear to the consumer if they are buying regular meat or lab-grown meat,” says Surico. In fact, federal law prohibits food from being misbranded or carrying false or misleading labels, and regulators have said cultivated meat products must be clearly and accurately labeled.

As for the price, that remains to be seen. Cultivated meat is currently more expensive to produce than most conventional meat and seafood, but Swartz says the long-term goal is to bring production costs down to match or fall below those of conventional options.

Not very. “You should not be freaked out,” says Wright. “If anything, you should see it as potential, because the future of lab-grown meat could give consumers more options and alternatives to what they buy and put into their body.”

Swartz agrees, pointing out that the benefits go beyond offering another way to produce meat. “Whether you care about the environment, animal welfare, food safety, or public health, there’s a lot of potential benefits for cultivated meat that are additive in terms of value for the consumer,” he says.

Of course, that doesn’t mean everyone has to embrace cultivated meat—traditional meat isn’t going away or being phased out of grocery stores or restaurants. The goal is simply to give consumers another option, not replace the foods they already know and enjoy. And if cultivated meat isn’t your jam, that’s perfectly fine, too.

It doesn't come only from good writing and characterisation, but from all the little conversations, actions, reactions and animated moments when they meet up or succeed. You'd have a heart of stone to go out of your way to ruin their romance, because you can. You monster.

Controlling when and how they meet up is part of GTA V's character-switching development. Moving between them is nearly instantaneous: the camera pulls out to a global view, zooms back down, and you can message the other to hook up or tell them to meet you somewhere. And you do get committed: when I occasionally Irish-goodbye'd Jason, I felt the need to call him and check up.

The central narrative picks up what worked in Red Dead Redemption 2, splitting the story into chapters that each introduce a new gang or cast and a grand setup to plan towards. The structure gives you plenty to play with, and the story spans more years as the couple bounces from backwater crimes to epic, militarised heists. These feed into cinematic tentpole moments that draw on everything from the later Fast and Furious movies to Assault on Precinct 13, with even a little nod to Die Hard as Lucia sparks a lighter in an air vent, laughs and paraphrases the classic line, "Come out to the coast, we'll get together, have a few laughs."

It's also where Rockstar's love of a good heist movie really shines. Some of the bigger set pieces have the clockwork precision of an Edgar Wright film, with different characters, objectives and moving parts all working towards one carefully choreographed finale. The editing is part of the fun: the action cuts between Jason and Lucia as they tackle their separate jobs, each sequence dovetailing with the next as the plan unfolds, the clock ticks down, and the stakes rise.

The grand finales of each chapter are proper spectacles that never play out the same way and deliver 'cinema'. You might be flying gunships over a stormy ocean, or swapping between Jason and Lucia as they complete different parts of a larger plan. Jason races to a rooftop to stop mobster reinforcements while Lucia hacks a vault, and you're jumping between the two as the action cuts back and forth, the tempo building with each switch. One moment you're holding off a gang; the next, you're working through the other side of the operation, and it all clicks together like clockwork. It's a wonderfully cinematic way to make character switching part of the action, not just a convenient way to get around the map.

Around these set pieces that take time to build are lower-key missions, some tailored to each character, and this is where Rockstar earns its rewards. The satire of modern America, social media, celebrity culture and consumerism that we love about GTA is here in abundance. A favourite is a storyline in which Lucia becomes embroiled in a bored-housewives vape cult that's like Goop meets Elizabeth Holmes.

Then there are the random encounters that make Leonida feel active, and never run how you think. An elderly woman screaming for help on a back road took me to her farmhouse, where I expected a robbery or a rescue. Instead, a client had 'died' in a bondage orgy gone wrong, and I needed to dispose of the body in a river. Even that didn't end as I expected.

Even 40-plus hours into the main story, Rockstar finds new ways to surprise me. It introduces a vault-hacking mini-game that uses haptics and controller prompts for one bank job, then drops in a drug-dog sidekick for a single mission. Ideas are picked up, enjoyed and dropped before they go stale. GTA VI has so many that it never has to hang around with one for long.

Shooting and weapon handling are superb, and each gun feels distinct. The cover system and enemy AI respond nicely even in busy rooms, and stealth is reliable and effective. While the big set-piece story missions can feel a little restrictive and scripted because they need to reach the cinematic heights Rockstar is aiming for, anything can happen outside them.

A bank heist, for example, needs proper planning. Park a getaway car, choose the right vehicle, pick the rear door lock, hack the cameras, wear a disguise, and don't overstay your welcome. The world is so well designed and systematised that everything feels connected. Next time, go in through the front door, or steal a truck and pull an ATM from the wall. Heists in backwater towns are easier because there are fewer police around. And nothing plays out quite as you expect; every bank is unique, and after two seamless jobs I go for a third and it all goes wrong – an unexpected teller wanders into the office for a coffee, an alarm is tripped, and before I realise it, I'm shooting my way out, bags shouldered, and looking for a new car to steal… the best-laid plans are shot.

The police react to all this more deeply than in previous GTAs. There's no longer just a star rating and a zone to escape, but how you escape matters. Swap cars and clothes, and they'll lose you, because they're looking for your description, not you, me, the player. All the old tricks are still here, from roadblocks and air support to increasingly aggressive police AI, but you have new ways to shake them off beyond just driving fast.

It ties into car ownership too, another Red Dead Redemption 2 influence. You can't carry everything, so it's worth stowing a change of clothes and weapons in your car. Carrying a gun openly gets the police involved, but some Vice City passers-by will also decide to take you out as well.

Driving feels familiar from GTA V, but with enough nuance to feel like an evolution, as cars have different weights and handling, and each one reacts differently through the DualSense triggers: sports cars resist less than family saloons when you pull to accelerate. Boats, bikes, planes and helicopters all work as you'd expect, but even here there are subtle shifts in how responsive each can be – one helicopter I stole was uncontrollable and spun into the swamp.

Character models, facial animation, skin, hair, and clothing all deliver the hyperrealism I expected from a new GTA, though they're still larger-than-life caricatures built to feel relatable rather than photorealistic.

Animation, including the transitions between gameplay and cutscenes, is where GTA VI takes its biggest strides. Lucia and Jason are wonderfully handled, whether it's the little snuggles and embraces or the way they hold up a bank and play off one another, but the breadth of animation across GTA VI is what impresses. Every Leonida resident feels uniquely animated, and it's fun just to people-watch. Early on, a drunk girl follows Jason around a bar and is authentically annoying, and the nightlife feels realistically overwhelming. Bar bands are animated to sell their next song, and even the wildlife has moments: I watched a white-tailed doe and buck nuzzle each other lovingly and naturally through Jason's rifle scope. It was cute enough to make me take a breath, but Jason needs more money, so the shot rang out and the wildlife scattered.

Leonida has a recognisable visual identity, bold and vibrant, with every corner full of quirks, from the deep forests of the north to the out-of-town mall and rows of copy-and-paste suburban houses. Fictional adverts, billboards, shops, signage, and TV build the world, as if you see an ad for a beer or car, and you can buy it (or steal it). Beyond signage, graffiti walls, sculptures, exhibits, and more invite you to linger and just take in the world.

It also feels like a realistic evolution of the 1980s Vice City I knew and loved, a city with a history inside a broader world, with references to Liberty City aplenty, from car number plates to Jason and Lucia's accents. It's somewhere to wallow, whether you're having a cocktail under palm trees swaying in a breeze or tearing up Vice City in a neon-lit supercar while crypto bros trade and wannabe rappers parade.

It's a wonder this all runs on a base PS5. I remember the mess of equally ambitious games like Cyberpunk 2077 at launch, but GTA VI is in a different league. The frame rate is locked at 30fps, and it's fine: stable, with little slowdown or stutter, and controls and handling feel spot on. There's occasional soft pop-in, but few visual compromises or trip-ups. You can feel the work that's gone into making everything run smoothly, and GTA 6 is polished to perfection.

I'm really only scratching the surface. Familiar street races, bank heists, and chilled fishing and hunting trips are joined by crypto rug pulls, golf tournaments, and deep-sea treasure hunts. Or I just drive, hunt a swamp thing, stop to solve a mystery from a local ad, or relax with a game of pool and a Patriot beer. You can take on the State's sports and races, go sailing, or listen to 80s FM hits, the biggest dance tracks, or to the MAGA-inspired Richard Bastion Show – "more volume than logic" – who's mad about everything.

This game has video games inside it that feel more complete than some real games I've played, from the top-down sci-fi shooter Time Jackers: Rewind to a lightgun score-chasing bar game Bang For Your Buck. And in typical Rockstar fashion, they all have a satirical edge, like the indie auteur game Tenebrae Lux, which pokes fun at the po-faced walking sims that were all the rage: it's black-and-white, minimal, and filled with overwritten prose like a Taylor Swift song about metaphors.

GTA VI is a world that exists beyond its main story, where everything feeds into and off each other. It's the best world-building I've experienced in a game in years, partly because its systems all work in a coherent, purposeful and layered way, but also because it's not afraid to be weird, whether that's discovering the dumpster lawyer, becoming a frog, slobbing on the sofa and watching an episode of Whose Dick's Whose, or finding an influencer in the boot of a stolen car who wants to be kidnapped for clout.

There are minor restrictions, mainly when the big cinematic missions take over and funnel you down a more scripted path, but for good reason, and the 30fps cap won't suit everyone (yet it's really not an issue). But in a game that otherwise gives you this much freedom, they're the exception.

13 years? The wait has been worthwhile. I still set off with a plan, get distracted by something ridiculous at the side of the road, and lose another hour to a game I thought I'd finished. I laughed within minutes of starting, and I laugh every time I play. I've also been stressed, revolted, astonished, baffled, heartbroken and euphoric. I've run the full gamut of emotions, and I still do each time I return to Vice City.

The patient: A 35-year-old man in the Czech Republic

The symptoms: The man was injured while working in a factory. His co-workers learned he'd been hurt after he crawled from the ground floor to the first floor and shouted for help. He had severe abdominal pain and rectal bleeding, and he said he had been injured while conducting "a silly experiment" involving a pressurized air system. However, he did not describe exactly what had happened.

"He repeatedly stressed that the incident was his own fault," doctors later wrote in a report of his case.

What happened next: The man was flown by helicopter to a nearby hospital, but during the flight, he became unresponsive and showed signs of hemorrhagic shock — a type of shock caused by significant blood loss over a short period. Shock, broadly, is when organs and body tissues don't receive enough oxygen due to decreased blood flow; if untreated, it can lead to organ failure and death.

Medical attendants in the helicopter worked to revive the man, but he died before he could be admitted to the hospital. Because the fatal injury occurred at the man's workplace, the police ordered a medicolegal autopsy, which both identifies the cause of death and collects details for law enforcement agencies, as part of an inquest procedure.

The autopsy: The coroner examined the body and noted that the soft tissues of the man's face were swollen and abnormally distended from retained air. The man's anus and genital areas were similarly distended and densely coated with dried blood, with radial tears in the skin surrounding the anus.

Next, the coroner conducted an internal examination. The man's brain was swollen, and he had air bubbles in his brain and chest cavity. Blood had collected around his diaphragm (the large muscle that helps inflate and deflate the lungs). It had also accumulated in the wall between the heart chambers on the left side, in tubes leading to his lungs, and in the membranes covering his lungs and heart. Reservoirs of blood also pooled in the man's abdominal cavity, in the tube leading to his right kidney, and in his digestive organs.

The man's heart was "shrunken and empty," according to the report.

The autopsy also revealed foreign objects stuck between loops of the man's small intestine. These included three pieces of a black rubber pipe stopper and scraps of a plastic bag. His rectum — the final straight portion of the large intestine — had detached violently from his anus. It was surrounded by blood and marked with a laceration that was nearly circular, indicating that an "explosive rectal injury" had triggered hemorrhagic shock and caused the man's death, the coroner concluded.

Further investigation: Additional evidence from the factory and rubber fragments in the man's small intestine provided the police with further insight into his injuries; additional clues came from his internet search history, including photos of himself that he'd submitted to an amateur sex website.

The police concluded that the man had been performing autoerotic anal stimulation while at work, using a rubber inflatable pipe stopper measuring 2 to 3 inches (50 to 70 millimeters) in diameter, and an air gun, a handheld device for directing powerful jets of compressed air.

They suggested that the man likely attached the expandable pipe stopper to the muzzle of the air gun, which was in turn connected to the factory's air distribution system by a long, high-pressure hose. He then covered the pipe stopper with a plastic bag, inserted it into his rectal cavity, and pressed the air gun's trigger, causing "an unexpected abrupt inflation and explosive bursting of the pipe stopper within his rectum," the doctors wrote in the case report.

What makes the case unique: This case is the first recorded example of a person dying from a "rectal explosion during self-gratification," the doctors wrote. Piecing together the circumstances leading up to the man's injury was important for the hospital's doctors and law enforcement to understand how the fatality likely occurred, the authors said.

When performed responsibly and consensually, rectal insertion of foreign bodies typically does not cause significant injury, the authors added. However, "the introduction of some objects into the rectum might have unpredictable and even fatal effects."

There are precious few development studios who are willing to play in these sorts of contemporary urban environments. Such is the inherent complexity of regulating an underlying simulation where even the simplest act creates such a wide array of possible consequences. Rockstar Games hasn't just set a new benchmark; it has delivered what is undoubtedly the highest-fidelity sandbox in existence.

How Rockstar has been able to build something of this scale, scope, and quality will be dissected for years to come. It's easy to marvel at the near total lack of loading screens, the speed of character switching, and an exceedingly stable performance with minimal pop-in – despite absurd population density and the speeds at which you may traverse such a topographically diverse playspace.

These aspects are certainly impressive, but they aren't what ultimately elevates GTA 6 into being such a singular experience. Rather, there's a maturity behind the ethos of its core mechanical design, a cohesion in the way that its systems combine, which helps separate it from what the studio has accomplished in the past. In GTA 6, you can feel Rockstar's relentless pursuit of iteration, and the studio's ambition to create the most grounded instalment of the Grand Theft Auto franchise to date.

GTA 6 has the potential to be exceedingly divisive, particularly for those whose only point of interaction with the series thus far has been GTA 5. The life of crime you build for yourself in Leonida is far less chaotic and caustic than the adventures splattered throughout the streets of San Andreas. If the 2013 instalment was a faint echo of a Michael Bay blockbuster, then its successor is more Michael Mann – no less entertaining, but more considerate in how action feeds carnage and frames characters.

Jason Duval and Lucia Caminos – partners in crime, ensnared in a criminal conspiracy that quickly outstrips their understanding of an underworld that they once played in for idle thrills. The dual-protagonist foundation works exceedingly well for GTA 6, allowing you the space to grapple with two distinct personalities who are, in a sense, eternally bound to one another. You get to decide why that may be on a daily basis – for family, for honor, or for the simple promise of a bigger payday.

The interplay between the two characters – born through a combination of nuanced writing, strong performances, and intricate character animations – feeds what is perhaps the most realistic and adult portrayal of a romantic relationship in video game history. Everyday acts of kindness bring Jason and Lucia together, indifference drives them apart. This isn't captured as part of some tawdry in-game checklist, but through deliberate, empathetic actions.

In GTA 5, all that L2 was ever good for was aiming a weapon – in GTA 6, it also lets you hold hands with your partner. Interactions only get more detailed from there. Check in with each other over the phone, hit the gym together, hustle drunks at bars, or raid banks with his-and-hers matching tactical rifles. The connection you organically foster between the two characters (or allow to decay) only fuels a more considerate narrative that acts as a catalyst for endless free-flowing excursions across Leonida.

The thematic coherence necessitated by the narrative arc acts as a foundation, feeding other critical changes to the way Grand Theft Auto has traditionally played. If Jason and Lucia's relationship feels real, then so too should the world they express themselves within.

You can only carry two handguns and two longarms at a time, for example, and brandishing a weapon will cause the public to react in different, volatile ways. Police presence isn't some glorified cone of vision, but rather it works as a communicative force to be feared – able to track your appearance, vehicles, and criminal activities between city limits. All of this (and more) contributes to a feeling of true, immersive role-play within Leonida.

With Red Dead Redemption 2, Rockstar Games sought to create a reactive virtual world. The studio aspired to near-total immersion – action, interactions, and character development feeding off the sandbox's simulation of an everyday reality. In that sense, GTA 6 is a true sequel to RDR2. Rockstar has pursued relentless iteration of those Old West foundations here, leaning into those immersive qualities where it makes sense for the criminal-fantasy GTA fosters, and pulling back when they don't.

Unlike RDR2's Arthur Morgan, your hair and beard won't naturally grow over time. Weight and health fluctuations are less severe. Eating and exercising will impact the definition of Jason and Lucia's bodies, and resting helps to ease the exhaustive toll of constant criminality, but you'll never find yourself entirely at the mercy of these systems. Engagement with the Vice City lifestyle simply helps further the feeling that you are living life on your own terms.

Rockstar has gone to great lengths to inject more "freedom and flexibility" into its main mission design, all in an effort to reduce the number of fail states you encounter for daring to be experimental.

GTA 6 also features a more nuanced version of the Red Dead Redemption 2 honor system, with your reputation fed throughout the world more organically. It's one of the more fascinating new systems, and a sharp diversion from prior games in the series that have never really commented on your criminality. Criminal Profiles are built gradually over time, ranging from Professional to Irredeemable, based on the way you commit crimes and handle pressure.

The improvements to graphical fidelity and animation quality, and the lengths Rockstar has gone to create a virtual world with a functioning ecology, have meant that there are more human reactions from the populace to your presence than ever before. This intersects well with the Criminal Profile system, which (while never overly judgemental) does gradually convince you to define a moral compass and follow your own value system.

There was a point where I realized that I had inadvertently sunk the entirety of my BuckMe bank account into an arsenal dedicated entirely to stealth action. I was raiding banks, drug labs, and gang compounds like GTA 6 was some sort of perverse Florida-set Metal Gear Solid. Sneaking my way into cover and onto rooftops, tracing enemy patrol patterns with a cigarette in one hand and silenced pistol in the other. I'd like to tell you that smoking before every firefight was because it temporarily boosts the Focus bar, but at some point I had become addicted to the aesthetic of it all.

A stealth-action approach to encounters in GTA 6 worked in my favor because I was pursuing a Professional criminal profile – assigned by the worldwide reputation system for those who lean towards non-lethal incapacitations and clean getaways. I gravitated towards this style of play because of the Focus system, which iterates smartly on aspects of RDR's Eagle Eye and Max Payne's Bullet Time. Skillfully shooting weapons out of hands, or capping enemies in the knee before watching them tumble down staircases in slow motion, is just as satisfying as striding out of cover with a shotgun.

Combat is one of many aspects where Rockstar has built precipitously beyond GTA 5. Placing bullets requires real aiming and precision. Weapon feedback is weighty, with each weapon type and manufacturer variant carrying its own unique handling profile. Survival requires movement and cover. Improvements have been made to the driving models too, with narrower streets necessitating more dexterity behind the wheel – not to mention countless more variables at play, from the threat of running out of fuel to the scores of modifications that can be applied to your favorite cars.

There are degrees of sophistication to everything that GTA 6 does. There isn't an aspect of the series' gameplay that hasn't been improved over past instalments. It's a little slower and certainly more balanced, but no less entertaining. It's immediately noticeable that the artificial intelligence guiding the actions of both your partner and the NPCs who inhabit Vice City is greatly improved too, with some of GTA 6's best moments coming as you ask Jason to take the wheel while you lean out of a window as Lucia, a chattering assault rifle chewing through the tyres and rotary blades of a pursuing police force.

If more nuanced storytelling has the potential to be divisive, then the revisions to the Wanted and Heat systems could be downright controversial. The police are more attuned to your actions than ever, actively tracking a number of variables at any one time in an effort to curtail crimes both big and small.

The return of a six-star wanted level is great for those looking for more of a challenge, although the Heat system – which can identify you after a crime based on factors such as your appearance, vehicle, outfit, visible weapon, partner's appearance, and whether you were recorded by CCTV – introduces a whole new dynamic to Grand Theft Auto. Some will love it, others will hate it.

This system can fluctuate between being a little frustrating (particularly at lower Wanted levels, for more incidental infractions) and utterly thrilling once the pressure is on, particularly if you're holding hundreds of thousands of dollars in stolen jewellery, market bills, and narcotics between Jason, Lucia, and the storage of their personal vehicle – loot is lost if not properly funnelled through fences. And when the Heat and Wanted system are on your back, it evokes this almost desperate pressure to survive a city that is collapsing in on you.

There's a sort of useful friction introduced through the revised Wanted and Heat system, overlapping with more freeform opportunities presented throughout the world itself. While GTA 5 is best remembered for its cinematic, structured heists, its successor finds its best moments as you decide on a whim to stick up a gas station or start smashing up the display cabinets in a jewelry store whilst you're between objectives. Bank robberies present massive opportunity and carry enormous risk, necessitating careful, tactile preparation before you burst through doors with weapons raised.

The high-speed pursuits which can unfurl out of these encounters get gradually more out of control until they don't – Busted, Wanted, or free to fight and love for another day. Your Heat level and the world's awareness of your crimes is tracked intuitively on a more dynamic HUD, and I appreciate just how many opportunities there are to escape the police depending on any number of external factors.

One of my favorite escapes saw me fleeing a large bank in Downtown Vice City – a densely populated area with streets packed full of police, pedestrians, and other vehicles. Throwing stash bags to my partner, spilling out of the vault and onto the streets, I was able to gain just a little distance from the cops and pull into a multistory carpark. Precious seconds to hide Jason's weapons in the trunk of his car, swap out Lucia's outfit, and hotwire a new vehicle. Clambering into the car, I manually shut off the headlights and engine, and ducked beneath the steering wheel – the patrolling police passed none the wiser, affording me the space to slip out of the garage slowly.

The sheer scale of Leonida is something to behold. It's a playspace which rewards curiosity and creativity. It's impressive, especially when you remember it has all been "curated, considered, and created by a human hand."

It's these sorts of unscripted sequences where GTA 6 shines brightest, although that isn't to say that the main story isn't packed with its fair share of memorable moments. There will be some debate as to whether the standout GTA 6 heists rise to the levels of GTA 5's 'The Paleto Score' and GTA 4's 'Three Leaf Clover', but they certainly come close.

Core mission design is still cinematic and largely linear by design, although you do have wider space to experiment in approach and escape than in previous Grand Theft Auto games – mission parameters can be broad, and it's up to you to decide how to achieve them. Rockstar has introduced the ability to manually retry a mission from its last checkpoint, further exorcising some of the series' more frustrating demons.

GTA 6 also happens to be exceedingly well-written. Missions may be grounded in the virtual reality of Leonida, but they still have scope to be ridiculous. Mysteries unfurl from QR codes embedded in the world. Snippets from a radio news broadcast can make you laugh in one moment and pay off as a chance encounter in the world some 40 hours later. Random citizens will make you laugh, moments between Jason and Lucia will make you cry. It's a true joy to spend time in this world.

I have Jason and Lucia start their road trip up to Lummox County in Key Lento, just a short drive to the federal penitentiary where they had once served time. Now, it's just the open road. Their favorite outfits packed into the trunk of an over-torqued Vapid Dominator, along with a small arsenal of weapons, ammo crates, body armor, and parachutes.

The sun shines brightly; the radio tuned to V-Rock as hits from the '80s soundtrack a transition from lush ocean views to the quiet character of Hamlet. A quick stop at Sara's Unisex Salon for a fresh cut for Jason, and makeover for Lucia. Later, an unintended diversion into Southside after a small collision on Interstate 97 led an aggravated driver to jackknife my whip and feed bullets into the windshield – a local Pay 'n' Spray draining what little Pocket Cash I had on me for repairs. The sun started to set.

I stop to refuel somewhere around Vice City's Little Cuba district. An eruption of rain washing Lucia's makeup down her face, her gaze fixed entirely on another bay at the garage – a drunk driver pours gasoline into the flatbed of his truck. On the road and out of the city, the subtle changes in landscape reveal a world of contrast and contradictions. The changing colors of police vehicles, the different accents and demeanours of everyday citizens at roadside restaurants, indicate a crossing of boundaries.

Day transitions to night, night back to day. The weather shifting constantly. The vehicle splutters as roads become more uneven the further we cruise away from the big city. I switch from driver to passenger, smoking cigarettes as Jason and Lucia chatter about their lives. Eventually, vast farmlands settle into dense forest canopies beneath the shadow of Mt. Kalaga National Park. It took me 80 hours to see the dense foliage and lush, leafy green hues of Lummox County. And there's no telling how many more hours await me there.

In Grand Theft Auto 6, Rockstar Games has created the ultimate time vampire. This is an open-world that is driven entirely by freedom and consequence, where every one of your actions ultimately impacts the lives of Jason and Lucia. GTA 6 invites you to live a life in Leonida. A world that is as beautiful and it is deadly; as full of heart as it is heartbreak. GTA 6 is an essential play for PS5 and Xbox Series X owners.

My GTA 6 review was written based on a PS5 Pro playthrough, with the game code provided to me by Rockstar Games. And it must be said, the improved ray tracing to help bolster lighting and shadow on PS5 Pro is pretty damned delightful. Vice City looks gorgeous at night.

So long as we're clear that I reviewed GTA 6 on PS5 Pro. I was very impressed with the performance. Rockstar targeted 30fps on all platforms, but I didn't encounter any fluctuations. There was infrequent pop-in, but that was only during play sessions which stretched beyond eight hours.

I spent over 100 hours playing GTA 6 for review. The Jason and Lucia story is all wrapped up, all optional missions are done, and I've made some pretty good headway on getting through a lot of the activities. Time to hunt for some collectibles!

I have completed every Grand Theft Auto game. Yes, even the ones released for PS1. I have very fond memories of attending the midnight launch for GTA 4, and even fonder memories of reviewing GTA 5 way back in 2013 for X360 Magazine.

This will be a contentious debate, but there's a very good argument to be made that Grand Theft Auto 6 is the best Rockstar game. It's certainly a better overall experience than GTA 5, and more entertaining than Red Dead Redemption 2.
