MTG Star Trek Card Featuring Patrick Stewart Sells At $26K

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Magic: The Gathering isn't exactly a stranger when it comes to ridiculously expensive cards, and while resellers aren't quite as rabid as some other TCGs, even somewhat less popular sets like Star Trek are still reaching hefty prices on the second-hand market. While the price might not compete with Lord of the Rings' over $2M One Ring card, getting one of MTG's new serialized cards will still cost you quite a bit.

While the seven hand-signed card variants introduced in MTG's Star Trek Collector's Boosters were already going to be a massive hit due to their limited availability, one of them has already broken the $20,000 mark, albeit for a bit of a morbid reason.

As shared by moombahh on Reddit, while most autographed Star Trek cards go for around $5-10K USD, a recent eBay listing for none other than Captain Jean-Luc Picard reportedly sold for a whopping $26K. While it's entirely possible the buyer sent a lower offer, it might even end up on the cheaper side of the spectrum if the other eBay listings are anything to go by.

With more recent listings having shot up all the way to $38K and an even more massive $45K, the price could wind up being well above the last sold price, and it's not hard to see why either.

For the first time ever, you can pack signed MTG cards, with seven to collect in total, but the first two revealed are of Captains Kirk and Janeway.

Dwarfing the Secret Lair card prices, the main reason players have been pricing their cards so high is that, since signing the cards, Jean-Luc Picard's actor, Patrick Stewart, recently passed away, leaving the serialized cards among the last items signed by the legendary actor.

While fans have pointed out that it's more than a little depressing that others are trying to capitalize on the late actor's passing by selling the cards for absurd prices, it's unlikely that sellers will be slowing down anytime soon, considering how limited their availability will continue to be.

Weichert, Realtors Joins HouseCanary’s Google Listings Program

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Housing-focused data and analytics company HouseCanary announced today that Weichert, Realtors® has joined the Google Listings Program as the tech giant continues to expand its latest foray into residential listing.

The active listings for Weichert’s company-owned offices in its five-state footprint of New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, Virginia and Maryland are now live and available for display in Google mobile search results in live markets, according to a release from HouseCanary.

Weichert is only the latest to join the program, following three others that included REMAX Advantage, Berkshire Hathaway HomeServices Florida Properties Group and ENRG Realty.

According to HouseCanary, these listings are now among those that appear in a carousel at the top of Google mobile search results. Each listing shows basic property details and listing brokerage attribution, and consumers can tap through to a detail page with more information about the home, the listing agent’s name and a click-to-contact button.

“Weichert is one of the most recognized names in residential real estate, and its decision to join reflects how the US’s largest brokerages are recognizing the value of getting as much exposure for their listings as possible,” said Chris Rediger, CEO of HouseCanary. “We are thrilled to have Weichert, Realtors in the Google Listings Program.”

HouseCanary also connects directly with brokerage companies, such as Weichert, Realtors®, to bring their listings into the program, via a participating MLS.

“Joining the Google Listings Program is an exciting step forward toward maximizing visibility for Weichert listings and delivering high-intent leads to our agents,” said Joseph McDonald, president of residential sales and real estate services for Weichert, Realtors®. “The initiative creates a streamlined home search experience for consumers and a direct path for buyers to connect with Weichert agents. It also reinforces our commitment to keeping our agents ahead of the curve in digital real estate marketing.”

All the maneuverings come as HouseCanary tries to wind down following court approval of its Chapter 11 bankruptcy reorganization. In an email to RISMedia, a HouseCanary spokesperson wrote that “(t)he company continues to operate normally. Its proposed plan, subject to court approval, targets emergence (from Chapter 11) before year-end, with full recovery for all approved creditors.”

Launched in early 2026 and expanding to all eligible U.S. markets this fall, the Google Listings Program is powered by HouseCanary, which establishes agreements with MLSs to feed their brokerage members’ listings to Google.

The listings of Weichert’s company-owned offices come via My State MLS. Other MLSs participating in the Google Listings Program include ARMLS, Bright MLS, California Regional MLS, IRES MLS, Permian Basin Board of Realtors®, REcolorado and San Diego MLS.

Skydance to Combine Warner Bros. Television Group and Paramount Television Studios as One Big Studio

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The corporate shakeups keep coming at Skydance, this time to the TV department. Warner Bros. Television Group and Paramount Television Studios are being combined as one big studio under Skydance, with WB’s Channing Dungey and Paramount’s Matt Thunell serving as presidents of the combined studio.

The new studio, which officially launches in early 2027 (the two entities will keep operating independently until then), will be known as Warner Bros. Television Studios. The entire studio will report to George Cheeks, co-chair and Chief Content Officer of Skydance TV. As previously announced, Dungey also serves as Chair of Skydance Global Cable Media, with oversight over all 50+ WB Discovery and Paramount cable brands. Together, Dungey and Thunell will lead all of scripted and unscripted programming, overseeing all creative departments. They will also oversee BET Studios and Warner Bros. International Television Production’s studios around the world.

CBS Studios will continue to operate independently, headed by President David Stapf, who also reports to Cheeks. CBS Studios International, Skydance Sports Entertainment and See It Now Studios will operate under the CBS Studios umbrella.

The new Warner Bros. Television Studios will be home to more than 120 active television series and some of the industry’s biggest global franchises, including “Harry Potter and The Philosopher’s Stone” (HBO), “The Pitt” (HBO Max), “Ted Lasso” (Apple TV), “Georgie & Mandy’s First Marriage” (CBS), “Reacher” (Prime Video), “Marshals” (CBS), “Landman” (Paramount+), “Emily in Paris” (Netflix) and the upcoming series “Neuromancer” (Apple TV).

Pictures. It also recently shook up DC Studios, with James Gunn and Peter Safran exiting and giving way to Jeremy Latcham.

Television Studios gives us two world-class studios, with the breadth, depth and creative ambition to deliver for Skydance platforms and third-party partners across the industry.”

“Matt has extraordinary creative instincts, and a deep understanding of great storytelling. I couldn’t ask for a better partner as we bring these storied studios together,” Dungey said in a statement. “We have a unique opportunity to create something truly special for our creative partners and audiences around the world. I’m very excited to begin this new chapter.”

Thunell said, “Channing is one of the most accomplished and respected executives in our industry, with an extraordinary track record of building successful businesses, developing enduring partnerships and delivering some of television’s most acclaimed programming. Ever since our time together at Netflix, I’ve hoped we’d have the opportunity to work together again, and I’m thrilled that day has finally come. We’re bringing an unmatched roster of talent under one roof, building on the remarkable legacies of two storied studios. The possibilities ahead are extraordinary, and I couldn’t be more excited to partner with Channing to champion the ambitions of our creative talent and bring exceptional stories to audiences around the world.”

Kia went from this one quirky EV 40 years ago to an entire electric lineup

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Kia Besta EV hit the roads in 1986 as the brand’s first fully electric vehicle. 40 years later, Kia continues to “lead the pack” with a full lineup of EVs ranging from entry-level cars and SUVs to three-row models, and just about everything in between.

Kia wanted to design a new vehicle for the 1986 Asian Games, which were held in Seoul, South Korea, one that did not emit any toxic fumes in front of runners.

So, it created the Besta EV. The Besta van was initially developed 40 years ago by Kia’s Central Research Institute as the successor to the popular Bongo van, which is still sold in Korea and other markets today.

Unlike the Bongo, it was developed using Kia’s in-house tech rather than sourced from another automaker and rebadged as its own.

Kia swapped the 2.0L four-cylinder diesel engine for a 35 kWh battery and electric motor, which provided a range of more than 26 miles (42 km) on a single charge.

While the Besta EV was never actually sold to the public, it laid the groundwork and offered an early glimpse at Kia’s shift to electrification.

The first mass-produced Korean EV certified for commercial sale was the Kia Pride EV in 1993. In 2011, the Ray EV became Kia’s first series-production electric car.

Kia expanded its electrification ambitions to Europe and the UK in 2014 with the first-generation Soul EV, the beloved boxy, funky crossover that was discontinued after the 2025 model year.

The Korean automaker has come a long way since the Besta EV, with a full lineup of electric vehicles ranging from compact cars to mid-size and three-row SUVs, vans, and just about everything in between.

Kia now offers an electric option for nearly everyone with seven-passenger EVs on sale in the UK and Europe, including the EV2, EV3, EV4, EV5, EV6, EV9, and PV5 Passenger van.

With its first software-defined vehicle (SDV) and a new B-segment electric hatchback set to launch by the end of 2027, Kia has no plans to slow down. On top of this, Kia will expand its PBV lineup with the PV7 next year, a larger sibling to the PV5.

Four decades after the Besta EV arrived, Kia said it continues to “lead the pack today” with one of the most diverse all-electric EV lineups of any brand.

Through October, Kia has sold over 135,000 EVs in the UK, with fully electric vehicles now accounting for over 37% of total sales.

Thanks to its new EV lineup, Kia became “Europe’s fastest-growing top 10 passenger-car brand.” According to the European Automobile Manufacturers’ Association, Kia registered 377,078 vehicles between January and August 2026, up 9% compared to the same period last year.

Sjoerd Knipping, Chief Operating Officer of Kia Europe, said the growth is thanks to having “one of the broadest and most advanced electrified line-ups in Europe.”

In the US, Kia launched the compact EV3 (check out our review of it) this year, starting at just $29,890. It joins the EV6, EV9, and Niro EV in Kia’s US electric vehicle lineup.

What is Nvidia without AI’s sugar daddy in charge?

If the glorious AI revolution rests entirely on the well-being of one man, AI is perhaps not as robust a technology as I had previously been led to believe. Nonetheless, chunks of Silicon Valley are now insisting he is indispensable. For instance, if Elon Musk is telling the truth, then Jensen Huang (the former CEO of Nvidia, a company with a market cap of about $3 trillion) has been making deals that were “a handshake with Jensen” with suppliers — “not a written contract,” Musk wrote.

Fascinating stuff to tell shareholders. But after listening to Nvidia’s first earnings call with its new CEO, Colette Kress, I do think something may have shifted. In her former role as CFO, Kress generally told Huang to stop spending so much money on financing its customers, because telling the CEO to stop spending so much goddamn money is the CFO’s job. Apparently he belittled her about this, but that’s the CEO–CFO relationship, baby! Anyway she’s the boss now, at least temporarily. I think it’s reasonable to assume that though Nvidia has for years been the AI industry’s sugar daddy, without Huang and his handshakes in the picture, it may be less liberal with its cash.

Kress shot down the idea that Huang’s departure would lead to a brain drain within Nvidia

Kress’ job was never going to be easy. Even without tantrums from the likes of Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney and Musk, both of whom seem to have a lot of free time for guys who are ostensibly running multibillion-dollar companies, having to steer a company after an event on the scale of Huang’s meltdown is a miserable task. But this woman also is now having to deal with a bunch of guys publicly insisting that Huang was the only reason Nvidia succeeded. It amused me on the call that she did the thing I often do when men decide they can bully me, which is to tap in another man who is theoretically or actually in charge of me — and so on the call, Nvidia board Chair Harvey C. Jones politely told these losers to get bent. The board does not intend to reinstate Huang, and the company won’t be taking questions about him.

On the call, Kress pointedly noted that Nvidia’s agreements with suppliers last through the end of the decade, so I suppose we must entertain the possibility that when it comes to the handshake deals, Musk is, once again, lying. This is an important thing, though — as SemiAnalysis noted in its bizarrely conspiratorial post on the situation, Nvidia is successful because it has choked off the supply chain.

Kress also shot down the idea that Huang’s departure would lead to a brain drain within Nvidia, or the kind of open internal revolt that got Sam Altman reinstated at OpenAI during “the blip” in 2023. She told investors that “our employees, we believe, are committed. They are committed to doing their life’s work at Nvidia.” Nvidia’s employees have “been working seamlessly on what they need to complete,” she added. “From a business perspective, it’s business as usual.” I’m sure we’ll hear more about this in the coming days, but unlike with Altman, Huang resigned, and we all know why. And while some employees support reinstating Huang, I notice that there’s a real dearth of “we’ll quit with him” in those sentiments.

I don’t think this is going to shut the Branch Nvidians up, but I do think the board doesn’t have another choice here. Hilariously, we learned this weekend that Nvidia did not buy complete directors’ and officers’ insurance. While the individual executives’ and board members’ personal assets are shielded from lawsuits that stem from its executives’ behavior, the company is not. So if someone, or perhaps several someones, were to file a suit because of, say, Huang’s personal life and its damaging effects on the stock price, Nvidia itself is on the hook.

The scandal isn’t mainly about the affairs, as much fun as we all have been having with them

This is to say nothing of the Securities and Exchange Commission’s investigation — which, given how cliqued up Huang was with President Donald Trump, may be investigating on his behalf rather than investigating him — or the California attorney general going on Bloomberg TV to say that he was going to start kicking the tires on an investigation, or Huang and general counsel Tim Teter’s upcoming congressional testimony. The three actual lawyers left at the Department of Justice may be doing their own investigation, too. Elizabeth Warren seems mad. Marco Rubio’s name has been publicly attached in stories on the leaks, which makes me a little suspicious about who’s been doing the leaking.

A reminder: The scandal isn’t mainly about the affairs, as much fun as we all have been having with them. It is about Nvidia customers allegedly circumventing a ban on selling chips to China. The scandal is already big enough that US policy has been altered; the Department of Commerce informed us all on November 16th that there were new restrictions on selling chips to countries such as Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil. Also, data centers around the world need licenses from the US to lease compute to companies headquartered in China.

“No one could tell them ‘no’ or put them in their place.”

You may be wondering how these restrictions will affect Nvidia’s sales. The company also addressed this, though somewhat obliquely: It raised its forecast, saying that revenue will grow by 75 percent next year. Still, given that Singapore alone was responsible for $104 billion of revenue in 2025, I am curious about the number; at the time, it was the second-biggest geographic area by revenue, dwarfed only by the US. I can’t tell you how much Singaporean sales matter to Nvidia now, because around the time that Singaporean sales reportedly started to represent half the company’s revenue, Nvidia switched its reporting methodology. The company had previously noted that “most shipments associated with Singapore revenue were to locations other than Singapore and shipments to Singapore were insignificant.” Notably, Megaspeed had a Singapore address — and was the subject of a truncated Commerce Department investigation into whether its chips were being diverted to China. Fun stuff!

You know what else is fun? Jensen Huang’s children are still at the company, at least as far as anyone knows. His daughter Madison, described as Huang’s “chief of staff” by The New York Times, even helped direct Nvidia’s response to inquiries about Megaspeed, which have been mounting now for a year, the Financial Times reported. Her husband, Nico Caprez, “was promoted to a role that covered international deals with cloud computing clients in Asia” around the time those inquiries were getting started. “They had a lot of power and influence and were allowed to operate unchecked,” an anonymous source told the FT. “No one could tell them ‘no’ or put them in their place.”

His kids — one of them, Spencer, ran a bar before his role at Nvidia — likely haven’t endeared themselves to their colleagues, based on some of the reporting now trickling out about them. What was Spencer doing at Nvidia? Unknown. “Some executives said they had never encountered Spencer Huang in the course of business,” The Wall Street Journal wrote.

As a person who loves mess, I think the Branch Nvidians should keep this shit coming

Employing the CEO’s relatives was already a governance risk. If the outside agitation continues to reinstate Jensen Huang, there are three key lambs that Nvidia can slaughter, and all of them are his relatives. I suppose the thing I find most astonishing is that Huang doesn’t see the writing on the wall; he has posted that if “the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO.” The post was met with, among other things, Altman posting a green heart in response.

Now, as a person who loves mess, I think the Branch Nvidians should keep this shit coming. I mean, Huang can nominate two people to the board of directors before the company’s shareholder meeting next year. Let’s have a stupid proxy fight and see what else leaks about Huang’s leadership and children! Of course, that will be difficult for Kress to manage — but that’s I guess what she’s getting paid for.

Up until now, Kress had been trying to rein in Huang’s spending on the AI ecosystem — financing, investments, and so on — in a huge swath of companies, according to stories in The Information and The Wall Street Journal. According to the Journal’s story, Huang noted that the vaunted deals he’d made with suppliers “were coming at him like an ‘avalanche,’” while “major cloud providers were running away from Nvidia.” This does explain Nvidia’s role as the sugar daddy for the neocloud industry. Kress reportedly pushed back on some of its agreements, apparently significantly downsizing a financial commitment to OpenAI among other things. Now, while she’ll be spending plenty of her time managing the fallout from Huang’s departure, she won’t be directly fighting with him over Nvidia’s finances.

It publicly tells us there are limitations on what Nvidia is willing to support

On the earnings call, analyst Stacy Rasgon of Bernstein Research asked about a new paragraph he’d noticed in the earnings release about neoclouds. Nvidia had been supplying guarantees to some neoclouds by saying it would purchase compute if no one else would. The new paragraph reads, in part, “Our commitments take effect only if the neoclouds secure financing to purchase the data center infrastructure products and the related compute capacity is deployed, made operational, and accepted by us.” Kress clarified that this meant that if neoclouds’ buildout was delayed — or they otherwise had a challenge in building the warehouse to stick the chips in — Nvidia’s guarantee “doesn’t exist anymore,” she said. “Is it new? No. Did we change anything? No,” she said.

I have no way of knowing if this language had been ready to go in the press release before Huang resigned, but it does seem notable all the same. It publicly tells us there are limitations on what Nvidia is willing to support. And knowing Kress wanted Huang to spend less on this stuff, I think it tees us up for an interesting period. Passing under the radar last week were some indications that the Federal Reserve may be about to do an interest rate hike, which is trouble for a number of companies, such as CoreWeave, that rely on debt for their data center buildouts. If we are entering a period where financing is more expensive or more difficult to get, we may see some neoclouds falter. The new paragraph in the earnings release suggests that Nvidia wants us all to know that it’s not going to bail them out forever. Bring Huang back if you want, I guess, but even he can’t control the Fed — or the increasingly odd outlook for data center financing. In the coming months, Nvidia may be better off with a woman who wants to keep the costs down.

You know who is getting money under Kress, though? Nvidia staff. They can expect raises and equity in December.

Tesla toughens FSD driver monitoring in Europe while loosening it in the US

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tesla is rolling out stricter driver monitoring for its “Full Self-Driving” system in Europe, with earlier warnings, a full strike for drivers who look away during a manoeuvre, and a hands-on-wheel requirement above 130 km/h (81 mph).

In the US, Tesla has spent the last year going the other way. Its CEO told owners they can text and drive on FSD, and recent US builds were tuned to nag drivers less.

On November 16, the Dutch vehicle authority RDW announced that it signed off on a new update to Tesla Assisted Driving, the name Tesla now uses for FSD (Supervised) in Europe. It covers HW4 vehicles and goes out over the air.

According to the RDW, drivers now get attention warnings earlier. If a driver isn’t paying attention while the system performs a manoeuvre, they get a “full strike.” The regulator says the update also adds measures to prevent misuse of the system, but it didn’t spell out what they are.

There’s a speed component too. Above 130 km/h (where legal), the driver has to keep their hands on the wheel. And setting a maximum speed above what the system displays now requires the driver to confirm it by scrolling the wheel up, with the speed limit sign pulsing red when the car goes over.

The RDW says it tested the update on its own track and on public roads and found no adverse or unintended effects. Ten other countries have adopted the Dutch approval so far, with Finland now on the list, while EU-wide approval is still waiting on a vote at the TCMV, the committee of member states.

This comes two weeks after Tesla cut the system’s speed margin from 50% to 10% over the limit in Europe and dropped the “Full Self-Driving” name there. Germany had made both a condition of supporting EU approval.

Now look at how Tesla handles the same software at home.

At Tesla’s shareholder meeting last November, Musk complained that the car was “a little strict about keeping your eyes on the road” and said Tesla would “allow you to text and drive essentially” within a month or two. A month later, an owner asked whether FSD v14.2.1 allowed texting. Musk’s answer: “Depending on context of surrounding traffic, yes.”

Texting while driving is illegal in 49 states. FSD is still a Level 2 system, and Tesla’s own fine print says the driver is responsible at all times.

In May, Musk confirmed that FSD v14.3.3 was built to nag drivers less. Tesla did add better eye-gaze tracking and eyewear handling in a June build, to be fair. But the pitch to owners hasn’t changed: fewer nags is sold as a feature.

And US drivers have been allowed to go hands-free on FSD at any speed since Tesla moved to cabin-camera monitoring in 2024. There’s no 130 km/h rule here.

NHTSA noticed. In its engineering analysis of FSD crashes in low-visibility conditions, the agency asked Tesla to justify a list of its marketing claims, and it specifically cited Musk’s texting post. In September, Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi asked NHTSA to look into Tesla’s driver monitoring after dozens of videos appeared to show drivers asleep on Autopilot and FSD.

The results are on the road. Last month, a driver told the CHP he was asleep on FSD when his Tesla drove through a freeway closure and hit a patrol car, seriously injuring two officers. Owners are also filming themselves defeating Tesla’s driver monitoring and posting it for clout.

Both markets get the same cabin camera and the same HW4 computer.

But in Europe, a type-approval authority tests every update before it ships and can refuse it. In the US, nobody pre-approves driver-assist software. Tesla self-certifies, and NHTSA only steps in after complaints and crashes pile up, which is how you end up with a CEO publicly telling owners to text while the regulator is still writing letters about it.

This update proves Tesla can build stricter driver monitoring whenever a regulator demands it. The code exists. It’s running on HW4 cars in 11 European countries right now.

That's because Musk is buddies with the current federal government that would regulate his not-full-self-driving system. Whereas Europe doesn't kowtow to his whims. We'll see what happens in 2029 with a new administration.

That makes the US approach hard to defend. Tesla’s argument for relaxing supervision is that FSD is now good enough. But if that were true, it would be showing the RDW data proving looser monitoring is safe, not adding earlier warnings and harsher strikes to get approved. It’s doing the opposite in Europe because it has to, and the opposite of that in the US because it can.

We’ve been saying it for a while: FSD gets more dangerous as it gets better, because people trust it more and watch the road less. The answer is monitoring that tightens as the system improves. Europe is forcing exactly that. In the US, Tesla is loosening it, encouraging illegal phone use from the top, and leaving drivers with 100% of the liability when it goes wrong.

So here’s the question for Tesla. If European owners are getting earlier warnings and instant strikes, why not American owners on the exact same software? Or is NHTSA going to have to force it?

With Kyle Shanahan and Brian Flores, Vikings-49ers will be a matchup for the modern NFL

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

In the long-running anime One Piece, there's an incident at the World Government headquarters, Marineford, known as "The War of the Best." The strongest pirates in the world of One Piece fought the strongest Marines the World Government had to offer, and it ultimately became the most important arc in the One Piece world, shaping the direction of the story that's still being told today.

In the NFL, it feels like we have a game every year that is similar to War of the Best -- two titans facing off against each other, where the outcome will shape the next wave of the NFL on both sides of the ball:

Kyle Shanahan vs. the Legion of Boom in 2016.

Sean McVay vs. Vic Fangio, then Bill Belichick, in that year's Super Bowl.

Andy Reid vs. Lou Anarumo in the second half of the 2022 AFC Championship.

Sean McVay vs. Mike Macdonald last season.

This year, the new NFL meta will be defined Sunday night in Mexico City, where the San Francisco 49ers and Shanahan will face the Minnesota Vikings and defensive coordinator Brian Flores in one of the most anticipated matchups of the year. Both the Niners' offense and Vikings' defense are resetting what their respective sides can and should do, and seeing which side blinks first could be a blueprint for how offenses and defenses attack each other in the near future.

If we recall a story I wrote earlier this season, NFL teams are blitzing at an astronomical rate. Over ten weeks, defenses league-wide have a 32.3% blitz rate, the second-highest since 2010 and one of only four seasons with a blitz rate over 30% (2011, 2013, and 2010). The number jumps even higher when teams go under center to an astounding 50.5%, the highest since 2010 and the only season TruMedia has recorded in that time period with a blitz rate over 50% against under-center offenses. When these defenses blitz, they have a 54.9% defensive success rate overall, and that number drops to 50.1% when offenses are under center.

At the forefront of this are Flores and the Minnesota Vikings, who are redefining what it means to be aggressive. The Vikings currently sit with an absurd 72.6% blitz rate, by far the highest for any team through 10 weeks since 2010. In fact, you have to go all the way back to the 2006 Miami Dolphins to find a team that went higher than that (what was Nick Saban cooking in 2006?). The way that Minnesota can dictate the flow of not only a play, but the entire game through their pressure looks that completely stymie opposing offenses.

The Vikings are essentially playing rock-paper-scissors on every play, daring you to throw anything other than rock. One way they can dictate the flow is by forcing teams to leave extra pass protectors in to help against pressure looks.

Imagine you're Packers quarterback Jordan Love. You come to the line of scrimmage, and this is what you see:

The alarm bells are ringing. This is the most obvious Cover 0 all-out blitz look, eight guys up at the line of scrimmage. The common answer for the Packers and Love has been to keep the RB and TE in the protection, calling it max protection. This is the Packers throwing rock, hoping to catch the Vikings in a Cover 0 look and hit them for an explosive.

The Vikings actually spin everyone out of this except the four defensive linemen and run Cover 3, frazzling Jordan Love before he ends up throwing it 10 yards short of the first-down marker on third down. The Vikings got the Packers to throw rock, then they throw paper.

This ability to force offenses to keep seven people in to protect the QB is unprecedented at the NFL level. According to Sumer Sports, the 2026 Vikings are facing dropbacks where all five eligible receivers are out to catch a pass on 35.7% of their dropbacks, the lowest in the NFL by a significant margin. They're the only team in the NFL below 50% in that metric, with the New York Giants in second place…at 58.5%! Flores and the defense force offenses to play a completely different sport when they play Minnesota, using pressure to dictate the terms of the passing game. They force you to waste backs and tight ends in pass protection, then force you to play with a hand behind your back.

If you want to get four out in the passing concept, good luck, because that doesn't work either.

You're probably wondering, "Yes, the Vikings defense is amazing, but what does that have to do with the 49ers and how this will decide the state of NFL schematics?" The answer lies in the protection plans. The Vikings see the lowest amount of five-out in the passing concept in the NFL. Well, guess which team leads the NFL in percentage of dropbacks with all five eligible receivers out in the passing concept? That's right, Kyle Shanahan and the San Francisco 49ers.

I'd describe the Niners' offense as "malleable." They can use multiple players in multiple ways, keeping their best players on the field while putting linebackers in a blender. They do that by getting all five guys out in the passing concept. If we include chip blocks, the Niners get all five eligibles out at an 80.1% clip, the highest in the NFL (not including chips, that number drops to 60.8%, second in the NFL behind the Pittsburgh Steelers). Against pressure looks where linebackers are mugged, or showing that they're blitzing, the 49ers like to get everyone into the concept and spread teams out. The Niners (and Bengals as well) are zagging when everyone else is zigging, spreading out and getting five eligibles into the concept against the blitz.

With simulated pressures, there's always a law of equivalent exchange: for every guy you add, a guy has to drop. For most defenses to stay structurally intact while showing these pressures in two-high shells, someone has to drop from the defensive line. Shanahan knows this, and knows he has the personnel to live in five-out looks (we'll get to that later). If we're going to the rock, paper, scissors comparison again, the Niners are choosing to throw paper instead of rock, because if they can find the weakest point in the pressure, they'll hammer it.

This 3rd down against the Cowboys is a great example. Dallas lines up in a pressure look that has seven at the line of scrimmage. The Niners see this and have five guys as eligible route runners. Instead of checking into max protection, they stay with what they have, giving RB Christian McCaffrey a choice route on the backside. Cowboys spin out and drop into Cover 2, but CMC has already found the hole, and Brock Purdy finds him to move the chains.

The reason the 49ers can live this way is not only because of Kyle Shanahan, but because of the personnel on the field. LT Trent Williams is a mute button at left tackle. Having McCaffrey also helps in a big way: a guy who can run the offense as well as anyone and run choice routes against zone coverage. But this all comes together because of Brock Purdy and his ability to create out of structure. If the Niners go to five-man protection, and there's someone hot off the edge, Purdy is trusted to make them miss and extend the play. These two coordinators have squared off before as members of the Vikings and Niners with Purdy under center, but I'm almost tempted to throw both games out. In their 2023 game, LT Trent Williams didn't play and it was before players like LBs Blake Cashman and Eric Wilson were added to the Vikings' roster. The 2024 tilt is more accurate, but even then it feels like watching two completely different teams. The Vikings weren't leaning nearly as much into their blitzing (the 2024 Vikings only had a 42% blitz rate, still a league high then but a far cry from where we are right now). Conversely, the Niners' offense feels more mature now than it was in 2024, with Purdy playing at a much higher level (plus, McCaffrey didn't play). It's a testament to how much both coordinators have grown and how their respective teams have grown with them, to become the Charizard version they both are today.

This battle between the two most prominent minds in the sport feels like it's going to set the trends for what teams will look like in the future. Already this season, we've seen teams lean more into seven- and eight-man fronts at the line of scrimmage, trying to force offenses to condense their formations and limit receiving options. The best offenses, however, have decided to spread out and get all five eligibles into the passing skeleton, forcing defenses to show their hand and find eligible playmakers in space.

Two of the most prominent minds at the forefront of this push-and-pull will go to battle in Mexico City. What happens during the game could shape how teams approach offensive and defensive design.

This Supreme Court "Deserves Our Celebration, Not Our Condemnation"

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Prof. William Baude on the Supreme Court and the false promise of court reform.

Professor William Baude has an op-ed in the New York Times, "This Is What an Independent, Competent Supreme Court Looks Like," defending the Supreme Court against its critics, left and right, and suggesting that many calls for court reform are misguided.

Noting that the Democratic electoral sweep has bolstered calls for reforming, and perhaps expanding, the Court to alter its course, Baude writes:

The left, understandably, strongly opposes the court's recent rulings on abortion, the Voting Rights Act, the unitary executive and so on. These decisions are a big deal. But court reformers still suffer from a failure of perspective. If Chief Justice John Roberts could write an opinion essay, I would imagine him saying: "What? Ruling against the administration's misuse of the National Guard, sweeping emergency tariffs and attack on birthright citizenship, plus preserving the independence of the Federal Reserve and the integrity of the midterm elections, wasn't enough for you?"

The court has ruled against the administration in high-stakes cases on those issues all in the past year. And it has done so even though six of the justices were appointed by members of the president's party — three by President Trump himself. . . .

Under the circumstances of the second Trump term, the Supreme Court is doing amazingly well. If anything, it deserves our celebration, not our condemnation.

Indeed, the Court's conservatives, on the whole, have shown themselves more willing to constrain Trump's excesses than were the Court's liberals when faced with executive branch overreach by Presidents Biden or Obama.

Baude notes that the Court's willingness to reject Trump Administration priorities has caused some on the Right to question the conservative legal movement's commitment to originalism, and cautions that "what goes around comes around, and principles will be worth more in the long run." Of course, some conservatives criticisms of the Court are for its failure to adhere to the principles some justices espouse, not that those principles are getting in the way of making America great again.

On the question of judicial reform, Baude strikes a cautionary note, particularly if court reform is nothing more than an effort to reverse the Court's current trajectory.

In the abstract, there is nothing wrong with bipartisan judicial reform. The Supreme Court should not be preserved in amber. But partisan judicial reform — reform motivated by frustration with specific judicial decisions and the hope that the reformed court will rule differently — is a dangerous game. Because partisan reform is a tactic equally available to both sides, it accomplishes little in the long run except to accelerate the court's transformation into a political superweapon. Such a tactic might be a short-run necessity in extreme circumstances, but we are far from those extremes.

He also--quite correctly--calls out the obsession with judicial ethics as "superficial." He could add that such concerns are nearly always one-sided and selective. While it may be appropriate to further limit the justices' outside income or ability to accept gifts, we should not pretend that any such reforms would have any effect on the Court's decisions.

The justices' views on big issues are well formed. The justices themselves aren't for sale; book income reflects their increasing celebrity, not any specific quid pro quo. To be sure, it is quite possible that travel and gifts and book sales subtly reinforce the justices' incentives to play to their respective fan bases. But this kind of corruption is quite indirect, and at the same time comes from so much more than money. So ethics reform is unlikely to change much about what the court actually does.

In the end, Baude thinks the best argument for court reform is as a placebo: To convince the public that something has been done so that attention can turn to more pressing issues. As he concludes this point: "If pointless reforms cause many Americans to believe that the court is better — more independent, less corrupt and so on — that would be great for the country."

I largely agree with Baude's piece, though I may be more open to certain sorts of reforms than he reveals in this piece. Reforms that represent genuine political engagement with the work of the courts, such as measures to alter or channel jurisdiction could be quite beneficial. Measures to reduce backlogs, limit forum shopping, constrain unwarranted or overly expansive equitable relief, and expand the Court's mandatory jurisdiction are all worth considering. They also represent the sort of meaningful legislation that Congress should engage in--legislation that requires Congress to take seriously its constitutional responsibilities. (In this regard, I view the willingness to engage with jurisdictional questions in the pending infrastructure reform bill to be a welcome sign, even if I might not endorse every jurisdictional measure the bill contains.)

As Baude ends his essay, courts are not above criticism, and this includes the Supreme Court. It is nonetheless is important to distinguish between criticisms of how the Court operates and performs its job from substantive or ideological disagreement with the decisions the Court reaches.

CoinShares, Europe’s largest digital asset manager, has sold its entire corporate Ethereum treasury and half its Solana holdings to raise $19.3 million for a share buyback.

The move came with its third-quarter earnings report, as the company looks to return more capital to shareholders.

In a recent announcement, CoinShares confirmed that it sold its entire ETH treasury, valued at $16.6 million. Along with the Ethereum sell-off, it also sold 50% of its SOL holdings worth $2.7 million.

Together, the sales raised $19.3 million, covering nearly half of the $40 million share repurchase program approved by the company’s board.

CEO Jean-Marie Mognetti said the move gives CoinShares more flexibility to act when market conditions allow. The board approved the buyback on October 22 after shareholders authorized the plan at a September 15 meeting.

“We took the same approach to preparing for the share repurchase program.”

The decision means CoinShares is using part of its corporate crypto holdings to support its own stock instead of keeping those assets on its balance sheet.

CoinShares’ decision follows a similar move by other crypto-focused companies that have chosen share buybacks over holding more tokens.

In July 2026, GameSquare Holdings, a media and technology firm, sold 1,209 ETH to help fund its own share repurchase program. CEO Justin Kenna said buying undervalued company shares offered a better return than holding the tokens, leaving the firm with roughly 14,300 to 15,630 ETH.

ETHZilla followed a similar strategy in October 2025, selling about $40 million worth of ETH to repurchase shares trading well below the net asset value of its crypto holdings.

The treasury sale came alongside CoinShares third-quarter earnings for the period ended September 30.

The company reported $39.6 million in net income, compared with $31.2 million a year earlier. Revenue, however, fell 44.9% year over year to $27.7 million.

Assets under management rose to $7.4 billion from $5.5 billion at the end of June, helped by $125 million in quarterly net inflows. Operating income reached $28.4 million, while Segment EBITDA increased to $20.6 million from $4.5 million in the second quarter.

CoinShares’ $16.6 million ETH sale comes as Ethereum has fallen roughly 27% over the past 14 days, from around $2,437 to $1,711.
