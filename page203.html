The Acer Predator Atlas 7 sticks a middle finger up to RAMageddon and strikes the right balance of price, raw performance and efficient software — overcoming a few ergonomic quirks and a smaller battery to become my favorite handheld of 2026.

Handheld gaming has become one of the most exciting, yet terrifying, categories in tech. It feels great to have your PC library with you everywhere, but to get there, you either grab something that’s underpowered and overpriced, or you skip rent for a month to get something actually good.

The Acer Predator Atlas 7 is the rare exception to that rule.

At £900 in the U.K. (not available in the U.S. in this base configuration yet), it is a rather unassuming slab of a handheld that hides a monster inside: an Intel Arc G3 chip paired with 16GB of LPDDR5X memory.

It easily outmuscles more expensive AMD Ryzen Z2 Extreme competitors while dodging massive component price hikes. And while you will have to manage that smaller 60Wh battery and some loud fans, the sheer gaming grunt for that price makes it my undeniable handheld of the year.

7-inch HD (1920 x 1080) LCD 120Hz refresh rate

1x Micro SD, 2x Thunderbolt 4, 1x 3.5mm audio jack

So as I started taking this on solo trips to the pub (yep, I’m that guy) and on my travels all over the place, I started to really see the strengths in the Atlas 7.

The real superpower is what’s inside: the highly efficient Intel Arc G3 equipped with a B370 GPU and 16GB of LPDDR5X memory. Up until this point, I’d only tested the Arc G3 Extreme, so I was a little nervous heading into its smaller, weaker sibling.

The Arc G3 gives you enough breathing room to push your 1080p settings, and sits comfortably above the capabilities of the Ryzen Z2 Extreme in the ROG Xbox Ally X20 — a handheld that’s a third more expensive.

Plus, when you slap Intel’s XeSS and multi-frame generation on top of a solid baseline frame rate, it feels properly zippy and responsive.

On a compact 7-inch screen, you don’t see the ghosting or sizzling artifacts that you would on a bigger desktop monitor with the Arc B580 GPU.

Asus ROG Xbox Ally X (AMD Ryzen Z2 AI Extreme)

There is an anomaly in the Black Myth: Wukong benchmark, but for every other game it was all Intel domination. I think that may be down to optimization for particular silicon.

On top of that, it is also an extremely capable docked system; Star Wars Galactic Racer ran incredibly smooth at 1440p on medium settings with XeSS set to Performance mode.

And don’t get me wrong. You’ll get much more from the Extreme variant, but you’re paying much more to take it up a notch. With the Arc, this much power at this price is right on the money.

Acer nailed the inputs here. They opted for TMR joysticks, which are incredibly smooth and highly reliable since there are no internal gears grinding away. The face buttons offer a satisfying, tactile clickiness, and while the triggers have a decent amount of depth, I do wish they had a slightly more defined flick to catch my fingers.

That being said though, this is a nice lightweight system that sees its mass evenly distributed for level playing.

Windows 11 has finally improved significantly for handhelds. This has been the result of months of work sharpening up the setup process — no more nagging screens asking if you want an Office 365 subscription, just a quick login, some updates and you are ready to play.

For hardware tweaking, that’s where Acer’s PredatorSense overlay comes in. Pressing the top left button triggers it, and while I’d say Asus’ ROG overlay has a slightly nicer UI, PredatorSense puts all the essential customization settings right within a thumb’s reach without overcomplicating things.

But to get something together as a value for money proposition, you have to make some compromises.

The most obvious one is the battery. It’s still sort of decent longevity wise, especially if you get into dropping the TDP to its lowest possible setting and cranking out some indie games. But in our lab test, it’s not great.

This surprised me given the effectiveness of those efficiency cores on the MSI Claw 8 EX AI+ and its Arc G3 Extreme. But no amount of efficient silicon can overcome a smaller cell.

Out of the box, the Atlas 7 shares similar ergonomics with the old ROG Ally and Steam Deck, featuring shallower grips that aren’t as ergonomically sound as the chunkier grips on the Ally X20. The end result is a system you’d lean on a table more than comfortably suspended in the air.

Thermally, the dual-fan cooling system delivers impressive sustained performance, keeping the back of the device nice and cool even at peak output. But the trade-off is noise; those fans do get loud when the system is fully unleashed.

And finally, the 120Hz display has color and is visible indoors, but the brightness isn’t quite there for playing by a window/strong light source. Plus the glossy finish turns that screen into a mirror, though Acer thankfully includes a matte screen protector in the box.

The Acer Predator Atlas 7 leaves me conflicted, but in the best way possible.

For all intents and purposes, this is the best handheld of the year. Yes, there are devices with better displays, chunkier ergonomics and better battery life. But in terms of striking a strong enough mix across all metrics, Acer’s nailed it.

At £900, it is significantly cheaper than the competition it beats, but I also admit that it's hard to call almost a grand “cheap.” That being said, in the midst of a component crisis where competing devices approach the £2,000 mark with worse performance, the Atlas 7 actually makes you feel like you’re getting your money’s worth.

Hyperliquid Open Interest Surges as Perpetuals Rival Large Exchanges

Hyperliquid's derivatives business has grown large enough to challenge some familiar centralised exchange names on a key measure: open interest. A third-quarter research report put positions open on the platform at approximately $16.8 billion at the end of September, a major increase over the previous quarter. For a decentralised trading venue, the scale is striking. But comparing it with conventional exchanges requires more than ranking a single number.

Open interest is the value of contracts that remain outstanding, not the amount of cash a venue has collected in fees or the value of trades settled that day. It reveals participation and leverage, while leaving important questions about market depth and resilience unanswered.

The Block's report on Hyperliquid's Q3 figures described open interest reaching roughly $16.8 billion, nearly 80% above the level recorded at the end of June. The report compared that snapshot with data for exchanges including Bybit, Kraken and OKX, though rankings depend on which products, venues and measurement conventions are included.

The headline number suggests that traders are comfortable maintaining substantial derivative exposure on Hyperliquid. It does not prove that all of that exposure is unhedged directional speculation. Some participants may be market makers or arbitrageurs carrying positions that offset risk elsewhere, while other traders could be using leverage to express very concentrated views.

The same Q3 analysis estimated approximately $191 million in protocol revenue, an increase of about 21.7% from the second quarter but a decrease of roughly 36.5% compared with the year-earlier period. That contrast is a useful warning against assuming that more open contracts automatically translate into stronger year-on-year economics. Revenue depends on trading activity, fees and the mix of products used.

Stock-linked perpetual products were another standout, with reported quarterly trading volume of about $153.8 billion, up approximately 161% sequentially. Yet volume can be affected by incentives, volatility and the churn of high-frequency participants. Healthy trading infrastructure also needs reliable pricing, liquid order books and a credible process for handling extreme market moves.

Perpetual futures let traders maintain leveraged exposure without a fixed expiry date. That flexibility comes with funding payments, margin requirements and liquidation risk. During a sharp market decline, forced closing of positions can magnify volatility. A platform's decentralised components do not eliminate the economic risk of carrying leverage or the operational need for robust safeguards.

This is particularly relevant after the week's crypto sell-off. Market watchers should compare open interest with trading volume, funding costs and liquidation activity rather than treating rising positions as inherently bullish. A platform can be technologically impressive and still host dangerous levels of speculative risk.

The rise of on-chain perpetuals adds to the broader discussion about where exchange activity should take place. NewsBTC's coverage of institutional interest in DeFi through Aave shows that traditional crypto firms are exploring decentralised infrastructure. Meanwhile, Aave's reserve-factor debate illustrates the governance decisions that shape the economics of on-chain services.

Hyperliquid's Q3 performance is evidence that decentralised derivatives can operate at considerable scale. The harder test comes during severe volatility, when users will judge it on execution quality, orderly liquidations and transparent risk management. Those outcomes will say more about the durability of its position than any single exchange league table.

Dystopian Thriller ‘The Flaw’ Is a Cinematic Puzzle About Totalitarianism: “Each One of Us Is a Regime”

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

David Dawson is an interrogator of The Regime, and Arthur McBain is a suspect in a cat-and-mouse game in Theophilos Papastylianos' Tallinn-premiering debut feature, based on a bestselling novel.

In a dark, dystopian future, the Regime decides who belongs and who doesn’t. And the Regime puts the cold and calculating Interrogator on a man who becomes the Suspect to prove his guilt. He puts a ruthless plan of psychological manipulation into action. But could there be a shortcoming in the master plan?

The dystopian thriller The Flaw, a Greece–Latvia co-production, is based on Antonis Samarakis’ novel of the same name, which is a modern Greek classic and bestseller. It is also writer-director Theophilos Papastylianos’ (Algorhythm, Showtime) feature debut, and it is about to get its world premiere, appropriately in the Baltics. The film will debut in the Baltic Film Competition of the 30th edition of Estonia’s Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF) on Wednesday, Nov. 18. British talent leads The Flaw, with David Dawson, who plays Derek Jarman in Elsinore, portraying the Interrogator, and Arthur McBain (After the Flood, Napoleon) playing the Suspect. Also part of the ensemble cast are Ralf Little, Angela Brouskou, Lyne Renée, Sarah Akokhia and Georgia Small. Thodoris Zacharakis is the cinematographer, while Yannis Chalkiadakis is the editor. Produced by Panos Papahadzis and Papastylianos, the co-producers are Alise Rogule and Lina Yannopoulou. Jacques Kruger is the sales agent.

Papastylianos talked to THR ahead of the film’s premiere about just how timely and urgent The Flaw feels, its cast and visuals, and why the film is like one giant puzzle.

How did you approach adapting the novel after you read it?It was my second time reading the book, and it was a revelation for me because the perspective was completely different. I wasn’t reading it as a teenager, like the first time. Now, I was reading it as a director.

The most important part when I wrote the script was to take a step back and say, “Okay, this is an amazing idea, but the film is going to be released in five to seven years. Is it going to be amazing in seven years?”

That said, the film feels scarily timely.

It was instinct because I was seeing how the whole world was progressing and how society was changing. I felt this book was timeless, and as the years go by, it’s going to become more up to date all the time.

The weird thing for me is that, in a sense, I’m happy that the film is up to date. But on the other hand, I’m sad because that means our society and the world are taking a very dramatic shift towards a place that is not good.

Can you talk a bit about the themes of totalitarianism and beyond that The Flaw explores?

For me, the totalitarian regimes and everything is just the first level. I wanted the film to act as a strong allegory. You have the regimes, the governments, or society, that is all around us. But I actually think that each one of us is a regime by himself.

We perceive the world through our own eyes and our own perspective, and through our own prejudice. So, by definition, we are a regime by ourselves. Imagine a hunter walking in the woods, and all of a sudden, he sees a deer. So he lifts up his rifle and looks through the scope. At that moment, he sees prey. If he could just take a moment and lower the scope, he would see a deer again. And I think it’s the same thing with people.

We meet somebody, and we judge them right off the bat – their sexual orientation, [how they look], how political or non-political they are. During COVID, for example, there were two groups of people: the anti-vaccine and the vaccine people, but nothing in the middle. But for me, that’s the interesting thing: people meet in the middle.

The two protagonists in The Flaw go through an evolution, but I didn’t fully notice it until the end…

What we were aiming for was a slow change that the characters go through. We wanted to be so slow that you realize that the characters you’ve been seeing from the beginning, all of a sudden, at the end, are completely different characters.

The film has some spectacular-looking buildings. How did you think about locations?

We shot in Greece, and we also shot in Riga in Latvia. Riga was a very important part of the whole process in order to get that feeling of brutalism and all this dark, timeless sensation.

But then we had to combine Greece with Riga, and that was a challenge, so we could get a timeless space and locations where you don’t know they were actually shot in two different countries. The production design team did an amazing job. And lighting and framing were very important for us. I believe that when we shoot, we make conscious choices that actually affect people’s subconscious.

So every shot is there for a reason. Every close-up is there for a reason. We choose what we want to show or what we don’t want to show the audience.

The Flaw features Greece and Latvia as locations. But the two lead characters are played by British guys. Tell me a bit about the casting decisions.

It was amazing to work with all these guys. They are amazing professionals. I actually sent paintings, portraits, to the casting director. And I said, “Look, this is the energy that I’m looking for as a character.” I used a lot of [Amedeo] Modigliani in terms of the paintings that I sent, because he has this energy and these figures that are surreal, but at the same time, they’re actual people.

My process with the actors once they know the scenes is this. When they ask me what I think about a scene and the characters, I always say to them, “I don’t have anything to say to you. You know the script. You know the scenes. You know your character. Just present your approach.” You need to give actors space so they can breathe on their own.

Tell me a bit about Greek theater and film actress Angela Brouskou and her role of the “Head”?

What we were aiming at was that the Head should not be a natural person but should be on the edge of becoming an idea. She represents the Regime. She is the Regime. So you have a character, but the way she’s depicted, sometimes you think that she is a non-character. It’s like she’s becoming the idea of the regime. She becomes a persona that turns into an idea.

[Warning: This question and answer contains what may be considered a spoiler.]David and Arthur have such a fascinating dynamic and chemistry that keeps shifting. How did you approach their characters’ relationship?

For me there was a key thing. One is the alter ego of the other. So, our big challenge was to [make sure you] see hidden qualities that slowly start coming out more and more. And before the shoot, I showed them the paintings, the portraits, that [I had shared with the casting team]. And I also gave them two pieces of music they could listen to. I truly believe that our work has a lot to do with the energy of the people.

I am glad you mentioned music because that seems to play into the characters and story of The Flaw very strongly…

Yes, I got Dimitris Fessas, the composer, and we worked a lot with him. We wanted to have these rough-edged, train-like pieces. We wanted to use a lot of the electric guitar combined with violins or violas, and then he actually used a traditional Greek instrument called the Cretan lyra. There are actually scenes where the Cretan lyra is having this discussion with the electric guitar. And then a violin steps in, and the whole sensation is a little bit out of this world – in a good sense.

We created the melody of the Interrogator and the melody of the Suspect, but in the end, these two melodies have to interweave. The sound design was also an important [element of that]. We paid so much attention to every detail, especially sound-wise.

Talking to you makes me understand why I experienced The Flaw as pieces of a puzzle slowly emerging and a bigger picture coming into view.

I think that’s the best way to describe it. That’s amazing how you put it. Yeah, the whole film is pieces of a puzzle that have to come together.

Helsing backer Plural raises $1.1B to fund Europe’s next moonshots

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Operator-led VC firm Plural has raised €1 billion ($1.1 billion) in new capital, joining a growing cohort of larger European funds that also includes Mundi Ventures’ Kembara and EQT-managed Scaleup Europe Fund.

Despite its size, Plural’s fundraise isn’t (only) for a growth fund: two thirds will go to its third early-stage fund, and one third to a new “opportunities” fund making select follow-on investments, TechCrunch learned from Plural partners Khaled Helioui and Carina Namih.

The two buckets are fixed, but the check size is not. Plural is “still very committed to being an early stage fund,” Namih said. But Helioui added that some of the early-stage “moonshots” it plans to back may require “raising a couple hundred million” to get off the ground.

“Moonshot” is an appropriate term to describe some of Plural’s portfolio highlights, such as Proxima Fusion and The Exploration Company — and its new fund will be looking for equally promising opportunities in energy, AI infrastructure, defense, health, and more.

Many of these are capital-intensive, but this is no longer stopping European entrepreneurs, especially second-time founders, and companies like Daniel Ek’s Helsing are “not isolated cases,” said Helioui, who backed the startup’s seed round alongside two Plural partners.

“If you’ve built and scaled a company, chances are, you’re more likely to be driven and passionate about having an impact on Europe’s ability to defend itself, procure its own energy, or own its operations and manufacturing, rather than just another app,” he said.

With bigger pockets, Plural will be able to keep up longer with the larger and larger rounds its portfolio companies have gone on to raise. It also made an effort to raise more of this capital from Europe, Namih said.

While Europe’s institutional LPs were, for a long time, more likely to back banker types than founders hoping to displace those bankers, the current shift towards Europe’s sovereignty and resilience creates tailwinds for Plural, whose office in Tallinn played a key role in its decision to invest in defense tech — including in a missile company, Hypersonica.

“The reality is today still, you have a different level of conversation and a different sense of appreciation of the urgency and the criticality of these topics depending on how far from the border to Russia you are,” said Helioui.

Two of Plural’s six partners are Estonians, and Skype alumni: Wise cofounder Taavet Hinrikus and Teleport’s Sten Tamkivi. However, there is more to Plural than Tallinn; the fund also has offices in London, where Helioui, Namih and Ian Hogarth are based; and in Paris, where it hired former Uber executive Pierre-Dimitri Gore-Coty as a full-time partner in October 2025.

According to a press release, each partner will lead a small number of new investments a year. These companies will then get access to Plural’s growing portfolio support, which already ranges from hiring and communications to finance, Helioui said. That’s another indicator that VC firms in Europe are increasingly starting to look like their U.S. peers.

Hertha BSC’s attacking numbers underline impressive start to 2. Bundesliga season

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Berliners have scored 25 goals so far, the joint-third highest total in the division, while their underlying numbers suggest their attacking success is built on more than simply clinical finishing.

According to Bundesliga Match Facts powered by AWS, Hertha have accumulated an expected goals (xGoals) rating of 22.9, the second-highest figure in the division. Only Heidenheim have generated a higher total, highlighting the quality of opportunities the capital club have consistently created.

Their shot efficiency rating of 2.1 also places them sixth in the division. Although several teams have been more clinical in front of goal, Hertha’s combination of regular chance creation and effective finishing has established them as one of the 2. Bundesliga’s most dangerous attacking sides.

One of the encouraging aspects of Hertha’s attacking development has been the contribution of different players throughout the squad, having relied heavily on individuals in years gone by.

Sebastian Grønning has provided an important focal point in the final third, while other members of the squad such as Oluwaseun Adewumi, Josip Brekalo and Mads Pedersen have also demonstrated their ability to contribute in attacking areas.

Rather than depending entirely on one goalscorer, Hertha have demonstrated an ability to find contributions from different areas of the pitch.

Their 5-3 victory over Eintracht Braunschweig provided an excellent example of that attacking depth. Despite falling two goals behind, Hertha responded with an impressive comeback, demonstrating their ability to create opportunities and punish opposition mistakes.

Their 2-1 victory over Wolfsburg before the break was another significant result, with Adewumi and Deyovaisio Zeefuik finding the net in front of a packed Olympiastadion. Against one of their promotion rivals, Hertha showed they could translate their attacking quality into important points.

The relationship between Hertha’s expected goals and actual goals scored is particularly interesting.

Their 22.9 xGoals figure indicates that they have consistently created opportunities worth more than 22 expected goals, while their 25 goals demonstrate that they have also managed to convert those chances effectively.

Although the difference suggests a degree of overperformance in front of goal, the gap is relatively modest compared to the overall volume of opportunities created.

This is important when assessing whether their current position is sustainable. Hertha are not simply benefiting from an unusually clinical spell; their underlying chance creation suggests they have the attacking foundations to remain competitive throughout the campaign.

Ranking second for xGoals, joint-third for goals scored and sixth for shot efficiency, they are performing strongly across all three attacking indicators.

For a club that has endured several frustrating years since dropping out of the Bundesliga in 2023, the current campaign represents a welcome change in fortunes.

Hertha have not only produced the results required to reach the summit but have also developed an attacking profile that compares favourably with the division’s strongest sides.

There is still a considerable amount of football to be played, and maintaining their current standards over an entire campaign will be the real challenge.

However, the numbers provide plenty of encouragement for supporters in the capital. With consistent chance creation, contributions from across the squad and an increasingly convincing attacking record, Hertha have established themselves as genuine contenders for promotion.

Their position at the top of 2. Bundesliga is no longer simply a reflection of a good run of results. The underlying statistics suggest there are solid reasons to believe their resurgence can continue.

Man City: Why the international break could be vital for Maresca

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Axel Clody | 16 Nov 2026 06:59

Manchester City began the season with the impression that adapting to Enzo Maresca’s work would be quick. With seven wins in the opening Premier League rounds, the team took the lead and at one point opened up a six-point advantage over Arsenal. The run, however, was abruptly interrupted.

Defeats to Aston Villa, Brighton and Nottingham Forest dropped the team to third place and allowed the current champions to pull clear at the top.

More than the scorelines, the performances exposed difficulties in competing physically, winning duels and protecting the defence when possession is lost. Maresca himself acknowledged that his team could not keep up with the intensity of the opposition in the first half.

So the November international break comes at a good time. The pause will not erase the problems that have appeared in recent weeks, nor does it mean that all the players will have days off. But it offers the manager a valuable opportunity to assess performance, recover players and work on adjustments that would hardly be possible with a run of matches.

The drop in City’s level cannot be explained only by a lack of efficiency in front of goal.

The team can still control possession and produce offensive volume. Against Forest, for example, it finished the game with 79 per cent possession and 12 shots. The problem is that this control has not been enough to stop opponents finding space and creating dangerous situations.

One of the main issues is defensive protection. City have conceded 17 goals in eight matches and have not kept a clean sheet in any of their last nine games. The high defensive line, a hallmark of Maresca’s teams, demands coordination between the units and a quick ability to react to losing the ball. When the counter-press does not work, the defenders are left exposed to fast attacks and have to defend large spaces.

The absence of Rodri helps to explain part of this difficulty. The Spaniard offered reading of the game, interceptions and the quality to organise the build-up. More than a defensive midfielder who protects the defence, he is a player capable of anticipating danger and stopping counter-attacks before they gather speed. Without him, the rest of the midfield has to share responsibilities that Rodri used to take on almost alone.

Elliot Anderson has been important in this context, but so too is the distribution of roles. The presence of players such as Enzo Fernandez and Rayan Cherki adds technical quality and creativity, but also demands defensive cover. If the two play together without a structure that protects the central area, Anderson can be overburdened in covering and in physical duels.

The break allows Maresca to work on these relationships. One possibility is to adjust the make-up of the midfield, ensuring more balance between creation and protection. Another is to review the team’s positioning without the ball, especially the distance between the defensive line and the most advanced players. It is not necessarily a question of abandoning the style of play, but of finding ways to make it less vulnerable.

The calendar and the physical condition of the squad are another important aspect. City lost rhythm during a demanding period of fixtures, and the recovery of players can help the team regain the intensity needed to execute Maresca’s model.

It will also be important to monitor the situation of Marc Guehi and Josko Gvardiol. Both were substituted at half-time against Nottingham Forest because of physical problems, according to Maresca. The break could be useful to assess the extent of the issues and avoid important players returning before they are fully fit.

There is, however, a caveat: the international break is not a holiday for the whole squad. Several starters have been called up by their national teams, among them Erling Haaland, Gianluigi Donnarumma, Ruben Dias, Gvardiol, Guehi, Enzo Fernandez and Cherki. Travel and international commitments reduce the recovery time for some players, while others will be able to stay in Manchester and work directly with the coaching staff.

Even so, the break offers an opportunity to reorganise the workload and prepare the squad for what follows. In a season in which intensity is essential to the functioning of Maresca’s system, managing physical wear and tear is also part of the recovery process.

The break arrives at a time when City need to recover not only results, but also confidence in their own way of playing. The seven opening wins showed that the team has the resources to compete for the title. The three defeats that followed, on the other hand, revealed that there are still important adjustments to be made to sustain that level over an entire season.

The first challenge after the break will be against Fulham at the Etihad Stadium. Then the team will face an even more significant fixture: the visit to Arsenal on 29 November. The six-point gap makes the direct meeting an important opportunity to close the distance, although the result will not be enough, by itself, to settle the title race.

For Maresca, the international break is a chance to find answers before the run of negative results turns into an even bigger problem. The manager needs to restore defensive solidity, find the best distribution of roles in midfield and make possession dominance translate into effective control of matches again.

City do not need to abandon their identity to react. Above all, they need to find a better balance between what they do with the ball and what they can do when they lose it. If the break allows Maresca to progress with this work and recover important players, the team could return better prepared to face a decisive stretch of the season.
