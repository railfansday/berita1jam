BA’s Best Pecan Pie

📅 Nov 12, 2016  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Our classic pecan pie recipe has no chocolate chips, no booze, and no hard-to-find ingredients. Instead of focusing on embellishments, we focused on what matters most: the deep flavor of toasted pecans, a caramely custard, and a buttery, flaky pie crust.

Brown sugar and a spoonful of dark molasses add depth to the filling, while toasting the pecans before incorporating them makes their flavor stand out and their texture more robust. Blind-baking the crust—baking it before adding the filling—helps keep the bottom crisp beneath the gooey custard.

When the pie comes out of the oven, the edges should be set and slightly puffed, but the center should still wobble like Jell-O. It will continue to set as it cools, so resist slicing too soon. Serve it all on its own, or gild it with vanilla ice cream or whipped cream.

Can I make this pecan pie without corn syrup?

Yes. Corn syrup gives this classic pie its perfectly gooey texture, but there are alternatives. Brown rice syrup will add a toasty flavor and can be used in equal parts. Golden syrup, honey, and maple syrup are all too sweet and will change the pie's flavor and texture.

For a pie developed without any syrup, try our Bourbon-Date Pecan Pie.

Can I use chopped pecans instead of halves?

Yes. We love the crunch you get from pecan halves, but if you’re a chopped pecan person, use about 225 g in place of the 2 cups of halves. Watch closely when toasting; chopped pecans will need less time in the oven.

Pulse 1 Tbsp. sugar, 1½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt, and 2 cups all-purpose flour in a food processor until combined. Add 5 Tbsp. chilled unsalted butter, cut into pieces, and 3 Tbsp. chilled lard or vegetable shortening and pulse until mixture resembles coarse meal with a few pieces of butter and lard visible, about fifteen 1-second pulses. With motor running, drizzle in 1 Tbsp. apple cider vinegar and 5½ Tbsp. ice water and pulse until dough is still crumbly but just holds together when squeezed (add 1 tsp. water at a time if necessary, but be careful not to overwork dough).

Turn out dough onto a work surface. Knead 1–2 times, pressing just to incorporate any shaggy pieces. Flatten into a 6"-wide disk. Wrap with plastic wrap and chill at least 1 hour.

Preheat oven to 425°. Roll out dough on a lightly floured work surface to a 14" round. Transfer to a 9" pie pan. Pick up edges and allow dough to slump down into dish, letting excess dough hang over dish. Trim, leaving about a 1" overhang. Fold overhang under; pinch and crimp. Chill 30 minutes.

Line dough with parchment paper or foil, leaving some overhang. Fill with pie weights or dried beans. Bake until crust is dry around the edges and just beginning to lightly brown, 25–35 minutes. Carefully remove parchment and weights and reduce oven temperature to 350°. Bake until crust is set and beginning to brown in the center, 10–15 minutes. Transfer to a wire rack and let cool.

Do ahead: Dough can be made 3 days ahead; keep chilled, or freeze up to 2 months. Crust can be baked 1 day ahead; tightly wrap and store at room temperature.

Preheat oven to 350°. Toast 2 cups (225 g) pecan halves on a rimmed baking sheet until browned and fragrant, 8–10 minutes. Let cool.

Reduce oven temperature to 325°. Whisk 4 large eggs, room temperature, beaten to blend, 1 cup light corn syrup, ⅔ cup (packed; 134 g) light brown sugar, 1 Tbsp. robust-flavored (dark) molasses, 1 Tbsp. vanilla extract, and 1 tsp. Diamond Crystal or ½ tsp. Morton kosher salt in a large bowl until smooth. Slowly whisk in 6 Tbsp. unsalted butter, melted, slightly cooled, until combined. Fold in pecans and pour into cooled crust.

Bake pie until edges are set and slightly puffed but center is recessed and wobbles like Jell-O, 55–70 minutes (it will continue to set after baking). Transfer to a wire rack and let cool at least 3 hours before slicing. Serve with vanilla ice cream.

Do ahead: Pie can be baked 1 day ahead; tightly wrap and chill. Serve warm or at room temperature.

31 Thanksgiving Dinner Alternatives to Turkey

📅 Nov 18, 2014  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Honestly, anything can make for an alternative Thanksgiving dinner. It’s your house, your rules. Our only suggestion: Make it festive, fun, and at least a little decadent.

A classic Thanksgiving menu is all well and good, but one of our colleagues swears by serving nachos on the fourth Thursday of every November. If a big bloomin’ bird just isn’t ca-caw-caw-calling your name this year, serve a whole roasted fish, pork tenderloin with sour-sweet cherry sauce, or a flaky carrot Wellington instead.

Or go completely rogue with lobster pasta or a big pot of chili. You can do that. Below, find vegetarian centerpieces, celebratory roasts, chicken dinners, and seafood mains for a Thanksgiving that looks a little different. BA’s Best Pumpkin Pie can stay.

Can carrots really count as a main course? They can when they’re roasted, glazed, and tucked into puff pastry with goat cheese, Gruyère, and walnuts. This Wellington feeds eight to ten.

Red wine, browned mushrooms, pearl onions, and carrots give this meatless braise plenty of heft. Ladle it over mashed potatoes for a vegetarian anchor to your Thanksgiving menu.

Ruffled savoy cabbage, melty cheese, and a buttery cream cheese crust make this free-form tart worthy of the holiday table. Cut generous wedges for dinner, and stick with savoy: Ordinary green cabbage won’t work here.

A make-ahead casserole, like this pumpkin-infused strata scented with nutmeg and herbs and balanced by sharp cheddar, is just what your holiday schedule needs. Assemble it the night before; bake it on Thanksgiving.

Turkey isn’t the only holiday cooking project worth clearing the counter for. Delicate homemade crepes are rolled around ricotta, mozzarella, and Parmesan, then baked in marinara. Make the batter and filling a day ahead to split up the work.

Kale, pinto beans, and charred poblanos make these cheesy enchiladas a satisfying holiday main for four to six. Get a head start by making the charred salsa verde up to three days ahead.

This autumnal risotto works as a standalone dinner or alongside your favorite Thanksgiving sides. Set the table with shallow bowls and pick a fall salad full of color and crunch to balance the buttery rice.

British beef Wellington meets Cantonese barbecue-style pork in Peter Som’s pastry-wrapped centerpiece. A shiitake mushroom filling and gravy made from the marinade complete the picture; assemble the Wellington a day ahead.

These impossibly tender short ribs are begging to be served with mashed potatoes and buttery milk buns with garlic butter. Give them what they want—and give yourself a head start by braising the ribs the day before Thanksgiving.

Brisket more your speed? Try our Brisket Bourguignon →

Turkey skin has competition. Tender pork belly, crisp crackling, and a hard-cider pan sauce make a persuasive case for skipping the bird. Pass the chile vinegar to cut through the richness; this roast feeds eight to twelve.

Ayesha Curry’s spice-crusted prime rib takes its cues from her mother’s braised oxtails, with habanero crema on the side. Prefer a simpler garlic-and-rosemary approach? Try this Low-and-Slow Rib Roast.

For a roast-and-sides Thanksgiving without the turkey, garlic-and-rosemary-rubbed beef tenderloin makes an excellent centerpiece. Rosemary butter dresses the sliced roast, while tangy horseradish yogurt gives everyone something to swipe their next bite through.

Toasted rice, fried onions, and garlic form a dark, peppery crust on this slow-braised pork shoulder. Set out the big platter and pass the coconut-vinegar pan sauce; this roast feeds eight to twelve.

Maybe save the brown sugar-glazed ham for Christmas? This whole leg of lamb makes for a centerpiece that’s just as showstopping. And, like ham and turkey, leftover lamb can be deliciously repurposed in sandwiches.

A platter of sliced pork tenderloin with glossy cherries and browned shallots has plenty of holiday presence without a long roast. Honey and red wine vinegar give the sauce its sweet-sour bite, and frozen cherries are welcome here.

If you’re going to make tamales, it’s a good idea to make a lot of them. Better yet, turn the process into a party, with everyone spreading masa and folding corn husks around the red-chile pork filling.

This pasta packs a whole spectrum of holiday flavors into each bite, with crumbles of Italian sausage, shreds of brussels sprouts, and toasted pecans. Streamlining dinner into one dish leaves you more time for Thanksgiving pies.

For some people, spending all Thanksgiving in the kitchen sounds like a good time. For everyone else, there’s this beef-and-bean chili. A can of pumpkin purée thickens the broth, keeping things seasonal without calling for a single roasting pan.

Give homemade fried chicken an even more festive angle with sage and thyme in the dredge and cranberry-mustard sauce for dipping. Start the buttermilk marinade the day before.

This whole roast duck comes with a built-in Thanksgiving side: The duck fat drippings go into basmati rice that cooks while the bird rests. Plan for an overnight dry brine before the glossy pomegranate-honey glaze gets its moment.

Cooking for a smaller crowd? This skillet delivers the stuffing and the bird—chicken thighs, in this case—in one tidy package. Rendered chicken fat enriches the boxed stuffing mix, with dried cranberries and lemon zest brightening things up.

Two diminutive birds make a festive roast for four. A fiery guajillo chile, ginger, and vinegar marinade flavors both the hens and the potatoes beneath them, so the platter arrives with its own very good side.

You just need to make green beans, because this Thanksgiving main is already a two-in-one: Thinly sliced potatoes cook beneath a whole chicken, catching its drippings and crisping around the edges in the same cast-iron skillet.

If your Thanksgiving table could use a little red sauce (and spice), skip the whole bird in favor of this take on chicken parm. Gochujang and gochugaru bring the heat; melted mozzarella keeps things gloriously gooey.

José Andrés turns chicken wings and a colorful assortment of vegetables into a grand centerpiece, with saffron and smoked paprika flavoring the rice. You’ll need a 15-inch paella pan or comparably sized two-handled cast-iron or cast-aluminum pan.

There’s nothing like pasta to stretch a spendy ingredient (hello, lobster). Serve with a big crunchy salad with Italian dressing and garlic confit toast that diners can pass around for Thanksgiving party vibes.

Presenting a whole fish at the holiday table is wildly celebratory, even when you’re cooking for just two or three. Bring out lettuce wraps, kimchi, rice, and ssamjang so everyone can build each bite a little differently.

A large roasted salmon fillet looks right at home on a holiday platter, especially under a crunchy, garlicky nut salsa. Make the salsa up to four days ahead, then roast the fish when it’s time for dinner.

The stuffing doesn’t have to be a side. Here, crushed boxed stuffing mix joins Parmesan, lemon zest, and parsley in a crisp crust for mustard-coated white fish. Dinner lands on the table in 25 minutes.

This dark, glossy coconut caramel—savory with fish sauce and garlic, sharp with pickled jalapeño brine—turns pan-seared salmon into a holiday-worthy main. Serve with rice to catch every last bit of sauce.

Like many of the best Thanksgiving dishes, this one starts with a good stock. Carla Hall builds a fennel potlikker with celery, onions, and Pernod before layering those flavors into a hearty bowl of shrimp and grits.
