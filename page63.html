Middlesbrough's £20m Will Lankshear gamble has a hidden upside

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Middlesbrough have struck gold with their Will Lankshear signing from Tottenham Hotspur, with the young striker already looking like one of the Championship's outstanding acquisitions.

It is only early November, yet the 21-year-old has scored 13 league goals and 15 in all competitions for Kim Hellberg's side. That is five more than any other Championship player this season already.

In fact, only seven players across the entire 2025/26 campaign managed to outscore his current league tally. Middlesbrough's investment is already looking inspired, but there is an interesting financial detail behind the deal to consider.

Lankshear's spectacular brace against Charlton Athletic provided another reminder of just how special a finisher Middlesbrough have acquired. Both goals were excellent left-footed efforts, although the second was arguably the finest of his professional career to date.

He is making an extraordinary impact at the Riverside Stadium, and the reported £10 million initial fee, as per BBC Sport, is already looking like outstanding business. However, the widely reported £20 million headline figure includes approximately £10 million in potential add-ons, meaning Boro are not necessarily committed to paying the full amount.

If they do, it will be a club-record deal. The exact conditions of that have not been publicly confirmed yet, but they could — and indeed are likely to include — appearances, goals, assists, promotion from the Championship, and potentially even international recognition, such as an England call-up.

There is often a downside to these arrangements, with clubs sometimes finding themselves committed to expensive conditional payments or future obligations that eat into subsequent transfer budgets. That's an inherently risky strategy for any club, let alone ones without parachute payments aiding them. Yet Lankshear could be a very different proposition for Boro.

If Middlesbrough are paying those additional sums because he has helped them achieve major sporting objectives, particularly promotion, then the extra money will hardly represent a costly mistake. It should actually feel like a drop in the ocean to them on current trajectory.

In fact, a young English striker who fires Boro into the Premier League would suddenly be worth considerably more than £20 million, making those add-ons a relatively small price to pay for an extraordinary investment. Spurs are protected somewhat by matching rights for Lankshear, but while hindsight is a wonderful thing, it's already looking like a far better deal for Boro than Tottenham as time goes by.

Lankshear's may be killing it for Middlesbrough but his development has not been entirely straightforward, with his first Championship loan at West Bromwich Albion failing to deliver the impact he would have wanted in January 2025 for half a season.

However, his subsequent spell at Oxford United was considerably more productive, scoring 12 goals despite playing for a side that created relatively little and ultimately suffered relegation to League One back in May.

If he maintains this form and stays fit, he could even start to eye up and begin dreaming of challenging Aleksandar Mitrovic's extraordinary Championship record of 43 goals in a single season for Fulham. That remains a monumental target, but his start has been outstanding.

There is also a potentially significant international dimension to Lankshear's development. He has the profile to become a serious England contender, with the long-term ambition of eventually succeeding Harry Kane as the national team's leading centre-forward.

There are not many players worthy of competing with Kane, but Lankshear is on the right path. The parallels are interesting, too, given Kane's own progression through a series of EFL loan spells before establishing himself at Tottenham.

The difference is that Spurs have now cashed in on Lankshear rather than sending him out on another temporary deal. His finishing repertoire is already impressive, having scored regularly with both feet and his head this season. He is becoming a remarkably complete penalty-box striker.

Spurs correspondent and journalist Alasdair Gold has even hinted at the Kane-esque comparison recently after seeing Lankshear's strike against the Addicks. It's fair to say that if Lankshear continues on this trajectory, Tottenham could eventually find themselves collecting those add-ons through his England progress as well as the aforementioned club achievements.

The OG sci-fi browser game is still going strong.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

If you were on the Internet in the early 2000s looking for free browser games to play, OGame was a blooming sci-fi strategy sensation that couldn't be ignored. With online servers providing the structure for a living and evolving universe where players fought, traded, and created empires from scratch, its limited visual presentation was no issue.

Times have changed, but GameForge hasn't gone anywhere. OGame has been ported to mobile devices, and content updates have kept it alive and fresh for much longer than a plethora of installable MMO games.

With the Orion update, which will hit the newly launched universes (servers) on November 6, OGame is introducing conflicts against non-player-character (NPC) enemies, wave-based battles, co-op missions, and completely new systems that can also be disrupted by other players.

The game's endurance and such a major reinvention are stunning achievements, so we asked GameForge's Martin Liedtke (Director, Products of Browser Games) and Enrico Mareth (Senior Project Manager for OGame PC and Mobile) about the many challenges of working on OGame alongside the community for so long, what Orion is bringing to the table, and what's next for this worldwide phenomenon.

Some quotes have been editorialized and lightly trimmed for better flow.

"OGame has an enduring core: You build an interstellar empire, and your decisions matter... Keeping it fresh means adding meaningful choices to that foundation," said Liedtke. "Orion adds missions against automated opponents (PvE) alongside missions open to other players (PvP)... It supports different appetites for risk."

The GameForge veteran noted, however, that it's the community that "supplies another kind of freshness" after the studio releases the new content. "We develop the systems; players create much of the history."

Part of this approach is keeping tabs on how very different kinds of players approach what's available. "Some commanders concentrate on their economy; others enjoy expeditions or carefully coordinated fleet operations... Orion follows that approach; more experienced players can dig into fleet composition, progression, and cooperation. The aim is to offer worthwhile choices."

Liedtke also noted that "OGame's PvP has become a sophisticated contest of information and coordination" over time. Some players like to scout targets and organize combined attacks when enemy fleets are vulnerable. Those battles resolve automatically, but it's the planning that "can be remarkably intricate," especially after so many years of updates.

The PvE in Orion doesn't sacrifice any of those intricacies or density. In fact, it's adding a "more structured layer" that gives players clear objectives. In most cases, they'll have to cooperate to obtain better rewards. "There is a strong case for introducing that kind of cooperative PvE earlier. It gives players another reason to build and use fleets together."

Over two decades later, how people choose to engage with OGame has changed, pushing GameForge to consider supporting new platforms. Today, mobile devices have become essential to free-to-play games' success over time.

Mareth told us their importance "has made touch controls, navigation, and clarity on smaller screens central development considerations," even if thousands of players still remain attached to PC as their main platform. "We're currently working on a design overhaul of the app, combining what we've learned with players' feedback."

Liedtke also said there is more overlap between PC and mobile players than you might imagine.

"The same player might check their empire on a phone and spend a longer session planning in the browser." Liedtke also noted that "Different playstyles also have different needs," adding that activities like mining and trading fit "comfortably" into short mobile sessions, but complex fleet operations benefit from sitting in front of one's PC.

While it's a game of interstellar conflict and sci-fi spaceships, OGame does take a lot of inspiration from real-life science, even if it stretches that science well past its limits. "When ships are destroyed in battle, the debris field can form a moon around a player's planet," explains Liedtke. "The chance (of that happening) is linked to the real lunar cycle: it is higher around a full moon than a new moon."

He noted it's a "playful connection" to astronomy, "rather than a simulation of how real moons form," but the developers thought it was cool to influence the game via things that happen in our own sky.

This possible in-game "event" also led to one of the best examples of player ingenuity in OGame: Moonshots. Liedtke explained that "players organize deliberate battles and exchange sacrificial fleets to help one another obtain those valuable moons. They have turned destruction into a cooperative project. For a designer, the lesson is to examine what players can achieve together, as well as what an individual action rewards."

As the team figured out how to develop "the more imaginative side of science fiction" in OGame's universe, they also decided to explicitly use deuterium for fusion research and as fuel. Star Trek fans will recognize this as the starship fuel of that universe, but it is a real substance: one of two stable isotopes of hydrogen. "Those scientific references give the universe familiar foundations," remarks Liedtke.

In OGame's shared galaxy, Orion is introducing "anomalies" as a central new feature, which Liedtke described as "mission sites you uncover with a new scanner" before it's required to send in ships. "Combat missions ask you to hold position against waves of automated fleets; peaceful missions put your cargo ships to work collecting rewards." The twist is these missions are open to co-op... but also to rival commanders that can attack the objectives marked as PvP. While you can theoretically ignore them, better rewards await for daring commanders.

With PvE bringing all sorts of new design opportunities to the table, Liedtke pointed out the Orion update "gives us a foundation to learn which kinds of missions players find rewarding" as GameForge looks to the strategy MMO's future. "Personally, I would like to explore encounters that make commanders reconsider what to send and how to work together. Discovery becomes more interesting when it changes your plan."

Sounds exciting, though Liedtke quickly added that this isn't an official announcement of future features.

OGame's 25th anniversary is in 2027, and while the team is tight-lipped on what the team has planned, Liedtke noted that "Reaching a quarter of a century in 2027 will be an extraordinary moment for OGame. In both worlds, legal compliance can restrict access, yet the remedy process and transparency of decisions matter just as much as the initial intervention.

Tether said on November 2 that it froze more than $1.5 billion in USDT linked to the late-October Binance attack, working with investigators and industry partners. The company said it moved quickly after receiving actionable intelligence. The claim describes issuer-enforced restrictions on specified token balances; a freeze is not the same thing as completed asset recovery or money already returned to victims. The dated original disclosure is the starting point for the figures and product scope discussed here.

Centralised stablecoins can include smart-contract functions that allow the issuer to block transfers by designated addresses. This gives law-enforcement partners a tool that does not exist in the same form for native Bitcoin. But it also makes a particular promise implicit in the product: the ability to transact depends in part on an issuer retaining authority over the transfer rules.

The case illustrates a genuine trade-off. The power to immobilise suspected stolen funds may increase the chance that investigators preserve value while they work. The same central control raises questions about mistaken identification, appeals, transparency and the geographic reach of private restrictions. Stablecoins are often used because they feel like simple digital dollars, yet their governance can be as consequential as their transaction speed.

The phrase “digital dollar” can obscure important differences between products. A stablecoin holder faces an issuer and its reserve arrangements; an exchange user faces trading and custody arrangements; a merchant faces conversion and settlement partners. A good assessment follows that chain of obligations instead of assuming a one-dollar target eliminates counterparty risk.

Stablecoin adoption depends heavily on ordinary financial services at the edges. Funding accounts, meeting identity rules, accessing banking partners and redeeming balances remain necessary even if a token transfer settles quickly. A project that solves those steps can be useful, but that usefulness cannot be established from headline issuance or a partnership announcement alone.

Watch for validated recoveries, legally authorised unfreezing or transfer procedures and clearer accounting of overlapping industry freeze figures. The longer-term issue is whether issuers can develop fast, reviewable processes that protect innocent users while meeting legitimate law-enforcement demands. A successful freeze is the start of an asset-preservation story, not necessarily its final chapter.

We’ve worked with a ton of different freelance writers at Kotaku over the years. Maybe you want to be one of them! Here’s what you need to know before pitching us stories.

The good ones. Especially if you have a unique angle or access to provide our readers with exclusive insights, information, or high-quality writing about something they never knew they wanted to read about before. This includes, but is not limited to, feature stories, opinion pieces, essays, reported scoops, sweeping investigations, and fascinating interviews.

Most of the pitches we get are for reviews and previews. These are also the easiest things to say no to because everyone else will be publishing them. What Kotaku is most interested in when it comes to freelance pitches are original stories that readers won’t be able to find anywhere else because of a unique angle you’ve crafted, insight you’ve unearthed, experience you’ve had, or information you’ve obtained.

This could be an interview with a developer that surfaces a fascinating insight about a game they worked on a decade ago. It could be a bizarre but intriguing drama that played out in a video game Discord community last month. It could be insights from a studio about why a troubled project still hasn’t shipped or a profile of a group of modders attempting something incredible in one of their favorite games.

The best stories inform and entertain. They have characters, conflict, and tension. Some stories have exciting conclusions. Others remain unresolved and are richer because of it. You don’t have to be pitching the next Pulitzer Prize-winning article to be thinking about these types of things.

Send us your idea for what you want to write with a projected word count and timeline for when it would be filed. Suggest a sample headline and explain what makes the story interesting, why we have to have it, and why there’s no version of it that we’ll be able to find online within five minutes of Googling for it. This pitch should be brief, punchy, and as exciting as the final version of the article you’re going to turn in.

Please also share a link to relevant past work and any public profile you maintain. Why I'd Choose the Oura Ring 5 Over the Cheaper Ultrahuman Pro

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ultrahuman’s Ring Pro is now officially on sale in the U.S., which makes this the perfect time to consider how this new device compares to the reigning champ in the smart ring market, the Oura Ring 5. Here's the short answer: While there’s a lot to like in the Ultrahuman Ring Pro, ultimately I think the Oura Ring 5 is just better at doing its job.

I’ve spent plenty of time with both rings, and admittedly, many of the differences between the two will come down to personal preference—for example, do you like the biohacking concept and the “next best actions” suggestions in the Ultrahuman app, or do you favor the soothing vibes of the Oura app? But when it comes to the devices themselves, there are big differences between them in terms of size, cost, and overall ease of use. Here’s my take on each, and why Oura is still my top choice.

The Oura Ring 5 is impressively small, and when you have to wear something on your finger, size matters a lot—and generally, smaller is better. The Oura Ring 5 is the smallest and most comfortable smart ring I’ve ever worn. I have a ton of photos here comparing it in size to other smart rings (including RingConn, older Oura rings, and an older Ultrahuman); suffice it to say, the Oura Ring 5 wins this category easily.

When it comes to the interior fit, both rings are similar: mostly smooth on the inside, without any intrusive bumps. But considering the overall shape and feel, the Oura Ring 5 is just more comfortable to wear throughout an entire day.

The Oura Ring 5 comes with a plug-in charger, which isn’t very convenient, but will get the job done. You can buy a slim, rounded charging case that easily fits in your pocket for $99. You need to match the size of the case to the size of your ring, and the charging case is not compatible with earlier versions of the device. It’s good for 30 days of charge, according to Oura.

The Ultrahuman Ring Pro comes with a charging case that is heavy and bulky with prominent corners, but good for an extra 45 days of battery life. The case fits any size of Ultrahuman Ring Pro, but is not compatible with previous versions. You can have the case play sounds if you’ve misplaced it.

The Ultrahuman ring lasts slightly longer on a charge: eight to 10 days, according to the company, versus six to nine for the Oura Ring 5. The Ultrahuman ring also has several different battery modes, with “chill mode” lasting me about two weeks.

To sum up: the Ultrahuman ring lasts longer on a charge and has more capacity in its charging case. You also get the charging case for free. But the Oura Ring 5’s charging case is smaller and much easier to carry around. Once again, we’re looking at two decent options: The Oura case is more expensive for what you get, but it’s also nicer to use.

Oura is the most expensive smart ring out there, and it requires a subscription. That makes it the priciest option, giving Ultrahuman an opening to beat it simply by virtue of being cheaper. But this one is closer to a wash, depending on the choices you make. If you’re looking for the cheapest possible smart ring, well, you’d probably end up with something else entirely, like a RingConn Air. But between the Oura Ring 5 and the Ultrahuman Ring Pro, the latter is cheaper than the Oura Ring 5...but only if you’re saying no to all the extras. Let me break it down.

The Oura Ring 5 costs $399 for the shiny black and silver models, and $499 for gold, rose, and the matte versions of the black and silver. The subscription costs $5.99/month (or $69.99/year, which is only a $2 savings), and the Oura is basically useless without the subscription.

Here's how the cost works out on an annual basis:

Oura Ring 5 in black or silver: $399 + $5.99/month subscription = $470.88 for one year, $542.76 for two years

Oura Ring 5 in other colors: $499 + $5.99/month subscription = $570.88 for one year, $642.76 for two years.

The Ultrahuman Ring Pro is $479 for all colors, and there is no subscription for basic use of the ring. But you can't necessarily stop there, because the Ultrahuman app includes “PowerPlugs” that will give you extra features or analysis screens. Some are totally free, including the caffeine window,  smart alarms, a new parent mode, and a limited version of the cycle and ovulation tracker, but others carry a monthly fee. And if you consider all the optional subscription fees for different PowerPlugs, they can quickly add up:

HealthEx, to connect medical records: $4.99/month

Most people probably wouldn't choose all of them, but even if you just wanted, say, respiratory health and cycle & ovulation pro, you’d quickly outstrip the price of an Oura Ring’s subscription. On the other hand, Ultrahuman also offers an Ultrahuman Max plan for $9.99/month or $99.99/year that includes all the PowerPlugs mentioned above, plus the Les Mills workouts that normally run $11.99/month. That's cheaper than subscribing to all of the options individually, but it will also make the Ultrahuman Ring Pro more expensive than the Oura Ring 5: $578.99 total for one year, or $678.98 for two years. In short, if you want everything that each ring has to offer, the Ultrahuman will cost you more.

Here's the big caveat: I wouldn't recommend bothering with most of the PowerPlugs. For example, "UltraSync for Tesla" recommends settings for your car based on how rested you are, which will be useless if you don't have a Tesla, and probably even if you do. "Cardio adaptability" measures a metric that Ultrahuman says is subtly different from HRV; Oura doesn't measure this adaptability metric, but it's not clear to me whether it's even worth measuring at all (I'd argue probably not).

Ultrahuman's respiratory health PowerPlug ($3.99/month) records sounds while you sleep, which Oura doesn't do; but Oura reports breathing disturbances based on the ring's data, which Ultrahuman doesn't do. Ultrahuman's feature is more expensive.

Ultrahuman offers a premium version of its cycle tracker for $3.99/month, while Oura has a partnership with Natural Cycles that cost $149.99/year. Either will provide more detailed (and, each company says, more accurate) cycle tracking than the basic tracking each app provides without the extras. Oura's option is more expensive.

Ultrahuman's HealthEx Powerplug ($4.99/month) displays information from your medical records throughout the app; Oura lets you import your medical records for free, but uses them mainly in the Advisor (chatbot) and dedicated areas of the app. Ultrahuman's option is more expensive.

Oura's app gives you scores for your readiness, sleep, and recent activity, and it tracks how stressed you seem to be throughout the day. A message at the top of the main screen gives you advice for what to do that day—for example, scaling back your workouts if you seem to be tired or stressed. Ultrahuman does pretty much the same thing, giving you a "next best action" that changes throughout the day, but tends to stick to predictable themes: get more activity if you've been sitting, and that sort of thing.

Both give detailed breakdowns of the factors that contribute to your scores, and both likely overstate how useful their scores are (you don't actually need to change your workout plans just because you had a less-than-perfect night of sleep). But the apps handle the basics pretty similarly: Both have partners and integrations, and you can book blood tests through either app, if you want to do that for some reason.

Ultrahuman gives you a bit more guidance on circadian rhythms, urging you to get in extra steps during certain times of day, and offering to micromanage your caffeine intake. It has a "longevity" tab that puts your long-term habits in terms of how many years they are supposedly subtracting from (or adding to) your lifespan, including measurable metrics like VO2max and a few things that are more inscrutable, like arterial stiffness.

Oura has a whole tab of "vitals" that it calculates from your day-to-day data, which I find more useful than Ultrahuman's data views. The items here include "resilience," which compares your stress to your recovery; a "symptom radar" that can sometimes flag when you're sick; and metrics of sleep regularity and how you're breathing. And, of course, you can check your "cardiovascular age" to see how much younger (or older) you should feel.

Overall, I prefer Oura's approach because the metrics make more sense, are easier to find, and are less likely to feel like they're going way out on a limb relative to the underlying science. Ultrahuman wants you to feel like a cutting-edge biohacker; Oura does a bit more to keep things down to earth. It is a bit funny to remember that both apps, and their wealth of data, are basically just pulling from heart rate readings throughout the day and night. Neither app gives you any truly important information the other doesn't, but Oura's makes more sense to me.

Hinge is making a surprising change to its subscriptions. Here’s who stands to benefit

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Hinge users may soon have access to a more affordable subscription tier. The app’s parent company, Match Group, announced Monday it is testing a new feature geared toward women.

During its third-quarter 2026 earnings call, Match Group said the new tier will give users more control through additional filters and custom location boundaries.

“It’s designed for women in particular, but also for price-sensitive users,” Steve Bailey, chief financial officer of Match Group, said during the call. 

Hinge currently offers three tiers: a free version with limits on filters and daily likes, and the more customizable Hinge+ and HingeX, priced at  around $89.99 and $99.99 for three months, respectively. 

Tinder offers similar subscription tiers, with Tinder Plus and Tinder Gold offering perks like unlimited swipes and top picks for around $25 and $35 a month respectively.

“Hinge is one of the highest-priced products we have. The revenue per payer, for example, at Hinge is almost twice that of Tinder,” Bailey added. “It also has one of the lower payer penetrations. Its payer penetration is only slightly more than half that of Tinder.”

The company did not respond to Fast Company’s request for additional details about the new lower-cost subscription tier. 

Hinge’s new, lower-priced offering comes as consumers grow increasingly frustrated with the dating ecosystem. According to a February 2026 Harris Poll, 72% of Americans feel that romance is a transaction due to dating apps, while 77% feeling like dating has become a marketplace.

But despite the pushback on dating apps, Hinge’s monthly active users are up 12% year over year, with the new tier presenting a more affordable version for the growing user base.

While Tinder is still bigger, with around 8.9 million paid users, its base seems to be shrinking, with its paid users down 4% from last year. Tinder has since leaned on a different strategy to keep users entertained including in-person experiences to combat user burnout, shifting the focus away from swiping.

Match Group also owns other major dating platforms including Tinder and OkCupid.

World Court Set to Rule in Myanmar Genocide Case

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image People watch the ICJ hearing at a restaurant in a Rohingya refugee camp in Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh, on January 23, 2020. © 2020 Allison Joyce/Getty Images

(The Hague) – The International Court of Justice has announced that it will deliver its judgment on November 19, 2026, in the case brought by Gambia against Myanmar for alleged violations of the Genocide Convention, Human Rights Watch said today in releasing a question-and-answer document on key issues in the case.

In August 2017, Myanmar’s security forces began a sweeping campaign of massacres, rape, and arson against ethnic Rohingya in northern Rakhine State that forced more than 700,000 people to flee to neighboring Bangladesh. In November 2019, Gambia filed a case before the International Court of Justice alleging that Myanmar’s atrocities against the Rohingya constitute genocide and violate the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide. This is not a criminal case against individuals, but a request for a legal determination of Myanmar’s state responsibility for genocide. 

“The International Court of Justice’s Myanmar judgment will mark a crucial moment in the Rohingya’s quest for justice,” said Maria Elena Vignoli, senior international justice counsel at Human Rights Watch. “The ruling will also be a stark reminder that the oppression of the Rohingya is ongoing, and that rights-respecting countries need to act decisively to support them, promote justice for atrocities, and ensure their safe and voluntary return.”

The judgment, which is final with no possibility to appeal, will conclude the proceedings in this case before the court.

Chris Führich reflects on missed turning point in Galatasaray defeat

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Stuttgart suffered their first defeat of their Champions League campaign in Istanbul, with Aleksey Batrakov scoring twice in the opening 15 minutes to secure a 2-0 win for Galatasaray.

VfB had made an encouraging start, with Jamie Leweling striking both posts with the same effort after just eight minutes.

The visitors then improved considerably after the interval, and thought they had found a route back into the contest when Chris Führich found the net from range, only for his effort to be ruled out for an offside against Deniz Undav.

Speaking to DAZN after the final whistle, the 28-year-old reflected on a frustrating evening in Istanbul.

“We invested a great deal. We started exactly as we had planned, with a huge chance for Jamie. If that goes in, it's obviously a completely different situation,” Führich explained.

"Then we conceded the first goal because we were a little too passive, and suddenly there's an energy here that you have to withstand.

"The second goal came straight afterwards, which was very bitter.

“We recovered quite well. I think we played a good game in the second half, with lots of chances and a few situations we could have played out better. But in the end, we were missing the goal.”

The German international was Stuttgart's best player on the night, and was particularly disappointed that his second-half strike was disallowed, believing that it could have provided Die Schwaben with a way back into the contest.

"It's a shame about the offside goal. I think that could have been another turning point, but it wasn't to be.

“We gave everything, put everything into the game, and I think we can still take a lot of positives away from here.”

Se domattina ti alzassi e fossi Paolo Scaroni, lo richiameresti Maldini?

“Io Maldini lo richiamerei, poi non so se Paolo accetterebbe. Però io lo chiamerei. Paolo fuori dal calcio e fuori dal Milan… Non mi piace vederlo a casa. Può dare tanto. Poi dopo non so, le scelte le deve fare lui”.

Invece, se fossi Paolo Maldini a che condizioni torneresti al Milan?

“Non lo so. Io e Paolo non abbiamo lavorato insieme nel post carriera.
