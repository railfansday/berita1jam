Tesla wants its sworn Cybercab answers to NHTSA kept entirely secret

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Tesla has answered NHTSA’s Special Order demanding it prove, under oath, that the Cybercab meets federal safety standards. The response landed on the October 30 deadline, and Tesla asked the agency to keep all of it confidential.

The only part of the filing the public can read is a 30-page guide for firefighters. Everything about how a car with no steering wheel, pedals, or mirrors was certified as compliant is blacked out.

We reported in September that NHTSA had ordered Tesla to show its work on the Cybercab’s certification. The order came one week after the company started charging for Cybercab rides in Austin on September 3 and put 21 questions to the company, with civil penalties of up to $139 million for an incomplete answer and criminal exposure for a false one.

Tesla first tried to buy time. On September 18, it asked for eight more weeks, until November 20, citing “the voluminous nature of the request” and the work of searching its records and writing “thorough narrative formats in response to each question.” NHTSA gave it 30 days instead. The new deadline was October 30, and Tesla met it.

Three Tesla documents now sit in the AQ26002 docket, and two of them are empty.

The first is Tesla’s response letter, dated October 30 and addressed from 1 Tesla Road in Austin. It is one page long. The only text is: “The entirety of this file is confidential business information.”

The second is a placeholder for Tesla’s answer to Request 8, which asked the company to explain, standard by standard, how it determined the Cybercab complies: which subpart it certified to, and whether it ran physical tests, simulations, or engineering analyses. Per NHTSA’s note, the responsive documents carry a confidentiality request “for the entirety of each document.”

The third is the only substantive document: Tesla’s Cybercab First Responder Interaction Plan, version 1.2, dated September 3. It’s a real document with useful information in it. It’s also something Tesla has had on its website since July, aimed at police and fire crews, not regulators.

So the public gets a tow-truck manual, and the certification argument stays sealed.

The Special Order wasn’t a fishing expedition. It named the rules the Cybercab doesn’t obviously meet, and it seeks to understand how Tesla believes it can get around it.

FMVSS 135 says “the service brakes shall be activated by means of a foot control.” NHTSA has stated in public that a manufacturer of a vehicle with no foot brake can’t certify to it, then asked Tesla how it did. FMVSS 108 requires a turn-signal control that cancels itself when the steering wheel turns. FMVSS 111 requires mirrors. And the agency wanted to know whether Tesla certified the car with temporary driver controls bolted in and then removed them, which would run into the Safety Act’s “make inoperative” prohibition.

It also asked how the Cybercab is legal without a Part 555 exemption, the route Amazon’s Zoox took for its own steering-wheel-less robotaxi in July. Zoox applied, made its case, and got an exemption capped at 2,500 vehicles a year. Tesla self-certified and started charging for rides.

Every one of those answers is now under a confidentiality claim.

The first-responder plan was written for a different audience, but it brushes against several of NHTSA’s questions.

It says the Cybercab is “not typically equipped with a steering wheel or acceleration and brake pedals.” The word “typically” is doing work there. The same document notes that a Cybercab with a steering wheel and pedals “is typically an engineering or test vehicle, and operates at SAE Level 2 autonomy,” and includes instructions for driving one. That’s relevant to Requests 5 through 7, which asked whether the subject vehicles can be driven with temporarily attached controls.

It describes an operational design domain covering “all public roadways, such as freeways, highways, city streets, and rural roads,” at all hours, in light and moderate rain, fog, and snow. That is a far wider envelope than the Austin geofence the cars run in today, and it references an “Arizona Areas of Operation Appendix.” Request 1 asked for the ODD and Request 2 for scaling plans.

And it confirms that first responders can be granted access to on-board touchscreen controls to move the car, after showing a badge to the B-pillar camera (that’s interesting). Request 20 asked whether the touchscreen can move the vehicle and under what restrictions.

Nothing in it touches brakes, mirrors, telltales, or the certification itself.

Confidentiality requests go to NHTSA’s Chief Counsel under Part 512. The agency decides whether Tesla’s claim holds, and it can order a public version released if it doesn’t. NHTSA hasn’t said anything about the request, and it hasn’t found the Cybercab noncompliant with any standard.

This is familiar. In June 2025, Tesla asked NHTSA to hide its answers about the original Robotaxi launch in Austin, a service running on supervised FSD while FSD was under investigation. Those answers never surfaced.

NHTSA’s opening resume for the audit put the affected population at about 1,000 vehicles. The Cybercab is still carrying passengers in Austin while the agency reads a filing nobody else can.

Tesla’s position in September was that the Cybercab complies with every applicable federal standard, including the one that requires a brake pedal. If that’s true, the argument for it is the most interesting document in the autonomous vehicle industry right now, and Tesla just asked the government to make sure nobody reads it.

We get why a company redacts test data and supplier details. But the legal theory for how a car with no foot brake meets a foot-brake rule doesn’t sound like a trade secret to me. Zoox’s exemption petition is public. Waymo’s safety framework is public. Tesla’s case for why it didn’t need to ask is sealed, cover letter included.

NHTSA now has to decide whether “the entirety of this file” is really confidential business information. If it says no, we’ll finally see how Tesla certified this car. If it says yes, about 1,000 Cybercabs keep driving on a certification the public isn’t allowed to check.

Nearly a billion people face heatwaves and wildfire smoke in the same year

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Heatwave wildfire smoke days now reach about 916 million people a year, and the heaviest burden falls on countries rarely linked to fires.

Most research on overlapping heatwaves and wildfire smoke has focused on the United States.

A global analysis now shows that people in Zambia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Malawi experience the most days each year when these hazards coincide.

Between 2000 and 2019, about 916 million people a year lived through at least one such day, and the number grew by 287 million every decade. The reason is mostly more heatwaves, not more smoke.

Yao Wu of Monash University‘s School of Public Health and Preventive Medicine led the count with Yuming Guo and Shanshan Li, in collaboation with researchers from Peking University and the University of Tasmania.

The team matched daily temperatures to estimates of fire smoke in the air, square by square, on a grid about 17 miles (28 kilometers) wide.

The researchers then overlaid population maps to estimate how many people were exposed.

Overlap days per person rose from 1.0 a year in the first decade to 1.7 in the second.

When the team split that growth into its parts, 70.5 percent came from people living through more overlap days and 29.5 percent from population growth.

In an interview with Earth.com, Wu explained what that increase means.

“In other words, the problem is growing mainly because people are experiencing these two hazards together more frequently, not simply because there are more people,” Wu said.

Heat explains most of that. Heatwave days increased in 87 percent of the squares on the map, but smoke days increased in only 28 percent of them, mostly in North America.

So a larger share of smoky days now coincides with a heatwave, while the share of heatwave days that also carry smoke has barely moved.

The two hazards also showed a clear timing pattern.

Across the globe, the chance of a smoke episode starting began to climb about two days before a heatwave began, and it peaked on the heatwave’s first day.

A smoke day, in this count, was any day when fine particles or ozone in the air exceeded the World Health Organization’s daily guideline.

At least half of that pollution had to come from landscape fires, a category that includes forest fires, grassland fires and the burning of crop waste.

A heatwave meant at least two consecutive days with temperatures above the local 90th percentile – hotter than 90 percent of days in the reference period.

That is the most lenient common definition, and the team chose it to catch as many hot days as possible.

Africa accounted for about 39 percent of all person-days of overlap, with one person-day representing one person exposed for one day.

People in low-income countries averaged 4.4 overlap days a year, against 0.4 in high-income countries.

That gap widened over the two decades, because the low-income countries also had the fastest increase.

Five countries accounted for half of all person-days between 2010 and 2019: the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Indonesia, Mexico, Brazil and Russia.

A different pattern emerged when the team compared annual exposure per person.

Zambia ranked highest that decade, averaging 28.8 days a year, followed by the Democratic Republic of the Congo at 24.3 and Malawi at 21.7.

“These findings highlight that some of the countries experiencing the greatest exposure are not necessarily those receiving the most international attention for wildfires,” Wu told Earth.com.

Asked by Earth.com which country’s trend surprised the team most, Wu named Cambodia.

It ranked 40th in overlap days per person in the first decade, at 1.6 days a year, and ninth in the second, at 12.1 days.

“What makes this particularly noteworthy is that Cambodia experienced substantial economic growth during this period, yet exposure to these overlapping environmental hazards also increased sharply,” Wu said.

Economic development, Wu said, does not necessarily translate into less exposure.

The Democratic Republic of the Congo showed the largest absolute rise, from 11.8 overlap days per person a year to 24.3. Europe and Oceania saw slight declines that were too small to separate from chance.

Wu’s advice for such a day is to guard against both hazards at the same time.

Check the local heat and air quality warnings, limit outdoor activity and especially hard exercise, and stay somewhere cool with clean air.

Air conditioning and air filtration help, and so does drinking enough water.

“The challenge is that protective measures for heat and wildfire smoke can sometimes conflict,” Wu said. “For example, opening windows may help cool a home but can also allow smoke indoors.”

For a household with neither air conditioning nor a filter, Wu suggested shading the windows and cutting indoor heat sources.

Another option is a public space that offers both cooling and cleaner air. Older adults, children and people with existing health conditions may need particular attention, Wu added.

The study counted exposure, not illness. It did not track anyone’s health, so it reports how many people breathed smoke on a hot day and nothing about what happened to them afterward.

Earlier work suggests that heat and smoke together may do more harm than the two added separately.

The smoke figures are also estimates from a computer model of the atmosphere, checked against ground monitors where they exist.

The model handles the height of a smoke plume imperfectly, which can overstate pollution near a fire and understate it downwind. Carbon monoxide was left out of the count.

And the record stops at the end of 2019. Wu said the heatwaves and fires since then “raise concerns that combined exposure may have continued to increase, particularly in low-income countries,” but the team can’t yet put a number on it.

The next step is to use these maps to study how combined exposure affects health, especially in countries that have received little research attention.

The findings could also help shape warning systems that address heat and smoke together.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

US regulators reportedly contacted banks following last week’s $6.3 billion Binance hack, as cybersecurity experts raised concerns about how financial institutions handle crypto-related security risks.

Officials from the Federal Reserve and Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) began contacting banks on Monday to ask about “unusual activity” or service disruptions, Reuters reported Thursday, citing people familiar with the matter.

The reported inquiries could be followed by closer attention to how banks manage crypto-related risks, including custody, counterparty exposure and incident response, according to Stefan Muehlbauer, head of US government affairs at CertiK.

“After an incident of this scale, it would be surprising if the Fed and OCC didn’t check in with the banks they supervise,” Muehlbauer told Cointelegraph. “Their mandate is to protect the financial system, and that means understanding where exposures sit and how quickly they can respond.”

According to Reuters, banks have been assessing the hack and checking their systems to figure out whether they could absorb or manage similar threats.

Reuters’ report also noted that a cybersecurity team at one of the largest US banks contacted Binance within hours of the hack. A Binance spokesperson confirmed with Cointelegraph that the exchange shared its publicly released incident report with all its banking partners and was not aware of any vulnerabilities affecting them.

Muehlbauer explained the procedure currently unfolding is “how a maturing framework should work.” He added that “it’s not a signal that regulators are pulling back from the industry.”

The inquiries also come after the Federal Reserve removed a requirement in April 2025 for state member banks to notify regulators before engaging in crypto activities. The Fed said it would continue overseeing those activities through its regular supervisory process.

A Federal Reserve spokesperson told Cointelegraph that the agency had nothing to share regarding the reported inquiries. Cointelegraph also contacted the OCC for comment but has not heard back at the time of publishing.

Binance said in its Nov. 3 incident report that approximately $6.34 billion in digital assets were affected by the attack on Oct. 29, with $2.85 billion frozen or intercepted as of the report.

According to the crypto exchange, the attack began with compromised software installed by an engineer in June. Hackers later employed social engineering, stolen credentials and malicious software to get access to wallet systems and carry out unauthorized transfers.

Dr. Darren Williams, founder and CEO of cybersecurity firm BlackFog, told Cointelegraph that the methods used in the attack pose potential risks to traditional banks, particularly those offering crypto custody and payment services.

“Attackers no longer need to break encryption or steal private keys if they can compromise the trusted systems and people authorized to move money,” Williams said.

He warned that hackers could use compromised employee devices, accounts or third-party services to move money without triggering any alerts. He also said the growing use of agentic AI in financial operations could pose even more risks.

“The priority must be stopping unauthorized transactions and data exfiltration before assets or sensitive information leave the organization, not simply investigating the damage afterwards,” Williams said.

Binance has since said that it has strengthened its security controls and was bringing in independent cybersecurity experts to review the attack.

California’s Potential Billionaire Tax Has Already Cost The State

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

California’s latest proposed tax increase has already led to so much wealth fleeing the state that tax revenue is set to fall, not rise. Golden State politicians announced the upcoming opening day of hunting season on billionaires and are about to find out they can’t fund welfare programs by taxing people who have already left.

At issue is California’s Proposition 40, a one-time 5% tax on billionaires’ net worth, which proponents claim will raise $100 billion for state-funded health care. Unfortunately for them, the wealthy were paying attention. At least seven high-profile billionaires left the state before Jan. 1 of this year, the residency date that determines who owes the tax.

They took with them roughly $539 billion of the state’s $1.9 trillion in billionaire wealth. Google co-founders Larry Page and Sergey Brin alone shrank the expected haul by $25 billion.

The fact is, $100 billion was never a realistic revenue projection. That estimate ignored losses from departures and legal challenges and both are certain to keep coming. The retroactive residency date alone invites a raft of constitutional challenges.

Supreme Court precedent bars retroactively imposing a “wholly new tax.” Locking people into paying whether they stay or leave, before voters have even approved the tax, burdens the constitutional right to travel. And as the Tax Foundation notes, uncapped penalties on appraisers of hard-to-value assets raise Excessive Fines Clause concerns, since they’re grossly disproportionate to good-faith errors.

>>> California Risks Killing the Golden Goose With Proposition 40

Once departures and litigation are counted, Stanford economists Joshua Rauh and Benjamin Jaros estimate the tax would bring in closer to $30 billion. That’s less than one-third of what was promised, which is bad news for Medi-Cal, the state’s Medicaid program.

But the bigger problem has already arrived. While a wealth tax is collected once, an income tax is collected every year. Rauh and Jaros estimate California’s billionaires pay between $3.3 billion and $5.8 billion in state income tax annually, and every billionaire who relocates takes his share with him permanently.

Weigh that lost income tax revenue against Prop 40’s one-time haul, and the net present value of the tax comes out to negative $38.9 billion.

In other words, the tax is a money-loser for the state. And this estimate isn’t a mere outlier from unlikely assumptions: economists ran 100,000 simulations with different revenue estimates and discount rates, and the state came out behind in 85% of them.

California already depends on a sliver of total earners for most of its tax receipts, with the top 1 percent of earners supplying roughly half of all personal income tax revenue. These are the people creating business, funding startups, and employing thousands of Californians. Losing even a handful of them does real damage to state and family finances alike.

>>> New York’s Property Tax Hike Chases Wealthy Wallets Away

Ironically, the billionaires who flee suffer less than the average Californian. As F. Scott Fitzgerald said, the rich are different from you and me; they can leave for Florida, Tennessee, Texas, or another low-tax state. It’s California’s public services, funded by their income, that will suffer.

Medi-Cal and the other programs counting on this money will still need funding. When $100 billion becomes $30 billion, followed by a permanent reduction to income tax revenue, politicians will clamor for more tax hikes.

But with a shrinking tax base of billionaires, the hikes will inevitably fall on the middle class. A tax sold as making billionaires “pay their fair share” will end up costing the residents not wealthy enough to leave.

Supporters will point to research showing high-earners rarely move because of taxes. But the best-known study examined ordinary income tax rates over many years. A levy on someone’s entire net worth is a different animal and will prompt more people to leave, as seven just did.

Before Californians vote on Prop 40, they should understand how much the tax will realistically raise—and who will get stuck with the actual bill in the long run.

D&D’s World of Warcraft Designer Hid a Personal Easter Egg in Plain Sight

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The team also obsessively counted toes, banners, and mountain peaks

Mounts in World of Warcraft are status symbols, a way of showing off the challenging raids you’ve completed or how far in the PvP ranks you’ve climbed. Dungeons & Dragons: World of Warcraft lead designer James Wyatt couldn’t help showing off himself a bit in the book’s art, where he slipped in an image of his Night Elf warrior riding the Spirit of Eche'ro. Acquiring the spectral moose required spending hours digging up bone fragments with the Archaeology profession, an activity so tedious it was discontinued after the 2018 expansion Battle for Azeroth.

Fortunately, getting those rare cosmetic items is much easier in the Dungeons & Dragons expansion than in WoW. A character proficient in history can spend an hour or a short rest searching the site of Highmountain Tauren ruins for Archaeology fragments. If they gather six, they can be assembled into a staff that casts the Find Steed spell, summoning the famed moose once ridden by the Tauren hero Huln Highmountain. Wyatt also lets players acquire the Cenarion War Hippogryph, another mount he spent a lot of effort on, by raising their Renown with the Cenarion Circle through protecting the natural balance of Azeroth.

“Cosmetic rewards in WoW are about bragging rights, which is an individual achievement, and D&D is not a game about individual achievements. It's a game about stuff you do as a group together,” Wyatt told Polygon in a virtual interview. “Having mounts appear as Renown rewards and other special things was important for the feel of this cool element of WoW that I want to see in D&D, but the sort of rare achievement aspect of it falls into the category of mechanics that we didn't try to translate.”

Some of the book’s other Easter eggs are nods to WoW’s signature characters, with the Banshee Queen Sylvanas Windrunner appearing on a scale used by a Forsaken alchemist. Others are meant to establish the close ties between WoW and D&D. A gnome is used as the signature Warlock in both D&D: WoW and the Player’s Handbook, and the WoW book’s gnome art depicts them constructing a mechanostrider mount just like the Player’s Handbook version has them building a golem.

Wyatt and lead art director Sergio Jaramillo spent a lot of time playing WoW so they could provide artists with guidelines to ensure the book’s illustrations were true to the game.

“People have seen these places for thousands of hours on their computers,” Jaramillo said. “They know each peak that Icecrown Citadel is supposed to have and how many banners Ogrimmar has. We have to go through it, compare it, triple-check, and then maybe it’s ready. We sent it to Blizzard, and maybe Blizzard catches something we didn’t. A couple of times there were like, ‘We didn’t know this was there. Nice catch.’”

The team wanted to be precise with its modeling of characters, and even carefully counted the fingers and toes for Azeroth’s various species. They wanted to be sure to replicate unusual features, like trolls’ hunched posture, and the full range of eye and hair colors. They would build characters in the Wowhead Dressing Room, a digital tool that lets you recreate any character build and deck it out in equipment, and then commission artists based on the renders. There were times Blizzard couldn’t help them because the models simply didn’t exist in the game. Visit the Tauren city of Thunder Bluff in WoW and you’ll see plenty of Tauren adults and some babies, but no children. Jaramillo’s team had to come up with the proportions for the Tauren kids they wanted to show riding a kodo, the traditional Tauren mount, in the book’s character origins section.

WoW players can be very passionate about their preferences for playing Horde or Alliance, and Wyatt and Jaramillo wanted to make sure they didn’t appear to favor one faction over another. They made sure to have balanced representation across illustrations showcasing everything from the game’s professions to its boss fights. They even put the Collector’s Edition book in a slipcase so players could choose whether to have the Horde or Alliance logo displayed.

Getting the details of another game right was a challenge, but Wyatt said that in many ways, the project was easier than the work he did designing D&D books based on the Magic: The Gathering worlds of Ravnica, Theros, and Strixhaven.

“Worldbuilding on Magic is like building a movie set,” Wyatt said. “It feels like you’re setting up fronts that are held up by two-by-fours in the back, and if you start pushing too deep, you’re going to realize it’s all going to fall over. D&D worldbuilding has to account for characters who can do anything and go anywhere, and so does WoW. It is a very fleshed out world already. The lore is there.”

Under the surface, unseen layers lie. They stay there, silent. They don’t demand the spotlight. They don’t seek glory. And yet, like a beating heart, they form the innermost foundation of any real achievement. Where passion, enthusiasm, and discipline meet, the logic of excellence takes shape. Such is the story of Under the Surface, the newest creative collaboration from Canali.

Under the Surface is a look at the unseen architecture of a sartorial jacket by Canali. Every Canali jacket is born from a complex system of about 200 steps, each executed with meticulous care. Around 15 specialists are involved in the process—one that sees more than 520 feet of thread sewn into 22,000 stitches, both machine- and hand-made. Unseen when looking at the finished garment, the process informs each piece’s quality, comfort, shape, and durability.

It’s not just a matter of talent: rather, it’s about staying faithful to one’s goals, pursuing them with dedication even when no one is watching. It’s then that quality truly makes a difference, and the end result becomes the visible manifestation of a long creation process that demands great effort, experience and willpower.

That commitment of excellence unites the sartorial world of Canali and Ethan Hawke, the brand’s new Global Ambassador. The actor, writer, and director has dedicated almost four decades to perfecting his art, with endless, ever-learning curiosity. For their new Fall/Winter 2026 campaign, Canali has decided to capture what goes on behind the scenes. In the film, Hawke pores over his scripts, rehearses lines, refines each motion: a painstaking introspective process made of research, study, and exercise, preceding each of his performances.

The behind-the-scenes of a Canali jacket starts with selecting the finest fabrics, sourced from the best Italian wool mills. Then, each textile is examined and interpreted. While technology now allows extreme precision and control, some assessments are still left to the eye and expertise of human specialists. Even tiny details such as slightly different shades of color or variations in texture can influence the final product.

The fabric is then stabilized via steaming cycles and specific treatments, to ensure its behavior remains consistent over time. Then it’s time for digital mapping, guiding every cutting room decision: here, each of the jacket’s pieces is carefully positioned to also help keep waste to a minimum. It’s the first irreversible step.

The garment’s three-dimensional structure is then shaped upon the canvas. Canali crafts theirs in-house, adapting it to each model’s fit and fabrics. Made from horsehair, plant fibers, and natural reinforcements, the canvas is modeled on custom-made concave shapes, known as Schiena d’Asino (Donkey’s Back), which replicate the chest’s anatomy. The canvas is crucial for determining how a jacket drapes over the body, avoiding stiffness. It’s here that the volume of the chest, the balance of the silhouette and ease of wearing begin to be defined.

Then it’s time to craft the front, lapel, padded shoulder, and the sleeves. From planning to execution, nothing’s left to chance. The tailor observes, corrects, iterates. It’s a patient process, requiring time, knowledge, and skill. Lastly, the various layers are united through basting, stitching, and ironing processes. As these details interact, the garment takes on its structure and sophisticated character.

Something special also takes place behind the scenes with Hawke: day after day, the actor’s preparation subtly transforms his thoughts and behavior until, almost by instinct, he takes on each role’s intimate characteristics.

Time underpins the whole process. After being assembled and pressed, the jacket is allowed to rest, to dissipate the steam and stabilize the cloth. This is how the fabric finds its balance, and the garment gains a sort of memory: the ability to naturally find its shape again, even after having been worn, folded, or packed into a suitcase.

In over 90 years of heritage, Canali has refined their techniques through repetition, experience, and experimentation. Each garment is built upon the time-tested values of care, know-how, precision. The Canali style is not one made of grand gestures, but daily practice, intimate and subtle: the constant research into a shape that lasts. After a 16-year long dev period, Faraway, a shooting star arcade game about siphoning the energy of other stars, is out now

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Lord almighty, video games take a while to make, 'ey? Especially if you're just one person working on it by yourself. Such is the case with Faraway, out today, an arcade game that reminds me of the early days of PS3 indies "about a shooting star that creates constellations to stay alive," all made single-handedly by creator of cult-classic mobile game Eliss, Steph Thirion. Did I mention that Faraway took 16 years to make? Like I said, game development is a lengthy process.

Looking at Faraway, and knowing how long it took to produce, it doesn't surprise me to come from a dev who managed to release a mobile game in the early days of the platform, long before free-to-play got its chokehold on the industry. It also really does feel like it should be sitting amongst the likes of PixelJunk, this thing should be something I'm desperate to play on my PS3 while I wait for my controller to charge.

As mentioned, you are a shooting star, a "spark meant to shine and vanish in a blink." But still you persist, connecting to and wrapping yourself around starts to "harvest their energy," in turn lengthening your own life, and of course, in arcadey fashion, racking up a nice score. It looks like game design as poetry, everything you're looking at procedurally generated to bring freshness to every run. I can fully envision this as a "one more run" kind of arcade game, the best kind, especially in an age long past the need to shove in another of your hard earned coins.

Faraway conceptually charms me because it taps into a style of game we don't get to see much of any more. What I'm saying is, I'm just a teensy bit sick of how many roguelikes there are, but that doesn't mean I don't want a game I can just hop in and out of at my own whims. Sure, arcade games might not be in vogue, I just miss them a bit. In any case, Faraway is available to pick up on Steam right now. Seems like it'll be worth a punt!

‘Wild Horse Nine’ Review: Martin McDonagh’s Prickly but Unconvincing CIA Satire Recasts ‘In Bruges’ as the Stuff of a Classic Western

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

[Editor’s note: This review was originally published on September 3, 2026 during the 2026 Venice Film Festival. Searchlight Pictures will release it in theaters on Friday, November 6.]

Another melancholically splenetic Martin McDonagh jag about a banter-mad pair of murderers who go on a boys trip to purgatory (where they search their souls as they wait for death to find them), the prickly but unconvincing “Wild Horse Nine” strains to translate the post-Guy Ritchie bark of “In Bruges” into the language of a classic American Western. In this case, an American Western set on Easter Island and backdropped by Augusto Pinochet’s rise to power in the fall of 1973.  Related Stories ‘North of North’ Season 2 Review: Netflix’s Defiantly Nice Arctic Sitcom Is for the Warmhearted F**k-Ups ‘Hen’ Review: A Cockeyed Comedy Sees Europe’s Migrant Crisis Through the Eyes of a Chicken

McDonagh’s decision to recast his same old script against the backdrop of such a real and terrible event grants this new telling a welcome sense of scale, both in terms of its characters’ morality, as well as in regards to their greater place in history. But that decision is a double-edged sword that cuts the film off at the knees. Where “In Bruges” kicked off with the killing of a fictional altar boy, “Wild Horse Nine” is overshadowed by the deaths of thousands upon thousands of very real people, and that reality bleeds into the edges of the frame even when the seriocomic absurdity of McDonagh’s writing demands a world of pure invention.

Does the overlap make “Wild Horse Nine” feel glib? Yes, and thank god for that — if not for glibness the movie would lose the majority of its few solid laughs. The problem is that it also makes “Wild Horse Nine” feel as transparently manufactured as the CIA-backed overthrow of a foreign democracy. I can accept that an inexperienced hitman might have a dark night of the soul after shooting an innocent child during confession. By contrast, I struggle to believe that a veteran CIA agent who openly brags about his involvement in the Kennedy assassination would have a come-to-Jesus moment after orchestrating the demise of Salvador Allende. McDonagh has some good fun wrestling with whether or not we should care about such a mottled change of heart, but the point is gradually rendered moot in a movie whose emotions are less credible than its conspiracies.

The original Company man, Chris (John Malkovich, more locked in than he’s been on screen since “Burn After Reading”) is a natural hinge for the off-kilter comedy that McDonagh builds around him, in that he can be a joy to watch even when you don’t buy a word he’s saying. More than just an ordinary CIA agent, he’s the living personification of America’s efforts to destabilize foreign governments across the 20th century. He was close with Patrice Lumumba until the day the Congolese leader was executed by a firing squad. He was in Cuba to spur on the exiles who failed to overthrow Fidel Castro in 1961. He was even in Dallas, USA on a Friday afternoon in November 1963, doing things his ornery boss MJ (Steve Buscemi) would much prefer he didn’t tell you about.

He traveled to all of those places, and got all of those people killed, with the blithe imperiousness of a flag waving in the wind. Chris says that he’s killed 68 people over the course of his career, and he offers that information as easily as a rumor — in a way that makes it sound like the number could be half that, or double, and it wouldn’t make a difference.

Recently, fate has blown Chris and his office husband/platonic life partner/exasperated straightman Lee (McDonagh vet Sam Rockwell) down south to Chile, where the two of them have been hard at work orchestrating the September 11th coup against the democratically elected socialist president, knowing full well that it would plunge the country into decades of brutal dictatorship. Chris appears to delight in sowing chaos, and the fact that he gets to wear a cute little Panama hat while he does it only boosts his joie de vivre.

But Chris is fraying at the seams in ways that we can’t see at first — ways that will only begin to reveal themselves when he and Lee decide to celebrate their imminent success with a sightseeing trip to Easter Island, a short 2,300 miles off the Western coast of Santiago. There are still a couple of months to go before the coup, but the wheels are in motion, and there’s little for these men to do for now besides fly to Rapa Nui, look at the “big heads,” flirt with a couple of beautiful young leftists (the prissier but gentler of whom is played by “Ema” mega-talent Mariana di Girolamo), and mordantly reflect upon their role in the awfulness Pinochet will soon inflict upon his own people. That task is complicated and confused by the fact that Chris seems to be losing his memory, which casts Lee in the role of his peevish but loving caretaker.

“Wild Horse Nine” is only the second feature that’s ever been shot on the ultra-remote soil of Rapa Nui (Kevin Reynolds’ 1994 epic “Rapa Nui” is credited as the first), and McDonagh rightly exalts in the fact that he’s brought Hollywood to the end of the world. The first half of his movie largely adheres to the fiction that Chris and Lee are just on Easter Island to fuck around, which offers a semi-relaxed opportunity for us to take in the sights. Ben Davis’ sun-bleached cinematography renders the hills and beaches of Rapa Nui with the veneer of a lost idyll, the ancient moai statues assuming an especially sacred charge when contrasted against such profane characters.

Chris bemusedly laughs the statues off with ethnographic glee, mocking one of the shorter figures as a dwarf (in case the “In Bruges” déjà vu wasn’t already strong enough), but it’s clear that he’s shaken by the fact that some of them almost 750 years old. That they’ve stood there in silent tribute to Polynesia’s ancestors as empires rose and fell on faraway shores, each of them a graveyard of forgotten people just like him. By the same token, it’s similarly clear that he’s trying to get at something in his own mind as he and Lee listen to Judy Collins in their rental car, argue over whether or not any Nazis made it into heaven (among other even more aggressively screen-written subjects), and whisper sweet nothings to the wild horses that run about the island, but the specific nature of that something is hard for him to pin down.

Malkovich has the time of his life with that search for Chris’ last speck of humanity, which shines up from the bottom of his soul as his mind continues to erode. At once both insidiously relaxed and also choked with Catholic guilt, Malkovich’s performance marries the nonchalance of a cowboy to the paranoia of a bounty target. One minute he’s waxing poetic about dinosaurs, and the next he’s rigging his luggage with explosives (maybe) and checking the “Memento”-like reminder notes he’s slipped into his jacket pocket.

“Wild Horse Nine” loves Chris too much to pretend like we’re not supposed to, but Malkovich never asks for our sympathy because he knows that he’ll get it anyway, and not just because the purpose of Rockwell’s character is to ensure that we do. It’s Chris’ job to make rooting for the bad guys feel like the right choice. It’s the cornerstone of America’s post-war foreign policy.

So while Chris might be cut from the same cloth as John Wayne (to the extent that Monument Valley is revealed to be a place of great importance to him), his arc is aimed in the opposite direction of the one that Ethan Edwards traces across “The Searchers”; Chris is a major asshole this movie gradually puckers into a wayward hero, and while it’s all too easy to root for him to complete that transformation before time runs out, at no point does “Wild Horse Nine” sell us on the premise that it would mean anything in the end — even to him.

The movie, blessedly, isn’t Catholic enough to offer Chris a big moment of divine expiation; in lieu of building to a grand confession, he and Lee simply run afoul of their CIA higher-ups until their PTO turns into a matter of American national security. Guns are fired. Hostages are taken. The word “fuck” is used like a punctuation mark, and regrets are muttered into the wind long after there’s any way for them to matter.

It all gets a bit too busy and madcap as “Wild Horse Nine” — freighted with sadness during even its cheekiest bits, as is McDonagh’s way — scrambles to make up for the general listlessness of its first half. Never as funny as it is sardonic, and always reaching so hard to be more of both, the film’s most successful moments are mined from the ambient tension between memory and erasure.

Chris, we begin to suspect, would do anything to forget what he’s done, and his own mind is graciously willing to get him halfway there. The rest of the CIA will do anything it takes to make sure that Chris doesn’t share whatever it is that he still remembers. Surrounding them on all sides are statues that predated America by several hundred years, and will likely continue to watch over Rapa Nui long after our empire has been wiped away like a tear in the rain. In “Wild Horse Nine,” American interventionism is less effective as the set-up for some jokes than it is as their punchline.

Walmart Will Have Three Major Shopping Events This Holiday Season

If you're thinking of picking up any big-ticket items soon—whether for yourself or as a gift for someone on your list— I would advise you to wait until you see what retailers have in store for the holiday sales season, which will kick off in earnest in mid-November. Walmart is the first major retailer to announce its Black Friday and Cyber Monday plan for 2026, and they actually involve three major shopping events between now and the December holidays.

As it did in 2025, Walmart will hold three sales between Nov. 12 and Dec. 2, as outlined in a recent press release. The sales will be both online and in-store, and will target a mix of Walmart+ members and general shoppers.

The events will take place on three different dates:

Walmart's Black Friday Deals Event 1: Nov. 12–15

Available online and in stores starting at 6 a.m. local time

Walmart+ members get six hours of early access beginning online at 12:01 a.m. ET on November 12

Walmart's Black Friday Deals Event 2: Nov. 23–29

Online only: Nov. 23 starting at 12:01 a.m. ET

Online and in stores: Nov. 27 starting at 6 a.m. local time

Walmart+ members get six hours of early access online beginning Nov. 23 at 12:01 a.m. ET

Walmart's Cyber Monday Deals Event: Nov. 30-Dec. 2

Walmart says that you can expect discounts on electronics, toys, fashion, beauty, home goods, gifts, and everyday essentials. Although the company doesn't provide much in the way of specifics in its press release, last year's sales included discounts on TCL TVs, Dyson vacuums, Dreo appliances, and toys from LEGO.
