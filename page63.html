Singapore Airlines Unveils the ‘Largest Double Bed in the Sky’ As Part of a $1.6 Billion Redesign

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Getting a good night's sleep on a long-haul flight isn't easy, even when you're lucky enough to be flying first class. But Singapore Airlines—consistently voted as one of the best airlines in the world by CNT readers—is taking things to a whole new level, unveiling what it claims will be “the largest double bed in commercial aviation” as part of a major revamp of its long-haul cabins.

The airline announced the $1.6 billion redesign on Friday, November 6, with the first aircraft expected to enter service in the second half of 2027.

Singapore Airlines's new first class cabin

The biggest change is coming to first class, where passengers traveling together will be able to transform two adjoining seats into a double bed measuring 80 inches long and 60 inches wide—the same size as a standard queen bed in the US. The seats can also be converted into a two-person sofa, while curved sliding doors and retractable dividers will offer additional privacy.

And the luxury doesn't stop there. The new suites will feature Italian Poltrona Frau leather upholstery, personal wardrobes, dedicated dining spaces, and 32-inch 4K entertainment screens. Passengers can also expect proper bedding, including cotton sheets, duvets, a choice of pillows, and mattress toppers from German bedding specialist Paradies.

The new business class seats also come with adjoining seats, offering the option to sleep alongside a traveling companion.

Business class passengers won't be left out either. Redesigned seats will come with sliding privacy doors, improved lumbar support and fully flat beds, with selected adjoining seats offering the option to sleep alongside a traveling companion. Smart controls in both premium cabins will even remember passengers' preferred seating positions for future flights.

But what about those of us traveling farther back? Premium economy will benefit from additional personal space, adjustable leg rests, dedicated storage, and larger entertainment screens. Meanwhile, economy passengers can look forward to redesigned seats with more leg space, adjustable headrests, and new storage compartments.

The new economy class will feature more leg space, adjustable headrests, and new storage compartments.

The upgrades will be introduced gradually across Singapore Airlines's new Boeing 777-9 aircraft and 41 retrofitted Airbus A350-900 aircraft, including its ultra-long-range fleet. The latter will also receive first class cabins for the first time.

There's no confirmed date yet for when passengers will be able to try the new double beds, but one thing is certain: cuddling up at 35,000 feet has just become a little more appealing.

London’s Fractile raises $720M for memory-first AI chips and hits $7B valuation

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

London-based chip designer Fractile has raised $720 million in a Series C round, betting that memory bandwidth, rather than raw computing power, is the main constraint holding back AI performance.

The funding values the four-year-old company at $7 billion, according to the Financial Times, which cited people familiar with the deal.

Thrive Capital, Founders Fund, Lightspeed, and Redpoint led the round. Other investors include Alphabet’s CapitalG, DST Global, BlackRock, Bain Capital Ventures, T. Rowe Price, Shopify Ventures, Balderton, the UK’s Sovereign AI fund, and the British Business Bank.

The valuation is a sharp jump from May, when Fractile raised around $220 million at a valuation of roughly $1 billion. The new figure puts it among the UK’s most valuable startups, alongside companies such as Revolut.

The Financial Times also reports that Anthropic has expressed interest in becoming an early customer for Fractile’s chips, citing people familiar with the discussions.

Founder Walter Goodwin’s argument starts with a growing imbalance between computing power and memory bandwidth.

Over the past 25 years, chip performance has increased roughly a millionfold, while memory bandwidth has grown only about 40 times. That leaves a 25,000-fold gap between the amount of data processors can calculate and how quickly they can access it.

Goodwin told the FT that today’s frontier models are “drinking through a straw”.

Fractile’s proposed solution combines custom DRAM and logic in a three-dimensional stack. The company says this architecture can deliver substantially higher bandwidth than high-bandwidth memory (HBM), while retaining enough capacity to run large models with long context windows. Goodwin claims it could make inference, the process of generating responses to user queries, up to 10 times faster.

Those performance gains have yet to be demonstrated in a production chip. Fractile has not released a commercial product, and its first chip is planned for next year. For now, the $7 billion valuation is based on its technology and prototypes rather than a product in the market.

The company is also trying to avoid a growing constraint in the AI supply chain. Many competing chipmakers rely on HBM to move data quickly between memory and processors.

The technology is expensive, and demand from AI companies is putting pressure on suppliers such as SK Hynix and Micron.

Fractile is entering an increasingly crowded market. Nvidia acquired Groq last year and launched its own inference chip in March, while startups including Etched, MatX, and fellow British company Olix are developing alternatives.

The opportunity, according to Thrive Capital’s Philip Clark, is an inference market that could soon become “at least as large as the market Nvidia is addressing today”.

He sees particular potential in AI agents, which need to run continuously for hours or days rather than respond to a single prompt.

The funding is also a significant moment for the UK’s effort to build domestic AI infrastructure. Goodwin described the government’s Sovereign AI fund as a driving force behind developing frontier AI technology from London.

Fractile also has offices in the US and Taiwan, however, and is building for a global market. Its next challenge is to turn its prototypes into working chips that can deliver the performance it has promised.

20 surprising facts about the U.S. Capitol from a 21-item countdown

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

From marble bathtubs to private subways and a ghost cat, 20 of the 21 curiosities in a 2013 Capitol countdown.

Most visitors picture the U.S. Capitol as a single finished monument, kept in the form its founders gave it. In fact, Congress has torn out and rebuilt its rooms more than once, so a walk through the building passes through work from very different eras. The Old Senate Chamber was reconstructed from 1815 to 1819, according to the Library of Congress. The Architect of the Capitol says that the chamber where senators meet today was first used in 1859 and rebuilt in 1949 and 1950. Each round of work left something behind, including fixtures, rules and habits that stayed in place long after the reasons for them had passed. Those leftovers make the Capitol feel strange to a newcomer, and they connect the curiosities that follow.

A quirk that looks merely odd usually turns out to be a working rule about who gets in and who goes first. Across the building, access depends on an invitation from someone on the inside rather than on a ticket. Aides and guests ride the senators' elevators at a senator's invitation, according to The Washington Post, and friends of Capitol staff can ride the private subway alongside a staffer. The result is two versions of the same building: a managed one for the public and a privileged one for insiders and the people they bring along. A visitor who notices that split can read each curiosity on the tour as a record of how Congress decides who may go where.

BuzzFeed News set out to share little-known, quirky facts about the Capitol for visitors to D.C., from marble bathtubs in the basement to private subways, a fallout shelter beneath the crypt, and paw prints that some people credit to a ghost cat. The facts were gathered for people touring the building, so their value lies in what a visitor can learn by looking twice. Many of the jokes point to something serious, such as a deliberate design choice, a privilege kept for members, or a story nobody ever verified. The slides that follow take each curiosity at face value first, then ask what it says about the institution that keeps it.

Some galleries above the House and Senate floors still carry labels from a time when Congress seated men and women apart, according to BuzzFeed News. Both chambers followed the practice. The House of Representatives and the Senate alike kept the sexes in different seats. The labels are odd because they survive at all, and the seating still names an arrangement that most people in the galleries would never guess.

It would be easy to assume that separate seating for women was added later to a room built for everyone. In the old Senate chamber, the separation was part of the architect's plan for the rebuilt room. Architect Benjamin Latrobe's plan set a ladies' gallery around the entire chamber, according to the Library of Congress. The visitors' gallery sat along the chamber's east wall. Women were not given a leftover corner. In Latrobe's plan, their seats wrapped around the room, while the visitors' gallery filled only one wall.

A gallery label can look like a quaint relic, but it marks a rule that once decided where people sat in both chambers of Congress. In the old Senate chamber, that rule went beyond a sign over a doorway. It set the layout of the room itself, and it gave women in the gallery far more space than a visitor would expect.

A bathtub beneath the Senate sounds like a gag, but it marks one of the first comforts Congress built for its own members inside the place where they work. Congress installed marble tubs in the Capitol basement in 1859 so that senators without baths of their own would smell better, according to BuzzFeed News. Most senators of that era lived in boarding houses, the U.S. Senate reports, and the Washington Aqueduct that would carry water into the city was nearly completed in 1858. With water on the way, members gave themselves at work what their lodgings did not offer. Nor were the tubs plain fixtures: each was cut from a single piece of marble.

A privilege that serves only a few people has few people to remember it once they stop using it. The remaining tubs lay forgotten until workers uncovered them on Aug. 17, 1936, according to the U.S. Senate. Even the man who knew the building best had no explanation for fixtures in its own basement. “I’ve been here over 40 years,” Capitol Engineer Arthur Cook said, yet he could not account for the tubs.

The trail is faint enough that the accounts disagree on how many tubs survive. BuzzFeed News counts a single tub, while the U.S. Senate reports that two remain, and a visitor has no way to settle the question. Congress built these baths for a privileged few, and those few left so thin a record that even the number of survivors is in dispute.

Whatever sits in the basement is less a bath than a reminder that Congress built comforts for its insiders and that those insiders later lost track of them.

Senators work in a room with no view of the outside world. Visitors know the Senate from the gallery, where they look down at the rows of desks, but the people at those desks see the room from an angle the public never gets. BuzzFeed News shows, in photos taken from the floor, what senators see from their seats, and everything in that view belongs to the chamber itself. A senator who looks up from a desk sees walls, galleries and colleagues, and nothing of the city or the sky outside. Members debate the country's laws in a room that shows them only the Senate.

The closed-in view was planned from the beginning. The original design left the chamber with no windows at all, the Architect of the Capitol reports, and the skylight above the room came out during the reconstruction of 1949 and 1950. Congress wanted that rebuild to help senators hear one another better and to keep fresh air moving through the room. Those were practical goals for a hall where members spend long hours in debate. Once the skylight was gone, a senator on the floor sat in a room shaped around hearing colleagues, with nothing beyond its walls in sight.

A chamber sealed off from the city suits a body that does its business among its own members.

Viewers who watch a lawmaker on the evening news tend to picture grand rooms behind the interview, but many of the Capitol's interview rooms are less glamorous than people expect, BuzzFeed News reports. The plain setting is not a matter of taste. On the Senate side of the Capitol, a video crew with a tripod may film only from stakeout positions the rules have approved, according to RTCA. A senator on camera therefore stands wherever a tripod is allowed, not wherever the room looks best. Two of those positions are the Ohio Clock Corridor and the Senate Subway's Capitol end, both passageways, so a Senate interview on television often takes place in a hallway.

The House and Senate each decide for themselves where a lens may go. The House rules out video around the second and third floors of its chamber, according to RTCA, which closes whole floors near the room where members meet. The same Congress looks different on screen depending on which chamber a story comes from, because each side has drawn its own frame.

These limits reach the entrance the public uses. Crews need prior approval from both the House and Senate galleries to shoot video in the Capitol Visitor Center, according to RTCA. A visitor on the tour may look freely at spaces that a camera can show only with sign-off from both chambers. The tour and the broadcast are two separately managed views of one building.

When a senator talks to reporters in a plain hallway, the setting reflects the rulebook, not any lack of grandeur in the Capitol.

The Senate makes no secret that some of the Capitol's elevators belong to senators alone. Visitors may not use many of the building's elevators, according to BuzzFeed News. The Washington Post reports that in 2010 one car reserved for senators was marked "SENATORS ONLY" on both a placard and a digital readout. Senate historian Donald Ritchie defended the reserved cars that year on the grounds of the voting clock. Senators who are in their office buildings when the bells ring have 15 minutes to reach the chamber and vote. Seen that way, the reservation is about the vote count, not a senator's comfort.

Holding a car for senators matters because the ride itself takes almost no time. A trip lasts about eight seconds, according to The Washington Post, so the only real delay for a senator hurrying to a vote is waiting for a car to arrive. Automatic elevators replaced the old cage cars in the 1960s and 1970s, yet the Senate kept human operators on its members-only car as of 2010. In this case, "off-limits" also meant a car with its own operator, a service the Senate still provided to its members decades after automatic elevators arrived.

The same rule that gets senators to votes on time can also keep a senator away from questions. In the Hart Senate Office Building on March 1, 2010, Kentucky Republican Sen. Jim Bunning invoked the rule against Jonathan Karl, a correspondent for ABC News, according to The Washington Post. "This is a senator-only elevator," Bunning said as Karl questioned him about his opposition to extending unemployment benefits. Ritchie's defense and Bunning's use of the rule are both accurate. The reserved car helps senators reach the floor before a vote closes, and it also gives a senator a door to close on a reporter.

A visitor who reads the "SENATORS ONLY" sign is looking at a scheduling rule that senators can also use to keep the press out.

Picture clerks counting votes by hand, and Congress sounds stuck in another century. BuzzFeed News reports that each vote is tallied by hand. That description fits only one of the Capitol's two chambers. The House takes its recorded floor votes by electronic device, according to the Library of Congress. Senators, by contrast, lack any electronic means of casting their votes. A visitor who wants to see votes counted by hand has to watch the Senate floor, because House members record their floor votes another way.

On a roll call, the Senate's hand count becomes a spoken count, and each senator's vote becomes a public declaration. During a roll call, a clerk calls each senator's name and the senator answers aloud, according to the Library of Congress. Each answer is heard in the chamber, one name at a time, as the clerk works through the roster. The procedure that looks the most dated is also among the most open, because on a roll call no senator can cast a vote in silence. Much of the Capitol is set aside for members alone, from reserved elevators to rooms the public never enters. This rule does the opposite.

When the clerk calls the roll, every senator has to answer on the record, out loud and in public.

Wiring the Capitol for modern connections was a construction problem before it was a technology problem. The Capitol was 200 years old when Congress began wiring it for high-speed internet, according to BuzzFeed News. That meant asking for full modern service from walls that were never planned to carry it. The age problem reached past the walls to the furniture. Senators worked at chamber desks that were nearly 200 years old, Sound & Video Contractor reports, and new equipment had to reach the members at those same seats. Senate officials allowed no new holes in the desks and no other changes to them. Preservation, not an engineer's convenience, set the limits on where the new equipment could go.

The building had no cable paths made for this kind of work, though crews could reuse a few existing ones, according to Sound & Video Contractor. The clearest record of the problem comes from the Senate's rebuild of its chamber sound system, a closed audio network rather than internet service. In that job, crews cut through 6 feet of the Capitol's granite foundation, which put the work closer to construction than to an ordinary office upgrade.

The Senate kept meeting during the sound system rebuild, so the crews had to plan their job around lawmakers. Sound & Video Contractor reports that the chamber's sound system had no downtime during the upgrade. Crews managed that by installing the new system in stages during the Senate's scheduled recesses.

In the Capitol, the people doing the work answered to the old stone and the sitting Senate first, and to the technology last.

Beneath the Capitol complex, getting from one building to another is a benefit of working there, not a service open to the public. Congressional staff ride their own trains between Congress's office buildings. A tourist cannot simply walk up and board, so the quickest route between the offices where members' business gets done belongs mainly to the people who work in them. The arrangement matches a pattern found throughout the building: the parts of the Capitol that save time and effort go first to insiders.

Congress has kept this private ride for almost as long as it has had office buildings to connect. The first subway line opened in 1909, according to BuzzFeed News, so Congress has moved its own people underground for more than a century. Riders could even smoke on those original cars. That detail shows how far back the line reaches, to an era with very different habits from today's.

Over the decades, Congress changed how the cars work, but it did not change who rides them. On the older carts, one man worked a lever that controlled the ride, and each trip depended on that single operator. The newer cars are automated and need no operator at the controls. Those upgrades made the trip easier for the people already allowed on board. Through every change of equipment, the line has remained a ride for the Capitol's own workers rather than a route for the general public.

The Senate chaplain works beside a window that is oval and can still be opened, according to BuzzFeed News. Most of what visitors notice in the Capitol is there to be looked at, but this window is there to be used. The oval shape sets the chaplain's room apart from the square-cornered office a visitor would expect. The fact that the window still opens matters as much as its shape, because it means the window has kept working over the years. The person in that office can let in fresh air from outside without leaving the room. In an ordinary house, a window that opens is not worth noticing. In the Capitol, it counts among the building's curiosities.

The window also fits a pattern that runs through the building: a feature attached to one person's post rather than offered to the public. The Senate chaplain holds a single office, and the oval window comes with that office. It does not keep anyone out or decide who goes first. It shows only whose room it is. That makes it a quieter clue than a locked door or a reserved seat. A visitor who has not heard about the window could walk the halls all day without learning it exists.

In the chaplain's office, the curiosity is a window that someone can open.

One of the Capitol's grimmest stories is true at its core: a reporter killed a former congressman inside the building. BuzzFeed News reports that the shooting happened in a Capitol stairwell, on bloodstained marble stairs. The gunman was Charles Kincaid, a correspondent for the Louisville Times, and the man he shot on Feb. 28, 1890, was William Preston Taulbee, a former Kentucky congressman, according to NPR. The two men had been enemies for years before that day. Kincaid's stories about a scandal had ruined Taulbee's career, and the first of them ran in 1887.

Kincaid went free even though he had shot a man inside the Capitol. He was charged with murder, NPR reports, and a jury acquitted him on grounds of self-defense. Taulbee's friend fled, so no eyewitness to the shooting remained, and the jury decided a case that nobody had seen happen. Kincaid stayed in journalism for the rest of his life, which ended in Cincinnati in 1906.

The bloodstain is the one part of the story nobody can prove. Accounts of the stain amount to rumor, according to NPR, and the mark is unverified as blood. A visitor should treat it as tradition rather than evidence. The facts of the scandal that started the feud were also in dispute as of 2011. Both the cause of the killing and its mark on the stairs remain open questions. The effect of the killing on how the public viewed Congress is clearer. "Concern with corruption, concern with the civility on Capitol Hill, all of a sudden became things the public was thinking about," Julian Zelizer, a Boston University historian, told NPR in 2011.

Where you eat in the Capitol depends on who you are and what time it is. Members of the House and Senate have private dining services, BuzzFeed News reports, while anyone in the building can fall back on a burger from a vending machine once the cafés close. Anyone can buy that burger, so the members' advantage is being waited on, not simply being fed. The Senate, for one, keeps a members' dining room where workers bring lunch to the table, according to The Associated Press.

Someone carries every plate in the members' room, and from the diner's seat that person is easy to overlook. A Capitol food worker who had stayed in the job longer than seven years made about $16 or $17 an hour, The Associated Press reports, and the lawmakers being served were paid $174,000 a year. The people on either side of the counter live on very different incomes, even though they spend their working days in the same rooms.

That gap follows from choices Congress made. The Senate ran its own food operations until 2008, The Associated Press reports, and the House and Senate food contracts that followed set no hourly rates. Leaving wages out of the contracts left pay to the companies that hold them. "It's really not within the scope of this committee nor subcommittee to micromanage all contracts," Rep. Tom Graves, R-Ga., said. Lawmakers did have a chance to set a floor themselves, but a living-wage amendment offered by Wasserman Schultz failed.

The tour shows a private dining room, and behind it is a rule: the building's insiders get the service, and Congress leaves the pay of the people who provide it to contractors.

Most of the U.S. Capitol is open to visitors, but Congress keeps some rooms closed to them, and one of those rooms is set aside for prayer. BuzzFeed News reports that the members-only chapel is a locked room where lawmakers alone may retreat for quiet prayer and guidance. Congress reserves the room for its own members, so getting inside depends on holding a seat in the House or Senate. Use of the chapel is a privilege of membership rather than a service the building offers the public.

The chapel's stated purpose ties a member's private faith to the public's work instead of keeping the two apart. "Its only purpose is to provide a quiet place to which individual senators and representatives may withdraw a while to seek divine strength and guidance, both in public affairs and in their own personal concerns," the Architect of the Capitol said, as reported by BuzzFeed News. By that description, lawmakers may weigh decisions that affect the whole country in a room the public never enters. The chapel is one small place in the Capitol where members of Congress may think through public business entirely out of public view.

For a visitor, the chapel shows that the tour covers the public face of the Capitol and not the whole building. A visitor may walk past its door but cannot open it. That locked door marks where the visitors' Capitol ends and the lawmakers' Capitol begins, and the boundary holds even for a room meant for conscience.

A member's departure from Congress ends with boxes waiting for pickup. When members finish their time in Congress, their belongings are boxed up and held until the owners come to collect them, according to BuzzFeed News. The process begins with the end of a member's service, whatever the member feels about leaving. Until the owner returns for them, the boxes stay behind in the building the member has left. A former lawmaker's exit is not finished until the last box is collected.

The election calendar explains why the clear-out works this way. Outgoing lawmakers empty their offices during the same weeks that newcomers go through orientation, The Associated Press reports, and the two groups share the halls while it happens. More than 100 new members arrive before the new Congress is sworn in on Jan. 3. That leaves a large share of offices to be emptied and handed over against a date nobody can push back. The boxed belongings of departing members are the visible side of that deadline.

The cardboard looks the same for everyone, but the people it belongs to are leaving for very different reasons. The House meets the new term with "some of us enjoying victories and others enduring disheartening defeats," House Chaplain Margaret Kibben said, as reported by The Associated Press. The boxing draws no line between those groups. It covers every member whose time in Congress ends, however that time ended. Some of those belongings are never claimed, and those pieces of a career are left behind.

For a building that guards its privileges closely, the last privilege of membership is having your things held for you, and some former members never use it.

Visitors remember the Capitol as a building that rises, with its dome above the city and its marble steps below, but the stranger fact is how much of it lies below ground. There are "miles and miles" of tunnels beneath the Capitol, according to BuzzFeed News. That phrase conveys sheer size rather than a measured length, and it gives no dates or purpose for the passages. The scale is the reason the tunnels count among the building's surprises. Congress works in one of the most public buildings in the country, yet a large part of that building lies where visitors never go. Staff members also ride private trains underground, but a reader shouldn't assume those tracks make up the miles of tunnel. The trains and the tunnels are separate pieces of the Capitol's underside.

The tunnels are best understood as a hidden layer whose full reach is still an open question, not as a system with known dimensions. A visitor can walk the rotunda and the galleries in an afternoon and still have seen only the top layer of the building. That fits the pattern of the whole tour. The Capitol a visitor walks through is the version Congress puts on display, and much of the working building lies past it, out of sight and largely unmapped for the public.

Beneath the most famous skyline in Washington is a second Capitol that almost nobody sees.

Anyone who wants a name painted on wood should run for Congress, according to BuzzFeed News. The advice is lighthearted, but it ties even a modest honor to a seat in Congress. A visitor pass doesn't qualify a person for it, and neither does a staff badge. A candidate first has to persuade voters and win an election, and only then does the painted name come within reach.

The gap between the effort and the reward makes the line funny. A congressional campaign asks a great deal of anyone who tries it, and the prize offered here is a name in paint on a wooden surface. Most people would not go through a race for office to get something so plain. Nobody expects a voter or a candidate to take that trade seriously. Still, the small prize makes a real point about the Capitol, because it treats winning a seat as the way to get anything in the building that bears your own name.

For a visitor, the painted name is the lightest example of a rule found across the Capitol. Many of the building's privileges are serious, and this one is small enough to joke about. It follows the same rule as the others, though: the honor belongs to the people who won their seats.

Congress, a body that answers to voters, keeps George Washington painted as a god above the heads of everyone who enters its building. A painting inside the Capitol dome shows the first president as a divine figure rather than as a soldier or a statesman, and BuzzFeed News places it sixth in its countdown of 21 little-known facts about the Capitol. Every senator and representative who walks beneath it holds office only until the next election, yet the man pictured above them is raised beyond the reach of any vote.

This quirk differs from most of the Capitol's strange features because people were meant to notice it. The members' perks, the closed doors and the old rules that outlived their purpose were never designed for an audience. The painting was made for visitors to look up at. It reveals nothing about who may enter or who goes first. Instead, it shows the image of itself that the institution chose to put on public display. For once, the Capitol asks nothing of a visitor except to look up. A visitor who does should see a deliberate choice: Congress honored its founding leader in the language of worship, not in the language of public office.

A visitor standing in the Capitol's crypt is in a room built for a man who never came to rest there. BuzzFeed News reports that the crypt was meant to hold George Washington's body, yet he is not entombed there. Washington wanted his grave at his own estate, Mt. Vernon, so he declined the tomb that the Capitol's builders had made ready for him. The empty room is the trace of that refusal. The young country wanted to claim its founder, and he said no.

The planners went further than drawing up a tomb, and workers got as far as digging openings for a chamber from which people could view it. That detail shows the planners meant Washington's resting place to be seen, not just housed. They built the chance to look at the founder's grave into the structure itself. The planners could prepare that sight, but they could not provide it without Washington's consent. His decision kept the view the crypt was built around out of public reach.

Below the room built to honor the past lies a space built for catastrophe, a nuclear fallout shelter beneath the crypt. The two levels answer to very different concerns. The crypt was shaped around a single revered man, while the shelter was built with a nuclear disaster in mind. One room looks back to the country's first president, and the other prepares for a danger no one in his time could have pictured.

The Capitol's ceremonial center rests on two plans, one to honor the nation's founder and one for its worst day, and the founder himself turned down the first.

Some oddities in the Capitol last because nobody has explained them, not because they serve a rule. Just outside the old Supreme Court chamber, the floor carries the tracks of a cat, and no one has offered an account of how they were made. The prints were already in the stone before it was brought to the Capitol, according to BuzzFeed News, so they came into the building with the stone rather than being made inside it. Some people believe a ghost left them, and that legend has a name of its own, "the Ghost Cat of the Crypt." The name turns a puzzle in the floor into part of the building's shared lore.

Much of the Capitol records who may enter and who goes first, but this mark records something else: what the people who work and visit there choose to say about the place. A stone that arrived with the prints already in it leaves room for an ordinary cause, yet some people still prefer the ghost. That preference suggests a legend can matter more to a building's sense of itself than a plain answer would. A visitor should read the prints that way. They are not evidence of anything supernatural. They show how a working building turns its unexplained corners into tradition over time.

In the Capitol, the rules are written down, and the legends are set in the floor.

The Capitol's dome holds marks left by the people with the least standing in the building. BuzzFeed News reports that interns, the most junior workers on Capitol Hill, left graffiti above the dome. The structure built to honor the republic carries, out of the public's sight, the writing of its newest arrivals. Interns cast no votes and hold no office, yet some of them left a trace on one of the best-known structures in Washington. That makes the graffiti different from the painted names and reserved rooms elsewhere in the Capitol. Those features mark the standing that Congress grants its members, while the graffiti marks something the interns did on their own.

The graffiti also says something about how people who work in the Capitol think of it. Interns pass through Congress quickly, and few people outside the building remember their names. Writing on the structure above the rotunda gives them a lasting place in a building that otherwise reserves its privileges for members. Most of the Capitol's oddities record rules that members set for everyone else, from who rides which elevator to who eats in which dining room. The graffiti records the reverse: the workers with the least power leaving a mark without asking anyone's permission.

The grandest part of the Capitol also keeps a record of the people who held the least power inside it.

Reporters who cover the Capitol have a painting that honors them, even though they spend their days in a building full of rules about who may enter and who goes first. The press corps has more than reserved seats in the galleries, because Capitol reporters have a painting of their own. BuzzFeed News places the painting at No. 2 in its countdown of 21 facts about the Capitol. "Being a reporter at the Capitol isn't all that bad," Benny Johnson of BuzzFeed staff said, pointing to the tribute. A building that controls so carefully what the public sees also holds a tribute to the people who report on it. Seen that way, reporters are more than visitors to be managed. They hold a recognized place in the institution they cover.

Congress keeps its elevators, subways and dining rooms for insiders, yet the people who report its business to everyone else have a tribute of their own. Reporters belong to both groups. Their job is to question and scrutinize members from the outside, but they also work in the halls every day, wait at the same doors and come to know the corridors well. The painting reflects that double standing. It honors people who answer to readers and viewers rather than to Congress, in a building where most privileges go to members. For a visitor, the lesson is plain. The guided tour shows the Capitol as Congress chooses to present it, but much of what the public learns about Congress comes from the reporters this painting honors.

Movement abilities in Metroidvanias are a fundamental part of their appeal, determining both the pace of exploration and our ability to interact with the surrounding world.

The genre is built on understanding spaces, their composition, and how to traverse them, making movement a crucial variable regardless of the game's perspective, mechanics, or philosophy.

As a result, the possibilities a game enables for movement along the Cartesian plane largely determine how satisfying, tense, or rewarding a campaign will be, depending on its intended purpose.

Therefore, in order to pay homage to those video games that make the best possible use of the philosophy of moving from one place to another, here are the ten most creative traversal abilities in Metroidvanias.

One of the main problems games in this genre face when creating spectacular movement abilities lies in their limitations, as they have to be balanced so that they aren't too powerful.

For this reason, Silk Soar from Hollow Knight: Silksong is a masterpiece of design. Among all the powers in Metroidvanias that let you fly upwards, it's part of the few that properly contextualize the limits of its capabilities.

Not only do you acquire it in the game's last third, unexpectedly opening paths out of nowhere, but its use is also tied to your needle, requiring the presence of a ceiling to be used, so you can't just break the map's traversal in its entirety.

In terms of creativity, it's on the spectrum's simpler side, yet it solves a major verticality imbalance by granting you a great deal of ease in exploration without making you overpowered, which is a tremendous feat.

Going in the opposite direction from the previous entry, Pipistrello and the Cursed Yoyo is a constant display of reinterpreting Metroidvanias' most common forms of movement, with Walk the Dog being the crown jewel.

The game plays with yo-yo tricks to establish its mechanics, adapting them with thematic coherence to make you feel that, more than just having powers within a video game, you're learning new maneuvers to show off.

In this particular instance, it's as simple as being able to move orthogonally across the map at a greater speed, but this is maximized thanks to the bounce pads distributed throughout all areas that change your direction and make the ability the key to numerous puzzles.

Pipistrello and the Cursed Yoyo is brimming with ideas magnificently adapted to its setting, making it by far one of the most inventive Metroidvanias to come out this decade.

Well Dweller breaks one of the genre's cardinal rules by not including a double jump at any point in its story, though it compensates with a Slingshot mechanic that is simply mesmerizing.

The title allows you to throw your weapon and embed it in any surface to use it as a springboard and jump higher than usual, encouraging inquisitive and creative exploration in a truly compelling way.

Most of the game's movement is based on the biomes you find yourself in, whether in the form of bubbles, air currents, or elevators, and it works precisely because this direct yet versatile ability adapts to any situation.

The tension between caregiving obligations and work responsibilities is often framed as a women’s issue. But it’s not just working mothers who find themselves in a bind: Plenty of men say they not only value caregiving but want to do more of it. So what’s holding them back?

A new report from the Better Life Lab at the think tank New America—which publishes research on workplace equity and family policy—indicates that men face some of the same hurdles that working women do, from limited flexibility or autonomy to a lack of structural support. 

Compared to 2019, men are taking more time off from work or reducing their work hours due to caregiving responsibilities, especially those who earn less or have children with special needs. At the same time, they continue to grapple with the kind of cultural stigma that can keep them from taking paid leave even when they’re entitled to it. 

The report’s findings suggest that many men actually want to spend more time on caregiving: In fact, a surprising number of fathers—six in ten—expressed an interest in staying home full-time. Men who helped care for adults or children with special needs were even more interested in being home. Over two-thirds of the men surveyed claim to think caregiving is as important as paid work, and that both parents should contribute equally. But the reality is quite different: Only about 40% of respondents who are caregivers and parents said they actually split those responsibilities equally.  

There are a number of potential explanations for this disparity. Over a quarter of dads said there are barriers to being the kind of parent they want to be, from their job to their financial situation; male caregivers of adults or children with special needs reported feeling this at similar rates. Men with higher-paying jobs—earning over $100,000—were even more likely to report experiencing those barriers. 

As the Better Life Lab points out, however, the data seems to suggest that men are actually underreporting the extent to which work obligations actually impact their ability to show up as a parent or caregiver. 

A third of fathers said they have “no control” over their schedules; when men reported having more control over their hours, that usually meant they couldn’t dictate which days they worked, and vice versa. The majority of parents and caregivers—74%—said they would prefer to work remotely or with a hybrid arrangement if they could, but only 40% of them currently do. Whether they were parents or caregivers, only about a fifth of respondents expressed a desire to work out of an office (though men are more likely to have a preference for the office.) Nearly two-thirds of fathers also said work obligations interfered with their family time. 

Perhaps the most interesting data point is that men are missing work or slashing their work hours at higher rates—a sign that men may be facing the same pressures that women have for many years. Among men who care for adults or children with special needs, the share who missed work due to care obligations rose from 64% in 2019 to 76% as of this year. About half of men also reported cutting back on their work hours, up from 42% in 2019.

As Fast Company reported recently, there has also been a noticeable shift in how men take time off when they become parents. Men are finally taking more parental leave, in part due to the rise of paid leave policies at the state level. Until recently, the data indicated that men took little time off, maybe a week at most, and often through some combination of sick days or vacation days or unpaid time off. 

But recent research shows that dads are filing just as many leave claims as moms in states like California, Colorado, and Washington, which have robust paid leave programs. In states that have had these programs for many years, leave claims from men have only increased, while states that introduced paid leave more recently have seen higher uptake from the start. 

In other words, it seems clear that when men have good access to paid leave, they’re more likely to take it—a pattern that also lines up with the Better Life Lab’s findings on how workplace culture can shape the caregiver experience. When fathers took leave, they overwhelmingly cited a supportive workplace as a reason (72%); many also pointed to a paid leave policy through their employer or state policy (63%). 

Still, even as men say they want to be more present, it is still women who bear the greatest burden of caregiving strain. Women are more likely to sacrifice their careers due to caregiving responsibilities, and this happens at higher rates when they are mothers. As the report’s findings suggest, women are also more quick to self-identify as caregivers and place more value on care work. 

While the culture around caregiving has shifted to some degree—especially with more men now taking leave and stepping up to care for aging parents—it’s clear that both men and women aren’t happy with the status quo.
