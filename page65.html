Rose Byrne To Head 2027 Tropfest Jury

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

EXCLUSIVE: Rose Byrne will head the jury at the 2027 Tropfest.

The long-running short film festival will take place on February 13, 2027, in The Domain in Sydney. Byrne previously served as the Tropfest jury head in 2017.

This year, the Tropfest films will stream globally on YouTube. The festival will feature as a week-long cultural event titled TropWeek. Programming across the week includes a three-day educational program at the National Institute of Dramatic Art (NIDA) exploring the craft and business of filmmaking, supported by Screen Australia and YouTube. The week culminates in a free day-to-night festival in The Domain on February 13, with live entertainment, followed by the evening screening of 16 world premiere shorts. Competition winners will be announced live on the night and via the YouTube livestream. Watch on Deadline

Tropfest film submissions open on Tuesday 1 December and close on Tuesday 5 January 2027. Filmmakers are required to create a short film of seven minutes or less featuring the 2027 Tropfest signature item: a necklace.

Prizes for winning filmmakers include a share of the CommBank Tropfest Emerging Filmmakers Fund’s $100,000. First, second, and third prizes will be $50,000, $30,000, and $20,000, respectively.

The Tropfest Foundation, a not-for-profit organisation launched in 2025, is led by a board comprising Chair Sarah Murdoch, Bryan Brown, Peter V’landys, Richard Weinberg and John Polson. Britt Wickes serves as Executive Director.

“I have such fond memories of watching great shorts at Tropfest with thousands of people under the stars in my hometown of Sydney,” Byrne said in a statement. “I’m thrilled to be back to lead the jury in 2027 and can’t wait to see what stories Australia’s next generation of filmmakers will tell.”

It was all change over the summer for the Futuro as they brought in Sergio Navarro as the head coach. The idea was to create some continuity with Ruben Amorim’s more continental style of football, and the pedigree that the Spaniard has suggested it was a positive move.

On November 7, the 3-0 derby defeat to Inter – which included a red card for Milan – served only to highlight the big gulf between the two teams at the moment.

Key players: Christy Grimshaw remains the shining light for Milan Women, with three goals and an assist to her name. The scoring charts then see Sara Stokic on three goals, with Sara Fornes, Park Soojeong and Monica Renzotti all netting twice.

Grimshaw is one of three players to start every game so far under Bakker, with the others being defenders Angelica Soffia and Nerea Nevado. Sandra Estevez has emerged as the number one between the sticks too.

How NBC’s Longtime Page Program Has Served as a Key Stepping Stone to Media Leadership

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Katie Hockmeyer now oversees “Saturday Night Live” as NBC’s executive vice president of late night. But she still can’t forget her first brush with the sketch comedy staple: As an NBC page assigned to the floor.

That’s right, Hockmeyer was a real-life Kenneth the Page, just like the one played by Jack McBrayer at the “TGS” production offices on “30 Rock.” In 2002, Hockmeyer was the NBC desk page at “SNL,” right when it was populated by some of the show’s all-time greats — including Tina Fey (who eventually created “30 Rock”), Maya Rudolph, Jimmy Fallon, Amy Poehler, Fred Armisen and Will Forte. Related Stories Amazon Debuts $30 Streaming Bundle That Includes Starz, BritBox, AMC+, MGM+ and PBS Masterpiece In One Place Alycia Baumgardner vs. Elif Nur Turhan on ESPN Unlimited: Here’s Where to Watch the Title Bout Live Online

“It was incredible,” she says. One of her duties was to escort that week’s guest host around 30 Rock. “I remember walking Mick Jagger from down in the basement up to Lorne Michaels’ office as a page. The idea that someone would say, ‘I designate you to walk Mick Jagger’ is so crazy to me! But they trust you to learn to be professional out of the gate. Honestly, it was an amazing experience, and I learned very quickly that I like being in a live environment with comedians.”

Hockmeyer clearly impressed the “SNL” team: After her stint as a page was over, she was asked to interview as one of Michaels’ assistants. She got the job — and eventually worked her way up to running the entire late night department at NBC.

“I only hire pages,” she says. “It’s the best post-collegiate program. You talk about the history of company. You basically meet everyone in the building. The access that you have is pretty incredible.”

Hockmeyer is one of many success stories to come out of the NBC page program. Other notable alum include former Disney CEO Michael Eisner, news broadcaster Ted Koppel, “Beef ” creator Lee Sung Jin, “The View” co-host Sara Haines, late TV host Regis Philbin, “Charlie’s Angels” star Kate Jackson and actress Aubrey Plaza (who even wore her old NBC page uniform on stage while hosting “SNL” in 2023).

NBC’s page program dates back to 1933, during the golden age of radio, and began as a way to assist in guest relations, ushering, tour services and more at the thenbrand new Rockefeller Plaza.

But over the past decade, the NBC page gig has become much more of a structured career-learning experience that acts like a paid apprenticeship with a beginning, middle and end. The highly competitive program selects around 22 to 25 people in New York and 12 to 15 in Los Angeles three times a year for what’s become a TV business and NBC boot camp.

According to Priany Hadiatmodjo, senior director of the NBC page program, around 3,000 people apply for each cycle to get one of those coveted spots.

“It’s a pretty rigorous process to get in,” she says. “We read every single application. There’s a video interview component that comes next, where you have to answer some questions that help us understand who you are. And then the final stage of the application process is a panel interview. The process is not for the faint of heart. A lot of people apply multiple times. Sometimes they get in on their second or even third try. I don’t think there’ll ever be a shortage of people who are really eager to work in this industry. But once you’re here, the expectation to excel is probably harder than the actual process itself.”

After an immersive two-week onboarding where they meet with and learn from NBCU leadership, pages are assigned to different portions of the business for 18 weeks at a time. The divisions where pages can work has been greatly expanded over the past 10 years to all facets of NBCU, and pages can express an interest in which divisions they’d like to work.

“I always like to say that if my team and I are doing our jobs right, the page program serves as a microcosm of NBCU,” says Hadiatmodjo. “Some people have called it the most intense masters in media that you could get. We’re more intentional about the assignments we offer, and those assignments are typically within parts of the company that are very future-focused. Because the hope is that we’re finding the best and brightest out there, and that they’ll one day become leaders of this industry. So we’re trying to put them into spaces, places and environments that will really challenge them daily and give them the experience of what it’s really like to work at NBCU.”

Once an NBC page has completed a year in the program and worked in three different departments, they can start to apply for jobs inside the company.

“By then they’ve hopefully been on a path of self-discovery and figured out some things that they really love doing,” Hadiatmodjo adds. “It really is a year of self-discovery and experimentation, and really understanding what this industry is all about. Our responsibility is making sure they’re ready to hit the ground running in their next early career role. Our ultimate goal is to find that talent, cultivate it, and hopefully, see them stick around.”

And that’s come true throughout the company, with execs like Hockmeyer. More than 20 years after she was a page, the experience is still top of mind. “I could give you a tour of 30 Rock right now,” she says. “I can tell you about the floating studio floor in 8H, where Arturo Toscanini used to perform. I can still remember a couple of my key talking points!”

‘No one asked for this’: AI is being pushed on people—and they’re sick of it

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The AI era is fully here—and while companies bill it as a solution to problems, caveats like mass layoffs, environmental effects, privacy concerns and the sloppification of culture have many people fed up with something they never desired in the first place.

The technology faces growing resentment from workers, who are being urged to embrace AI tools even as companies have rushed to reduce their workforces in favor of automation. 

Online, many posts about AI have a common refrain: no one asked for these tools or the disruptions they’re causing. 

“Nobody asked for Google to be worse—but AI made it happen,” one X user recently remarked. “No one asked for computer hardware costs to rise—but AI made it happen. No one asked for human customer service to be replaced—but AI made it happen.”

In another post, Sarah Hoyt, a science fiction, fantasy and mystery author, said she doesn’t hate AI, but is “begging all the crazy people out there to stop pushing AI where no one asked for it.”

The disenchantment plays out in the data: In 2019, 81% of Glassdoor reviews mentioning AI were positive, the company said, compared to 43% this year. Meanwhile, Pew Research found that 52% of Americans were more concerned than excited about AI, up from 37% in 2021; and in an Ipsos poll from June, 67% of respondents thought AI would worsen the worker experience.

“The closest analog is the replacement of physical labor throughout the long Industrial Revolution,” said Jiemin Tina Wei, an assistant professor of history at Florida International University. But in this case, it’s “brain work,” rather than manual labor, that’s being replaced. 

“And now, the industries that are getting allegedly wiped out are the ‘low-hanging fruit’ of white-collar work,” she said.

But livelihoods being gutted isn’t the only reason many are anti-AI.

The productivity paradox

Louis Hyman, a historian at Johns Hopkins University who specializes in labor and the American economy, points to a “productivity paradox”: a contradiction that was also generally true of the rise of computers in the ‘80s, in which similarly dizzying investments didn’t necessarily make people better at their jobs as promised.

Hyman points to snags like the frequency of software updates: that “every time you learn how to do something, suddenly they break it, and you have to relearn it.” With the introduction of AI, it’s “as if all software is being updated, all at once, everywhere.” 

Another part of the equation, he said, is that it isn’t clear how some people are using AI tools.

Hyman said he uses AI to write software and automate “boring tasks”—for example, he wrote a script to rotate the thousands of photos he takes in archives so they all face right-side-up. But, he said, “most people are not wanting to sit down and write Python code to automate their job. They just want to do their job.” 

Added to that is the deep fear of being replaced once the work is automated.

“I’m a tenured professor—I literally can’t be fired,” he said. “If I automate some boring part of my job, that just means I have more time to do the more interesting parts of my job. And that’s not true for all people, right?” 

A new occupational hazard

Another reason many people push back on AI is the lack of adequate guardrails—especially when it could cause major issues at work.

In one case, an AI tool deleted a software company’s entire database. In another, AI was blamed for deleting an entirely edited season of a Swedish reality show mere weeks before the scheduled premiere. In medical settings, AI scribes have put inaccurate information in patient records, raising the question of who would be held liable for resulting medical errors. 

Meanwhile, misreads by AI identification tools used by law enforcement have led to wrongful arrests and injuries, per the MIT AI Risk Initiative. Other AI-generated errors have popped up in court documents, federal reports, news stories and fast food drive-thrus. 

“The main point I’ve pushed and will continue to push re: AI is ‘no one asked for this,’ ” radio host Adam Xii said on X, in response to news that Anthropic hallucinated details in their defense during a music copyright lawsuit. “So when a product or service no one asked for suddenly becomes ubiquitous, one must ask: why? Because AI tools are meant to generate more wealth for the wealthy at the expense of creatives.”

At the least, AI’s convenience is being offset by a need to check its work that has created new opportunities for human error. The proliferation of “workslop” even led a tech company’s CEO to fully ban AI use, as Inc. reported this summer. 

“A lot of what I’ve seen is kind of annoyance at the proselytizers at work—or the people who are heavy adopters—creating a lot of extra work for the people around them,” because those people downstream then have to check the work, said Wei.

Wei said that content moderation and data annotation work “can be extremely mentally taxing.” Research has found that “good” challenging work will be both difficult and interesting to certain extents, she said. But checking output is both difficult, because you have to look for every small potential error, and boring.

“You’re not producing knowledge, but merely supervising the production of words, knowledge, content,” Wei said.

The unwanted technological advances continue

AI’s intrusive nature—accompanied, again, by the threat of replacement—is reflected in workers’ behavior, not just in fed-up tweetstorms. One report found that nearly a third of workers surveyed, and 44% among Gen Z, had tried to sabotage their companies’ AI strategies.

Section, an AI planning and upskilling company,  recently reported that 69% of workers surveyed said their organization had encouraged the use of AI agents, but only 16% actually used them. Thirty-eight percent said they use other AI features.

The frustration hasn’t seemed to deter tech companies.

“We heard you wanted Copilot Mode at work,” read an X post from Microsoft that promoted Edge’s Copilot Mode for Business.

“No, you heard wrong,” a computer technician replied. “Literally no one asked for all this AI. In fact, everyone wants to know how to remove it.” 

Questions of organizations

In some cases, it seems companies are starting to discover that automation has some shortcomings.

In the U.S., 32% of hiring managers who eliminated positions in favor of AI have had to reinstate the same or similar roles, according to research from consulting firm Robert Half. Similarly, Gartner, Inc. predicts that by 2027, half of companies that attributed reductions in staff to AI will rehire people to perform similar work.

Wei said the mismatch between the hopes for AI and the reality of its adoption relates to a quote from Bill Haywood, a famous labor organizer, who said that “the manager’s brains are under the workman’s cap.”

In other words, she said, “it’s really ultimately the workers who possess the knowledge and the know-how to run day-to-day operations. Some people have argued that the history of labor is just managers trying to take back that knowledge in an attempt to replace workers in the production.”

“And I guess some people experience AI that way—that it’s being [imposed] upon them so that they train, ultimately, their final, automated replacement.”

Hyman said that a better alternative to the “adapt or die” mentality would be for organizations to use the technology to empower their employees. They should “actually say, ‘okay, we’re gonna create organizational conditions that let you replace yourself, and then focus on the work that needs to be done’—because the amount of work to be done is not finite, right?”

“I get told on a nearly weekly basis that historians are going to be replaced,” Hyman said. “So, yeah: it depends on how valuable you think getting reliable information and history is.”

He added that a world “where the only source of history or journalism is from either Open AI or Anthropic” would feel like an “Orwellian nightmare.”

“That seems not a great road to go down,” he said.

Why 2 Pinterest vets and a neurosurgeon are building a Reddit for doctors

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Medicine’s complexity and ever-evolving nature means doctors will always have more to learn and discuss—but in the digital age, there’s also a question of where those conversations can best take place.

Online, healthcare workers’ discourse is distributed across various social media platforms, forums, and group chats. Meanwhile, offline, medical professionals are “hyper-specialized,” with limited opportunities to work with colleagues from other disciplines, according to Dr. Rohan Ramakrishna, chief of neurosurgery at New York-Presbyterian Brooklyn Methodist Hospital.

As a neurosurgeon who specializes in brain tumors, he said, “the doctors I interact with on a regular basis are radiation oncologists and neuro-oncologists, and that’s it.” Even hospital doctors’ lounges are disappearing, he added, removing another opportunity for doctors to socialize and discuss medicine.

So Ramakrishna teamed up with Vikram Bhaskaran and Arun Ranganathan—two Pinterest alums who previously led engineering teams related to creators, content, and partners—to cofound an app that could foster that sense of community. 

Initially, the trio envisioned Roon as a place for doctors to provide patients with information; but in the app’s early days, Bhaskaran said, they found that physicians were especially interested in seeing what their own peers had to say—so they decided to rework the platform to facilitate those interactions.

Now, a year after its launch, the platform is adding features that bring Pinterest’s focus on sharing to the thousands of physicians on the platform to share everything from treatment advice to conference photos. As social media apps shift and online misinformation proliferates, the founders are hoping Roon will help physicians separate the signal from the noise.

Where doctors gather online

In some ways, Roon’s concept echoes that of #MedTwitter, which became a widely used, informal hub for medical experts and others to exchange information, ideas, and opinions, creating “a completely open-source environment that democratizes medical information,” as two physicians wrote in 2021. The hashtag still exists, and some continue to use it, but Twitter’s transition into X—and the accompanying changes—scattered some of  #MedTwitter to other platforms.

But medical science is always evolving, Ramakrishna said, as are politics and policy changes, and doctors lack a “home” for making sense of it all. Roon is “a place where you have net new knowledge creation, because people are trying to unpack what it all means, and understand it together.” 

To help shape Roon’s knowledge-creation network, the company enlisted more than 400 “founding physicians” from a range of specialties and institutions, who the company said “are owners with equity in Roon.” 

“A year ago, we had kind of a thesis: that physicians should have a place online where they can collaborate, connect, and that was safe and private,” said Bhaskaran, and it’s “been really heartening” to see how physicians are now using Roon. 

Creating community for a core audience

Roon joins a growing number of communications platforms designed for clinicians. Doximity, a networking service that offers an AI scribe and other tools, says more than 85% of U.S. physicians are registered members. There’s also Sermo, Figure 1, Mednet, Doquity—which is used by clinicians across much of Asia—and other new ventures like Marit Health, where medical professionals can share salary data. 

Each of them varies in form and function. Roon, which has the benefit of being designed by Pinterest veterans, is built to look and feel like mainstream social media sites. On it, users discuss new research, policy changes, and health topics that emerge in the news cycle, Ramakrishna said, and ask for advice on patient care. For example, he said, a family medicine doctor who posed a question related to reproductive health and the pituitary gland got replies from peers in family medicine, OB-GYN, high-risk maternal-fetal medicine, neurosurgery and neurology. 

There’s also an ability to form private groups—for example, Ramakrishna said, there’s one for chief medical officers, and another for residential program directors—and a new, Pinterest-board-inspired “Collections” feature that lets users group related content and media together. 

Selective, intentionally

Roon also differs from most medical communications apps in that, for the time being, it’s exclusive to physicians—M.D.s and D.O.s—who are licensed in the U.S.

Bhaskaran says the criteria mean that Roon can function as a place to share expertise built in the field—something that augments published literature in an important way. 

“There’s so much of medicine that’s outside of the published literature, that’s not accessible via chatbot or via AI: that’s in people’s heads,” he says. 

Roon currently has tens of thousands of users, Bhaskaran says, noting the goal is to reach 250,000 by the end of next year. That would constitute almost a quarter of the 1.08 million physicians that were licensed in the U.S. as of 2024. 

Part of the challenge of growing is the fragmentation of physicians across various social media apps. Some physicians also say they value the diverse perspectives offered by other healthcare workers, like nurses and pharmacists, and by those in other countries. 

They do plan to invite certain non-physician specialties, like infectious disease scientists, Bhaskaran added, but they still have work to do to build up the community of physicians, which remains their first goal. They also plan to expand Roon internationally, “but again, very thoughtfully, because verification is just a little bit of a hard problem once you leave the United States.” In the meantime, the company has a waitlist for medical professionals who’d like to join.

Human amid AI

Roon currently employs AI for tasks like filling out physicians’ profiles, routing users’ questions to specialists, and generating summaries of content. Its team is also working on new AI-powered functions to keep users up-to-date, like an audio digest feature that summarizes medical news and related Roon posts.

Then, there’s the training question: a database of medical information based on real-life expertise would be valuable for training LLMs, and physicians on X have expressed concern about it being used that way. A company spokesperson said Roon “currently has no plans to sell its data,” though the company may use it to refine its own models, and hasn’t made a “definitive commitment ruling out all future uses involving third-party LLMs.” 

But in the face of AI’s all-consuming nature, “our thesis is actually [that] the human network will become more valuable over time, especially as most of the world goes, ‘hey, we’re unclear who wrote this content,’ or ‘everything you see on the internet is AI slop,'” Bhaskaran says. ”We’re taking kind of an old school bet—which is, there’ll be value for community, and that’ll transcend any specific technology.”

How can districts keep CTE programs relevant as tech evolves?

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The rapid pace of change can make it difficult for even employers to have clear answers for workplace readiness, an industry expert says.

How can leaders of high school career and technical education programs ensure those offerings remain relevant in preparing students to adapt in a rapidly changing workforce?

“That question is one everyone is trying to answer right now. I’m not sure there is one clear answer,” said Alisha Hyslop, chief policy, research and content officer at the Association for Career and Technical Education.

Hyslop said the main piece of advice for educators is to work closely with businesses and industries to ensure programs are preparing students with the skills they’ll need to be successful — and that’s probably the same, at least on the surface, as it would have been 10 or 20 years ago.

But there are important distinctions in 2026, Hyslop said.

Because technology is changing so rapidly, employers don’t always have a clear answer for workplace readiness approaches, she said.

Hyslop said ACTE is also hearing that students need to understand basic, foundational artificial intelligence skills and how to use them. Additionally, students should continue to "build up durable skills," which is "really critical," she said.

Despite employers’ own lack of clarity at times, regular conversations should definitely continue, Hyslop said.

“There should be a give-and-take between the CTE educators, and folks working on curriculum and instruction in CTE, and the employer community,” she said. And although technology will continue to rapidly evolve, “students are still going to need critical thinking and communications skills, and the ability to work in teams, and evaluate information — and all of those foundational skills that apply across technologies.”

CTE programs focused on information technology are changing the most rapidly, although Hyslop said she has seen plenty of research to show that across the spectrum of career fields, new AI tools are being implemented and deployed. One example is the use of the technology in the agriculture field to monitor soil health and predict crop growth, she said.

“We had some examples from a school in Bentonville [Ark.] that was using AI in health science programs. They had created a meal planning app for mothers with gestational diabetes, or for pregnant women who could get answers to specific questions; those were joint projects between healthcare and technology students," Hyslop said. "There were lots of applications in digital media, and lots of ethical considerations that students are exploring as they go through that process.”

While some industries are more impacted by the advent of AI and other changes in the workplace than others, at first glance, people might think it’s mainly an issue in technology classes, Hyslop said. “And that is not the case. Because we’re seeing applications across career areas, once students get into the workplace.”

During a panel discussion at a recent ACTE conference focused on the use of AI in CTE programs, Hyslop said, students emphasized how important it was that they learned foundational skills first.

One student pointed out the approach of using a calculator to solve math problems, she recalled. “But that’s not how we teach students in kindergarten. We teach them basic mathematical functions first, and then introduce the calculator to do more complicated things.”

By learning the foundational level skills in CTE programs, they could evaluate the answers given by AI more competently, the students said. For instance, a student studying automotive mechanics could examine AI-generated instructions on how to fix cars.

“If you had no real understanding of those instructions, you wouldn’t know if it made a mistake,” she said. “That was a really important takeaway from that panel — that even students realized that AI, or whatever the next technology is, is still going to need people to instruct, evaluate and modify all of these technologies.”

29 states will require finance course in high school by 2031, report predicts

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Ten states received an “A” for requiring standalone personal finance courses in the National Endowment for Financial Education’s latest report.

“We’re excited about the progress this year’s report shows,” said Taylor Stanek, managing director of policy and advocacy for NEFE. “There is momentum and movement behind the effort to create a national expectation that students receive financial education in high school.”

The center's research involved reviews of each states' approach to personal finance education in high schools, state academic standards on the topic, and related laws, guidelines and regulations.

Broadly speaking, states with an “A” grade have shown they are implementing financial education with fidelity, while those receiving a “B” or “C” offer some level of financial education but short of a full semester course. These states also may have some degree of requirements in statewide academic standards.

States receiving a “D” or “F” have little or nothing to offer at the moment, Stanek said, expressing optimism that all 50 states will receive an “A” eventually.

“There are a few things we are keeping a close eye on as states move forward,” he said. “Implementation is key; passing one of these requirements is just a start."

The states also need to make sure schools are offering the courses as expected, as a standalone commitment, with teachers who have been trained, and have curricular materials available to meet the needs of specific students, Stanek said.

"This is an opportunity for every state … to make sure they’re evaluating the effectiveness of these programs and maximizing their benefit and impact,” Stanek said. Each state takes a somewhat different approach based on their unique range of needs, he added.

Utah implemented the first semester-long financial education requirement in 2008 and conducts statewide audits on programs, while Missouri was the second state in 2010, Stanek said. Even states with “C” grades or lower have seen progress in certain localities, with Denver Public Schools adopting this ahead of the state of Colorado, for example, he said.

“In the majority of other states, there’s at least something in place,” he added. “It’s ensuring that it’s offered across the board — requiring it to make sure every student receives it. When it’s an elective, students of color, low-income students and female students are less likely to take it.”

The figures from the 2025 national opinion poll underscore why the momentum has grown, especially at a time of economic uncertainty, Stanek said. “There’s a recognition that people need extra support to be [financially] successful,” he said.

“This is no longer a niche side topic. Folks are saying this is something worthy of being a core academic discipline in our K-12 system.”

The previous five editions of the report were published by the Center for Financial Literacy at Champlain College in Burlington, Vt.

OBM and OCEAN bring DATUM to Foreman

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

CHEYENNE, Wyo., Nov. 18, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- OBM and OCEAN today announced DATUM on Foreman, helping miners reduce stale shares and protect their margins through the industry-leading fleet-management platform they already use. The integration distributes work locally and gives miners building their own templates access to OCEAN's 1% pool fee, half its standard rate.

DATUM on Foreman, helping miners reduce stale shares and protect their margins through the fleet-management platform.

Foreman gives operators control over the systems that determine their fleet's performance. From monitoring hashrate and power to coordinating curtailment and responding to issues automatically, it helps miners keep equipment productive and sites running efficiently. Foreman's Trusted Pools and Pool Change Approval tools also catch unapproved pool changes, so one compromised login can't redirect a fleet's hashrate.

The integration starts installation of a Bitcoin Core node and the DATUM Gateway on a supported machine running Foreman Pickaxe. The same hardware that supports fleet management can also support local template creation and work distribution, without requiring a separate dedicated DATUM device.

"DATUM gives miners real advantages, and now setting it up in Foreman is easier than ever," said Dan Lawrence, CEO and co-founder of OBM. "With just a few clicks, Foreman customers can reduce wasted work and take control of their block templates without adding new hardware or another system to manage."

Developed by OCEAN and launched in 2024, DATUM lets miners build their own block templates locally while participating in pooled mining. Operators choose which transactions their equipment works on, rather than leaving that decision to the pool.

DATUM also adds built-in encryption between the mining site's gateway and OCEAN. Traditional Stratum V1 connections typically transmit mining data in plain text unless a separate encryption layer is added. DATUM protects those communications in transit, giving operators greater privacy without changing the firmware on their mining hardware.

For miners at remote sites, including those using Starlink, connectivity can be a constraint on performance and a costly part of the operation. DATUM reduces that constraint by aggregating thousands of miner connections into one connection to the upstream pool. This reduces bandwidth usage by approximately 96% and allows miners in remote areas using Starlink or 5G to have essentially the same performance and efficiency as if they were using fiber optics.

"We already use DATUM and Foreman at our sites and are big believers in both," said Todd Perlmeter of Abundant Mines. "We've tested this integration, and bringing them together on the same machine just makes sense. It simplifies our infrastructure without giving up the capabilities we value. This is the kind of practical modernization that helps prepare mining operations for the future."

Adding DATUM to a mining operation should have noticeable results for miners. OCEAN estimates that DATUM's stale-share and bandwidth improvements can save a 1 EH/s operation 0.24â1.59 BTC per year versus direct-to-pool Stratum V1. This is possible because the miner's own Bitcoin node peers directly with the Bitcoin network. The local DATUM Gateway distributes work to the fleet over the local network, helping reduce stale shares caused by delays in receiving replacement jobs from a remote pool. It also aggregates machine connections, reducing upstream connection overhead.

"Miners invest heavily in turning electricity into useful work. Delays in getting new jobs to their machines can waste a portion of that work," said Jason Hughes, OCEAN's CTO and DATUM's creator. "DATUM brings template creation and work distribution on site. Integrating it with Foreman makes that infrastructure easier to deploy through a platform operators already trust."

Miners building their own templates with DATUM pay a 1% OCEAN pool fee, compared with OCEAN's standard 2%. OCEAN's transparent reward accounting lets miners verify how actual block rewards are distributed, down to the shares used in the calculation.

"Miners deserve to keep more of what their work earns," said Mark Artymko, President of OCEAN. "This partnership makes that easier, while strengthening the Bitcoin network their businesses depend on."
