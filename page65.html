How they desperately needed these three points after back-to-back defeats had left cause for concern heading into the international break.

Goals from Alexis Mac Allister, Florian Wirtz and Alexander Isak were enough to see off a valiant effort from Crystal Palace, who played like a team fighting for their manager even though Pierre Sage looked like a dead man walking.

The biggest illustration of Palace's misfortune was the fact that Liverpool goalkeeper Alisson was the player of the match, the Brazilian having a throwback performance to deny Palace time and again at 0-0, before keeping his side's lead intact in the second half.

Sage now looks set to be given a stuffing six weeks before Christmas and the Frenchman may feel hard done by given this spirited performance that saw his Eagles put up a fight against Liverpool.

At times during a chaotic first half at Selhurst Park, it felt like Pierre Sage would be the latest to benefit from that quirk.

The Frenchman came into this game with his job security under increasing danger, with fans and the Crystal Palace hierarchy unsure of the former Lens' manager.

Yet Palace played like a team fighting for the man in the dugout. They had 10 first-half shots as the Eagles could - and probably should - have taken the lead in the opening 45 minutes.

A limp Liverpool were cut open like a hot knife through butter and needed goalkeeper Alisson to put in his best performance of the season.

It came at a juncture where, for the first time in his Liverpool career, the Brazilian's ability had been called into question by some fans after a number of uncharacteristic mistakes in recent weeks.

But now 34 and in the final year of his contract, Alisson put in a classic performance, probably his best since a trip to Paris Saint-Germain in the Champions League knockout stages 18 months ago.

Are Tree Saddles Really Better Than Treestands for Hunting Whitetails?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 8, 2026 12:01 PM EST

We may earn revenue from the products available on this page and participate in affiliate programs. Learn More ›

Early in my bowhunting career, I set up a ladder stand on top of a ridge that had oaks and plenty of deer sign. On opening morning, I watched a deer parade go down a trail that was 20 yards out of range. I sat in that stand several more times and never had a deer come within bow range. If I had a mobile stand, I could have easily adjusted my hunting strategy to move closer to the active trail.

Mobile hunting for whitetails is all about making aggressive moves and constantly hunting fresh spots. Mobile hunters carry everything they need for a hunt so they can set up on the fresh sign and easily adapt to the conditions. That’s why they need lightweight and easy to use hunting platforms.

That old faithful ladder stand just won’t cut it when you need to slide another 100 yards closer to a bedding area. A mobile setup is also great for public land where you can’t or don’t want to leave your stand. It’s also less expensive than buying multiple permanent stands.

There are three main choices for the mobile hunter: a climber, a hang-on stand, and a tree saddle. I’ve hunted from all three of these platforms in my search for the perfect mobile hunting set up. To help with this comparison, I also reached out to Aaron Warbritton, from The Hunting Public YouTube channel. He’s been at the tip of the spear of the mobile hunting trend and offers a wealth of experience. Together we’ll see how each platform stacks up against each other in five categories: weight, setup, huntability, comfort, and price.

A heavy pack can feel fine for a few minutes, but when yards turn to miles, you feel every pound. For hunting deep in public land, a lightweight set up is a must. Weights will differ depending on the model of stand and climbing method. These weights will give you an idea of what you can expect a complete kit to weigh. Here’s how all three systems compared:

Model: Tethrd Mantis Saddle and Predator platform.

Kit Includes: Saddle, tether, platform, lineman’s belt, and three climbing sticks.

Total Weight: 14.1 pounds. *The three climbing sticks separately weigh 7.5 pounds.

Kit Includes: Stand, safety harness, lineman’s belt, and three climbing sticks.

Total Weight: 23.9 pounds. *The three climbing sticks separately weigh 7.5 pounds.

If weight is your primary concern then a saddle is the clear winner. You can even make the saddle lighter by using a lighter climbing system.

Weight isn’t only a factor when carrying a stand, it also matters for setting it up. This is especially true when hanging a saddle platform or hang-on stand. When you climb to the height you want to hunt, you have to hold the stand or platform in one hand while tightening the stand’s straps with the other. A light platform for a saddle, like the Predator platform (3.3 pounds), is much easier to hang then the heavier base of a hang-on stand (12.2 pounds).

“I would say that now 90 percent of my hunts are with a saddle and a platform; just because it’s lighter, the footprint is smaller, and it’s faster for me to set up,” Warbritton says.

With a saddle and a hang-on, you’ll need your choice of hang-on stand and a climbing method. I use the Lone Wolf Alpha stand and climbing sticks. The stand and sticks were designed to pack neatly together making it a very convenient and easy to use system. You can climb the tree and hang your stand in one trip with practice and some paracord. I’m pretty slow and careful when climbing and it usually takes me 15 to 20 minutes to climb and get set. The hang-on is fast, but the saddle is slightly faster and easier for me to set up.

A climber is attached to the tree before you climb and doesn’t require climbing sticks. There’s also no platform to hang once you reach hunting height. You can scale a tree fairly quickly with one, and it’s a less technical climb than a saddle or platform. However, that speed comes at the cost of versatility. Climbers need a straight, limbless tree, which isn’t always available. I much prefer the versatility of other platforms that allow me to climb almost any tree. If you live in an area that has a lot of trees suitable for a climber, then these negatives might not be such a big deal.

The size of a climber is another negative. “I haven’t hunted out of climbers a ton, but I’ve hunted out of them enough to know I don’t want to use them. Their footprint is so big that when you’re packing them in, the stand gets tangled on stuff,” Warbritton says.

Another reason to move away from climbers is the noise they make when climbing. It’s discouraging to put a lot of effort slipping into your spot, but then make a scraping noise as you ascend into position. This is especially true when hunting close to bedded deer.

By comparison saddles and hang-ons are much stealthier to carry and set up. If you are proficient with either of them, you can be up a tree within earshot of a bedded buck without issue.

Proficiency doesn’t come overnight with any of these platforms, but tree saddles especially take time to learn. The saddle has a steeper learning curve because it’s so different than a traditional treestand. The first time you lean back into your saddle it might feel like leaning back on a rocking chair at the edge of a cliff. However, that uneasy feeling quickly fades and I personally feel much safer in my saddle than I ever felt in a hang-on or climbing treestand. That security comes from the fact that if you fall off your platform it is extremely easy to get back on because the tether is always pulled tight—making the drop almost unnoticeable.

Beyond the saddle’s learning curve, the hang-on and saddle are neck and neck when it comes to packing, setting up, and hunting out of them.

“They’re really close. They definitely have their differences. Whichever one that you decide to use, you need to practice with it. Whether you’re using climbing sticks or strap on steps, you need to practice packing, hanging, and then shooting out of them because they’re different,” Warbritton says.

When the rut kicks in and you basically live in a tree all weekend, a comfortable stand is a must. While all three mobile options are pretty comfortable, the standard treestand seat is hard to beat. Warbritton agrees with that preference.

“The only thing I like about a treestand is if I’m going to sit all day, I’d rather have a treestand,” he says.

Tree saddles are comfortable, but a lot of the comfort in a saddle depends on your tether height because it controls the weight distribution. You’ll need to find a sweet spot that works for you and that takes some practice. I haven’t found that sweet spot for comfort just yet, but I do think that I’ll eventually get the saddle to be as comfortable as my treestands.

Like weight, the price will depend on the manufacturer and model. Just like our weight evaluation, we’ll compare the products I have personally owned and used.

A Tethrd mantis tree saddle, platform, lineman’s belt, tether, and a set of Lone Wolf climbing sticks will run you around $650. Without climbing sticks the system is about $500. If you want to add some accessories like Ropeman ascenders, pouches, and a recliner, you’ll be looking at spending around $900. It’s not a cheap investment, and you’ll have to decide if the above-stated benefits outweigh the cost.

Hang-ons and climbers are less expensive. A premium climber like the Summit Explorer SD and Hunter Safety System safety harness will run you around $450. A Lone Wolf Alpha hang-on, climbing sticks, Hunter Safety System safety harness, and lineman’s belt will set you back around $500. Again, prices will vary depending on what models, climbing methods, and add on accessories you choose.

PHP .user.ini can make auto_prepend_file a persistence path

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

PHP's per-directory .user.ini files are configuration, but they can influence which code runs before a requested script. The auto_prepend_file directive names a file that PHP parses before the main file, as if it had been loaded with require. If an application permits an attacker to create or modify a .user.ini file in the document tree, a temporary upload or template-write flaw can become a persistent execution path.

The manual says .user.ini processing is available for the CGI/FastCGI SAPI. PHP scans from the requested script's directory upward to the document root, and only settings in the allowed modes are recognized. auto_prepend_file is a per-directory setting. The file is resolved with include_path, which adds another configuration value to the review.

An uploaded file is usually not executed directly. The problem begins when a user can place content in a directory that PHP scans for .user.ini, or when a deployment process writes generated files beside application code. The next request in that directory can inherit settings that the application did not intend.

The cache matters. user_ini.cache_ttl controls how often the per-directory configuration is reread. A response to an incident may remove a malicious file while a running FastCGI worker retains the previous setting for some period. Treat the cache as part of containment, not as a reason to assume the file is harmless.

The risk is not limited to a file named shell.php. A prepended file can contain initialization logic, logging hooks, or code that changes the application's runtime. Reviewers need to look at who can write any path that may be treated as configuration and at what code that configuration can include.

Inventory all document roots and every writable directory below them. Include uploads, cache, generated thumbnails, plugin directories, and deployment release folders that are web-accessible. Search for .user.ini files and for auto_prepend_file or auto_append_file references in deployment templates. Check for symlinks and path normalization differences between the web server and PHP.

Inspect the effective PHP-FPM configuration for user_ini.filename, user_ini.cache_ttl, open_basedir, include_path, and allow_url_include. Confirm whether web-server location rules deny requests to dot files and whether the denial covers every virtual host and alias. An application-level 404 is not a filesystem restriction if PHP still scans the directory.

Review the deployment user's write permissions. A release process that can replace application code is highly privileged; it should not also accept untrusted filenames or extract archives into the live document root. Separate writable data from executable code, and keep the web process unable to create PHP files where it can also write configuration.

If per-directory configuration is not required, set user_ini.filename to an empty value and deploy the decision through the PHP-FPM pool configuration. Where it is required, deny direct access to .user.ini, set a short cache lifetime appropriate to the deployment, and make the file immutable by the web user. Use open_basedir to bound file access, and do not allow remote inclusion unless the application has a documented and reviewed need.

Set a trusted include_path and disable URL wrappers for application files. Protect the FastCGI socket and pool configuration from unprivileged users. A user who can change the pool configuration can bypass many application-level controls.

In a staging copy, create a harmless .user.ini that points auto_prepend_file at a test file, then request a script in that directory. Confirm that the test file is included and record the cache delay. Remove the file and repeat the request after the cache expires. Try the same path through the public web server, an alias, and any alternate hostname.

Review application logs for changes to .user.ini, unexpected include failures, and requests for dot files. Correlate those events with upload and deployment activity. The useful control is a path boundary: untrusted data must not be able to change which code PHP loads before the application starts.

Here's Why I'm Still Investing in the S&P 500, Even if a Bear Market Is Coming

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Buying before a market decline feels awful. But waiting indefinitely carries its own risks.

The thought is inescapable: If I invest today, what if the market drops tomorrow? It's a question that can give everyone pause. After all, no one wants to see their newly invested money lose its value.

Yet the alternative creates an even more dangerous trap: Once you start waiting, what could possibly convince you it is ever safe to buy? That's why, when dealing with money you won't need for many years, it's better to build a plan based on uncertainty than to keep waiting for the perfect entry point.

War. Economic turmoil. Apocalyptic forecasts. The reality is that these things come and go. Sometimes the market falls as a result. Sometimes it doesn't. In reality, an informed person can always find what may seem like a reasonable excuse to postpone investing.

Worse, this anxiety often shows up no matter which way the market moves: Rising prices can feel expensive; falling prices feel like a warning sign. Nevertheless, studies have made it clear: Those who put money to work in the stock market have consistently outperformed those who have tried to time the market or remained in cash.

One way to clear the psychological hurdle of investing in a falling stock market is to recognize that lower prices can offer benefits. First, they mean you can buy more shares.

For example, imagine an investor who puts $200 to work in the stock market each month, buying an index exchange-traded fund (ETF) valued at $100 a share. That results in two shares per month. However, if the index ETF falls to $80 a share, the same $200 investment buys 2.5 shares per month.

Granted, the earlier-purchased shares have lost value, and buying more doesn't erase that loss. However, for those buying with the intention of holding for decades, the lower price can help them accumulate many more shares.

In the long run, that will result in more assets, which will in turn generate added returns. In other words, it can help power the compounding mechanism that drives buy-and-hold investing.

This is the essence of dollar-cost averaging. By regularly setting aside money for investment, a person can remove the emotional guesswork of investing and, if the market declines, lower the average price paid.

Another reason some people fear investing into a downturn is that they are putting the wrong money to work in the wrong way. That is, investment money should be reserved for long-term goals that won't be interrupted by short-term challenges. Emergencies, bills, and near-term purchases should come from a separate pool of money.

That way, investment money can fluctuate without affecting a person's ability to make ends meet. Moreover, by having a separate cash reserve, a person won't be tempted to sell investments during a downturn to raise cash.

Lastly, it's important to establish the right investing routine. If $200 per month is too much, lower the amount; don't give up on the routine. If finding the right stocks seems overwhelming, start with an index fund that tracks the S&P 500 -- many are incredibly cheap, and buying shares has never been easier. Also, an S&P 500 index fund is a great way for investors to stay diversified, because the index itself spreads money across many large American stocks.

Is a bear market coming? Perhaps. Does that make me nervous about investing? No. That's not to say that a bear market drop wouldn't hurt. It's just that long-term investing requires the fortitude to ride out dips, corrections, and even bear markets.

The best investors are often the ones who can see downturns for what they are: opportunities for long-term investors to accumulate more shares at a lower price.

A confluence of factors has propelled Wall Street's major stock indexes to new heights, no two of which are larger than the evolution of artificial intelligence (AI) and the advent and early-stage proliferation of quantum computing. Both are trillion-dollar addressable opportunities that investors simply don't want to miss.

But following the stock market's parabolic climb, concerns are mounting about a possible AI and/or quantum computing bubble-bursting event. While history hasn't exactly been kind to next-big-thing technologies -- every game-changing technology has endured an early-innings bubble-bursting event since the mid-1990s -- there's an even more prominent bubble that looks ready to burst, based on what history tells us.

While history shows that the AI and quantum computing bubbles should eventually burst, the $1.5 trillion outstanding margin debt bubble may be the spark that upends the stock market's four-year rally.

"Margin" is money an investor borrows from their broker, with interest, to wager against (short-sell) or purchase securities. When margin is used to buy stocks or exchange-traded funds (ETFs), it represents a form of leverage.

Using margin can be risky. If an investor's wager moves in the desired direction, they can quickly amplify their gains. But if a stock or ETF moves in the opposite direction, losses can be quickly magnified. This dynamic makes outstanding margin debt a somewhat crude, yet intriguing measure of investors' willingness to take risks.

According to FINRA, outstanding margin debt rocketed to an all-time high of $1.502 trillion in June. Although margin debt is expected to steadily increase over time in lockstep with the overall value of the stock market, bad things often happen when the amount investors borrow from their brokers rises dramatically over a short time frame.

History makes it crystal clear that outsize risk-taking is the ultimate red flag on Wall Street. While margin debt can't tell us when the music will stop or what catalysts could cause the stock market to sink, it clearly shows the unsustainability of excess risk-taking, which is doubly problematic in a historically expensive stock market.

From a historical standpoint, the short-term outlook for Wall Street's AI- and quantum computing-driven rally isn't pretty. If history rhymes once more, investors can expect a sharp bear market downturn in the presumed not-too-distant future.

While deep red arrows are known to tug on investors' heartstrings, significant pullbacks in Wall Street's major stock indexes can also represent generational buying opportunities for optimistic, long-term investors.

Despite most investors disliking stock market corrections and bear markets, these events are a natural and healthy part of the investing cycle. Given that downturns are commonly driven by emotions, there's not much that can be done from a monetary or fiscal policy perspective to prevent double-digit declines from occurring.

The silver lining for investors is that bull and bear market cycles aren't linear.

A May 2026 post on X (formerly Twitter) from Bespoke Investment Group examined every S&P 500 bull and bear market since the start of the Great Depression in September 1929. What researchers found was a mile-wide gap between bull and bear markets.

The current bull market that began on 10/12/22 is now the 9th longest in S&P 500 history, surpassing the 1,324-day bull that ended on 2/9/1966: https://t.co/4mGsS2t2ft

On one hand, the typical S&P 500 bear market found its trough after an average of 286 calendar days (roughly 9.5 months). By comparison, the average S&P 500 bull market persisted for 1,023 calendar days, or approximately 3.6 times as long as the average bear market.

More than a century of backward-looking S&P 500 total return data from Crestmont Research provides even more for patient investors to cheer about.

Analysts at Crestmont calculated the rolling 20-year total returns, including dividends, of the S&P 500 since 1900. This meant tracking its components in other major indexes prior to the S&P's official inception in 1923.

Crestmont's analysis yielded 107 rolling 20-year periods (1900-1919, 1901-1920, and so on, through 2006-2025), all of which produced a positive average annual total return. I repeat, 107 of 107 rolling 20-year periods generated a profit, including dividends.

Even if someone hypothetically invested at a stock market peak, or during a recession, depression, war, pandemic, etc., they would have always made money if they held an S&P 500-tracking index for 20 years.

Patience is a virtue and a path to potential riches on Wall Street. If the $1.5 trillion margin debt bubble bursts, consider it an opportunity to pounce.
