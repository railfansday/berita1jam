Essential Yoga Poses That Deliver Serious Knee Strengthening

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The thing about practicing yoga for knee strengthening is you typically don’t know you need to do it until there’s an issue. YouTube yoga teacher Yoga With Kassandra takes you through a routine designed to emphasize the muscles that support knee strength and explains how to modify the poses to suit your needs.

Whether you’re wanting to build knee strength to enhance your everyday functionality or you’re recovery from a knee injury and finally at the point where you can practice yoga, you need strengthening exercises that support all the muscles that support and stabilize your knees. Basically, you want to work on the hip adductors, the quads, the glutes, the hamstrings.

The following poses literally work all of the muscles that contribute to knee stability. You’ll also get to play around a little with some dynamic movement and try some balancing exercises that help you understand how to properly engage the muscles that support the knee. Then you can take that awareness into other yoga poses as well as your everyday movements.

Regularly practicing this yoga for knee strength routine will help anyone build knee strength. I know from personal experience just how frustrating a knee injury can be, so if that applies to you, know that the more you dedicate time towards rehabilitating yourself, and the more diligent you are with your practices, the more likely you’ll feel a difference in your ease of movement. And remember that rest is equally as important as stretching and strengthening.

You will need a block or a block-like item for several of these poses. If you experience pain in any of the poses, slowly come out and skip ahead to the next pose. Always consult with your physician or physical therapist prior to introducing new exercises.

Start lying on your back and bring your feet about hip-distance apart and your heels pretty close toward your glutes. Place the block directly in between the inner thighs and squeeze the block as much as you possibly can. Then push down into all four corners of both feet and slowly lift first the pelvis off the mat, then the lower back, and then the mid back in Bridge Pose. The higher you lift, the harder it becomes to squeeze your block, but keep squeezing it anyway. This helps train your inner thighs, or adductor muscles, to contract. Building inner thigh power can also help you become better at balancing poses.

Take about 3 breaths here, pushing down through your big toes. Then exhale and slowly roll down, inch by inch. Move your block to the side.

From lying on your back, lift your right leg toward the sky and straighten it without locking your knee or hyperextending it. Interlace your fingers behind your right hamstrings. Instead of making this a passive stretch, push your leg against your palms as much as you can, and feel your arms resist. Keep pushing, pushing, pushing with your hamstrings. This should be challenging. You’ll also get a little arm workout. Try to keep your lower back neutral and close to the mat. Continue for a few more breaths.

You might find that your leg starts coming a little closer toward your chest, and that’s fine, but don’t force the stretch. Then release and switch legs.

Re-extend your right leg and bring your hands behind the back of your calf muscles. If that means you need to bend a little in your knee, that’s perfectly fine. If you can’t reach that far, bring your hands to the hamstrings again, but shift them closer to the knee, which creates a different sensation. Same thing, you want to push against your hands and resist. Then repeat on the other side.

This is not a yoga pose, but it’s absolutely amazing for your adductors. Grab your block and place it between your inner thighs again. Instead of keeping your heels close to you as in Bridge, walk your feet out so it feels almost as if your legs are almost straight and there’s about 6 inches between the mat and the backs of your knees.

Flex your feet, push your heels down into the mat, and see if you can squeeze your block and lift your hips off the mat. They’re only going to lift about an inch or so and that’s fine. This is an intense pose. You’re gonna feel it in your legs pretty much right away. If you don’t feel it, it’s because your knees are too bent and you need to straighten them a little more. Squeeze your block, push down into your heels, and try to drag your heels toward your shoulders. Hold here for a few more breaths. Then lower and move your block off to the side.

Come into Downward-Facing Dog. Normally I would suggest some Cat and Cow stretches before going into Down Dog, but if you’re recovering from knee issues you might not be able to apply bring your knees to the mat because of the pressure, so consider those stretches optional or take a seated version of them. You’re pushing into the heels here to stretch your hamstrings, lengthen your arms, and lengthen your spine.

Reach your right leg toward the sky in Three-Legged Dog, then step forward toward the top of the mat. Push down into your feet and come up into High Lunge. You want your feet hip-distance apart for stability. It’s find to bend your back knee if that feels more doable. It’s important that your front knee is going in the same direction as your toes and is stacked over your ankle. You don’t want the knee to be swaying to one side or jutting forward in front of your toes.

Bring your hands to your heart and keep your right knee bent and focus on its stability as you tilt your chest forward into a variation of Warrior 3. Keep the knee of your supporting leg bent as you reach your left leg back and find your balance.

As you inhale, push your right leg straight, and then exhale gently as you bend your right knee again. Think of this as almost like a one-legged squat. So inhale, straighten the leg. Exhale, bend the knee.

Continue for several breaths. Keep your right knee pointing straight over the second and third toes. Last one, inhale to straighten and hold here.

From Warrior 3, reach your left leg straight toward the ceiling and bring your chest toward your right thigh in Standing Splits. You might want to bring your block underneath your hands or fingertips.

Then exhale, bend both knees as much as you can, and see if you can bring your knees together to touch, so you’re in a little ball with your left foot hovering above the mat.

Then inhale, open back up into Standing Splits. Look at your right knee and make certain it’s not shifting to the right or the left. Do that once more.

Bend both knees and bring your left foot to the mat with your big toes together and your heels slightly apart in Chair Pose. If this creates tension in your lower back, you can keep your feet hip-distance apart. Rock your weight back into your heels and keep your tailbone reaching back and your thighs hugging the midline as if you were still squeezing that block. Stay here or reach your arms alongside your head.

Lean more weight onto your right leg, and see if you can pick up your left foot and step it back into your High Lunge. Then very carefully pivot the back foot almost parallel to the short side of the mat, keeping your shin and your knee facing the same direction, in Warrior 2. Your right knee is still bending. Squeezing the glutes helps press that right knee open as it points forward. Reach your arms straight out from your shoulders.

To work on strengthening, inhale as you straighten the front leg and bring your hands together in front of you, and then exhale back into Warrior 2 and reach your arms. Repeat several times. The goal is not to rush this but to do it with control. You want to really feel that knee tracking directly over the second and third toes and not wobbling.

Straighten your right leg, being careful not to lock or hyperextend your knees. Then shift forward into Triangle Pose and reach your left arm upward. Draw your left shoulder slightly back as you open across the chest.

From Triangle, look down at the floor and circle your hands to the mat. Step to Downward-Facing Dog.

Repeat these poses on your left side, starting with High Lunge and continuing to Dynamic Warrior 3, Standing Splits, Chair Pose, Dynamic Warrior 2, Triangle, and Down Dog.

From Down Dog, walk your feet to the top of the mat. Bend your knees a little, grab your block, and come all the way to standing. Come into Mountain Pose with the block in between your thighs, just as you did in Bridge Pose. Squeeze the block and hug in toward the midline. You can feel the adductors engage. Plant down firmly through all four corners of both feet.

As you inhale, stand a little taller. As you exhale, continue to squeeze your block as you lower yourself into Chair Pose. Repeat this movement about 5 times, moving slowly and steadily, continuing to squeeze the block. You might need to adjust your block a little.

Slowly fold forward in Standing Forward Bend. If you need to bend your knees, that’s perfectly fine. Even in this fold, can you squeeze your block? Then bend your knees a little more and release your block.

Slowly roll all the way up to standing. Notice how you feel as you attempt the pose, because if you’re still injured, the shape might not be accessible to you, and it’s fine to skip it. Lean onto your right foot, bend your left knee, and catch hold of the inner edge of your left foot behind you with your left hand. Keep your left hip and knee drawing inward toward the midline. So imagine you’re still squeezing your block in between your thighs. That is really important.

Reach your right arm forward. Instead of dipping the chest down and thinking of lifting the leg up, focus on pushing your foot into your palm. You’re not going super far forward in your Dancer Pose. You’re not working on flexibility, you’re working on strength, although you might feel an intense stretch along the front of your lifted quads.

To help with balance, you can always practice within arm’s reach of a chair or a wall so that you can reach out if needed for extra steadiness.

Then release and repeat on your other leg, foot into the right hand.

Make your way onto your belly on the mat. The shape of this pose is very similar to Dancer, so if this isn’t working for you, work on Locust Pose. If you want to do Bow Pose, bend your knees and see if you can catch hold of your feet and imagine you still have the block in between the inner upper thighs. Squeeze in and start to push the feet against your hands.

If you’re not doing Bow Pose, then bring your arms alongside your body, palms facing the mat, and slowly lift your feet and legs and chest off the mat Focus your gaze forward and down to keep the back of your neck long. Hold here or slowly release and then lift several times.

Flip over onto your back. Grab your block, lift your hips, slide your block underneath your pelvis, and just let yourself settle here. Slow down your breath and start to unwind.

When you’re ready, press into your feet, lift your pelvis, and move your block to the side before lowering yourself down. Maybe you draw your knees toward your belly and bring your arms underneath your calves and stay still or rock a little side to side.

Extend your arms and legs, close your eyes, and notice how you feel now as opposed to when you first started this practice. Let yourself settle into this space.

When you’re ready, begin to lengthen your breath and wiggle your fingers and toes. You can reach your arms up overhead, take a big stretch, and roll onto one side. Come up to take a seat, sitting up tall, maybe closing your eyes for a moment.

‘Avalancha’ at 30: The LP That Recharged Cafe Tacvba After the Burnout of ‘Re’

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Thirty years ago, the members of Café Tacvba found themselves physically and emotionally wiped out. The Mexico City quartet had completed an extensive tour promoting its sophomore effort, Re, which since then has been routinely hailed as one of the best albums in the history of Latin rock.

To make matters worse, Tacvba’s label expected a new record, and the demos that the band had been taping between tours were far from ready to become a cohesive whole. Things got a bit tense.

“We played the Viña del Mar festival in Chile, and our longtime producer Gustavo Santaolalla happened to be there,” remembers Tacvba keyboardist Meme del Real. “He saw what was happening to us. ‘You’re always playing covers of songs by other artists,’ he said. ‘Why don’t you make an EP of covers? That would get you back in the studio and working on a different frequency.’ He had the right perspective, and pointed us to a path of healing.”

The result was Avalancha de Éxitos, a mini-album of radically reimagined songs that, experienced as a whole, sounds significantly more epic than the sum of its parts. Released on Nov. 5, 1996, it was not only commercially successful — five of its eight songs were released as singles — but it also made Tacvba stars in countries like Argentina, which had never embraced Mexican rock before.

A brisk, exuberant record, it is also subtly conceptual, depicting Latin American culture as a chaotic, dissonant tapestry where beauty and depth can be found in a musty bolero from decades ago (“No Me Comprendes”), a melancholy pop ballad (Leo Dan’s wide-eyed “Cómo Te Extraño Mi Amor”), or a tropical anthem of hope transformed into son huasteco (Juan Luis Guerra’s socially alert “Ojalá Que Llueva Café.”)

Tacvba’s sense of humor, playful and subversive, injected an adrenaline rush into provocative Mexican rock songs like opening stomper “Chilanga Banda,” with its indolent groove, and the industrial corrosion of “Alármala De Tos,” originally by Eighties troublemakers Botellita de Jerez. David Byrne guests on “No Controles,” an aggro inversion of the 1985 techno-pop hit by Mexican female group Flans (itself, a cover of Spanish band Olé Olé). “I remember that record with so much affection,” says singer Rubén Albarrán from his home in Mexico City. “We were so inspired by the process of stepping out into the streets of the cities that we visited and discovering the spirit of Latin America. We were coming face to face with the realities that have become more obvious since then: the intervention of foreign countries; the genocide of our people. Our resistance was formulated by selecting ingredients from the blend of cultures and histories, and coming up with something new. We were deeply moved by discovering who we really were.”

The same irreverence that Tacvba had exhibited on original songs like “La Ingrata” seeped into the Avalancha sessions.

“We had the impudence not to give a damn about what the composers of the original songs may think,” laughs Albarrán, who fittingly chose the moniker of Anónimo for the project. “Perhaps they would even be offended.”

“It’s so much easier to quote and unquote destroy a song if the author is not present,” adds del Real. “That was a big part of the fun — the element of adventure involved in every single arrangement. And it wasn’t like the songs were required to suffer a profound transformation. It was more like, ‘What would happen if we grab these songs and push them to the limit? What if we choke them so that they are forced to breathe again in a completely different context?’ We were really pushing the envelope.” Related Content ‘Re’ at 30: Café Tacvba Look Back at Seminal Album Inspired by Mexico City, Ministry, and Bach New Doc to Examine Terror Plot That Canceled Taylor Swift Concert Watch Karol G Perform Unreleased Song During Mexico City Celebration Haywire’s Video for ‘Flex Your Head’ Documents Their Times Square Siege

“I think the fact they had created a work as imposing and sonically diverse as Re left them in a complicated position in terms of the follow-up album,” says Enrique Blanc, a Mexico-based music journalist and author of the book Café Tacvba — Bailando por nuestra cuenta. “Making Avalancha was like going into recess for them; a fun project echoing their beginnings as a band that played covers.”

Originally, the group planned to record Duran Duran’s debut single “Planet Earth” (according to del Real, a demo still languishes in the vaults.) But Albarrán felt awkward singing the English lyrics, and the song selection remained Latin American throughout — a powerful statement in itself.

“We have a different cultural context than Pink Floyd or Radiohead,” explains Albarrán. “We need to talk about other things that are more important to us and the people we share our lives with. Something happened with Avalancha, where the pristine sound quality achieved by Santaolalla and [co-producer] Aníbal Kerpel merged with our conscious decision not to copy anything from the Anglo mainstream. We have our own emotional world. It’s just as valuable as anyone else’s.”

Interestingly, one of the album’s most haunting moments arrives during an obscure track: “No Me Comprendes,” a cabaret miniature by Cuban eccentric Bola de Nieve. Like most of Avalancha, it conjures up a stately, fetishistic atmosphere by combining psychedelic guitar with hand percussion and a musical saw. Toward the end, the tropical bolero suddenly morphs into a lilting bossa nova segment — almost as if the record itself chose to embrace the power of beauty over chaos and satire.

“That’s one of my favorite Tacvba songs,” says Albarrán. “It’s such a gorgeous moment when that bossa bit blooms — it nourishes my heart and soul. The mocking undertone in my voice mirrors Bola de Nieve’s original interpretation. He was such a strange and uncomfortable character, and the song itself is difficult to pin down. The saw and the bossa segment — it’s like the sound of fairies, or the song of sirens.”

“I remember putting on the headphones and recording my part on a grand piano,” adds del Real. He is in a hotel room in Querétaro, a few hours before stepping onstage for a concert presenting La Montaña Encendida, his debut solo album. “It felt like dining at an expensive restaurant in Paris — the kind of establishment you only get to visit once or twice in your life. I was having such a wonderful time playing, and I think my enjoyment can be heard on the final product.”

It was the album’s closing track, “Cómo Te Extraño Mi Amor,” that became its biggest hit — a quintessential Tacvba moment. Whereas the Leo Dan original is fragile and wounded, Tacvba’s bouncy cover bursts out of the speakers with the shuffle of a ska beat and youthful Beatlesque confidence.

“That’s Gustavo’s [Santaolalla] magic,” says Albarrán. “He captured that fleeting impression, how happy and young we were. We were experiencing success doing what we loved. It was just thrilling, and on this song you can hear the lightness in our step. It wasn’t only the music that we tackled with that kind of energy: so much laughter, and attitude, and desmadre. It was life itself.”

Avalancha’s brilliance was acknowledged by both critics and the band’s fans the moment it was released. It was the record that everyone talked about as 1996 came to a close, and it generated another massive tour. Eventually, Tacvba returned to the demos of original material that had been pushed aside, and in 1999 released the stunning double album Revés/Yosoy.

Still, Avalancha may well be the finest Latin covers album ever made. “I’m so proud of it,” says del Real. “Avalancha helped us realize how vulnerable we could be as a band. It defined our identity while helping us to recover from a few maladies we had picked up along their way. Once again, music and the creative process gave us the chance to rediscover why we remained together. Our group psychotherapy was commanded by a sense of adventure.”

“It wasn’t just us,” Albarrán emphasizes. “We were part of a movement that was taking over Mexico and many other countries. There was Maldita Vecindad and Santa Sabina. We were all reformulating our roots with different results. Music was our medicine and refuge; the one place where we could find an answer for everything.”

Matthew Lillard is officially returning as Shaggy in a new Scooby-Doo series arriving in 2027, marking what may be the most anticipated actor comeback of the year, if not the decade. For 2000s kids, this is nothing short of a dream come true, while also giving a whole new generation the chance to discover why Lillard's portrayal of the iconic character is so beloved.

As a 2000s kid, I grew up with the live-action Scooby-Doo (2002) and Scooby-Doo 2: Monsters Unleashed (2004) movies. In fact, the VHS tapes for these two movies were easily among the most loved and frequently watched in my collection, seeing hundreds, if not thousands, of playbacks over the years.

As a result, Matthew Lillard became the face and voice of "my Shaggy," just as he did for hundreds of thousands of kids worldwide. To this day, it's truly impossible for me to imagine another actor in the role who could even come close.

Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles' latest series has already delivered one of the coolest redesigns of a classic villain.

Now, 22 years after Monsters Unleashed, Lillard is reprising this legendary role, albeit with a twist. Rather than returning for another live-action project, Lillard will voice Shaggy Rogers in the upcoming series Yokoso, Scooby-Doo!, marking the franchise's first original anime series. And while this isn't quite the R-rated live-action return some fans may have hoped for, I couldn't be more excited.

Yokoso, Scooby-Doo! will be a Japan-set, anime-inspired mystery adventure that pairs Scooby-Doo and Shaggy with several new characters. However, Yokoso, Scooby-Doo! will mark his first time venturing into anime as Shaggy Rogers, making this latest return particularly notable.

The kids who grew up watching Matthew Lillard's Scooby-Doo movies are now all grown up, and it feels like the perfect time for the actor and his co-stars, Freddie Prinze Jr., Sarah Michelle Gellar, and Linda Cardellini, to reprise their live-action roles. An R-rated movie bringing back the Mystery Inc. gang for a more mature, all-grown-up adventure could be exactly what longtime fans have been waiting for.

Matthew Lillard is already a horror icon thanks to his roles in some of the most legendary horror franchises of all time, including William Afton in Five Nights at Freddy's and Stu Macher in Scream (1996), one of the original Ghostface killers alongside Billy Loomis. Pokémon TCG fans who pre-ordered Delta Reign ETBs still haven't been charged

Pokémon Trading Card Game fans have spent the last couple of days feeling anxious. Delta Reign, the next big TCG set, releases on Friday — and The Pokémon Center hasn't updated anyone about their orders. Typically, the official Pokémon storefront charges for pre-orders a few days before release; shipment updates tend to come the day before launch. It turns out the lack of communication reflects an actual, active issue with orders.

There are three major Delta Reign products set to release on Friday, Nov. 6: the Elite Trainer Box, Booster Bundles, and Booster Boxes. Pokémon Center ETBs, which come with extra packs and promo cards, are particularly prized by fans and resellers.

Pokémon customers from the U.K. recently received a notice from TPC informing them about delays for all three items. The company now says that its Delta Reign products will ship sometime in mid-November, but has not share any specifics beyond that.

Customers in the United States, however, have not been informed of issues with orders yet. Given the lack of charges and shipping updates, it seems likely that it is only a matter of time before TPC breaks the news to everyone. As of this writing, TPC's website is undergoing extensive maintenance.

According to Pokémon fans sharing their communications with TPC customer support, the delays are due to an internal mix-up. The storefront apparently told a number of customers that they had to reauthorize payments despite, in some cases, not actually needing additional information. This may also explain why the TPC website's checkout function hasn't worked for nearly a week.

Though TPC's shipments are delayed, Delta Reign's launch (and the 30th Anniversary's Ultra Premium Collection) is still proceeding full steam ahead at major retailers. One of the exceptions might be local card shops which are still waiting for stock to arrive — but such delays are common.

In his own statement, O’Neil reflected on building the league up from the rough.

"LIV Golf was fun, demanding, inspiring, at times challenging—but always rewarding,” he said. "We created memories that I hope will inspire a new generation of fans to follow, play, and fall in love with this great game.”

And though the league is headed towards a wind down, the announcement did note the company will remain open to and consider any “value-maximum alternatives” that might arise.

For most, that might seem a sign from the universe to take up a hobby—say, gentleman farmer. But Horner isn’t most men. As such, he’s been speaking at industry events like Sportico Invest London and the Autosport Business Exchange in New York, where I sat down with him before he went on stage.

Unsurprisingly to anyone who saw him on Drive to Survive, or at any of the 10,000 media events he did during his F1 career, Horner still has plenty to say. Even if he is trying to be polite about it.

“The racing has been entertaining, don’t get me wrong. I’m watching these races and seeing cars overtaking in Melbourne and other circuits that you don’t normally overtake at,” he says when discussing the current F1 season. “But they’re not really overtakes as opposed to ... drive-pasts.”

He is not, it seems, a particular fan of the 2026 power unit regulations that have caused consternation amongst teams and fans alike by forcing the gas-electric hybrid propulsion systems to split their output 50/50 between internal combustion-sourced and electron-derived power. Horner describes it as the biggest issue for F1 to tackle at the moment. “You would assume it would be a relatively easy fix, just to change that ratio, but it seems a little more complicated,” he says. “You hear the drivers not liking how these cars are to drive, because you’re not rewarded for driving quickly through a fast corner, for example.”

“I think they’re doing the right thing for 2030,” he adds, referring to FIA president Mohammed Ben ‌Sulayem’s plans to ditch the current hybrid V-6 powertrains for V-8 units. “I think it’s unfortunate for the current presidency because he inherited this set of regulations. So of course, he’s looking to address that for the next round of significant change to engine spec.”

The 2026 season’s power unit frustrations haven’t been all bad, at least in terms of entertainment value. They’ve played a role in keeping the constructors’ championship battle interesting, even if the driver’s championship was swept away by wunderkind Kimi Antonelli.

“It’s interesting, I think different teams have had different moments this year,” he says. “Mercedes started very strong; Ferrari then had a moment where they looked like they were gonna put a challenge together around Barcelona time—seeing Lewis [Hamilton] win his first race, Charles [Leclerc] winning at Silverstone. And McLaren started to get their act together, looked pretty racy around Zanvoort and Budapest time, and then obviously, the Red Bull cars have come on strong.”

But those cars at front of the field are practically in a different league from the other seven teams in this season, which Horner—despite his own successes captaining Red Bull teams that ran the table with Brady-Belichick efficiency—says isn’t ideal for the sport. “I think you want as many cars in the fight as you can get, and the best way to achieve that is stability. Because then they all [close on] the same solutions, and just iterate those same solutions. So if you want close racing, just leave those regulations, those chassis regulations, alone. And you’ll see them all converge onto one solution—the better solution.”

In other words, simplify, then add budget. Formula 1, as he points out, is both one of the most expensive sports in the world and the biggest team sport on the planet. “You’ve got over 2000 team members between engine and chassis, all producing two cars for twenty-four races a year.” Running all as team principal, unsurprisingly, is like spinning plates and juggling bowling pins at the same time, even before factoring in the spotlight.

“Challenges ... there are many. There’s the scrutiny you come under. The politics, the competition of people trying to nick your staff, your drivers, your sponsors. It’s such a competitive industry. There’s so much at stake with large amounts of money involved” he says. “It’s dog-eat-dog in that respect. You gotta get up pretty early in the morning to stay ahead of the rest.”

Having done so for so many years—and most of those seeing an above-average amount of success for the position—certainly suggests the 52-year-old Brit has what Liam Neeson might describe as a particular set of skills. Would you, I ask him, eventually like to circle back to Formula 1?

“I think ...” Here, he pauses. “You know, I had an incredible run. I’m very proud of what I did. It didn’t end the way in the way I would have liked. I’m still relatively young ... I would only go back—knowing the sacrifices it takes—for the right project. Only if I was in a position where I could influence change, drive change within the organization, with like-minded people that wanted to win ... I’ve won close to one-third of the races I entered. I don’t want to go back just to take part.”

But he’s not pigeonholing himself. “I have time on my side to be able to make decisions. I feel like I’m too young to retire,” he says. “Honestly, sitting right here, right now, I have no idea where the next twelve months are going to take me. And I find that kind of exciting.”

Both Koopmeiners brothers start in Thrusday evening’s Europa League clash between AZ Alkmaar and Juventus, as Luciano Spalletti keeps his attacking trident of Randal Kolo Muani, Nick Woltemade and Kerim Alajbegovic.

You can follow the action as it unfolds on the Football Italia Liveblog.

The Bianconeri are unbeaten after three matches of the Europa League season, having defeated NEC 5-0 in the first round of league phase fixtures, 2-1 away against Celta Vigo in the second and having drawn 2-2 with Rennes last time out.

Their form domestically has brought more ups and downs, with five wins, two draws, three defeats and a seventh-place ranking on their record after 10 games of the Serie A campaign. The Old Lady head into tonight’s game off the back of a 2-0 win over struggling Napoli in the league over the weekend.

AZ are one point worse off than Juventus in the Europa League after three matches, with two wins (against Hapoel Beer-Sheva and Ararat-Armenia) and one defeat (against Sunderland) thus far. They are hoping to get back to winning ways after consecutive defeats against Twente and Ajax in the Eredivisie over the last two weeks.

Injuries continue to be an issue for Juventus, with Kenan Yildiz, Nico Gonzalez, Francisco Conceicao, Khephren Thuram, Manuel Locatelli, Zeki Celik and Kamil Grabara all unavailable for tonight’s tie. Weston McKennie was the latest player to be added to the Bianconeri injury list.

Hosts AZ are without Jeroen Zoet, Jizz Hornkamp, Wouter Goes and Stije Resik. Kees Smit is in the squad for the first time since September.

Tonight’s game brings a reunion of the Koopmeiners brothers, who have both been included in the starting line-ups. Like his brother, Juventus’s Teun Koopmeiners also came through the AZ Alkmaar academy before joining Atalanta in 2021.

Spalletti has stuck with Kolo Muani at centre-forward and he will be joined by Woltemade and Alajbegovic in the Juve attack.
