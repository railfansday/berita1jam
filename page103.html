During his 22-year playing career, Javier Zanetti turned out more than 1,000 times for club and country.

The Argentina and Inter Milan great shared a dressing room with some of the greatest players of his generation and played under some of the game’s most influential coaches.

Few, however, left quite the same impression as Marcelo Bielsa, who handed Zanetti 52 international caps during his six-year spell in charge of Argentina.

Bielsa’s reputation for demanding the highest level of commitment from his players is well-documented, but for Zanetti, the former Leeds United manager’s eccentric methods went way beyond mere tactics and training sessions.

"He is a manager who knows how to get the absolute best out of his players,” Zanetti tells FourFourTwo. That was true for me, and it has been true for so many others.”

For Zanetti, what sets Bielsa apart from other elite coaches is his ability to connect on a much deeper, more meaningful level.

“He has a way of reaching something deep inside you, of reigniting that pure love of the game you had as a kid,” he continues.

Life can leave its mark on a planetary atmosphere, so learning what properties are the product of non-life will help astronomers identify those worlds that might have life.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

A new computer simulation that depicts Earth, or a rocky planet in general, without life is the latest tool in the quest to discover whether or not we are alone in the universe.

Life leaves its mark on a planet, particularly its atmosphere, in the form of a mixture of biochemically created gases. Some are obvious, while others can be produced by both biology and geology, meaning that distinguishing a planet that has life from one that does not, solely based on what gases we can detect in its atmosphere, can be difficult. The key is in knowing which atmospheric conditions can be explained without life.

To aid in distinguishing between habitable and inhabited planets, planetary scientist Samantha Gilbert-Janizek of the University of California, Riverside, developed a model that simulates the evolution of a rocky planet without life.

"The model can help us see how habitable but non-living planets might mimic living planets," Gilbert-Janizek, who was a PhD student at the University of Washington when the research was conducted, said in a statement.

Gilbert-Janizek built upon a model previously developed by Washington’s Rory Barnes that simulated the core, mantle, and crust of a planet. This new model pushed the simulation two steps further by incorporating the surface and atmosphere of the planet as well.

In all, the model simulates 19 properties of a rocky planet that are not related to life, including the amount of heat a planet receives from its star, the exchange of heat and chemicals from the core to the surface, and the outflow of certain gases from the subsurface.

Gilbert-Janizek’s starting point was to simulate the development of Earth had life never arisen here, therefore providing a baseline with which to compare to exoplanets. She ran the simulation from the birth of Earth 4.5 billion years ago to 500 million years into the future. The result was a lifeless world, but one that still had oceans and an atmosphere.

"When we interpret observations of an exoplanet’s atmosphere, this tool will help us determine the probability that what we observe indicates life on the surface," said Gilbert-Janizek.

What makes the simulation especially useful is its plug-and-play nature. Researchers can simply apply the bulk properties of whichever planet they are studying to the model, and then just let it run. If the result is a planet with an atmosphere that matches what is detected spectroscopically by the James Webb Space Telescope or, in the future, the various extremely large 30-meter-plus ground-based telescopes or NASA’s planned Habitable Worlds Observatory, then we know that planet probably does not have life as we know it.

However, if the simulated planet without life does not match what we observe on the real planet, then it becomes possible that life is present and altering the atmosphere.

"Dr Gilbert-Janizek’s work represents an exciting step toward understanding where life can exist in the universe,” said Barnes. "After more than a decade of work by many scientists, Dr Gilbert-Janizek has finally completed a model of the Earth that simultaneously reproduces properties of Earth’s core, mantle, surface and atmosphere. That calibration gives us confidence that future simulations of exoplanets will be accurate."

While any deviation from what is predicted by the model will not necessarily mean there is definitely life on a planet, it will help pick out those worlds that are worthy of follow-up studies by the next generation of telescopes.

Gilbert-Janizek’s model is described in a paper published in The Planetary Science Journal.

Heat broiler. Whisk ¼ cup honey, ¼ cup white miso, and 1 Tbsp. low-sodium soy sauce in a large bowl to combine. By all logic I should be sick of this chicken. I have cooked it, give or take, a hundred times. When I don’t know what to make for dinner—or, honestly, don’t want to make dinner at all—it is there for me, like a gentle friend.

The marinade is deceptively simple: equal parts miso and honey, plus a glug of soy sauce. Nothing more. There’s no gotcha moment, no pepper hiding in plain sight or olive oil I forgot to account for. The flavor excels because each ingredient punches above its weight. Both fermented, the miso and soy sauce add umami depth, while honey supplies sweetness and makes the chicken glossy, like ceramics pulled straight from the kiln.

I’m often deflated when I see the word marinade in a recipe. No, I don’t have time for “at least an hour” or, more likely, the dreaded “preferably overnight.” Don’t they know it’s a Tuesday? This marinade, mercifully, is immediate. Because it is more of a paste than a liquid, you can slather it directly onto the chicken before hurling the baking sheet into the oven. (Of course, if you’re the type of person who has the wherewithal to start meal prep the night before, that works too.)

A short stint under the fierce heat of the broiler transforms the miso coating into a burnished, sticky crust in 15-ish minutes while you work on the rest of the meal. Old-school drawer-style broilers can get a little overexcited, so start peeking in after 10 minutes. Now is not the time to check the mail or start a load of laundry.

This chicken shines with rice tucked underneath, which catches every saucy streak. But you could build it out with roasted vegetables. Sometimes I’ll add oiled-up broccoli or asparagus to the baking sheet. Other times it’s just the chicken, with a shower of scallions if I’m feeling fancy. Then I make it all again next week.

How to Remind Your Boss of Your PTO Without Pissing Them Off

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You're about to slam that laptop shut for two weeks when the boss messages, “See you Monday!”

We've all been there, staring down a manager who has forgotten about our paid time off—even though we told them!

When we communicate something that's important to us, we can forget that it's not necessarily as meaningful to the recipient. Surely you've been on the flip side, too, annoyed at a coworker who hasn't replied to your messages, until someone mentions that they're on holiday.

A key to efficient communication in the workplace is to repeat yourself. It sounds counterintuitive, but it's true. We're all better off when we overcommunicate by saying the same thing two or three times.

The trick is how and when you do it. Think of all the avenues you have for requesting, recording, and reminding people of your PTO. Use all the channels, and spread out the messaging so that each instance has relevant context and timing.

If you like to plan in advance, you might request time off four to six months out. Not all managers appreciate this much notice, however, because they might not know what the workload will be when your vacation rolls around, making it hard for them to approve your dates.

How far in advance you should request time off is a conversation to have with your direct boss. It depends on them as a person, the type of work you do, the size of your team, and other factors. If this conversation doesn't give you much clarity, four to six weeks before your time off is probably about right. Start there and adjust as necessary.

Occasionally, you need approval well in advance for an event that cannot move and will cost you a lot of money to cancel, like your own wedding or a nonrefundable trip. In those cases, six months or more is not unreasonable, but bring it up from time to time so no one forgets.

Whenever you put in your initial request, it must be recorded. Log it into a time-off system if your organization uses one (human resources software, a shared calendar) or write it in an email with a clear subject line, and make sure your boss replies saying it's approved.

About two weeks before your time off, remind your boss and colleagues verbally in meetings. The ideal settings are in a weekly one-on-one meeting with your direct manager and in a team meeting.

Save your info for the end of the meeting after all pressing business has been discussed. If you need to make sure your responsibilities are covered while you're out, raise that point privately with your manager rather than in a team meeting so the boss has time to decide how to handle it.

If you need to tell someone beyond your team—such as clients or another department—about your planned time off, do so in writing a week before you leave. This gives them plenty of opportunity to reach out while you’re still available.

Email is fine, though if you usually communicate with these people through Slack or Microsoft Teams, that may be a better option. How you format the message matters—see the next step for tips.

Two business days before your time off begins, send a short, clear message to everyone who needs to know that you will be out. If the norm is to use Slack or Microsoft Teams, use that.

How you format this message makes a huge difference. The first sentence should say something like, "Just a reminder, I will be out beginning DATE. I will be back on DATE." This sentence can be in bold. The second DATE is the day you return to work, not the last day of your PTO. What's relevant to your colleagues is the day you are available.

If you need to give more information, such as who is covering for you, put that in a new paragraph. Do not go overboard with detail. It will make people crazy. Should you need to leave detailed instructions, put them in a shared document and link to it.

Why send this message two business days out? Simple: If your first PTO day is Monday, you send this message on Thursday so that anybody who takes off or calls in sick on Friday still sees it in time.

My favorite PTO reminder is the status indicator in a team messaging platform. Set it to show you're out and add an appropriate emoji, like a palm tree or airplane.

Even if you dislike emoji, they are extremely efficient and effective communicators here. At a glance, everyone who sees it is reminded that you're out.

You can typically set a status message too; use this to tell everyone the date you’ll be available again.

For those supremely forgetful bosses and coworkers, you might set up an automated message that appears at 8:30 am the first day you're out. Slack, Teams, email, or even a text message will do the trick. Be concise. Be kind. And have a sense of humor. The point of the message is to be helpful—not make someone else feel like a forgetful jerk.

InfoQ Homepage News Entire's Git Forge Rethinks Git Collaboration for Coding Agents

Nov 08, 2026 3 min read

Entire is a Git forge designed specifically for collaboration between human developers and AI coding agents. Its premise is that traditional software development infrastructure, particularly the pull request (PR) model, is not well suited to an environment where AI agents generate, review, and iterate on code at high speed.

As Thomas Dohmke, former GitHub CEO and currently CEO of Entire, explains, GitHub and GitLab transformed human-to-human collaboration by centering it around asynchronous code reviews and version control. A multi-agent forge, by contrast, centers around the asynchronous orchestration, versioning, and auditing of AI agents working alongside humans.

Built on top of a fully distributed architecture that enables pushes up to 89 times faster than GitHub, Entire's core component is trails, which addresses the challenges AI coding agents pose to the traditional PR-based development model:

The pull request [timeline] has become an audit log that only the agent reads. It does not provide the information for the developer to decide whether they can merge with confidence.

Trails bring the most relevant information to the top of the page in the form of eval scores that are updated on every push. These scores help developers assess whether a PR has reached a stable state or requires another iteration of agent-driven coding and review:

Only when the confidence is high, risk is low, and all findings are cleared, the merge should happen.

A trail can be initiated from a bug report, a feature request, or manually, and can span multiple repositories. Whenever new code is pushed, a review agent running in a cloud sandbox evaluates the changes and returns a score and a rationale based on code quality, security, slop, and whether human code review is required. For example, if the slop score is orange, developers can ask their coding agent to improve the code and push a new change, thus triggering a new iteration.

The last 5 runs and the current run are shown for each eval. We believe this is crucial as one-shot loops are often missing a critical issue. Repeating the loops and stabilizing the scores is crucial for the success of agentic coding.

Evaluation agents can be configured through Entire's interface or repository-level JSON configuration.

Review agents can suggest fixes or improvements, which remain visible in the trail while it is open. All review findings must be either resolved or closed before a merge is allowed. Developers can also configure repository-level Gates to define the conditions required for approval.

Another foundational concept introduced by Entire is continuous memory, which preserves all accumulated knowledge in the repository so that agents can reuse it across sessions rather than starting from scratch. Continuous memory is implemented by two open-source tools, entire-graph, which builds a precomputed map of the Git repository including ranked code searches, definition and callers, types and routes, and more; and entire-brain, which creates a persistent memory layer by combining the codebase map with previous agent sessions and information recorded in Git history.

This allows agents to always have the right context at the right time, aka the "why" behind the "what changed."

As a final note on Entire, it also supports integrations with CI platforms like Buildkite and Depot.

Commenting on the announcement on Hacker News, user conartist6 expressed the view that engineers have delegated writing code to agents because of programming languages and tools that leave much to be desired. Haimantika, currently at Entire, points out that the problem has never been languages or tools, rather

it' s that the reasoning gets thrown away between the person and the artifact. Commit messages were always bad, docs always rotted, “why is this here” was always answered by whoever remembered. Agents just made that acute, because now there's no one who remembers. That's the bit we're trying to fix. If a better language fixes it too, honestly I'd take that.

With most homes damaged or destroyed by the war, Gazans are facing worsening humanitarian conditions due to heavy rain.

Heavy rains sweeping across parts of the Middle East this week have flooded tens of thousands of tents in Gaza's displaced persons' camps.

According to authorities in the enclave, at least 70,000 tents have been flooded over the past day, where tens of thousands of people took shelter.

No fatalities had been reported, although some people were injured.

Some of the tents have been washed away due to the weather, with mattresses and blankets soaked with water.

More than 1.6 million residents of Gaza's 2.1-million population now live in tents or other makeshift shelters due to Israel's war in the enclave.

UN data says some 80% of the enclave's structures have been damaged during the war, with two-thirds destroyed, leaving Gaza's residents — many of whom were displaced multiple times during the war between Israel and Hamas — to either live in tents or risk their lives and seek shelter in buildings that could be on the verge of collapse.

The Higher Presidential Committee for Church Affairs in Palestine released a statement appealing for humanitarian aid for the Gaza Strip due to the situation.

According to the committee, some 950,000 people are living in areas "vulnerable to flooding," with more than 400,000 living in areas of "high levels of risk."

"Heavy rainfall is compounding an already catastrophic humanitarian situation, leaving displaced families and those who have lost their homes exposed to the elements and deprived of the most basic necessities of life," the statement said.

Some 74,000 people have been killed in the Gaza Strip since October 7, 2023. The war was triggered when Islamist Palestinian organization Hamas and its affiliates launched a terrorist attack in Israel's south, killing some 1,200 Israelis, most of whom were civilians. The militants also kidnapped 251 hostages.

The UN, EU, US and Israel, among others, consider Hamas to be a terrorist organization.
