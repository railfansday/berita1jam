Mayo Clinic Platform launches AI-driven clinical decision support tool" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Mayo Clinic launched an AI-enabled clinical decision support solution embedded directly in electronic health record workflows. The CDS solution will put Mayo’s clinical knowledge and evidence-informed guidance at the point of care, rather than requiring clinicians to search across separate resources.

Mayo Clinic Platform, a strategic initiative from the Rochester, Minn.-based health system, developed the tool, and it will be expanded and updated over time, executives said. The solution draws on multiple clinical tools, reducing the need for clinicians to navigate independent resources to find trusted clinical information.

Mayo Clinic is making the CDS tool available to Mayo Clinic clinicians as well as healthcare organizations participating in Mayo Clinic Platform's provider programs. Mayo Clinic is essentially turning its clinical expertise into scalable AI infrastructure available beyond its own health system.

Beginning in November, eligible clinicians will have access to two key capabilities: a specialty-specific chat that covers more than 35 specialties and 22 clinical calculators available via chat or voice dictation.

Powered by a large language model, the specialty-specific AI chat tool surfaces relevant information to help clinicians reason through complex cases and cites responses from peer-reviewed literature. The tool is updated daily, giving clinicians access to relevant emerging evidence alongside established medical knowledge, executives said. The tool helps clinicians quickly answer patient-specific clinical questions, explore differential diagnoses and evaluate care options. And the specialty-specific AI chat mirrors Mayo Clinic's subspecialty model of care.

The clinical calculators, spanning cardiology, oncology, hepatology, endocrinology and other specialty use cases, provide industry-standard medical information and evidence-based decision support on clinical questions and patient care context. Proprietary Mayo Clinic-developed calculators are included in this tool, according to the health system.

"Clinicians have access to a vast amount of medical knowledge, but finding relevant information for individualized patient care takes valuable time," said Maneesh Goyal, Mayo Clinic Platform chief operating officer, in a statement. "With this new solution, we are bringing trusted Mayo Clinic knowledge directly into the clinical workflow so clinicians can spend less time searching for information across multiple resources and more time focused on patient care."

The platform is "designed to evolve" as new tools are added to Mayo Clinic Platform, executives say.

David C. and Flora C. Pratt Distinguished Chief Medical Officer for Mayo Clinic Platform Gelareh Zadeh, M.D., Ph.D., said in a statement the solution "does not replace clinical judgment."

"Mayo Clinic physicians have provided feedback on the different tools to ensure the solution supports clinicians and reflects Mayo Clinic's approach to patient care," Zadeh said.

To op­ti­mize the per­for­mance met­rics of this blog, I re­lied on Ama­zon Cloud­Front to serve the as­sets,1 and Route 53 for the geo-aware DNS2 to di­rect read­ers to the clos­est host serv­ing the con­tent. But Route 53 is ex­pen­sive: US$0.50 per zone per month, US$0.70 per mil­lion ge­olo­ca­tion queries, and US$0.75 per healthcheck per month[1], plus US$1 per month for the KMS key[2] needed to sign zones with DNSSEC. When I dis­cov­ered that Bunny DNS is al­most free[3] for up to 500 do­mains and fea­tures script­able an­swers, I made the jump! Plus, it’s a Eu­ro­pean com­pany based in Slovenia.

I had been host­ing me­dia as­sets on Cloud­Front since 2011. ❦

Route 53 had served bernat.ch since De­cem­ber 2021. I didn’t use healthchecks as they were too ex­pen­sive. I didn’t host most of my zones there ei­ther, as queries for haproxy.debian.net alone would have cost me around US$10 per month. ❦

Bunny.net[4] started as BunnyCDN in 2015[5]. They added IPv6 sup­port in 2017[6]. Switch­ing was un­event­ful. I don’t bring a cus­tom do­main: I serve the con­tent from the b-cdn.net do­main. As I de­pend on con­tent ne­go­ti­a­tion to serve AVIF and WebP im­ages[7], I con­fig­ure the CDN to cache each im­age for­mat sep­arately[8].

I dis­able log­ging[9] to avoid col­lect­ing per­sonal in­for­ma­tion. While the bunny.net GDPR page[10] prom­ises they anonymize data, it is un­clear what Cloud­Front keeps in its own logs when log­ging is disabled.

The only down­side of the bunny.net CDN of­fer­ing is the lack of HTTP/3. I asked their sup­port: two min­utes later, they an­swered they are work­ing on it but have no ETA to share.

Bunny.net in­tro­duced their DNS of­fer­ing in 2022[11] and re­cently re­duced its cost to al­most zero[3]. They sup­port ge­o­graph­i­cal rout­ing[12], but a very cool fea­ture is the script­able DNS[13]: it runs a piece of user-sup­plied Java­Script on each query to com­pute the answer.

I run seven small NixOS vir­tual ma­chines to host this blog around the world: Paris, Nurem­berg, Ash­burn, Sin­ga­pore, San Jose, São Paulo, and Syd­ney. Bunny’s script­able DNS lets me re­turn the IP ad­dresses of the two near­est servers. The browser falls back to the sec­ond server if the first one is down.

The most an­noy­ing draw­back of this method is that a user from Buenos Aires gets both São Paulo (1000 mi, 30 ms RTT) and Ash­burn (5200 mi, 140 ms RTT). Most re­solvers ran­dom­ize the or­der, so the user may pull the web page from Ash­burn. We could re­turn only the near­est server—and swap the scripted record with the built-in ge­o­graphic rout­ing in­stead. But if the server be­comes un­avail­able, the browser has no al­ter­na­tive un­til the DNS cache ex­pires. It leaves a short win­dow of un­avail­abil­ity, even with a short TTL.

Tesla to wind down Cybertruck production in Texas next year to ramp Cybercab, Musk says

📅 2026-11-06  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tesla CEO Elon Musk on Friday said the electric vehicle maker will "be winding down" production of the Cybertruck at its Texas gigafactory around the middle of next year.

Musk, who hailed the Cybertruck as the future of the pickup truck segment, said in a post on X early Friday morning that the end of production at the massive facility would make room for manufacturing of its Cybercab robotaxi.

The Cybertruck is exclusively produced at the company's plant in Texas alongside the Tesla Model Y and two-seat Cybercab, which Musk has touted as a crucial product for the automaker moving forward.

Musk did not say whether the company would produce the Cybertruck at a different facility, but he later responded to a user on X who called the update an "end of an era" and said the Cybertruck was the "best vehicle Tesla has ever made."

"True, but the Cybertruck fleet will be around for a long time," Musk said.

Tesla began Cybertruck deliveries in late 2023. The company did not immediately respond to a request for comment from CNBC.

The Cybertruck and its polarizing design gained a niche following, but it did not achieve mass-market sales, despite Musk's pre-launch prediction that annual sales would eventually total 250,000.

"Trucks have been the same for a very long time," Musk said when the truck was unveiled in 2019. "Like 100 years. Trucks have been basically the same. We want to try something different."

While Tesla does not break out its sales by model or region, Cox Automotive estimated sales of the Cybertruck last year at 20,237 units in the U.S., down 48% from 38,965 in 2024.

One big buyer of the Cybertruck last year was Musk's aerospace and tech conglomerate, SpaceX, which spent $131 million buying up the angular steel trucks at Tesla's suggested retail price, according to a SpaceX financial filing.

The distinct look of the vehicle made some owners a target of rude gestures and more serious road rage, one owner told CNBC last May, after Musk became the biggest funder of Donald Trump's presidential campaign and led the so-called Department of Government Efficiency, an effort to slash the federal workforce and regulations.

Wired wrote in an October 2025 feature story, "There is likely no object that feels more emblematic of US president Donald Trump's return to the White House than the Tesla Cybertruck." The magazine interviewed owners who described being spit on and sworn at due to their choice of vehicle.

The Cybertruck's specifications also fell short of those Tesla advertised early on, and in some cases fell short of those offered by leading fuel-burning pickups. There have also been many quality and production problems associated with the vehicles.

The 2024 Tesla Cybertruck was the subject of 11 voluntary recalls, according to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration website. Issues ranged from a "trapped" acceleration pedal risk that could cause unintended acceleration, to software defects and an inverter fault that could result in sudden loss of drive power.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/regulatory/industry-voices-new-majority-new-targets-where-healthcare-fraud-enforcement-goes-next" hreflang="en">Industry Voices—New majority, new targets: Where healthcare fraud enforcement goes next</a>

📅 2026-11-06  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Industry Voices—New majority, new targets: Where healthcare fraud enforcement goes next" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Control of Congress has changed. The bipartisan appetite for scrutinizing practices across the healthcare industry has not. A new Democratic majority will arrive with no shortage of investigative priorities and only so much time to pursue them, but recent history suggests that healthcare is likely to be among them.

Affordability dominated this election cycle, and healthcare sits at the center of that debate. Combating fraud in Medicare and Medicaid has endured as a rare point of consensus precisely because it allows policymakers of both parties to say they are protecting patients and taxpayers at once. The open question is not whether that work continues, but where it is directed.

Congressional oversight largely reflects the priorities of the majority, which controls committee agendas, hearings and subpoenas. If recent history is a guide, a Democratic majority will gravitate toward a familiar set of concerns: the practices of large insurers including those involved in Medicare Advantage, the stewardship of hospital systems, the role of private equity, vertical integration across the industry, drug pricing, and the hyper-growth sectors attracting new investment.

The administration has elevated healthcare fraud to the top of its enforcement agenda. The Justice Department’s 2026 National Health Care Fraud Takedown charged 455 defendants in schemes involving more than $6.5 billion in alleged losses, with particular emphasis on Medicaid, while CMS suspended or revoked the billing privileges of more than 2,400 providers. In an October 1 directive, DOJ’s newly created National Fraud Enforcement Division went further, designating healthcare fraud its foremost corporate enforcement priority.

That model has been defined largely by scale: the number of defendants charged and the dollar value of alleged fraud identified. It will persist regardless of which party holds Congress, carried forward by entrepreneurial prosecutors and agents. What a Democratic majority could add is a complementary lens, one that weighs not only how much fraud is charged but how the industry’s largest and fastest-growing participants are organized, owned and paid.

The best predictor of the next two years may be the last several. While Democrats controlled the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, its October 2024 majority staff report examined post-acute care denials by the largest Medicare Advantage insurers. That inquiry did not end with the change in Senate control. In July 2026, Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-Connecticut) and Sen. Josh Hawley (R-Missouri) sought updated data from one of those insurers on its prior authorization practices and its use of algorithms in coverage decisions. In the House, Democrats have called for closer scrutiny of insurer practices at nursing homes serving Medicare Advantage members.

Hospitals have drawn bipartisan attention as well. A January 2025 Senate Budget Committee report on private equity ownership of hospitals, led by Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse (D-Rhode Island) and Sen. Chuck Grassley (R-Iowa), raised concerns about staffing, hospital closures and distributions to investors. Months earlier, the Senate HELP Committee, then chaired by Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vermont), subpoenaed the chief executive of a bankrupt, private equity-backed hospital system, and the full Senate voted unanimously to hold him in contempt after he declined to testify.

Concern extends to the structure of the industry itself. In May 2026, Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-Massachusetts) and Sen. Josh Hawley, joined by House members from both parties, reintroduced legislation that would require companies that own pharmacy benefit managers to divest their pharmacies, a measure that would force significant restructuring at some of the largest vertically integrated healthcare companies. Drug pricing has drawn similar scrutiny. In September 2024, the HELP Committee called the chief executive of a leading manufacturer of GLP-1 drugs to testify about U.S. prices for its diabetes and weight-loss treatments.

To the extent healthcare commands the new majority’s attention, the tools at its disposal are considerable. Committee chairs can compel documents and testimony by subpoena, require executives to testify under oath and publish staff reports that place internal records in the public domain.

Committees’ influence extends well beyond the hearing room. They can call on the Government Accountability Office and the HHS Office of Inspector General to examine particular programs, press CMS for data and refer matters to the Justice Department. Through legislation and appropriations, they can push for reforms in areas such as prior authorization, Medicare Advantage payment and transparency in private equity ownership. Oversight does not generate cases on its own, but it shapes where agencies look, which documents surface, and what insiders conclude is worth reporting. It shapes public opinion as well. Under the glare of hearings and headlines, a misstep that might once have been resolved quietly can escalate into a reputational and regulatory crisis.

Scrutiny of private equity tends to follow the sectors where investment has moved fastest. Applied behavior analysis (ABA) therapy for children with autism illustrates the dynamic. The underlying demand is genuine: the CDC estimates that one in 31 children has autism spectrum disorder. Private equity firms have acquired more than 500 autism therapy centers over the past decade, and CMS reported in August that Medicaid and CHIP spending on ABA rose 421% between 2021 and 2025, far outpacing the 67% growth in the number of children served.

Whatever the causes of that gap, regulators have begun to respond. CMS issued a toolkit to help states strengthen oversight of ABA services, New York halved Medicaid reimbursement for certain ABA services in October, citing program integrity concerns, and Indiana paused new ABA provider enrollment. Sectors that combine rapid growth, substantial public funding and concentrated investor ownership are natural subjects of congressional interest, whichever party is in the majority.

Should the emphasis shift, Medicare Advantage is the likeliest starting point, given the sustained attention already paid to prior authorization, post-acute care denials, algorithm-driven coverage decisions and risk adjustment. Beyond it, the record points to three broader fronts that track how the industry is organized, owned, and paid. The first is integration, as lawmakers ask whether combining insurers, pharmacy benefit managers, pharmacies and providers under one corporate roof serves patients. The second is ownership, as committees examine how private equity affects staffing, billing and quality at hospitals and in hyper-growth sectors in healthcare. The third is pricing, particularly for prescription drugs.

For companies in these areas, the practical consequence is that exposure can arrive before any agency acts, and congressional and Justice Department scrutiny tend to reinforce each other. A single congressional letter can place internal records and decision-making in public view, and the attention that follows tends to encourage potential whistleblowers to come forward. Those insiders have strong incentives to take their concerns to the Justice Department as well. Under the False Claims Act, a whistleblower who files a qui tam suit may receive between 15% and 30% of the government’s recovery, and DOJ’s Corporate Whistleblower Awards Pilot Program offers awards for original information about criminal healthcare fraud, including fraud against private insurers.

The overlap in subject matter is considerable. The joint DOJ-HHS False Claims Act Working Group has named Medicare Advantage and drug pricing among its enforcement priorities, the same areas that have drawn sustained congressional interest. Testimony and documents made public through oversight can become a road map for relators’ counsel and prosecutors alike, and many healthcare fraud cases still originate with an insider.

For companies and investors, the prudent response does not depend on who holds the gavel. It begins with a clear-eyed understanding of where the business’s risks reside, followed by rigorous testing of controls in the areas of fastest growth and highest reimbursement, and a willingness to address problems early, with the benefit of counsel where the facts are uncertain. Enforcement priorities shift with each election. The expectation that companies will identify and remedy their own problems does not.

Kevin Lowell is a member at Miller & Chevalier and previously served as deputy chief of the National Fraud Enforcement Division at the U.S. Department of Justice.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services plans to trim several “unnecessary” requirements for hospitals and other providers participating in Medicare, including emergency services readiness requirements and staff training for obstetrical services that had been brought during the tail end of the Biden administration.

The proposed rule’s Conditions of Participation (CoP) rollbacks, shared late Thursday (PDF), followed a “comprehensive review” CMS conducted to identify and eliminate “obsolete, outdated and excessively burdensome regulations.”

Removing these, the agency estimated, would save $320 million and free up 3.26 million hours of staff time for provider organizations.

Among the chief changes is a repeal of requirements finalized in the 2025 Outpatient Prospective Payment System (OPPS) and ASC Payment Systems rule that tasked hospital and critical access hospital emergency departments to be “continually prepared” to provide emergency screening or stabilization regardless of whether they provide relevant specialty services, which were made effective in July 2025.

The 2025 rule paired those with baseline staffing and care delivery standards, annual staff training and maternal quality assessment and performance improvement (QAPI) requirements around obstetric care, which are currently set to take effect on Jan. 1, 2027. CMS also proposed eliminating those requirements this week.

CMS under the prior administration had said these requirements would improve safety, quality and the timeliness of care delivery for pregnant women.

On the other hand, CMS' new proposed rule looks to add a new obstetrics requirement for hospitals and critical access hospitals tied to the collection of birth tissue, “including standards for informed consent and policies and procedures to support families requesting their birth tissues for cultural, religious or personal reasons,” per a fact sheet.

Other proposed changes to the CoPs include clarification revisions to policies regarding distance-related eligibility for critical access hospital designation and to implement an exemption from the distance eligibility requirements for some critical access hospitals.

Long-term care facilities would also see changes around COVID-19 vaccination education for staff and uptake for residents, as well as exemptions for existing facilities from newer room occupancy and bathroom facility standards. Among other changes are removals or slim downs of respiratory illness data reporting requirements for multiple types of provider facilities.

The proposed rule will be open for public comment for 60 days. Among these, CMS said it is interested in hearing from stakeholders on additional requirements for participating providers, including flexibilities for physician supervision of certified registered nurse anesthetists, expanding the scope of practice of nurse practitioners and physician assistants in long-term care facilities, and reducing barriers for new transplant programs to achieve initial certification.
