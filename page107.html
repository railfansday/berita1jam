Oct. 17—AeviusClinical intelligence AI modelsSeries: N/AAmount: $22 millionInvestors: Led by Insight Partners

Aevius is a newly launched Mass General Brigham spinout founded by veterans of AstreaZeneca-acquired Modella AI. The company builds models that intake different forms of medical data to paint a multimodal picture of a patient's current state and trajectory. That information can then be used by healthcare, payer and life sciences organizations for potential application in care delivery, operational decision-making and clinical research.

“Our mission is to make medicine computable,” Rowland Pettit, M.D., Ph.D., co-founder and CEO of Aevius, said. “As a physician-scientist, I’ve worked across many types of clinical data, and there is far more knowledge in those records than we’re able to use today. We’re bringing general medical intelligence to that data, so organizations can build on a deeper understanding for their patients.”

The company said it plans to use the $22 million launch money to support continued model and product development. It also plans to expand its engineering, clinical and commercial teams, as well as to focus on commercial deployment.

Abby Care is an AI-native home care startup that equips family caregivers with professional training, certification and ongoing support from dedicated RN care managers.  The company works with health plans to arrange care at home for families that need it. Abby Care's platform also gives care teams real-time visibility into care delivered in the home, helping ensure families receive the support they need while enabling health plans to verify that care is being provided. Fortune reported the series C round.

The U.S. faces a major healthcare workforce shortage along with a rapidly aging population, and waitlists of vulnerable children needing care, noted Havi Nguyen, founder of Abby Care in a LinkedIn post."Family caregivers already know their loved ones best and are in the home. Better care and program integrity go together," Nguyen wrote.

Abby Care functions as a Medicaid provider to pay caregivers within Colorado, Florida, Georgia, Indiana, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Tennessee.

So far, Abby Care has helped families deliver millions of hours of care across many states, the company's members had 40%+ fewer reported hospitalizations in year one, she wrote.

Today, the company serves thousands of families across eight states, with additional markets expected to launch soon, executives said.

The funding will enable the company to bring its model to more families, deepen its work with health plans and continue building technology and clinical infrastructure, the company said in a blog post.

Allswell's therapeutic models were built for patients navigating depression and anxiety, identity-specific trauma, family dynamics and social transition. It is also developing its own curriculum on PTSD and trauma care.

The startup, though new, is already seeing positive outcomes. The company often keeps patients for over a year, rare for behavioral health. In its most recent cohorts, 77% showed improvements in anxiety and 71% showed improvements in depression after 60 days of treatment.

"Our patients aren't used to being served with care and respect, or to having something built for them. ... This is a sleeping-giant category, and it has been underestimated at every step. Our ambition is to continue developing new, thoughtful clinical programs specifically for the LGBTQ+ community, regardless of the political climate," Dawn Androphy, co-founder and CEO of Allswell, said in an announcement.

Sept. 24—Basalt Health AI layer for post-acute care admissionsSeries: AAmount: $20 millionInvestors: NEA led the round, with participation from Frist Cressey Ventures and 25m Health

Basalt Health uses AI to streamline post-acute care admissions. The company plans to use the capital to expand its platform and grow its team.

Basalt's technology automates the referral and admissions process for post-acute providers, analyzing incoming referrals from faxes, portals and EHR integrations and matching patients to appropriate facilities based on clinical and payer criteria. The company says its platform reduces administrative work and speeds placement decisions that are often delayed by manual processes.

Each year, roughly 14 million patients enter post-acute care—yet the placement process has failed to evolve. The archaic process typically involves case managers faxing lengthy clinical packets to 6.6 facilities per placement, then waiting for the right bed to respond. The resulting delays and mismatches contribute to an estimated $17 billion in avoidable readmission costs annually.

After nearly a decade at Accolade, where he saw administrative work increasingly pull clinicians away from patients, Ben Hackett founded Basalt in 2024 to build technology that fits existing workflows, reduces friction and gives care teams more time to focus on patients.

The startup reports strong early traction with major health systems. Basalt has been live with Lifepoint Health’s team since its seed round in 2025 and the company has reduced median chart processing time from 8.5 minutes to 1.2 minutes. Basalt is also being implemented across 49 additional Lifepoint markets by the end of 2026.

“Case managers are still sending 70-page packets and waiting for responses, while patients remain stranded in hospital beds,” Hackett, founder and CEO of Basalt Health, said. “Basalt turns that uncertainty into fast, defensible decisions. This funding gives us the power to transform one of healthcare’s most stubbornly inefficient systems.”

Basalt plans to expand beyond admissions into discharge planning and payer workflows, positioning its AI platform as infrastructure for managing patient transitions into post-acute care.

TPU Model Design Considerations and Optimization Guide

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google designed TPUs from the ground up to be exceptionally efficient for machine learning workloads, delivering top-tier performance per total cost of ownership (TCO). Because TPUs trade general-purpose hardware scheduling for wide systolic arrays, dedicated SparseCore engines, and custom interconnect fabrics, adhering to specific architectural best practices is essential to achieve maximum performance. To help developers get the most out of TPUs, we are launching the open-source AI-Hypercomputer/tpu-codesign guide. This post highlights its key principles—spanning shape alignment, architectural choices like LatentMoE, and parallelism strategies—to accelerate LLMs, Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, and other hardware-intensive workloads on TPUs.

Performance tuning starts with model definition. Architectural choices made before the first training step set permanent ceilings on MFU (Model FLOP Utilization), cost, and serving latency. This guide gives concrete, hardware-grounded design rules for teams building or adapting models for TPUs, organized into three groups:

How to use this guide. The shape rules apply to anyone designing a new model. If you are adapting an existing model rather than designing one, the Expressing Parallelism section applies without touching the checkpoint. The general rules hold for any large-matmul architecture (dense LLMs, multimodal, and world models), and the companion AI-Hypercomputer/tpu-codesign repository grows model- and workload-specific sections over time.

Eighth-generation TPUs. These principles carry forward to TPU 8t and TPU 8i and to the newly announced TPU 8tx and TPU 8i Max. The reasoning stays the same and the thresholds move with the hardware. TPU 8t and TPU 8i both add native FP4. For training, TPU 8t also brings more balanced Vector Processing Unit (VPU) scaling. For serving, TPU 8i adds the Boardfly topology, a Collectives Acceleration Engine (CAE), and 3x more on-chip SRAM. We note the effects inline in Principles 5–8, and follow-up posts will cover eighth-generation designs in depth.

Measured evidence. Alongside the published sources, notes marked Measured carry results from our own Ironwood profiling program: open 20–30B MoEs (a Qwen3-30B-derived LatentMoE variant; GPT-OSS-20B) plus dense controls, single slice (except for the 512-chip TPU v7x and 1,024-chip TPU v4 ablations in Principle 4), BF16, training shapes up to 32K sequence, all against tuned-systems baselines. Treat the trends and rankings as transferable, the exact percentages as config-specific.

TPUs are purpose-built custom ML ASICs designed from the ground up to deliver state-of-the-art performance per total cost of ownership (TCO). While their architectural choices—such as wide 256×256 systolic arrays, software-managed VMEM, dedicated SparseCore engines, and direct multi-dimensional ICI fabrics—enable exceptional efficiency, they inherently create specific structural constraints and programming patterns that must be carefully followed to unlock maximal performance. Reaching peak efficiency requires aligning model shapes, routing, and parallelism directly with these underlying hardware characteristics:

Make d_model, FFN and expert intermediate widths (d_ff), latent widths, attention projections, and padded vocabulary sizes (used as contracting or output dimensions) multiples of 256 to match the 256×256 MXU systolic array. Token/batch axes and counts such as heads, layers, and experts are exempt. The most frequently missed dimension is head_dim: at 64 or 128, flash-attention QK products leave the MXU ≥ 50% idle. Reshape to head_dim = 256 with proportionally fewer Q and KV heads, preserving total projection widths and logical KV cache bytes (iso-quality in our ablations).

Measured: Reshaping Qwen3-30B MoE attention from 32 × 128 to 16 × 256 (identical parameters and FLOPs) sped up end-to-end training, with gains widening at longer context:

These rules apply for non-quadratic attention as well, notably GDN ablations from Yang et al. 2025 also noted at par or better quality for head dimension 256 for their Gated DeltaNet Attention.

XLA compiles each program for exact tensor shapes, so every buffer has a fixed address and every loop a known trip count, and a new shape means a recompilation and a host stall. What about dynamic shapes? Dynamic workloads fit this model: the trick is to separate the shape of a buffer from the extent of the data inside it.

Instead of padding to the largest group or the longest sequence, we keep the buffers at fixed shapes (tokens sorted by expert and padded only at the tail, the KV cache as a fixed pool of pages) and describe the ragged structure with a few small integer arrays (group sizes and offsets; KV lengths, the page table, cumulative query lengths). Pallas copies these into the TensorCore's scalar memory before the grid starts (PrefetchScalarGridSpec), and the kernel reads them to choose the next tile and bound its loops, with a mask covering a tile that straddles a group boundary or a partly filled last page. The data dependence becomes an address and a trip count instead of a shape, so the compiled program is the same for every routing and every batch composition and the kernel runs at the unpadded FLOP count.

Standard EP dispatches every routed token at full d_model width, making EP + FSDP slower than plain FSDP in our Ironwood benchmarks. LatentMoE projects routed tokens from d_model down to latent width ℓ (d/ℓ ≈ 2–4×) before dispatch, executes experts in latent space, and projects back up after combine (introduced by Nemotron 3 and adopted by 962B MAI-Base-1 and 2.8T Kimi K3):

Measured: On a 30B / 128-expert MoE, an ℓ = 768 (2.67×) LatentMoE cut on-device permute time 2.8×, lifted end-to-end training throughput +14% tokens/s (322 vs 270 TFLOP/s/chip, or +19%, since the latent projections add ~5% FLOPs per token; tokens stay ℓ-wide past the down-projection, so on-device permute and combine shrink alongside EP all-to-all), and freed ~25 GB/device of activation HBM when remat checkpoints were anchored on the compressed latent. While current MoE recipes in tpu-recipes run FSDP (+ TP), LatentMoE + FP8 dispatch makes EP roofline-viable when per-device batch is too small to hide FSDP weight all-gathers. Under both FSDP and EP, avoid over-fragmenting experts: small tokens × K / N group sizes starve MXU tiles.

When candidate MoE layouts match on quality, pick the one with higher compute intensity at a similar active-parameter budget. A larger shared expert (wider d_shared) shifts work into dense, communication-free GEMMs that overlap EP dispatch/combine in training and amortize weight reads across the decode batch. Fewer, wider routed experts (halving N and K while doubling d_ff at fixed sparsity K/N) cut dispatch traffic in half and double per-expert GEMM fill. Optimal (N, K) grows with model size, and over-coarsening hurts routing diversity. Ablate expert granularity and the shared-to-routed split at your target scale rather than copying GPU co-designed models, since the TPU optimum is more shared-heavy.

Low-concurrency decode streams model weights every token (~300 concurrent tokens per weight shard are needed in dense FP8 to reach compute roofline). Combine two architectural levers with explicit concurrency sizing:

Norms, activations, masking, routing math and quantization scale applies run on the Vector Processing Unit (VPU), which is roughly two orders of magnitude below the MXU in throughput and executes in sequence with the matmuls unless XLA fuses it into an adjacent GEMM or we move it off the TensorCore. Note that fused VPU work is free only while its cycle count fits inside the MXU window it is fused into. A quick illustration of this concept is block-scaled fp8 GEMM.

With a scale per block of B elements along the contraction axis, the MXU emits one partial sum per output element per block and the VPU rescales each one (about three vector ops), so per output element the MXU does 2K flops and the VPU does 3·ceil(K/B) ops. The rescale hides when B ≥ 1.5 × r_mxu / r_vpu, which on v7x (fp8 peak 4,614 TFLOPS against a 19.2 TFLOPS vector peak, a ratio near 240) puts the threshold near 340. In simulation on a DeepSeek-V3 expert shape, block 512 runs at the per-tensor time, block 256 is VPU-bound at 1.2x and block 128 at 2.4x. Achieved MXU efficiency is lower than peak, which lowers the effective ratio and can make the smaller blocks workable; the way to decide is to measure the GEMM at each block instead of using peak ratio. This threshold is specific to Ironwood. TPU 8t is designed with specialized support for scaling so quantization for MXFP4 and MXFP8 formats has no overhead. Re-measure the block-size threshold there instead of reusing 340. The rule itself carries forward: keep vector work hidden inside a matmul window.

For workloads dominated by BF16-sensitive matmuls (most notably long-context quadratic attention where direct FP8 degrades accuracy), native single-pass BF16 leaves MXU capacity unused whenever hardware low-precision MXU throughput exceeds native BF16 throughput by more than the pass count. Use the multi-pass BF16 algorithm to execute BF16-accurate matmuls via multiple passes over the higher-throughput lower-precision MXU pipeline without losing numerical fidelity. This matters more as low-precision throughput pulls ahead of BF16. TPU 8t adds native FP4, which doubles MXU throughput. On Ironwood the FP8 to BF16 ratio is exactly 2:1, so two or more passes cannot beat native BF16.

What We Learned When Sabastian Sawe Finally Broke the Two-Hour Marathon

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In the early 2010s, the idea of breaking two hours in the marathon went from pipe dream to attainable goal. For the first decade of the new millennium, the world’s top distance runners had been chipping away at it. Paul Tergat first breached the 2:05 barrier with his 2:04:55 mark at the 2003 Berlin Marathon. Haile Gebrselassie ran 2:03:59 at the same race in 2008, and by 2014, Dennis Kimmetto held the record with a 2:02:57, less than seven seconds per mile away from sub-two.

Like the four-minute mile, first toppled by Roger Bannister in Oxford, England, in 1954, the two-hour marathon is an enticing target. It’s an even number in a popular event. It’s easy to describe, and therefore easy to market. Perhaps that’s why Nike decided to pour millions of dollars into their Breaking2 project, starting in 2016, bringing Eliud Kipchoge within 30 seconds of the barrier on May 6, 2017, in an exhibition race on the F1 track in Monza, Italy.

After that much-watched effort, Kipchoge became the torchbearer for breaking two hours. His next attempt in 2019 came with a perfectly flat looped course, a roster of pacers blocking the wind, controlled nutrition on a controlled course, and a finishing time of 1:59:40. Kipchoge showed the world that sub-two was possible. He made history. But he didn’t do it in a race.

It would take a special set of circumstances to see the result in a record-eligible setting. An elite athlete would need the right training, the right weather conditions, the right competition. The previous nine times the world record had been set, these circumstances came together in a fall race (Berlin eight times, Chicago once). But at the 2026 London Marathon on April 26, the stage was set. Sabastian Sawe had returned to defend his title, lining up alongside 2025 Chicago Marathon champion Jacob Kiplimo and Yomif Kejelcha, an accomplished track runner making his marathon debut.

On a clear, calm day, the three runners followed pacers through the halfway mark in 60:29. To drop below two hours, they would need to run the second half at least a minute faster, cutting the pace by at least five seconds per mile from their average of 4:37. By mile 18, Kiplimo was falling off the back, leaving just Kejelcha stubbornly hanging on to the shoulder of a hard-charging Sawe. Sawe continued ratcheting the pace down, running the eighth 5K of the race (from approximately mile 21 to 24) in a blistering 13:42—4:25 per mile. But Kejelcha hung on, staying on Sawe’s shoulder until the final four minutes.

When he crossed the line in a time of 1:59:30, Sawe became the first runner to break two hours in a record-eligible marathon. Eleven seconds later, Kejelcha became the second. Kiplimo’s third place time of 2:00:28 was also faster than the previous world record. The two-hour barrier wasn’t broken by an exceptional individual in a one-off performance. Ultimately, a true race with fierce competition and constant pressure was what it took to bring Sawe into the history books.

AG-UI Protocol: How Event-Based Streaming Replaces Request/Response for Agent-to-Frontend Communication

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 17 • Originally published at mech.app

Agents stream tokens, pause for tool calls, spawn subagents, and wait for human input. Traditional request/response patterns break under this workload. Every agent framework (LangChain, CrewAI, Mastra, Google ADK, Microsoft Agent Framework, Pydantic AI) implements its own streaming format, and every frontend team writes custom adapters to consume it.

AG-UI is a bidirectional event protocol that standardizes agent-to-client communication. The agent emits typed events (token deltas, tool invocations, state transitions, human-in-the-loop pauses), and any client (web, mobile, terminal, Slack) can render them without knowing which framework produced the agent. The same agent can power multiple surfaces without rewiring.

The protocol has 19,683 GitHub stars and is trending #1 for TypeScript. It ships as @ag-ui/core on npm and ag-ui-protocol on PyPI.

A typical REST or GraphQL call expects one request and one response. Agents produce a stream of partial outputs:

Each framework handles this differently. LangChain uses StreamingStdOutCallbackHandler and custom event schemas. CrewAI emits task updates via a proprietary callback system. Mastra has its own streaming API. Clients that support multiple frameworks end up with a branching adapter layer that translates each format into a common UI state model.

AG-UI eliminates the adapter layer by defining a canonical event set that all frameworks can emit.

AG-UI is a typed event stream over WebSocket, Server-Sent Events, or any bidirectional transport. The protocol defines:

AG-UI events carry a sequence_id field. Clients maintain a local event log and discard duplicates. If the WebSocket drops, the client reconnects and sends a resume_from message with the last received sequence ID. The agent replays missed events from its own event log.

For long-running agents, the protocol supports checkpointing. The agent emits a checkpoint event with a serialized state snapshot. The client stores the checkpoint in IndexedDB or local storage. On reconnection, the client sends the checkpoint ID, and the agent resumes from that point without replaying the entire history.

AG-UI provides adapters for six frameworks. Each adapter translates framework-specific callbacks into AG-UI events.

The adapter layer is thin. Most frameworks already emit structured events internally; the adapter just renames fields and adds sequence IDs.

AG-UI clients maintain three state buckets:

When a token event arrives, the client appends the delta to the current message. When a tool_call event arrives, the client renders a "Tool executing..." indicator. When a pause event arrives with reason: awaiting_approval, the client shows an approval button.

The protocol does not dictate UI behavior. It only defines the event schema. One client might render tool calls as collapsible cards; another might show a terminal-style log.

When a parent agent spawns a subagent, it emits a subagent_start event with a subagent_id. Subsequent events from the subagent carry the same subagent_id in their metadata. The client can nest the subagent's UI under the parent's message or render it in a separate pane.

When the subagent completes, the parent emits a subagent_done event. The client merges the subagent's final output into the parent's message thread.

AG-UI does not define authentication or authorization. It assumes the transport layer (WebSocket, SSE) is already secured with TLS and bearer tokens.

The protocol does include a user_id field in client-to-agent events. The agent can use this to enforce per-user rate limits, access control, or audit logging. The client must not trust the user_id field; the server should validate it against the session token.

For multi-tenant deployments, the agent should namespace event logs and checkpoints by tenant_id. This prevents one tenant from resuming another tenant's session.

AG-UI events are structured JSON. You can pipe them to any log aggregator (Datadog, Grafana Loki, CloudWatch) without custom parsing.

For debugging, the protocol defines a debug event type. Agents can emit debug events with stack traces, intermediate reasoning steps, or tool execution timings. Clients can toggle a debug panel that renders these events.

The event log also serves as a replay buffer. If a user reports a bug, you can export the event log, replay it in a local client, and reproduce the exact UI state.

AG-UI supports three deployment patterns:

For serverless deployments, you need a durable event log. The agent writes events to DynamoDB or S3, and the client polls for new events. This adds latency but avoids keeping the function alive for the entire session.

The protocol does not define retry semantics. Clients and agents must implement exponential backoff and circuit breakers themselves.

AG-UI solves the impedance mismatch between streaming agent execution and frontend rendering. It is a good fit for multi-framework deployments, multi-surface clients, and teams that want to avoid custom adapter layers. The protocol is young (launched in 2026), so expect breaking changes in the event schema as the community converges on best practices.

The main risk is fragmentation. If frameworks add custom event types outside the spec, clients will need adapters again. The protocol needs governance to prevent this.

For production deployments, pair AG-UI with a durable event log (Kafka, DynamoDB Streams) and a replay mechanism. The protocol assumes reliable delivery, but networks are not reliable.

A Core Merge Is Not a Feature

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 17 • Originally published at blog.kilo.ai

The work isn’t done when the PR merges. It’s done when someone can use it.

A word from our VP of Engineering Emilie Schario

Nobody uses Kilo Core directly. They use VS Code, JetBrains, the CLI, Agent Manager, or Desktop. Core is the shared code that powers all of those experiences.

This distinction matters because merging code is not the same thing as shipping value to a customer.

At Kilo, we've always optimized for getting value into users' hands as quickly as possible. We don't organize our work around monthly releases, big launches, or waiting until every client has the same functionality.

We organize around one question: What's the smallest thing we can ship that delivers value to a user?

Kilo clients release multiple times a week. Any engineer can cut a release. There's no release manager to wait for, no train to catch, and no reason to hold finished work until an arbitrary date on the calendar.

This is important because the economics of small changes are completely different when you can ship them immediately.

If you only release once a month, there's not much incentive to ship a small improvement independently. It's going to sit alongside dozens of other changes anyway.

But when you can release multiple times a week, every small improvement is an opportunity to get something useful in front of a customer, learn from it, and keep moving.

We would release every day if that were the best experience for users.

In fact, we learned that it wasn't. VS Code users told us that daily upgrade notifications were annoying, so we adjusted to releasing a few times a week.

The point isn't to release as frequently as humanly possible. It's to release as frequently as is useful to our customers.

When you're building on shared core infrastructure, there's a temptation to define a feature as complete only once it's available everywhere.

That sounds reasonable. It also creates unnecessary dependencies and slows down the time it takes to get feedback from real users.

Instead, we aim to build the behavior in Core and release it through one client first.

We get real user feedback without waiting for every client to implement its UI.

The next client can build on functionality that's already been exercised by real users, rather than duplicating the work.

In September 2026, we shipped Swarm, which allows agents to coordinate their work through a shared board. But we didn't ship it all at once.

September 2: Swarm cards and naming landed in VS Code.

September 3: Agent identity and execution state were exposed through shared Core code.

September 14: The shared board became enabled by default, after initially being opt-in.

September 15: Swarm became a top-level configuration setting.

September 17: JetBrains merged support for the board.

By the time Swarm was enabled by default, users had already been using it for nearly two weeks.

We'd already found and fixed issues, including a particularly fun one where agents could message themselves.

We didn't need to wait for the entire experience to be complete before we could learn from it. And we didn't need JetBrains to be ready before VS Code users could benefit.

There's a tradeoff here that we have to be intentional about.

Shipping to one client first is a great way to move quickly. But if we aren't disciplined about following through, we end up with a fragmented product where functionality depends on which client someone happens to use.

Enhance Prompt is an example of where we could have done better.

Enhance Prompt rewrites a user's prompt before they send it. We first shipped it in VS Code in March 2026.

JetBrains got it in June. Agent Manager got it in July. And Desktop doesn't have it yet.

The underlying functionality was already in Core. What was missing was the client-specific UI to expose it.

So why did it take four months to get from the first client to the third?

Not because the implementation was particularly complicated. Because we didn't treat the other clients as explicit follow-up work that needed to get done.

Swarm's identity, board, and configuration were built into Core from the beginning. JetBrains support landed just 15 days after the initial VS Code experience.

The difference wasn't that one feature was easy and the other was hard.

The difference was that with Swarm, we knew which clients needed to come next. With Enhance Prompt, we added support when someone noticed it was missing.

That's a process problem, not a technical one.

And the solution isn't to slow down the first release until every client is ready.

It's to make sure we know what needs to happen next, who owns it, and that the work doesn't disappear after the initial release.

There's a saying in engineering that sometimes you have to go slow to go fast.

Building shared infrastructure in Core, rather than implementing functionality independently in each client, can require more work upfront. But it pays off when the second, third, and fourth clients can reuse that functionality.

Swarm is a great example of making that investment early.

But I think engineers overapply this principle.

Too often, "going slow to go fast" becomes an excuse to keep designing, keep planning, or keep polishing instead of putting something in front of users.

Most decisions are reversible. Especially when you can ship a correction a few days later.

I'd much rather have a team make a decision today, ship something useful, and learn that they need to adjust it next week than spend a month trying to make the perfect decision without any real feedback.

The fastest way to learn whether something works is to let someone use it.

This is why our release cadence matters so much. It makes the cost of being wrong relatively low, and the value of learning quickly incredibly high.

But none of that works if we confuse making a change with delivering value.

I think one of the easiest traps for engineering teams to fall into is measuring progress by what they've completed internally.

The PR is merged. The demo looks good. The ticket is closed.

But none of those things mean a customer has received any value.

This is especially important when we're working in Core, because the distance between merging code and putting it in front of a user is easy to overlook.

So here's what I expect from engineers working on shared functionality:

Build shared behavior in Core. If multiple clients will need the functionality, don't implement it independently in each one.

Pick the first client and ship it. Don't wait for parity across all clients before getting value to users.

Make follow-up work explicit. Know which other clients need support and who is responsible for delivering it.

Own the release. Any engineer can cut a release. If your work is ready, don't wait for someone else to schedule it.

Measure completion by customer value. The work isn't done just because the code is merged. It's done when someone can actually use it.

Ultimately, I don't care how many PRs we merge or how many features we demo internally.

I care how quickly we can turn engineering work into something useful for our customers.

HTTP/3 Changes API Debugging: Observe at the Termination Point

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 17 • Edited on Nov 17 • Originally published at api7.ai • AI-assisted

Originally published by Yilia Lin on API7 on 2026-11-17.

This republication was prepared with AI assistance. The original article’s drafting process has not been verified.

When an HTTP/1.1 API fails, engineers often start with a proxy or packet capture. HTTP/3 changes what those tools can see. The exchange still has a request method, path, and headers plus a response status, but it travels over QUIC on UDP, its protected packets do not expose application data, and its headers use QPACK rather than HTTP/2's HPACK.

A recent hands-on post about intercepting HTTP/3 traffic reached Hacker News and sparked a useful trust-boundary discussion. The operational lesson is broader than one interception tool: API teams need to observe HTTP semantics where the protocol is terminated, then correlate that evidence with the gateway and service hops that follow.

The goal is not to decrypt production traffic everywhere. It is to give each layer enough trustworthy telemetry to answer whether failure occurred during HTTP/3 discovery, QUIC connection establishment, edge processing, gateway policy, upstream connection, or application execution.

RFC 9114 maps HTTP semantics onto QUIC. QUIC version 1 runs over UDP and uses TLS 1.3 or later as its handshake protocol. HTTP/3 is selected with the h3 ALPN token, and an HTTPS origin can advertise an alternative HTTP/3 service with Alt-Svc.

That produces a different wire path from HTTP/2:

The arrows are separate connections. A successful HTTP/3 client-to-edge request does not prove the edge-to-gateway or gateway-to-service hop used HTTP/3. Conversely, a gateway trace can show a healthy upstream request while omitting a delay that occurred before the edge finished QUIC setup.

QUIC packet protection is not an optional wrapper. RFC 9001 derives packet-protection keys from TLS and protects handshake, 0-RTT, and 1-RTT packets at different encryption levels. Initial packets use derivable keys and are not confidential, but application data is protected. A raw capture alone should not be expected to reveal request headers and bodies.

HTTP/3 also replaces HPACK with QPACK. QPACK uses encoder and decoder streams to coordinate compression state without assuming the total ordering available to HTTP/2 over TCP. That means “find the header bytes in this packet” is the wrong debugging model even after stream data is available.

No single view is “the truth” for the whole request. The useful unit is a correlated timeline with the trust and protocol boundary of each timestamp stated explicitly.

For example, an edge may return a 503 before forwarding anything. The gateway correctly has no trace. Or the edge may forward a request and the gateway may reject it before the service receives it. Missing application telemetry then reflects policy timing, not necessarily broken tracing.

RFC 9114 says connectivity problems such as blocked UDP can prevent a QUIC connection and clients should attempt a TCP-based HTTP version. That improves reachability, but it can conceal an HTTP/3 regression.

Do not infer that a 200 response proves HTTP/3 worked. A client may have retried over HTTP/2. Likewise, do not label every UDP failure an attack; enterprise firewalls, carrier networks, NAT behavior, and stale advertisements can all affect the path.

Canary HTTP/3 separately from the TCP fallback. Synthetic checks should report both the final HTTP result and the negotiated transport. During a rollback, remove or shorten advertisements deliberately and account for client caching rather than assuming every client immediately returns to the old path.

The W3C Trace Context specification defines traceparent and tracestate headers for propagating request context. Because HTTP semantics survive the transport change, a trusted edge can forward trace context to the gateway and application.

Trust still matters. A public client can supply traceparent, tracestate, or a request ID. Decide whether the edge accepts, validates, replaces, or links that context. A correlation value is useful evidence but is not a caller identity, authorization result, or proof that two events came from the same trusted actor.

Avoid placing credentials, full query strings, or personal data into span attributes. High-cardinality connection identifiers can also create cost and privacy problems; retain only the detail required for a bounded investigation.

API7's API observability guide explains how metrics, logs, traces, and SLOs complement one another. HTTP/3 adds another reason not to ask one signal to carry the entire investigation.

If an HTTP/3-capable CDN, load balancer, or edge proxy terminates QUIC and forwards HTTP/2 or HTTP/1.1, Apache APISIX can observe the gateway-facing request and its upstream work. That does not make the APISIX span an HTTP/3 handshake span.

In Apache APISIX 3.19, the opentelemetry plugin reports tracing data and supports binary-encoded OTLP over HTTP. The plugin is disabled in the default plugin list and must be enabled in configuration. On a route, its documented sampler defaults to always_off, so enabling the plugin binary without setting an appropriate sampler does not mean route spans will appear.

The same documentation notes that comprehensive request-lifecycle tracing adds span creation and export overhead that can affect throughput and latency. Choose sampling from the incident and SLO requirements, test collector failure behavior, and avoid changing every route to always_on during a traffic spike without estimating the added load.

This version-pinned description is intentionally narrow. It does not claim APISIX 3.19 accepts client HTTP/3 connections, exports QUIC transport internals, or reconstructs telemetry that an outer edge never forwarded. Collect HTTP/3 connection evidence at the component that actually terminates QUIC.

When decoded wire evidence is necessary, reproduce with a client and server you control. Wireshark documents TLS decryption using a key log file, and HTTP/3-aware tooling can combine secrets with QUIC and QPACK dissection.

Treat those secrets as sensitive. Do not enable broad production key logging merely to make packet captures convenient. A trusted interception proxy also changes the trust path: the client must trust its certificate authority, and certificate-pinned applications may reject it. Results from that lab prove behavior under the lab's trust configuration, not that arbitrary encrypted traffic can be inspected.

Document the client, QUIC implementation, edge configuration, and gateway version. HTTP/3 behavior can differ across implementations even when the HTTP contract is the same.

HTTP/3 does not make APIs unobservable. It makes the termination boundary impossible to ignore.

Start by drawing the real connection graph. Mark where QUIC ends, which protocol begins on the next hop, who generates the trusted request ID, and where each metric or span is recorded. Then test HTTP/3 success, UDP failure with fallback, edge rejection, gateway rejection, and upstream failure as separate cases.

For the gateway hop, review end-to-end tracing with OpenTelemetry and the Apache APISIX observability plugins. For managed API traffic policy and observability, explore API7 Enterprise while keeping HTTP/3 transport evidence at the component that terminates it.

Meta's Pipeline Migration Playbook for Data Engineer Interviews

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The worst migration I ever ran finished at 3:40am with a Slack message that said "looks good." 2 weeks later, finance found out a join key had changed type in the new system, and about 6% of revenue rows had been quietly landing as nulls. We'd checked that the job went green and never compared the outputs, so a green job was all we got.

So when Meta published its write-up on migrating data ingestion systems at Meta scale in May, I read it twice. The system incrementally scrapes several petabytes of social graph data from MySQL into the warehouse every day. The post says they had "tens of thousands of ingestion jobs to migrate," and they finished with this line: "We've successfully transitioned 100% of the workload and fully deprecated the legacy system."

I think this post is the best primary-source answer you'll get to the most common pipeline question in a Data Engineer system design interview: "How would you migrate this safely?" It's also the template you should be using if you're staring down the Airflow 3.4 upgrade right now.

Every job went through 3 phases: shadow, reverse shadow, and cleanup. That's the whole skeleton. It's simple enough to draw on a whiteboard in 90 seconds, and that's a big part of why it works.

In the shadow phase, the new job reads the same source as the production job but writes to a separate shadow table, so production output stays untouched. Meta continuously compared row counts and checksums between the production and shadow outputs, and "any mismatches were logged to Scuba, Meta's data management system for real-time analysis." Mismatches got fixed before anything moved forward.

In reverse shadow, the roles swap. The new job's output now goes to the production table, and the old job keeps running and writes to the shadow table. Comparisons keep going the whole time. If the outputs diverge, the post says "the job would be rolled back immediately and data consumers would not be impacted."

Cleanup comes once there are no discrepancies. The old job (now the shadow) gets removed, and the new system delivers alone.

The reverse shadow phase is the part most people skip, and it's the clever bit. Rollback is a role swap: the old job is still alive and still writing, so switching back costs you a config change. You never rebuild anything at 3am.

Validation doesn't stop at cutover. Meta kept comparing old and new after the new system owned production. Most migrations I've seen stop checking the moment the switch flips.

There's a catch, and it's my inference rather than something the post states. Cheap rollback has a shelf life. Once cleanup removes the old job, rolling back means rebuilding the old path. So the reverse shadow window is your insurance policy, and you're paying for 2 systems while it runs.

The other detail I love is how Meta handles bad data that already landed. A partition with a quality issue gets marked bad in its metadata. For a delta partition, new data stops landing and an alert goes to a human. For a target partition, "the system would instead select an older partition and merge it with more deltas," and flagged partitions get fixed later with a backfill. Rollback is per partition. You don't nuke the whole pipeline because one Tuesday went sideways.

Every job had to clear 3 gates before moving to the next phase. Verbatim from the post:

For critical tables, they "defined and agreed on extra migration criteria" with the teams depending on them. The post doesn't list what those extra criteria were. And tooling promoted or demoted jobs between stages automatically, so no human was squinting at dashboards deciding whether a job "felt ready."

The bar is non-regression. The new system has to be no worse on latency and compute, and improvement is a bonus. I've watched migrations stall for months because somebody decided the new platform had to be faster on day one. Meta's standard is "match the old thing, then optimize." That's the right economics; engineer time spent chasing a 10% speedup mid-migration is time not spent finishing the migration.

The post also gives zero numeric thresholds. It never names a match percentage, a batch size or a phase duration, so "no difference" is the whole spec. A sharp interviewer will ask how you handle timing skew between 2 live writers, and the post doesn't say. You need to define your own quantitative exit criteria, and saying so out loud in an interview scores points.

Cost shaped the sequencing too. Creating a new ingestion job kicks off "a full dump, which is typically slow and expensive." So Meta held back shadow jobs that still had known bugs, since every retry meant another full dump. They also reused snapshot partitions from the old system as initial snapshots. The post admits that "migration capacity was limited, we could not run all shadow jobs at once," so jobs went in batches grouped by throughput, priority, and special cases.

Most migration advice treats this purely as a correctness problem. At Meta's scale it was a capacity planning problem too, and the most expensive thing in the room was the backfill.

I've sat on enough panels to know how this question usually goes. The candidate says "we'd run both in parallel, validate, then cut over." The interviewer says "validate how?" The candidate says "compare row counts." The interviewer writes "shallow" in their notes. Interview over, mentally.

I haven't found any official rubric for how interviewers score migration answers, so what follows is my structure, built from Meta's post and from what I've seen land on real panels.

Start by sequencing the work instead of migrating everything at once. Group jobs by risk and throughput, and begin with low-criticality, low-volume jobs so you find tooling bugs cheaply. Hold back anything that triggers an expensive backfill until the known bugs are fixed. Then quote the Meta line about limited capacity forcing batches; it shows you've thought about cost.

Next, define the gates before any job moves. Row counts and checksums cover correctness, landing latency and compute/storage cover performance, and critical tables get extra criteria negotiated with their downstream owners. Then put numbers on them, because Meta didn't publish any and your interviewer will want yours. "Zero checksum mismatches across 7 consecutive daily partitions" is an answer. "Make sure the data looks right" is fog.

Design rollback before anything else, because this is where you separate yourself. Stripe's online migrations post lays out the dual-write pattern: dual-write to old and new, move reads to new, move writes to new, remove the old data. They caught mismatches with GitHub's Scientist library. It's a great post, and it doesn't address rollback at all. Meta's reverse shadow does: the old job stays alive as the shadow, so rollback is a swap. Mention both and explain why the second one answers the question the first one leaves open.

Then cover bad data that already landed. In a CDC pipeline, switching back to the old system doesn't undo bad rows sitting in the warehouse. Mark the bad partitions, stop the deltas, alert, backfill. Interviewers rarely hear this, and it's closer to the actual job, which is mostly figuring out why 2M rows went missing last Tuesday and making sure it never happens again.

Close with cleanup: say when the old job dies and what condition triggers it. Most candidates never decommission anything in their answer, which tells me they've never been the person paying for 2 systems for 8 months because nobody would sign off on turning the old one off.

You'll notice none of this mentions a specific tool, because shadow tables, checksums, latency gates, and partition-level backfill work on any warehouse and any orchestrator. The candidate who explains this with Postgres and cron beats the candidate who lists 9 vendor products and can't explain a rollback.

If you want to practice saying this out loud, do it under pressure, with someone interrupting you with follow-ups. For the coding half of the loop we're biased, since it's ours, but for python practice problems, datadriven is great, and the pipeline architecture prompts on it (one has you moving 60 production pipelines to a cloud warehouse with 6am reports that can't miss) are the closest thing we've seen to the follow-up questions you'll actually get.

Airflow 3.4.0 shipped on November 6, and the release notes have enough breaking changes that I'd treat it as a migration, with shadow runs to match. Highlights that will bite somebody:

Meta's checks compare outputs, but the biggest 3.4 change is about how many runs fire. A shadow environment can match production row for row and still trigger downstream Dags 40 times instead of once, so compare run counts and event consumption alongside row counts and checksums.

Meta wrote shadow output to separate tables for a reason, and your shadow Dags need the same isolation. If your shadow Dag writes the same asset URI as production, every Dag scheduled on that asset runs off the shadow write as if production had produced it, so give the shadow Dags their own asset URIs or pause those consumer Dags. Ask me how I know what happens when 2 environments both think they own the same table (actually, don't).

Meta's rollback was cheap because the old job kept running. Airflow's isn't: the downgrade path is slow, and db clean now destroys pending events. That makes shadowing against a cloned metadata DB your rollback window, because once you upgrade the real metadata DB, reverting gets expensive.

Meta's latency and resource gates line up neatly with the new connection pool and the heartbeat default. Neither changes a single output row. Both change DB load and how fast you detect a dead worker. Measure them during the shadow phase, the same way Meta measured "compute and storage quotas for the shadow jobs" before promoting.

Draining a Dag stops new scheduled and asset runs, then pauses the Dag once active runs finish, which gives you a ready-made version of Meta's cleanup step.

If you're a 3-person team with 40 Dags, you don't need Meta's full machinery. Running 2 of everything doubles compute for the length of the window, and Meta's post never quantifies that overhead. I'd cut the backfill first, because it's usually the expensive part. AWS documents a lighter option for its own migrations, sample-read validation, where scheduled jobs compare random subsets from both systems and it "doesn't require application code changes." For a small team, checksums on a sample of partitions plus a short reverse shadow on your 5 most important Dags covers most of the risk. That's my judgment, not a measured result; nobody I've found has published a cost comparison for small teams.
