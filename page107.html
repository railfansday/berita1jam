Sky-high hype: ‘Pilot Picasso’ draws massive GTA 6 mural in 8-hour flight over Arkansas

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Screenshot by Destructoid via @FlightRadar24

The anticipation for Grand Theft Auto 6 has gotten so high, you can even see it if you look up in the air. Kind of.

A 28-year-old commercial pilot and content creator took to the skies over Arkansas in the US for eight hours to create a mural dedicated to GTA 6, saying “I’ve been waiting 11 years for this moment.” It’s actually been 13 years since GTA 5 first released, but the idea still stands.

Benjamin McDaniel, known as The Pilot Picasso, often uses flight trackers to create art in the sky, as evidenced by his Instagram page that’s overflowing with creations. In the past, he’s done all sorts of artwork inspired by different passions such as Spider-Man, The Lord of The Rings, The Odyssey, and even other games like Skyrim.

This time, he wanted to highlight his excitement for Rockstar Games’ latest title, and the detail in the final image is actually pretty wild, caught by @FlightRadar24. Along with spelling out the full GTA 6 title, the air-painting also includes a helicopter, palm trees, an alligator, a sports car, and of course, a non-descript baddie in sunglasses holding a gun.

Apparently, this sort of thing is done by inputting a flight pattern and then following along with lines dictated by tech on-board the plane, but that doesn’t make it any less impressive, especially when you consider the dedication it takes to fly around in circles like this for eight hours or more.

We’re now less than two weeks away from GTA 6 launching, so I’d imagine that the marketing and other publicity stunts like this will be ramping up leading up to release on Nov. 19. I’m all for it.

Now, we can all only hope that this sort of thing can be recreated in GTA 6 itself with in-game planes.

Tech stocks rebound after a massive AI selloff spread to Asia

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The Nasdaq rose 1.9% Friday morning after a 5.5% drop put it in correction territory, while the Dow was down 99 points

The Nasdaq $NDAQ Composite rose 1.9% in morning trading Friday as chip and data center stocks recovered from a selloff on Thursday that cut about $2.5 trillion from the index and spread to Asian markets.

Micron $MU Technology stock climbed nearly 7% Friday, recovering part of Thursday's 17% slide, and Nvidia $NVDA stock gained 6% after dropping 9%. Intel $INTC and CoreWeave stock were also climbing. The S&P 500 gained 1%, but the Dow Jones Industrial Average lagged, off by 99 points for a 0.2% decline.

The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.5% on Thursday and finished over 10% under its latest peak, which puts it in correction territory. The S&P 500 shed 4.4%, and the Dow gave up upward of 1,800 points, a 3.4% slide. Those were the worst one-day losses for the three averages since April 2025, according to CNBC.

Tech-focused benchmarks across Asia declined on Friday, echoing the rout on Wall Street a day earlier. SoftBank stock lost 10% of its value in Tokyo, while indexes in Europe declined by over 1%. In South Korea on Thursday, Samsung Electronics stock dropped 7.5% and SK Hynix stock lost 9.5%.

Investors sold on worries that data center construction could slow after Democrats won the House, the Senate and key governorships, according to CNBC. Top Democrats have pushed to rein in the expansion of data centers ever since the election.

Texas Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa, whose state is a major data center hub, said in an interview Thursday evening, “We will have laws to protect Texans, and we need to stop what we are doing with data centers until we have those protections,” according to CNBC.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that employers added 165,000 jobs in October, and the unemployment rate was 4.1%. Economists polled by Dow Jones had expected a gain of 59,000 jobs and an unemployment rate of 4.2%, according to CNBC.

Investors had sold AI-related chip and data center stocks after the midterm elections on Thursday. Qualcomm $QCOM stock also fell after the chipmaker's earnings beat estimates but its outlook disappointed.

Hinojosa defeated Republican Gov. Greg Abbott $ABT in Texas, according to CNBC. Wells Fargo $WFC estimates Texas holds 27% of planned U.S. data center gigawatts. Republicans hold the majority in the Texas Legislature, which limits how much Hinojosa can change policy.

Despite Friday's gains, all three major averages are headed for weekly losses. The Nasdaq is down 3% this week, the S&P 500 is down 2%, and the Dow is down 1%.

New Laws Could Add an Estimated 103,000 Homes a Year, Study Finds

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

New state laws to encourage homebuilding could bolster the nation's home production by six figures in coming years—but there’s a small catch.

The American Enterprise Institute tracked 10 state bills passed this year and estimates they could potentially lead to 103,000 new homes a year across the country. It's positive progress to solving the nation's housing supply shortage, which is complicated by red tape.

AEI’s data, though, shows that about half of those housing units are accessory dwelling units, or ADUs. But dozens more bills and legislative efforts put forth this year could bring more progress, AEI said at a housing conference in Washinton, DC, this week.

This has been the “year of the starter home,” said Ed Pinto, co-director of AEI’s Housing Center. Thank the bipartisan 21st Century Road to Housing Act, the largest housing reform package in decades. Americans are broadly concerned about affordability, and many young people have grown pessimistic that owning a home will be attainable.

The two largest contributors are California's AB 956 and Washington state's SB 6026.

California's AB 956 loosens requirements around building ADUs. AEI thinks that could result in 48,000 new units a year. Behind that is Washington state's SB 6026, which allows homes in commercially zoned areas. AEI thinks it could mean 37,000 new homes a year.

Other big contributions come from Idaho's SB 1352, which allows homes on smaller lots and could mean 5,000 new homes a year. Kansas' SB 418 brings similar potential in that state. Maine, Virginia, and North Carolina also passed new pro-housing laws.

Tobias Peter, co-director of AEI’s housing center, noted that doesn't necessarily mean every new home will come to fruition. Other kinds of reforms could also unlock hundreds of thousands more homes a year.

At the same time, AEI released a study that found broad support for changes to housing policy. Two rounds of polls of about 5,000 people this year found about 83% support a state law allowing more affordably priced starter homes on smaller lots in new neighborhoods.

AEI found 61% said they’d rather see a torn-down home replaced with two smaller homes, compared to a larger and more expensive home. But people’s opinions trading housing development versus individual property rights can be complicated.

Two of the most popular pushbacks on housing reform fit into two categories. “Supply skeptics” don’t think more homes will improve affordability. Others oppose change to the character or scale of established neighborhoods. Bur rather than housing reform being a "YIMBY versus NIMBY" debate, AEI pictures a spectrum of reform supporters and skeptics.

Some of the largest efforts have focused on the legal red tape around where homes can be built. For instance, several states have put forth laws to allow homes on smaller lots.

But more legislation is needed to correct the slowing construction of starter homes in the nation. Freddie Mac found the U.S. built an average of 418,000 new starter homes a year in the 1970s, 34% of all new housing starts. By 2020 that fell to 65,000 starts, or less than 10%.

“You really need a local champion, a quarterback who organizes at the state level,” Peter said, noting some successful bills where the governor, lieutenant governor, or a group of legislators shepherd a bill. “And it’s also important you have the evidence in your back pocket to counter some of the narratives."

Affordability also requires local governments to respond—because zoning and building code reform, by and large, happens at the local level.

Local government controls some of the most significant levers of affordability, says Yadira Ramos-Herbert, mayor of New Rochelle, NY. And that city's updates to its zoning code have meant incremental progress over time.

New Rochelle developed a form-based code that allows developers to build with fewer requirements around building use. It also streamlined its approval process for new developments, with some taking as few as 90 days to achieve approvals.

The program has helped it approve 4,500 new homes since 2020. New Rochelle, a winner of the University of Utah's 2026 Ivory Prize for Housing Affordability, has seen its median rent decline as national averages surge.

"Our core objective is to ensure New Rochelle remains affordable, accessible, and economically resilient," Ramos-Herbert tells Realtor.com. "Affordability remains the defining challenge of our time, and cities have a responsibility to respond with urgency."

He was previously a senior reporter at Bloomberg Law, and before that covered real estate for the Washington Business Journal. DoJ's 'Super Intelligence Task Force' up and running

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Organization responsible for countering anyone who wishes to scupper the US's ambition for AI supremacy

The US Department of Justice (DoJ) says its Super Intelligence Task Force (SINTF) is now up and running, after president Donald Trump ordered the move in a memo last month.

Headed up by director of national intelligence Jay Clayton, as well as associate attorney general Stanley Woodward and associate deputy attorney general Stephen Weiss, SINTF's responsibility is to protect the US's lead in artificial intelligence development.

Thursday's announcement, which was littered with the "super intelligence" Trumpism, said the task force will ensure the US's SI/AI leadership remains uncompromised by foreign AI "abuse," including by state and state-backed groups, criminal enterprises, individuals, or corporate entities.

Per Trump's October 13 memo [PDF], SITF will take "affirmative measures, as appropriate," to ensure the US stays ahead in AI development. It did not elaborate on what this nebulous phrase meant, but it can be reasonably assumed this means a breadth of proactive steps.

The memo also stated that the task force will organize the DoJ's criminal and civil lawsuits involving AI, recommend enforcement priorities concerning public safety and AI misuse, develop prosecution guidance for unlawful AI conduct, and feed into legislative proposals to prevent "burdensome regulation."

Addressing a Wall Street Journal tech conference this week, Pentagon CTO and SITF vice chair Emil Michael said the team would release details about its AI regulatory landscape later this month.

The task force will also shoulder various cross-departmental responsibilities, such as "deconflicting investigations, cases, and other matters" among departments and the offices of US attorneys.

According to quotes from senior officials, chief among the concerns facing ordinary US citizens are AI attacks that target individuals, as well as those that aim to disrupt critical infrastructure and other national security interests.

"The creation of the DOJ-SITF marks an important moment for the Department to help advance SI technologies and, at the same time, protect American innovation," said Weiss.

"The Department also has a responsibility to utilize law enforcement tools at our disposal to deter improper use of SI, harden regular reporting on SI risk and response, and ensure companies exercise vigorous internal controls so that SI technology behaves as intended. The Department is committed to protecting all Americans and our business interests."

Trump compelled the US to start using the term "super intelligence" in September, claiming "artificial intelligence," the widely used and original term coined in the mid-1950s, was a better descriptor for the technology's capabilities.

The US government's dedicated website for SI states that "America is in a race for Super Intelligence. We intend to win it."

It also highlights a number of initiatives the government has taken to promote its AI supremacy, including legal guidance to streamline federal approvals, leading to more data centers being built.

The pivot toward renaming the technology in all official capacities raised confusion at the time.

A somewhat contradictory move, the Trump administration heavily promotes its own AI innovators, but in one swoop essentially antiquated the trading name of arguably its most recognizable AI lab, OpenAI.

One financier previously floated the idea that Trump ordered the shift to boost the value of .si domain names, many of which have recently been registered using MAGA-related terms and resold for millions of dollars. The Swissport Lounge at Chicago O’Hare Airport (ORD) Terminal 5 has just quietly closed. I’m about as sad as I’ll be on the day when Delta retires its latest Boeing 767-300ER…

Historically, Chicago O’Hare Terminal 5 has been a pretty lousy facility, though it has started to get better in recent years, thanks to an expansion to the terminal, and also thanks to the opening of new lounges.

Arguably the single most disappointing thing about Terminal 5 was the Swissport Lounge, which I’ve labeled the worst lounge in the United States — clearly many people agreed with me, since my review of the lounge had over 200 comments. It’s just truly one of those facilities that makes you wonder why we even bother using lounges.

The lounge had no windows, the most random collection of furniture, and a very limited selection of drinks and snacks. Unless you wanted to just guzzle alcohol, you’d be better off sitting in the terminal, in my opinion. Unfortunately for all too long, many premium airlines sent their customers here, given the lack of other lounge options. It’s quite underwhelming to be flying business class on a carrier like Etihad Airways or Qatar Airways, and then be sent here.

That brings us to the interesting update — the Swissport Lounge Chicago has closed as of October 31, 2026, so passengers are no longer being subjected to this facility.

It remains to be seen what happens with this lounge space, as that hasn’t been announced one way or another. In April 2025, Swissport’s head of North America lounges said that the company had “submitted a proposal to the Chicago Department of Aviation (CDA) for a multi-million-dollar renovation and expansion of our current Terminal 5 lounge at O’Hare International Airport.” However, I haven’t been able to find any updates on that, so I’m not sure what the latest is there.

If we get another lounge at Terminal 5, that would of course be awesome, so I’m curious to see how this evolves. Chicago O’Hare is the biggest airport in the country to not have any sort of a credit card branded lounge, so I hope that eventually changes. The issue is the extent to which the terminals are dominated by their primary tenants (American and United), combined with the lack of airside connections to Terminal 5.

The Swissport Lounge Chicago O’Hare Terminal 5 has suddenly closed. I’m not sad to see the United States’ worst airport lounge close, though I am curious to see what is next for this space. Fortunately the Terminal 5 lounge situation has improved nicely in recent years, and there are now several solid new options. I do wish we’d still see a proper credit card lounge, though.

Several years ago, rummaging through the archives of the Academy of American Poets, I came upon a box labeled “Ballots 1950” — the record of the secret vote by the chancellors the year the Academy’s prestigious fellowship was awarded to E.E. Cummings, catapulting him into renown. The voting process is a black box — no one outside the Academy ever finds out who else is in the running and by how much the winner wins.

Leafing through the ballots, one other name appeared over and over, so much so that I was impelled to count.

Marianne Moore had lost by one vote, never knowing how close she had come. It would be many more years until, at 77, she was finally awarded the fellowship.

Long before that, before she won the Pulitzer Prize and the National Book Award (sharing a table with Rachel Carson at the ceremony), Moore had set down her views on writing in a series of essays later collected in the out-of-print gem Predilections (public library). Pulsating through them is a reckoning with the impossible task of the writer — to weave tapestries of truth and meaning from the tenuous thread of words on the ramshackle loom of language.

In an essay titled “Feeling and Precision,” Moore writes:

Feeling at its deepest — as we all have reason to know — tends to be inarticulable. If it does manage to be articulate, it is likely to seem overcondensed, so that the author is resisted as being enigmatic or disobliging or arrogant.

How we name what we feel is not so much a matter of our writing style as of our style of being, because in order to articulate something we must first apprehend it and we apprehend every smallest thing with the whole person — with the frame of reference that is our entire life, the sum of our experience and memory. When “one of New York’s more painstaking magazines” asked Moore to distill her poetic style into a formula, she fought back the “dictatorial” reflex to quip:

You don’t devise a rhythm, the rhythm is the person, and the sentence but a radiograph of the personality.

And yet a personality can write with more or less persuasion — that is, write more or less well — depending on what the person brings to the writing. In another essay from the collection, Moore identifies the three psychological elements necessary for persuasive writing: “humility, concentration, and gusto.” A generation after Mark Twain assured his friend Helen Keller when she was accused of plagiarism that “substantially all ideas are second-hand, consciously and unconsciously drawn from a million outside sources,” she writes:

Humility… is armor, for it realizes that it is impossible to be original, in the sense of doing something that has never been thought of before. Originality is in any case a by-product of sincerity; that is to say, of feeling that is honest and accordingly rejects anything that might cloud the impression.

By “concentration” Moore means a kind of discipline — annealing the essence of the sentiment by cutting away all superfluous explanations, elaborations, and distractions of stylistic posturing, being maximally truthfully in the most minimal way possible. Observing that there is always a “helpless sincerity which precipitates a poem” and that a good poem is always “a concentrate,” she writes:

Concentration — indispensable to persuasion — may feel to itself crystal clear, yet be through its very compression the opposite… I myself would rather be told too little than too much.

Long before we had the language of extrinsic and intrinsic motivation, of finite and infinite games, Moore uses a lovely word, now dusty, for that peculiar private zeal propelling all creative work with its twin dynamos of discipline and deliverance: “gusto.” Echoing Rachel Carson’s abiding advice on writing — “If you write what you yourself sincerely think and feel and are interested in,” she had counseled a young writer, “the chances are very high that you will interest other people as well.” — Moore offers:

Gusto thrives on freedom, and freedom in art, as in life, is the result of a discipline imposed by ourselves. Moreover, any writer overwhelmingly honest about pleasing himself is almost sure to please others.

She maps the fundamental relationship between the three:

Humility is an indispensable teacher, enabling concentration to heighten gusto.

When creating in integrity with these three values, it ceases to matter how the work is received because the process of locating and articulating the truth as you feel it, the world as you see it, is its own reward. In what may be the best advice I have encountered on how to orient to your own work, Moore writes:

There are always objecters, but we must not be sensitive about not being liked or not being printed… The thing is to see the vision and not deny it; or care and admit that we do.

Complement with Walt Whitman on how to keep criticism from sinking your soul and Mary Oliver’s advice on writing, then savor the moving story of how Marianne Moore saved a rare tree with a poem.
